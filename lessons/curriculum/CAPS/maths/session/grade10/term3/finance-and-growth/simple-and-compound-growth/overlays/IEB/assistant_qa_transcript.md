**Thandi:** Before everyone logs off — a few questions came in from the class. The most popular one: both deals used the very same 7 percent, so how can compound finish with more money? Shouldn't the rate decide everything?

**Tutor:** A sharp question, because it exposes the half of the story people miss: a rate means nothing until you know what it is charged ON. Under simple interest the base is frozen — every year the 7 percent is taken of the original four thousand rand, so every year pays exactly two hundred and eighty rand, no matter how the balance grows. Under compound interest, each year's payment is folded into the balance, so the base itself swells: year two charges 7 percent on four thousand two hundred and eighty, year three on four thousand five hundred and seventy-nine rand sixty. Same rate, growing base, growing payments. The sixty rand and seventeen cents of difference is entirely the earnings of earlier interest. So whenever you compare offers, ask two questions, not one: what is the rate, and what does it get charged on?

**Thandi:** Here's one I got twice in the break: buying a laptop on hire purchase feels like borrowing, and borrowing sounds like the compound story. So why does hire purchase get the SIMPLE formula?

**Tutor:** Follow the contract and the mathematics follows. In a hire-purchase agreement the shop performs one interest calculation, up front, on the full original price — ten percent per year times the number of years, all of it charged on the price tag — and then splits the grand total into equal monthly instalments. Nothing is ever added to a growing balance, so there is no enlarged base for next year's interest to feed on: the base stays parked at the original price, which is precisely the simple-interest shape. Hence the working rule for any assessment: the words hire purchase or simple interest commit you to the bracket formula; the words compound, compounded annually, inflation or population commit you to the power formula. The vocabulary of the question does the choosing for you.

**Thandi:** Last one, and honestly it's mine: if compound grows faster, why would anybody ever accept the simple deal? Is compound just better, full stop?

**Tutor:** Better depends on which end of the money you are holding. When the money is yours — savings, an investment, a unit trust one day — compounding fights for you: your interest earns interest, and every extra year multiplies the effect. When the money is owed, the same engine runs against you: an unpaid debt becomes the base for the next round of interest, which is why neglected loans swell so alarmingly. Inflation is the same machinery aimed at prices — each year's increase stands on last year's, which is why the taxi fare your grandfather paid sounds impossible now. So the mature verdict is that compound growth is neither kind nor cruel — it is powerful, in whichever direction it is pointed. Learn to see which way it points at you, and this one lesson will keep paying you back for the rest of your life.
