# Part 1 — Expert

Today's work answers the question an average can never answer: how SPREAD OUT is the data? The tools are the measures of dispersion — range, quartiles, the interquartile range, percentiles — and the five-number summary they assemble into, drawn as the box-and-whisker diagram. One dataset of eleven tuck-shop sales figures runs through the whole session, and by the close you will compute every measure, build the diagram from its summary, and read someone else's diagram like a report.

## Subtopic: Range and Why Spread Matters

Two small datasets, both test scores out of 100, both with a mean of exactly 50. Dataset A: 45, 48, 50, 52, 55. Dataset B: 20, 35, 50, 65, 80. Identical means — completely different stories. Dataset A is a consistent performer: every mark hugs the 50. Dataset B swings wildly from failing to excellent. Any summary that reports only the centre treats these two as identical, which is why every serious data report carries TWO kinds of number: a centre, and a spread.

The simplest spread measure is the RANGE: maximum minus minimum. Dataset A: 55 minus 45 is 10. Dataset B: 80 minus 20 is 60. One subtraction each, and the difference between the datasets is now visible in a number.

But the range has a weakness you must be able to name: it uses ONLY the two most extreme values, so a single outlier owns it completely. Add one learner with a mark of 98 to dataset A and its range explodes from 10 to 53, even though the other five marks still huddle around 50. The range is quick, fragile, and easily distorted — which is exactly why the quartile tools in the next section exist.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. They test the calculation and, more importantly, the weakness: know what the range cannot see.

## Subtopic: Quartiles and the Interquartile Range

The running dataset arrives: a tuck shop recorded how many toasted sandwiches it sold on eleven school days. Ordered: 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13, 15, 17, 18, 20, 25.

Quartiles cut the ordered data into four equal-sized groups, using three cut points. The middle cut is the median, also called the second quartile. Eleven values: the median is the sixth, five below and five above — count in: 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, then 13. The median is 13.

The LOWER quartile, Q1, is the median of the lower half — the five values below the median: 4, 7, 8, 10, 12. Their middle is the third: 8. So Q1 is 8. The UPPER quartile, Q3, is the median of the upper half: 15, 17, 18, 20, 25. Middle value: 18. So Q3 is 18. With an odd number of values, the median itself joins neither half — it is the fence between them, not a resident.

Now the star measure: the INTERQUARTILE RANGE, IQR, is Q3 minus Q1 — 18 minus 8, which is 10. Read its meaning precisely: the middle 50 percent of all the days sold between 8 and 18 sandwiches, and that middle half spans just 10 units. The IQR measures the spread of the middle half, so extreme days cannot touch it: the quiet day of 4 and the rush day of 25 sit outside the quartiles entirely. The IQR is the outlier-resistant range. The semi-interquartile range is simply half of it — 10 over 2 is 5 — sometimes requested by name, never harder than one halving.

Full range for comparison: 25 minus 4 is 21. Range 21, IQR 10: the extremes stretch twice as wide as the middle half — that comparison is already analysis.

Stop for this section's questions now. Order, cut, and remember: lower half's middle, upper half's middle, subtract.

## Subtopic: The Five-Number Summary and the Box-and-Whisker Diagram

Line up the five numbers you now own, smallest to largest: the minimum 4, then Q1 at 8, the median 13, Q3 at 18, and the maximum 25. That ordered list is the FIVE-NUMBER SUMMARY, and it is the complete skeleton of the dataset — centre and spread in five values.

The box-and-whisker diagram is that summary made visual, and you must be able to build it from words alone. Start with a horizontal number line covering the data — from 0 to about 30 here. Above it, draw a rectangular box that starts at Q1, which is 8, and ends at Q3, which is 18: the box IS the middle half of the data, and its length IS the IQR. Inside the box, draw a vertical line at the median, 13. Then the whiskers: a horizontal line from the left wall of the box out to the minimum at 4, and another from the right wall out to the maximum at 25. Five landmarks, one box, two whiskers — finished.

Now the reading rule that unlocks every interpretation question: each of the four sections — left whisker, left half of the box, right half of the box, right whisker — contains ONE QUARTER of the data. Roughly 25 percent of the days live in each section, always, no matter how long or short a section looks. A LONG section does not hold more days; it holds the same quarter of days spread over a wider stretch of values. Long section, spread-out quarter; short section, tightly bunched quarter. That single sentence is the entire art of reading these diagrams.

Check ours: the left whisker runs from 4 to 8 — four units for a quarter of the days, tightly packed quiet days. The right whisker runs 18 to 25 — seven units for the same quarter: the busy days are more spread out.

Pause now for this section's questions — build the diagram in your head from the five numbers, and read sections as quarters.

## Subtopic: Percentiles, Skew and Comparing Two Box Plots

Three finishing tools turn computation into commentary.

First, percentiles — the fine-grained cousins of quartiles. The p-th percentile is the value below which p percent of the data lies. Three of them you already know by other names: Q1 is the 25th percentile, the median is the 50th, Q3 is the 75th. So "the 75th percentile of sales was 18" and "Q3 was 18" are the same sentence. If a bursary requires the 90th percentile in a test, it demands a mark beaten by only 10 percent of candidates — percentiles rank you against the group, not against the paper.

Second, skew. A perfectly symmetrical dataset puts its median in the middle of the box with equal whiskers. When the right side stretches longer — median closer to Q1, long right whisker, like our sandwich data — the data is skewed to the right, also called positively skewed: most values bunch low with a tail of large values. Stretched left side: skewed to the left, negatively skewed. Say it with the evidence: "the median lies nearer to the lower quartile and the upper whisker is longer, so the distribution is skewed to the right."

Third, comparison — the favourite examination format: two box plots on the same number line. Compare them in two sentences, one per idea. Sentence one, centres: which median is higher — that group typically scored more. Sentence two, spreads: which box is longer — that group's middle half varied more, so it was less consistent. Class A with median 60 and IQR 12 against class B with median 55 and IQR 30: A scored higher on a typical day AND was more consistent. Never compare only centres; the memo reserves marks for the spread sentence.

The compressed method: order the data; median, then quartiles from the halves; five-number summary; box on the quartiles, whiskers to the extremes; read every section as a quarter; comment on centre, spread and skew. # Part 2 — Simplifier

The same tools again now, rebuilt with your hands — folding a line of people, stretching an elastic band, comparing two taxi routes. Nothing new arrives: the five numbers will land exactly where they landed. What changes is that spread will stop being a formula and become something you can feel.

## Subtopic: Same Average, Different Story

Two taxi routes both claim the same thing on their poster: average trip to town, 30 minutes. Route one is run by a careful association: trips take 28, 29, 30, 31 or 32 minutes. Route two depends on a driver who waits until the taxi is full: trips take 15, 20, 30, 40 or 45 minutes. Both average exactly 30 — yet you would never call them the same service. On route one you can promise your boss an arrival time. On route two you might be early, or you might be very late, and the "average" printed on the poster hides that completely.

The lesson inside the story: an average tells you the CENTRE of the data; it says nothing about how far the data WANDERS from that centre. Spread is the wandering. Two datasets can share a centre and differ wildly in spread, and the spread is often the fact you actually care about — reliability, consistency, risk.

The quickest wandering-meter is the range: longest trip minus shortest. Route one: 32 minus 28, a range of 4 minutes. Route two: 45 minus 15, a range of 30. One number each, and the poster's lie is exposed. But notice the range's blind spot: one single freak trip — a breakdown, a two-hour delay — and the range balloons, even if every other trip was perfect. It measures only the two most extreme stories, never the everyday ones. Keep that weakness in mind; the next section builds the fix.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on centre versus spread are coming to you right now. Ask of each scenario: is this about where the middle is, or about how far things wander?

## Subtopic: Folding the Line in Half, Then in Half Again

The tuck shop again: eleven days of sandwich sales — 4, 7, 8, 10, 12, 13, 15, 17, 18, 20, 25. Imagine the eleven days as eleven learners standing in a line, ordered, each holding their number.

Fold one: find the middle of the whole line. Eleven people — the sixth is dead centre, five on each side. The sixth holds 13. That is the median, the 50-50 cut: half the days sold fewer, half sold more.

Fold two: take the five people LEFT of the fold and find THEIR middle — the third person, holding 8. Fold three: the five people RIGHT of the fold — their middle holds 18. Three folds, three fence posts: 8, 13, 18. The quartiles are nothing more than the middle of the whole line, then the middles of each half. And the person AT the first fold, holding 13, stands on the fence — with eleven people, the middle person belongs to neither half.

Now read what the fences did: they split eleven days into four little groups, roughly equal in size. The middle two groups together — the days between 8 and 18 — are the middle half of all days, the ordinary days, no quiet extremes and no rush-day madness. How wide is ordinary? From 8 to 18: ten sandwiches wide. That width is the interquartile range — and because it is measured between the fences, the wild days at 4 and 25 cannot stretch it. The IQR is the range that ignores drama. Half of it, 5, is the semi-interquartile range — just the same fact, halved.

Your questions for this section are up now. Fold, fold, fold: middle of the line, middle of each half, and read the width between the fences.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Box in Words

The five numbers of the tuck shop, in order: 4, 8, 13, 18, 25 — minimum, first fence, middle, second fence, maximum. The box-and-whisker diagram is just these five landmarks drawn on a number line, and you can narrate it with your eyes closed.

Lay the number line down. At 8 and 18, raise two walls and join them into a box — the box is home to the middle half of the days, the ordinary days. Draw the median line inside at 13. From each wall, stretch a whisker: left to the minimum 4, right out to the maximum 25. The picture says: ordinary days live in the box; the whiskers reach out to the quietest and busiest days ever seen.

The one rule that makes you dangerous at reading these: every section holds a QUARTER of the days. The stubby left whisker, 4 to 8: a quarter of the days, squeezed tight — quiet days are all alike. The long right whisker, 18 to 25: the SAME quarter-sized crowd, stretched wide — busy days come in many sizes. Long never means many; long means spread. Say it before every reading question.

And when two boxes sit on one number line — two classes, two routes, two months — your comparison has two sentences, always. Where are the median lines? Higher median, typically bigger values. How long are the boxes? Longer box, more wandering in the middle half — less consistency. Centre sentence, spread sentence, and if the shape is lopsided, one bonus phrase: long right side means skewed to the right, a tail of big values.

You now own the whole toolkit: range with its known weakness, the three folds, the five landmarks, the box you can draw from words, and the quarter-per-section rule that reads any box back. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now — five numbers, four quarters, two sentences of comparison. Finish strong.
