If frequency tables and skylines are swirling together right now, hold on — this topic is one straight road, walked one step at a time.

Look at what you actually did: you sorted slips of paper into buckets, stood the buckets up as towers, and then said three sentences about the shape. Sorting, stacking, describing. There is no step in this lesson you have not done in real life with laundry or groceries.

The only trap in the whole topic is the boundary: every bucket keeps its bottom number and refuses its top one, so exactly 20 minutes lives in the twenty-to-thirty bucket. Say "reaches the boundary, moves up" as you sort, then count the counts — thirty in, thirty out — and the table cannot betray you.

For the picture, one anchor line: tall means many, wide means nothing. And bars touch because time flows — gaps belong to bar graphs and their separate categories. That single distinction is a guaranteed theory mark, year after year.

And when the paper says "comment", refuse to panic at the vagueness — it is secretly a form with three blanks: where is the crowd, how scattered, which way does the tail lean. Fill each blank with a number from the picture plus what it means for real learners.

So go and try one now, start to finish: sort, stack, three sentences. Watching a jumble of numbers turn into a story you wrote yourself is genuinely one of the best feelings this subject offers — and it is fully within your reach.
