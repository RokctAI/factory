# Part 1 — Expert

Today's work is the arithmetic of "typical": the mean, the median and the mode — the three measures of central tendency that compress a whole dataset into one representative number. One dataset of homework minutes runs through the first half of the session, a grouped frequency table takes over in the second, and by the close you will compute all three measures, defend your choice between them, and estimate a mean you cannot compute exactly.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median and Mode for Ungrouped Data

The running dataset: nine learners recorded how many minutes each spent on maths homework yesterday. In order: 12, 14, 15, 15, 18, 21, 23, 25 and 28.

The mean first — the share-out-equally number. Add every value and divide by how many there are. The sum: 12 plus 14 is 26; plus 15 is 41; plus 15 is 56; plus 18 is 74; plus 21 is 95; plus 23 is 118; plus 25 is 143; plus 28 is 171. Nine values, so the mean is 171 over 9, which is exactly 19 minutes. Interpretation: if the total effort were shared perfectly evenly, each learner would sit at 19 minutes.

The median next — the middle value of the ORDERED list. Order first, always; an unordered median is meaningless, and examiners plant unordered data deliberately. Nine values means the middle one is the fifth: four below it, four above. Count in: 12, 14, 15, 15, and the fifth is 18. The median is 18 minutes. For an EVEN number of values there is no single middle, so you take the mean of the two middle values — hold that rule; it returns shortly.

The mode last — the value that appears MOST often. Only 15 appears twice; everything else appears once. The mode is 15 minutes. A dataset can have one mode, two modes, or no mode at all when nothing repeats.

Three answers from one dataset: mean 19, median 18, mode 15. Three different numbers, all legitimately "the centre" — which is precisely why the next section exists.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. Order the data before anything else, and keep the three definitions separate.

## Subtopic: Outliers and Choosing the Right Measure

A tenth learner's time now arrives late: 120 minutes — two full hours. Watch what this single extreme value, an OUTLIER, does to each measure.

The mean: the sum climbs from 171 to 291, and dividing by ten gives 29,1 minutes. Read that against the data: nine of the ten learners are BELOW 29,1 — the "typical" value is now typical of nobody. The mean got dragged, because the mean feels every value; one giant pulls the share-out upward the way one very tall learner raises a class's average height.

The median: ten values now, so the median is the mean of the fifth and sixth values — 18 and 21 — which is 19,5 minutes. It barely moved. The median only cares about position in the ordered line, not size; the outlier is just one more body at the end of the queue. We say the median is RESISTANT to outliers.

The mode: still 15. Repetition did not change at all.

The examination skill is choosing and JUSTIFYING. When data is roughly symmetrical with no extremes, the mean is best — it uses every value. When outliers or strong skew are present — house prices, incomes, that 120-minute learner — report the median, and say why: the median is not affected by extreme values, the mean is pulled toward them. The mode earns its keep for categorical-style data — the most popular shoe size ordered by a shop — where adding and averaging make no sense. One sentence of justification is a mark; memorise its shape: "the median, because the data contains an outlier which distorts the mean."

Stop for this section's questions now — they hand you datasets with and without extremes and ask you to choose, and to defend the choice.

## Subtopic: Grouped Data and the Estimated Mean

Real datasets are often too large to list, so they arrive GROUPED. Forty learners wrote a test, marked as a percentage, and the results come as a frequency table with five class intervals. From 30 up to but not including 40: four learners. From 40 to below 50: six. From 50 to below 60: eleven. From 60 to below 70: twelve. From 70 to below 80: seven. The frequencies total 40 — always check the total first; a miscounted table poisons everything after it.

Here is the honest problem: the table has destroyed the individual marks. Eleven learners sit somewhere between 50 and 60, but nobody knows where. An exact mean is impossible — so we estimate, with one fair assumption: treat every learner in an interval as sitting at the MIDPOINT of that interval. The midpoint of 30 to 40 is 35 — add the endpoints, halve them. The midpoints run 35, 45, 55, 65, 75.

Now the estimated mean: multiply each midpoint by its frequency, add, divide by the total. Four times 35 is 140. Six times 45 is 270. Eleven times 55 is 605. Twelve times 65 is 780. Seven times 75 is 525. The grand total: 140 plus 270 is 410; plus 605 is 1 015; plus 780 is 1 795; plus 525 is 2 320. Divide by 40: the estimated mean is 58 percent.

Say the word ESTIMATED in your answer. The true mean of the forty actual marks is almost certainly not exactly 58 — the midpoint assumption is a fair guess, not a fact — and the memorandum rewards the candidate who knows the difference.

Pause now for this section's questions. Midpoints, products, total, divide — and check that frequency column before you trust it.

## Subtopic: Modal Interval, Median Interval and Reading the Table

Grouped data cannot give an exact mode or median either — it gives intervals, and finding them costs no arithmetic at all, only careful reading.

The MODAL interval is the class with the highest frequency. Scan the frequency column: 4, 6, 11, 12, 7. The largest is 12, so the modal interval is 60 up to below 70. Not 12 — the ANSWER is the interval, naming the class where marks pile up thickest. Writing the frequency instead of the interval is a classic thrown-away mark.

The MEDIAN interval is the class holding the middle of the ordered data. Forty values: the median sits between the twentieth and twenty-first values. Find them with a running total called the cumulative frequency. After the first class: 4. After the second: 4 plus 6 is 10. After the third: 10 plus 11 is 21. Stop — the twentieth and twenty-first values are both inside that third class, because the count reached only 10 before it and 21 by its end. The median interval is 50 up to below 60.

Assemble the full report, because this is what an examination question looks like: estimated mean 58 percent; modal interval 60 to below 70; median interval 50 to below 60. Notice they disagree — the mean sits below the modal class because the low tail of weak marks drags it down. Being able to SAY that — the low scores pull the mean below the mode — is the analysis mark that separates a distinction script from a computation script.

The compressed method for any grouped table: check the frequency total; midpoints for the estimated mean; largest frequency names the modal interval; cumulative frequency locates the median interval; one sentence comparing them. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — the table is different, the five moves are identical.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three measures again now, built from scenes you already know — sharing chips, lining up by height, counting shoe sizes. Nothing new arrives: the answers will land on the same 19, 18 and 15. What changes is that each measure will become a picture you cannot forget.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Say Typical

Picture one: the share-out. Nine friends each did some homework — some 12 minutes, some 28. Pour ALL the minutes into one big pot: 171 minutes. Now share the pot perfectly equally: 171 shared among 9 is 19 minutes each. That is the MEAN — what everyone would have if effort were communal. It is the only measure that touches every single value, which is its power and, as you will see, its weakness.

Picture two: the line-up. Ask the nine learners to stand in a line, ordered by their homework time, shortest to longest. Walk to the person standing dead centre — four people on their left, four on their right. Ask that middle person their time: 18 minutes. That is the MEDIAN — the value of the middle position. Nobody added anything; nobody divided. You only ordered and pointed.

Picture three: the show of hands. Call out each time and count hands. Only one time gets two hands: 15 minutes. The most COMMON value — the MODE. Think of a shoe shop: the manager does not want the mean shoe size of 7,3 — no such shoe exists. The manager wants the size most feet actually wear. That is mode thinking.

Three pictures: the pot, the line, the show of hands. Every question in this topic is one of these three, wearing formal clothes.

## Subtopic: When One Number Lies

Back to our nine learners — and in walks a tenth, who studied 120 minutes. Watch each picture react.

The pot: the newcomer tips two full hours in. The pot jumps from 171 to 291 minutes, and shared among ten people that is 29,1 minutes each. But look around the room: nine of the ten did LESS than 29,1. The share-out number now describes almost nobody — one enormous contribution distorted it. That is what an outlier does to a mean: the mean is fair to the TOTAL, not to the people.

The line: the newcomer walks to the far end of the line, and everyone else stays exactly where they stood. The middle of ten people is between the fifth and sixth — between 18 and 21 — so the median is 19,5. Barely a flicker. The line does not care HOW extreme the last person is; a 120 or a 1 000 at the end of the queue moves the middle by nothing at all.

That is the whole choosing rule, felt rather than memorised: extreme values wreck the pot but not the line. So when you hear "house prices in a suburb where one mansion sold for 20 million rand", think: pot ruined, use the line — the median. When the data is calm and even, the pot is excellent, because it listens to everyone.

Your questions for this section are up now. For each scenario, decide first: is there a monster value in the room? Then pick pot or line, and say why in one sentence.

## Subtopic: Grouped Data Without Fear

Last scene: the marks of forty learners arrive not as a list but as five buckets — thirties, forties, fifties, sixties, seventies. Four learners in the thirties bucket, six in the forties, eleven in the fifties, twelve in the sixties, seven in the seventies.

First feel WHY the exact mean is gone: the buckets hide the details. You know eleven learners scored in the fifties, but a 51 and a 59 look identical from outside the bucket. So we make the fairest possible guess — assume everyone in a bucket sits in its middle. Thirties bucket: call them all 35. Forties: 45. And so on. Some sat lower, some higher; the middle is the guess that balances out.

Then the pot, exactly as before, just bucket by bucket: four people at 35 contribute 140. Six at 45 contribute 270. Eleven at 55 contribute 605. Twelve at 65 contribute 780. Seven at 75 contribute 525. Everything in the pot: 2 320 marks. Shared among 40 learners: 58 percent. An ESTIMATED mean, honest about the guessing baked into it.

The other two measures become even easier — you just point at buckets. The fullest bucket, twelve learners, is the sixties: that is the modal interval. And for the median, line up all forty in mark order and find the middle pair — the twentieth and twenty-first learners. Count through the buckets: four, then ten, then twenty-one. The middle pair is standing in the fifties bucket. Median interval: the fifties.

Notice you already knew every move — midpoint guess, pot, fullest bucket, count to the middle. Grouped data is not new mathematics; it is the same three pictures squinting through a frequency table. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now — read the table slowly, trust the pictures, and collect what you have earned.
