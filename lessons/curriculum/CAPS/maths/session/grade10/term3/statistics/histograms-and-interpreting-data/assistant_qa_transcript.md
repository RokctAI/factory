**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up most with this topic. The classic first: histograms and bar graphs look nearly identical, so what is the actual difference, and how do exam questions test it?

**Tutor:** The difference lives on the horizontal axis, and once you see it there you can never confuse the two again. A histogram's horizontal axis is a continuous measurement scale — minutes, kilograms, centimetres — one unbroken number line chopped into intervals. Because the scale is unbroken, neighbouring bars share a wall: 19,9 minutes runs straight into 20 with nothing in between, so there is nothing for a gap to represent. A bar graph's horizontal axis is a list of separate categories — soccer, netball, chess — and the gaps are honest: nothing exists between two categories, so the bars stand apart. Examiners test this two ways. The one-mark theory version asks you to state the difference: continuous grouped data with touching bars versus categorical data with separated bars. The sneakier version shows you data and asks which display suits it — and the test is always the axis question: is this a measurement that flows, or a set of separate labels? Answer that, and the display chooses itself.

**Thandi:** Boundary values sink a lot of tables — the learner who travels exactly 20 minutes ends up counted twice or not at all. What is the convention, and how do you keep it straight under pressure?

**Tutor:** The convention: every class includes its lower boundary and excludes its upper one. The class from 20 to below 30 owns exactly 20, owns 29,9, but refuses 30 — the moment a value reaches a boundary, it moves up into the next class. Two habits keep it straight. First, write the intervals with the words built in — 20 up to below 30 — rather than a bare 20 to 30; the word "below" carries the rule on the page, and a marker can see you applying it. Second, when tallying, deal with boundary values the moment you meet them by saying the rule aloud: reaches the boundary, moves up. Then run the final safeguard, which catches every boundary slip ever made: total the frequencies and match them against the number of raw values. A value counted twice pushes the total over; a homeless value leaves it short. Thirty values in, thirty tallies out — only then does the table earn your trust.

**Thandi:** Why is the mean from a frequency table only an estimate — and comparing it with the modal class, which one should a summary sentence actually quote?

**Tutor:** It is an estimate because grouping is deliberate forgetting. The moment the thirty exact times became five buckets, the individual values were gone: eleven learners sit somewhere from 20 to below 30, and the table cannot say where. To compute a mean at all, each learner needs a stand-in value, and the midpoint of the class is the balanced choice — the learners below the midpoint and those above it roughly cancel. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency, total, divide by thirty, and you get 24 minutes — close to the truth, but built on an assumption, so the honest word "estimated" belongs in the sentence. As for what to quote: they answer differently shaped questions. The modal class — 20 to below 30 — names where the biggest crowd sits, and it is all a reader needs for "what is typical here". The estimated mean gives one number the whole dataset balances on, which is what you need for comparisons and further arithmetic. A strong summary quotes both in one sentence: most learners travel 20 to 30 minutes, with an estimated mean of about 24 — crowd and balance point together.

**Thandi:** Last one — the comment at the end. People lose those marks even with perfect calculations, and the complaint is always that "comment on the data" feels too vague to answer. What is the fix?

**Tutor:** The fix is knowing that "comment" is not vague at all — it is a three-slot template wearing a casual word. Slot one, centre: where does the data cluster? Quote the modal class or the estimated mean, then say it in context — a typical learner travels about twenty-odd minutes. Slot two, spread: how widely do values differ? Quote the reach of the data — from under ten minutes to nearly fifty — and translate: travel experiences differ hugely across one classroom. Slot three, shape: is there a tail? Name its direction and tell its story — skewed right; a few learners travel far while most live close. Three sentences, each one a number or feature from the display followed by its real-world meaning. Two failure modes cost the marks: sentences that never leave the maths — "it is skewed right", full stop — and sentences that never touch the display — "learners should leave home earlier". The first forgets the world; the second forgets the data. The template forces you to hold both, and that pairing — evidence, then meaning — is the entire skill the question is marking.
