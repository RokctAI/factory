**Thandi:** Question time before we go — and the inbox filled up fast today. Top of the list: how do you actually choose the class intervals? The lesson handed us 0 to 20, 20 to 40 and so on, but in a test, who decides?

**Tutor:** Sometimes the question decides for you — a printed table or given boundaries — and then your only job is to respect them. When the choice is yours, three principles govern it. First, equal widths: every interval the same size, or the picture lies. Second, full coverage: the intervals must run from below your smallest value to beyond your largest, leaving no number homeless. Third, a sensible count of classes — around five to eight for a dataset this size. Too few classes and everything piles into two giant bars that hide the story; too many and each bar holds two or three values and the shape dissolves into noise. Our forty times ran from about ten minutes to the mid-nineties, so five classes of width twenty covered everything cleanly. Choose so the shape can breathe.

**Thandi:** Next one — a classic mix-up: a few of us keep writing that the data is skewed LEFT because the tall bar sits on the left side of the graph. What's the cure?

**Tutor:** The cure is one firm sentence: skew follows the TAIL, never the peak. Picture the histogram as an animal lying down — the peak is its body, and the tail is the long thin part stretching away. Our homework data has its body over the twenty-to-forties and its tail stretching right, through nine, six and three, out toward the marathon workers. The tail points right, so the data is skewed right — even though the body sits on the left of the picture. In fact, that is the usual layout: a right-skewed distribution almost always carries its peak on the left. So in any test, put your finger on the longest thin stretch of the graph, say aloud "the tail points this way", and name the skew after your finger. The peak will try to distract you; ignore it.

***

**Thandi:** Here's one I promised to ask: the estimated mean used midpoints — 10, 30, 50 and so on. Why midpoints, and why do we keep saying ESTIMATED?

**Tutor:** Because the table has already thrown information away, deliberately. Once forty exact times became five pigeonholes, we no longer know whether the sixteen learners in the twenty-to-forty class worked 21 minutes or 39 — the grouping traded that detail for overview. The midpoint, 30, is the fairest single stand-in for everyone in the class: some sat above it, some below, and in the absence of better information the middle balances those errors. Multiply each midpoint by its frequency, total, and divide by forty — that gave our 42 minutes. But honesty demands the word estimated: the true mean of the original forty numbers is almost certainly not exactly 42, just close to it. Writing "estimated mean" is not hedging — it is a statistician telling the truth about grouped data, and it is worth a mark.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it made me laugh because it's so relatable: in a comparison question, one of us wrote "Class A's graph is nicer than Class B's." Zero marks. What should the sentence have been?

**Tutor:** The marker cannot pay for "nicer", because "nicer" lives in your taste, not in the data. A comparison sentence must pick one of the three slots — centre, spread or shape — and compare the SAME slot in both displays, with a feature quoted from each. For instance: "Class A's modal class is twenty to forty minutes while Class B's is forty to sixty, so a typical B learner works longer" — that is centre against centre. Or: "Class A's times stretch to nearly a hundred minutes while B's stop at sixty, so A's homework habits vary far more" — spread against spread. Then, if the question allows, land the meaning: what does that difference say about the two classes' evenings? Structure plus context is the entire recipe. Say something checkable about both graphs, in matching categories, and the marks follow every time.
