# Part 1 — Expert

Today's work carries a dataset all the way from a messy list of raw numbers to a finished statistical comment — the full journey the examiners call "organise, summarise, represent and interpret". The vehicle is the frequency table and its picture, the histogram, and the destination is the analysis sentence that earns the final marks. One dataset of thirty travel times runs through the whole session, and by the close you will build the table, describe the histogram exactly, and comment on the data like an analyst.

## Subtopic: From Raw Data to a Frequency Table

The raw data: thirty learners in a class each recorded, in minutes, how long they travel to school in the morning. The numbers arrive as a jumble — a 7, a 23, a 41, a 12, and so on, thirty of them. Raw data answers no questions in this state; the first professional move is always to ORGANISE it.

Continuous data — times, masses, heights, anything measured rather than counted — is organised into CLASS INTERVALS. Choose intervals that are equal in width and that cover everything. Here: from 0 up to but not including 10 minutes; from 10 to below 20; 20 to below 30; 30 to below 40; and 40 to below 50. Say the boundary rule aloud, because it is where errors breed: each interval includes its lower boundary and excludes its upper one, so a learner who travels exactly 20 minutes belongs to the 20-to-30 class, not the 10-to-20 class. Every value has exactly one home; no value has two.

Tally the thirty values into the classes and the table emerges: 3 learners in the 0-to-10 class; 8 in the 10-to-20 class; 11 in the 20-to-30 class; 5 in the 30-to-40 class; and 3 in the 40-to-50 class. Now the non-negotiable check: add the frequencies — 3 plus 8 is 11, plus 11 is 22, plus 5 is 27, plus 3 is 30. Thirty learners, thirty tallies. If that total misses, a value was dropped or double-counted, and every calculation downstream inherits the error.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. Boundaries and totals: the unglamorous checks are the ones that hold everything up.

## Subtopic: Building the Histogram

The histogram is the frequency table drawn as a picture, and you must be able to construct and describe one precisely in words.

The horizontal axis carries the measurement — travel time in minutes — marked continuously: 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. The vertical axis carries FREQUENCY — the number of learners — running from 0 up past the largest frequency, 11. Each class becomes a bar: the bar stands on its interval, so the first bar spans from 0 to 10 on the horizontal axis and rises to a height of 3. The second spans 10 to 20, height 8. The third, 20 to 30, height 11 — the tallest. Then 30 to 40 at height 5, and 40 to 50 at height 3.

The defining feature: the bars TOUCH. No gaps. A histogram's bars share their walls because the data is continuous — 19,9 minutes flows into 20,0 with nothing between them, so the picture is a connected skyline, not separated towers. This is precisely what separates a histogram from a bar graph. A bar graph displays CATEGORIES — favourite subjects, taxi routes, sports codes — and its bars stand apart, because there is nothing between soccer and netball. Gaps mean categories; touching bars mean a continuous measurement scale. Examiners ask for this distinction in one-mark theory questions, and they ask it often.

Label everything: axis titles with units, a heading for the graph. An unlabelled histogram is a shape, not a statistical display.

Stop for this section's questions now — bars, boundaries, heights, and the touching-bars rule with its reason.

## Subtopic: Reading the Shape — Mode, Middle and Skew

A finished histogram is read in three passes.

Pass one: the peak. The tallest bar sits on the 20-to-30 class — that is the MODAL CLASS, the most common travel-time bracket. Eleven of the thirty learners, more than a third of the class, travel between 20 and 30 minutes.

Pass two: the middle. Thirty values put the median between the fifteenth and sixteenth values. Run the cumulative count across the bars: 3 by the end of the first class, 11 by the second, 22 by the third. The fifteenth and sixteenth learners are both inside the third bar, so the median class is 20 to below 30. And the estimated mean, from midpoints 5, 15, 25, 35 and 45: three times 5 is 15; eight times 15 is 120; eleven times 25 is 275; five times 35 is 175; three times 45 is 135. The total is 720, and 720 over 30 gives an estimated mean of 24 minutes. Peak, median class and mean all pointing at the twenties — a coherent centre.

Pass three: the shape. Ask where the tail is. Our skyline rises quickly from the left, peaks at 20-to-30, then trails away to the right through heights 5 and 3. A longer tail on the RIGHT is called skewed to the right, or positively skewed: most learners live near school with a smaller scattered group enduring long commutes. A longer left tail is skewed to the left, negatively skewed. Roughly mirror-image sides make the distribution symmetrical. One vocabulary caution: the skew is named after the TAIL'S direction, not the peak's — a right-skewed histogram has its peak on the LEFT.

Pause now for this section's questions — peak, cumulative middle, tail. Three passes, three facts.

## Subtopic: Writing the Analysis Comment

The final examination skill: the comment. A question ends with "comment on the data" or "compare the two classes", and the marks go to structured sentences, not vague impressions.

The template has three slots: CENTRE, SPREAD, SHAPE. Each sentence quotes a number or feature from the display, then says what it means in context.

Centre: "The modal class is 20 to below 30 minutes and the estimated mean is 24 minutes, so a typical learner travels roughly twenty-odd minutes to school." Spread: "Times range across all five classes, from below 10 minutes to nearly 50 — a wide spread, so travel experiences differ greatly across the class." Shape: "The distribution is skewed to the right: most learners have short-to-moderate commutes, while a small group travels much longer."

For a COMPARISON of two histograms or datasets, run the same three slots side by side. Suppose a second class shows its peak on the 10-to-20 bar and no learner beyond 30 minutes. The comparison: their centre is lower — typical commutes are shorter; their spread is narrower — no long tail; so their access to the school is more uniform. Always compare like with like: centre against centre, spread against spread, never a centre against a spread.

Two hygiene rules seal full marks. Rule one: stay in CONTEXT — the sentence "the data is skewed right" earns less than "most learners travel a short time, with a few travelling very long", because interpretation means returning to the real world the numbers came from. Rule two: claim only what the display shows. The histogram of thirty travel times cannot reveal WHY commutes are long, and it says nothing about any learner's exact time — grouped displays trade detail for overview, and a distinction candidate says so when asked about limitations.

The compressed method: organise with clean boundaries and check the total; draw touching bars on a labelled continuous axis; read peak, middle, tail; then comment in centre-spread-shape sentences anchored in context. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — the table is new, the method is identical.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same journey again now, from pile of numbers to confident sentence — but rebuilt with buckets, skylines and stories. Nothing new arrives: the tallest bar will still be the twenties, the mean will still be 24. What changes is that each step will feel like something you would do naturally.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Pile Into Buckets

Imagine the thirty travel times written on thirty slips of paper, dropped in a pile on your desk. A pile answers nothing. Ask the pile "how does this class travel?" and it just sits there. The fix is the same one you use for laundry: sorting into buckets.

Line up five buckets, each labelled with a range: nought to under ten minutes, ten to under twenty, twenty to under thirty, thirty to under forty, forty to under fifty. Pick up each slip and drop it into the bucket its number belongs to. A slip saying 7 — first bucket. A slip saying 23 — third bucket. A slip saying 41 — fifth.

Only one rule needs care: the boundary slips. Where does exactly 20 go? Every bucket keeps its bottom number and refuses its top one — so 20 goes into the twenty-to-thirty bucket, and 19,9 into the one before. That way, no slip can ever claim two homes, and no slip is homeless. It is the same logic as airtime bundles: the moment you hit the next threshold, you are in the next bracket.

When the pile is gone, count each bucket: 3 slips, 8 slips, 11, 5, 3. And then the habit that saves marks forever: count the counts. Three plus 8 plus 11 plus 5 plus 3 — thirty. Thirty slips went in, thirty are accounted for. Now the pile can speak: most of this class lives in the middle buckets. That is a frequency table — nothing more than sorted laundry with labels.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the buckets are coming to you right now. Watch the boundaries; they are the only trap in the room.

## Subtopic: The Skyline Tells the Story

Now turn the buckets into a picture you can read at a glance. Stand each bucket's count up as a tower: a tower of height 3, then 8, then 11, then 5, then 3, standing side by side along the minutes line with no space between them — because time is continuous; 19,9 minutes touches 20 with nothing in between. What you get is a city skyline, and skylines are the fastest-reading pictures humans know.

Read this skyline like you would read any city. Where is downtown — the tallest tower? Over the twenties: that is where travel times crowd. Which way do the suburbs sprawl? To the right: after the peak, towers of 5 and 3 trail off toward the long commutes. So the story so far: a big crowd in the twenties, a thinning tail of learners who travel far.

One misreading to kill immediately, because it feels so natural: a WIDE bar never means more learners. Every bar here is ten minutes wide by design; only HEIGHT counts people. Height is frequency; width is just the bucket's label stretched out. Say it once: tall means many, wide means nothing.

And keep the skyline separate from its cousin, the bar graph. Favourite subjects, taxi ranks, soccer versus netball — those are separate categories, drawn as separated towers with gaps, because nothing lives between soccer and netball. The histogram's towers touch because the minutes line is one unbroken road. Gaps for categories, touching for measurements — one sentence, one theory mark, whenever asked.

Your questions for this section are up now. Read the skyline: find downtown, follow the sprawl, and trust height, never width.

## Subtopic: Saying What the Picture Means

Last skill, and it is the one the marks pile onto: turning the skyline into sentences. The recipe is three questions, asked in order, answered in context.

Question one: where is the crowd? Point at downtown. "Most learners — eleven of the thirty — travel between twenty and thirty minutes." If you computed the estimated mean, add it: "on average about 24 minutes." That is your CENTRE sentence.

Question two: how scattered are they? Look at how much of the road the skyline covers. "Times stretch from under ten minutes to nearly fifty — a wide spread; some walk around the corner while others cross half the city." That is your SPREAD sentence.

Question three: is the skyline lopsided? Follow the tail. "The long tail points right: a handful of learners have much longer commutes than everyone else — the distribution is skewed to the right." That is your SHAPE sentence. Name the skew after the tail's direction, never the peak's.

Three sentences, and notice what each one does: it takes a feature of the picture and hands back a fact about real learners. That is the whole meaning of "interpret" — the numbers came from the world, and your sentence must return there. "Skewed right" alone is maths homework; "a few learners travel very far while most live close" is analysis.

And when two skylines stand together — this class against another, this month against last — ask the same three questions of both and compare the answers in pairs: crowd against crowd, scatter against scatter, tail against tail. Never let a comparison sentence mix categories.

You now own the full journey: pile to buckets, buckets to skyline, skyline to sentences. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now — sort carefully, read the towers, and let the three sentences carry you home. This topic is yours.
