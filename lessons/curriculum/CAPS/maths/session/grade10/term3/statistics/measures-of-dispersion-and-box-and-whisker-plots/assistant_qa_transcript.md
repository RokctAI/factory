**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up over and over with this topic. First: with an even number of values, the halves for the quartiles confuse everyone. What is the rule when there is no single middle person?

**Tutor:** Take it in two stages, and let the median rule you already know do all the work. Stage one: the median of an even count is the value exactly halfway between the two middle values — with ten values, halfway between the fifth and the sixth. That median is a computed number, not a person in the line, so it cannot join either half. Stage two: split the data into its natural halves — the first five values and the last five — and take the median of each half exactly as usual. Run it on 2, 4, 6, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14, 16, 20. The median is halfway between 9 and 11, which is 10. The lower half is 2, 4, 6, 7, 9, and its middle is 6 — that is the lower quartile. The upper half is 11, 12, 14, 16, 20, and its middle is 14 — the upper quartile. The only genuinely new decision in the even case is that the halves are already clean: nobody stands on the fence, so nothing gets excluded. Odd count: the middle value is the fence and joins neither half. Even count: no fence needed. Two rules, and every dataset falls into one of them.

***

**Thandi:** A confusion that costs marks every year: people see a long whisker and write that it contains more data. Why is that wrong, and what should the sentence say instead?

**Tutor:** It is wrong because a box plot is built on COUNTS, not on lengths. The three cuts — lower quartile, median, upper quartile — are placed so that each of the four sections holds the same share of the data, about one quarter each. That is true by construction, before the diagram is even drawn. So length cannot signal quantity; every section's quantity is fixed at a quarter. What length signals is CROWDING. A short section means that quarter of the values are packed shoulder to shoulder — many similar values in a narrow stretch. A long section means the same-sized quarter is scattered across a wide stretch — the values there differ a lot from each other. The sentence the memorandum wants is therefore about spread, not amount: say the top quarter of the marks is spread over a wide interval, or the bottom quarter is tightly clustered. If you feel the wrong sentence forming, repeat the anchor phrase: long never means many; long means spread.

***

**Thandi:** Why do we even need the interquartile range when the range already exists and is quicker? That question comes up every single time this topic is taught.

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers answer different questions, and the range answers the fragile one. The range measures the gap between the two most extreme values ever recorded — and extremes are precisely the least trustworthy values in a dataset. One data-capture error, one freak day, one learner who wrote 98 while the class huddled at 50, and the range doubles or triples while the everyday behaviour of the data never changed. The interquartile range deliberately throws the extremes out of reach: it measures the width of the middle half only, from the lower quartile to the upper quartile. The quiet extremes and wild extremes sit outside those fences and cannot stretch them. So the IQR describes the ordinary heart of the data — how spread out a typical value is — and it holds steady even when something bizarre lands at the edges. The practical rule: quote the range as a quick first impression, but when outliers are present or suspected, the IQR is the honest spread measure, and saying that sentence in a justify-your-answer question is exactly what the mark is for.

***

**Thandi:** Last one — in comparison questions with two box plots, people write which median is bigger and stop. What does the full-marks answer actually contain?

**Tutor:** Three ingredients, and each earns separately. Ingredient one, the centre sentence, with values read from the diagram: class A's median of 60 is higher than class B's median of 55, so a typical learner in A scored more. Ingredient two, the spread sentence: A's box is shorter — an interquartile range of 12 against 30 — so A's middle half is tightly bunched and A is the more consistent class. That word consistent, or reliable, or predictable, is the interpretation the examiner is fishing for; spread always cashes out as consistency in context. Ingredient three, when the shapes differ, a skew phrase: if B's median sits close to its lower quartile with a long upper whisker, B's marks are skewed to the right — most learners scored low with a tail of high performers. Notice what every ingredient has in common: a number from the diagram, then a sentence about what it MEANS for the people in the data. A comparison that never leaves the numbers earns half; a comparison that translates each number into the story earns full.
