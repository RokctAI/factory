# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers solving problems involving surface area, volume and capacity of cubes and rectangular prisms, including unit conversions and multi-step reasoning. The anchor is a school water tank 2,5 m × 1,2 m × 1,5 m.

## Subtopic: Volume and Capacity Problems

The tank is 2,5 metres long, 1,2 metres wide and 1,5 metres high. Volume: 2,5 times 1,2 is three square metres of base; times 1,5 is 4,5 cubic metres. Capacity: one cubic metre holds one kilolitre, so the tank holds 4,5 kilolitres, which is 4 500 litres.

Filling time: a pipe delivers twenty litres per minute. Time to fill: 4 500 divided by twenty, 225 minutes, which is three hours and forty-five minutes.

Water depth: after the school uses 1 200 litres, 3 300 litres remain, which is 3,3 cubic metres. The base area is three square metres, so the depth is 3,3 divided by three, 1,1 metres. Depth equals volume divided by base area.

Partly filled containers: a tank filled to eighty percent holds 0,8 times 4 500, 3 600 litres.

Daily use: if 300 learners each use two litres of drinking water a day, that is 600 litres a day. A full tank lasts 4 500 divided by 600, 7,5 days.

The questions for this section are ready: volume to litres, filling time, depth and duration.

## Subtopic: Surface Area Problems

Surface area problems ask how much material covers or makes an object.

Painting the tank: all six faces? Usually not — the bottom stands on a slab and the top may be a lid that is not painted. Read carefully. Painting the four sides only: two times 2,5 times 1,5, plus two times 1,2 times 1,5: 7,5 plus 3,6, 11,1 square metres. Adding the top: plus three, 14,1 square metres.

Paint: if one litre covers ten square metres, two coats on 14,1 square metres need 2,82 litres, so buy three litres.

Open boxes: a box without a lid has five faces. A gift box ten by eight by six centimetres, open at the top: bottom eighty; front and back two times sixty, 120; sides two times forty-eight, ninety-six. Total 296 square centimetres.

Wrapping with overlap: real wrapping paper needs extra for overlaps — perhaps ten percent more. The calculation gives the minimum.

Pause here for this section's questions: decide which faces count, then add.

## Subtopic: Multi-Step Problems With Conversions

Many problems combine volume, capacity and unit conversions.

A juice carton is ten centimetres by six centimetres by twenty centimetres. Volume: 1 200 cubic centimetres, which is 1 200 millilitres, 1,2 litres. How many 250-millilitre glasses can it fill? 1 200 divided by 250 is 4,8: four full glasses with some left over.

How many cartons fit in a crate sixty by thirty by forty centimetres? Along the length: sixty divided by ten, six. Across: thirty divided by six, five. Up: forty divided by twenty, two. Six times five times two is sixty cartons. Note that dividing the crate's volume by the carton's volume also gives sixty here, but only because the cartons fit exactly; when they do not, counting along each edge is the correct method.

A swimming pool twenty-five metres by ten metres, filled to an average depth of 1,6 metres: 400 cubic metres, which is 400 kilolitres, 400 000 litres.

Always convert to one unit system before combining, and round according to the context.

Here come the questions for this section: cartons, crates and pools.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same school tank now: filling it, using the water, painting it, and packing juice cartons. Same calculations, a real project.

## Subtopic: Filling the School Tank

The school has a new water tank: 2,5 metres long, 1,2 metres wide and 1,5 metres tall. How much water does it hold?

Volume is length times width times height: 2,5 times 1,2 is three, times 1,5 is 4,5 cubic metres. Every cubic metre holds a thousand litres, so the tank holds 4 500 litres.

A pipe fills it at twenty litres every minute. How long to fill it? 4 500 shared into twenties: 225 minutes. That is three hours and forty-five minutes.

The school's 300 learners each drink about two litres a day: 600 litres a day. How long does a full tank last? 4 500 divided by 600 — seven and a half days.

After one day of use, 1 200 litres have gone — wait, that is two days' worth! — so 3 300 litres are left, 3,3 cubic metres. How deep is the water? The tank's floor is 2,5 by 1,2, three square metres. 3,3 divided by three is 1,1 metres deep.

Your questions for this section are with you now: volume, litres, time and depth.

## Subtopic: Painting and Packing

The tank must be painted to stop rust. Which faces? It stands on a concrete slab, so the bottom is NOT painted. The four sides and the top are.

The long sides are 2,5 by 1,5: 3,75 square metres each, two of them 7,5. The short sides are 1,2 by 1,5: 1,8 each, two of them 3,6. The top is 2,5 by 1,2: three square metres. Total: 7,5 plus 3,6 plus three, 14,1 square metres.

Paint covers ten square metres per litre. Two coats: 28,2 square metres, so 2,82 litres. Buy three litres — you can't buy 2,82!

Now packing. Juice cartons are ten by six by twenty centimetres. A crate is sixty by thirty by forty. How many cartons fit? Along the sixty: six cartons. Across the thirty: five. Up the forty: two. Six times five times two: sixty cartons.

Count along each edge — that way you only count cartons that really fit.

You can now solve real surface area, volume and capacity problems. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: which faces, how much inside, and what fits?
