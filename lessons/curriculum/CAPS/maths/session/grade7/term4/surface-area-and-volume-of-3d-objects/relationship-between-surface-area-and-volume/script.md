# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing the interrelationship between surface area and volume of cubes and rectangular prisms: prisms with equal volume but different surface areas, and the effect of changing dimensions on both. The anchor is 24 unit cubes arranged as different prisms.

## Subtopic: Same Volume, Different Surface Areas

Take twenty-four one-centimetre cubes. Every prism you build from all of them has a volume of twenty-four cubic centimetres. But the surface areas differ.

A long row, twenty-four by one by one: surface area two times twenty-four, plus two times twenty-four, plus two times one — ninety-eight square centimetres.

A flat slab, six by four by one: two times twenty-four, plus two times six, plus two times four — sixty-eight square centimetres.

A chunkier block, four by three by two: two times twelve, plus two times eight, plus two times six — fifty-two square centimetres.

So the same volume can be wrapped with anything from fifty-two to ninety-eight square centimetres of paper. The more compact the shape — the closer to a cube — the smaller the surface area. The more stretched out, the larger.

This is why packaging designers prefer compact boxes, saving cardboard, and why animals curl up in the cold: a compact shape exposes less surface and loses less heat.

The questions for this section are ready: calculate surface areas of different prisms with the same volume.

## Subtopic: Doubling the Side of a Cube

What happens to surface area and volume when a cube grows?

A cube with side one centimetre: surface area six square centimetres, volume one cubic centimetre.

Double the side to two centimetres: surface area six times four, twenty-four square centimetres, which is four times as much. Volume two cubed, eight cubic centimetres, which is eight times as much.

Triple the side to three centimetres: surface area fifty-four, nine times as much; volume twenty-seven, twenty-seven times as much.

So when the side is multiplied by a factor, surface area is multiplied by the factor squared and volume by the factor cubed. Volume grows faster than surface area.

This explains a lot in nature and design. Large animals have less surface per unit of volume than small ones, so an elephant keeps heat more easily than a mouse, and small creatures lose heat quickly. Crushed ice melts faster than one big block, because many small pieces expose much more surface.

Pause here for this section's questions: compare surface area and volume as a cube grows.

## Subtopic: Surface-to-Volume Comparisons in Context

A useful way to compare objects is to look at how much surface area each has for its volume.

Cube with side one: surface area six, volume one — six square centimetres per cubic centimetre. Cube with side two: twenty-four over eight, three per cubic centimetre. Cube with side three: fifty-four over twenty-seven, two per cubic centimetre. The bigger the cube, the less surface per unit of volume.

Packaging: a manufacturer must hold 1 000 cubic centimetres of tea. A ten by ten by ten box uses 600 square centimetres of cardboard. A twenty by ten by five box, same volume, uses 2 times 200 plus 2 times 100 plus 2 times 50, 700 square centimetres. A forty by five by five box uses 2 times 200 plus 2 times 200 plus 2 times 25, 850. The cube is the most economical.

But cubes are not always chosen: thin boxes fit on shelves, flat packs post easily, and cereal boxes have large fronts for advertising. Real decisions balance surface area against other needs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same 24 building blocks now, and cubes that grow: counting blocks inside and squares outside. Same relationship, things you can build.

## Subtopic: Building Boxes From 24 Blocks

You have twenty-four small building blocks, each a one-centimetre cube. Build a box using all of them — any shape you like.

Put them all in one long row. That is a box twenty-four long, one wide, one tall. Its volume is twenty-four — it uses all twenty-four blocks. To cover it with paper, count the outside faces: ninety-eight little squares.

Now build a flat slab, six by four, one block thick. Still twenty-four blocks. Count the outside: sixty-eight squares.

Now a chunky block, four by three by two. Still twenty-four blocks! Outside: only fifty-two squares.

Same amount of block — same volume — but the wrapping needed went from ninety-eight down to fifty-two. Why? In the chunky box, lots of blocks are hidden inside or touching each other, so fewer faces are on the outside.

That is why a cat curls up into a ball on a cold night: a compact shape has less skin exposed to the cold.

## Subtopic: When a Cube Grows

Take a little one-centimetre cube. Its six faces are each one square centimetre: six square centimetres of skin. Its inside, its volume, is one cubic centimetre.

Now build a cube twice as wide, two by two by two. How many small blocks? Eight. So its volume is eight times bigger. Its skin: each face is two by two, four squares, and six faces make twenty-four. That is only four times as much skin.

Build a cube three by three by three: twenty-seven blocks — twenty-seven times the volume! Skin: each face nine, six faces fifty-four — only nine times the skin.

So as a cube grows, the inside grows much faster than the outside.

That is why big animals like elephants stay warm more easily than tiny mice: they have lots of inside for their amount of skin. And it is why crushed ice melts faster than a big block — all those little pieces have lots of skin compared with their inside.

You now understand how surface area and volume are related. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: count the blocks inside, count the squares outside, and compare.
