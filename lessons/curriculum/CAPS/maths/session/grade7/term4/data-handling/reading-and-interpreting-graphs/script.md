# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers reading and interpreting data in bar graphs, double bar graphs, histograms and pie charts, and recognising misleading features such as axes that do not start at zero. The anchor is a library borrowing graph.

## Subtopic: Reading Values From Graphs

Start every graph by reading its title, its axis labels and its scale. Only then read the bars.

A library bar graph shows books borrowed: February 45, March 60, April 30, May 75. The vertical axis goes up in steps of 15.

Reading values: May had the most, 75. April had the fewest, 30.

Comparing: May had 75 minus 30, which is 45 more books than April. May had two and a half times as many as April.

Totals: 45 plus 60 plus 30 plus 75 is 210 books over four months, a mean of 52,5 books per month.

When a bar ends between scale lines, estimate carefully: halfway between 30 and 45 is about 37 or 38.

The questions for this section are ready: title, axes, scale, then values.

## Subtopic: Interpreting Pie Charts and Histograms

A pie chart shows fractions of a whole. If a pie chart of 200 learners' favourite sports shows soccer as a quarter of the circle, a 90 degree slice, then soccer is chosen by a quarter of 200, which is 50 learners.

If a slice is 72 degrees, it is 72 out of 360, which is one fifth, so 40 learners out of 200.

A histogram shows how numbers are spread. A histogram of learners' heights might show most learners in the interval 150 to 159 centimetres, with fewer at each end. You can say how many learners are in each interval, but not exactly how tall any single learner is, because grouping hides the individual values.

Interpret means saying what the graph tells you in words: most learners are between 150 and 159 centimetres tall; soccer is the most popular sport.

Pause here for this section's questions: slices to numbers.

## Subtopic: Misleading Graphs

A graph can be accurate in its numbers and still give the wrong impression.

The most common trick: a vertical axis that does not start at zero. Suppose a shop's monthly sales were 98, 100 and 102. If the axis starts at 96, the last bar looks three times as tall as the first, suggesting huge growth — but sales rose by only about four percent.

Other tricks: uneven scales, missing labels, pictures where both height and width grow so a doubled value looks four times as big, and leaving out months that do not fit the story.

When you read any graph — in a newspaper, an advert or on social media — ask: does the axis start at zero, is the scale even, and what is missing?

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same reading skills now, with class pets and a very close race.

## Subtopic: Read the Labels First

Imagine a bar graph about pets in your class. Before you look at any bar, read three things: the title tells you what it is about, the axis labels tell you what is measured, and the scale tells you what each line is worth.

If each line is worth 2, a bar reaching the fifth line means 10 pets — not 5!

Then compare: which bar is tallest? Which is shortest? How many more is one than another?

Labels first, bars second.

## Subtopic: Squashed and Stretched Graphs

Two friends ran a race. One took 20 seconds, the other 21 seconds. Almost the same!

But if someone draws a graph whose axis starts at 19, the 21 bar looks twice as tall as the 20 bar. It looks like a huge difference.

Start the axis at zero, and the bars look almost equal — which is the truth.

So whenever a graph looks dramatic, check where the axis starts.
