# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers organising categorical data with tally marks and frequency tables, ordering numerical data, grouping it into intervals, and showing it in a stem-and-leaf display. The anchors are a transport survey of 40 learners and fifteen test marks.

## Subtopic: Tallies and Frequency Tables

Raw survey answers arrive in no order: walk, taxi, walk, car, bus, walk, taxi... To count them, use tally marks. Each answer adds one stroke to its category, and every fifth stroke crosses the previous four, making bundles of five that are quick to count.

The frequency is how many times each category occurs. Our 40 transport answers give: walk 14, taxi 11, bus 6, car 7, bicycle 2.

A frequency table shows category, tally and frequency in columns. Always check the total: 14 plus 11 plus 6 plus 7 plus 2 is 40, the number of learners asked. If the total does not match, an answer was missed or counted twice.

Already the table shows that walking is the most common way to get to school in this sample.

The questions for this section are ready: tally, count and check the total.

## Subtopic: Ordering and Grouping Numerical Data

Fifteen learners scored these marks out of 50: 23, 31, 35, 38, 41, 27, 33, 45, 29, 36, 40, 32, 47, 38, 25.

First, order them from smallest to largest: 23, 25, 27, 29, 31, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 38, 40, 41, 45, 47. Count them to check: fifteen.

When there are many different values, group them into intervals of equal width. Using intervals 20 to 29, 30 to 39 and 40 to 49: four marks are in the twenties, seven in the thirties, and four in the forties. Four plus seven plus four is fifteen.

Intervals must not overlap and must not leave gaps, just like answer options in a questionnaire.

Grouping loses some detail — you no longer see each exact mark — but it shows the overall shape: most marks sit in the middle interval.

Pause here for this section's questions: order and group.

## Subtopic: Stem-and-Leaf Displays

A stem-and-leaf display groups data and keeps every value at the same time.

Split each mark into a stem, the tens digit, and a leaf, the units digit. 23 has stem 2 and leaf 3.

For our fifteen ordered marks: Stem 2: leaves 3, 5, 7, 9. Stem 3: leaves 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 8. Stem 4: leaves 0, 1, 5, 7.

Write the leaves in order, evenly spaced, and add a key: 2 bar 3 means 23.

Turned on its side, the rows look like bars: the 30s row is longest, so most marks are in the thirties. You can also read off the smallest, 23, the largest, 47, and the repeated mark, 38.

Here come the questions for this section: build and read a stem-and-leaf display.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same organising tools now, with gates of five and a tree of numbers.

## Subtopic: Counting in Fives

Imagine a teacher shouting out answers: walk! taxi! walk! car! You need to keep up.

Draw a stroke for each answer next to its name. When you reach five, draw the fifth stroke across the other four, like a little gate.

At the end, count gates in fives and add the extra strokes. Walk has two gates and four strokes: ten plus four, fourteen.

Put it all in a table: name, tally, frequency. Add the frequencies. If they total the number of people asked, you counted everything.

Your questions for this section are with you now: gates of five.

## Subtopic: Stems and Leaves

Take the numbers 12, 15, 21, 23, 23 and 30.

Split each into tens and units. The tens digit is the stem; the units digit is the leaf.

1 | 2 5 2 | 1 3 3 3 | 0

Read it back: stem 2 with leaf 1 is 21. Stem 3 with leaf 0 is 30.

It is like a tree: one branch for each tens, and the leaves hang on it. The longest branch shows where most numbers are.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now: split into stems and leaves.
