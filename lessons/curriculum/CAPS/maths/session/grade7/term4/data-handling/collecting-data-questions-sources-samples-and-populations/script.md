# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers posing questions about issues in the school or community, choosing appropriate sources of data, and distinguishing between a population and a sample. The anchor question is how learners at one school travel to school.

## Subtopic: Posing a Question Worth Answering

Every statistical investigation starts with a question. A good question can be answered with data, is clear about who it is about, and is clear about what is being measured.

Compare two questions. Do learners like transport? That is vague: like what, and which learners? How do Grade 7 learners at our school travel to school on a normal weekday? That is clear: the group is named, and the answer will be categories such as walking, taxi, bus, car or bicycle.

Some questions produce categories, like favourite sport or mode of transport. Others produce numbers, like how many minutes the trip takes or how many siblings you have. Knowing which kind you will get helps you plan how to organise and display the data later.

The data cycle runs: pose a question, collect data, organise it, represent it, analyse it, and report. Then often a new question appears.

The questions for this section are ready: spot clear, answerable questions.

## Subtopic: Sources of Data

Once you have a question, decide where the data will come from.

You can collect it yourself: by asking people in a questionnaire or interview, by observing, such as counting cars passing the school gate, or by measuring, such as timing how long learners take to run 100 metres.

Or you can use data someone else collected: school attendance registers, newspaper tables, or official figures from Statistics South Africa, which runs the national census.

Data you collect yourself fits your question exactly, but it takes time. Existing data saves time, but you must check who collected it, when, and whether it really answers your question.

For our transport question, the best source is to ask the learners themselves, because no register records how each learner travels.

Pause here for this section's questions: match the question to a sensible source.

## Subtopic: Populations and Samples

The population is the whole group your question is about. For our question, the population is all Grade 7 learners at the school — say 240 learners.

Asking everyone is called a census. Often that is too slow or costly, so we ask a sample: a smaller group chosen from the population, such as 40 learners.

A sample is only useful if it represents the population fairly. Asking only the learners who arrive in the school bus would be a poor sample, because every one of them travels by bus. The result would claim almost everyone takes the bus.

Fairer ways to choose: pick names at random from a hat, or choose every sixth learner on the class lists, so that every learner has a fair chance of being chosen.

A bigger fair sample usually gives a more reliable picture than a small one.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same ideas now, with a fruit survey and a hat full of names.

## Subtopic: Who Are We Asking?

Say you want to know the favourite fruit of everyone in your grade. Everyone in your grade is the population.

You could ask every single learner. That is a census. But if your grade is huge, that takes ages.

So you ask some of them: a sample. The trick is to choose fairly. If you only ask learners standing at the tuck shop next to the apples, you will hear apples, apples, apples! That is not fair.

Instead, write every name on a slip, mix them in a hat, and pull out thirty. Now everyone had the same chance.

Fair sample, fair answer. Unfair sample, silly answer.

## Subtopic: A Good Question Is Clear

Before collecting anything, write a question so clear that nobody could misunderstand it.

Not: Do people like sport? Which people? Like it how much?

Better: Which sport do Grade 7 learners at our school most like to play?

Then decide where the answers come from. Here, you ask the learners. For a question like how much rain fell in Durban last year, you would look up weather records instead.

Clear question, sensible source, fair sample. That is how every good survey begins.
