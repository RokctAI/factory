# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers designing simple questionnaires with yes or no answers and multiple-choice responses, writing clear unbiased questions, and making answer options that cover every case without overlapping. The anchor is a survey on after-school activities.

## Subtopic: Yes or No and Multiple-Choice Questions

A questionnaire is a list of questions given to each person in the sample. Simple answer formats make the data easy to count.

A yes or no question has two possible answers: Do you play a sport after school? Yes or no.

A multiple-choice question gives a list of options: Which after-school activity do you spend most time on? Sport, music, homework club, helping at home, other.

Including an option called other means nobody is forced into a wrong box.

For numbers, give ranges: How many hours of homework do you do on a school night? Less than 1, 1 to less than 2, 2 or more.

Each question should ask about one thing only. Do you like soccer and netball? cannot be answered honestly by someone who likes only one. Split it into two questions.

## Subtopic: Avoiding Leading Questions

A leading question pushes the person towards a particular answer. It makes the data biased.

Leading: Don't you agree that the school day is far too long? The words don't you agree and far too long tell the learner which answer is expected.

Neutral: How do you feel about the length of the school day? Too long, about right, too short.

Leading: Most learners love the new tuck shop. Do you? People tend to agree with what most others are said to think.

Neutral: How would you rate the new tuck shop? Good, okay, poor.

Also avoid words people may not understand, and questions that are too personal, such as asking about family income. Respect privacy: a questionnaire for learners should not ask for names unless necessary.

## Subtopic: Options That Cover Everything Once

Answer options must cover every possible answer, and no answer should fit two options.

Poor ranges for hours of sleep: 6 to 7, 7 to 8, 8 to 9. A learner who sleeps exactly seven hours fits two options, and a learner who sleeps ten hours fits none.

Better: less than 7 hours, 7 to less than 8, 8 to less than 9, 9 hours or more. Every answer fits exactly one box.

Order options sensibly, and keep the list short — about four to six options.

Finally, test the questionnaire on two or three people first. This pilot shows confusing questions before the real survey.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same rules now, with a movie, break-time snacks and a sneaky set of boxes.

## Subtopic: Fair Questions Get Fair Answers

Imagine a friend asks you: You don't really like that boring movie, do you? It feels hard to say yes, I loved it! That is a leading question.

A fair version: What did you think of the movie? Loved it, it was okay, didn't like it.

Good survey questions are short, ask one thing, and do not hint at the answer.

And give choices: Which do you eat most at break? Sandwich, fruit, chips, nothing, other. Now counting the answers is easy.

## Subtopic: Boxes With No Gaps

Here is a sneaky trap. A survey asks: How many brothers and sisters do you have? Options: 1 to 2, 2 to 3, 4 or more.

Someone with two siblings fits two boxes. Someone with none fits no box.

Fix it: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or more. Now every person has exactly one box.

Check your options like a detective: try the smallest possible answer, the biggest, and the in-between numbers. Each must land in exactly one box.
