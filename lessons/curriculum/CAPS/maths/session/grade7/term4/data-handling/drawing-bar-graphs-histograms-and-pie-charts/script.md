# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing bar graphs and double bar graphs for categorical data, histograms for grouped numerical data, and pie charts with angles calculated from frequencies. The anchors are the transport survey and the grouped test marks.

## Subtopic: Bar Graphs

A bar graph shows categories along one axis and frequencies along the other. Each category gets a bar whose height is its frequency.

For the transport survey: walk 14, taxi 11, bus 6, car 7, bicycle 2.

Rules for a good bar graph. Give it a title. Label both axes: mode of transport, and number of learners. Start the frequency axis at zero and use an even scale, such as steps of 2. Make all bars the same width, with equal gaps between them, because the categories are separate.

A double bar graph puts two bars side by side for each category, for example Grade 7 and Grade 8 learners, with a key to show which colour is which. It makes comparison easy.

The questions for this section are ready: build a correct bar graph.

## Subtopic: Histograms

A histogram shows grouped numerical data. It looks like a bar graph, but the bars touch, because the intervals follow on from each other without gaps.

Our test marks: 20 to 29, four learners; 30 to 39, seven learners; 40 to 49, four learners.

Draw the horizontal axis as a number line labelled marks, with interval boundaries at 20, 30, 40 and 50. Draw the vertical axis labelled frequency. Each bar stretches across its whole interval, with height equal to the frequency: 4, 7 and 4.

The shape is visible at once: highest in the middle and lower at each side, which is common for test marks.

Use equal interval widths, so that bar heights can be compared fairly.

Pause here for this section's questions: bars that touch.

## Subtopic: Pie Charts

A pie chart shows how a whole is split into parts. The whole circle, 360 degrees, stands for all 40 learners.

Each category's angle is its fraction of the total, times 360 degrees.

Walk: 14 out of 40 times 360 is 126 degrees. Taxi: 11 out of 40 times 360 is 99 degrees. Bus: 6 out of 40 times 360 is 54 degrees. Car: 7 out of 40 times 360 is 63 degrees. Bicycle: 2 out of 40 times 360 is 18 degrees.

Check: 126 plus 99 plus 54 plus 63 plus 18 is 360.

Draw a circle, draw one radius, then use a protractor to measure each angle in turn from the last line. Label each slice, or add a key, and give the chart a title. Percentages can be shown too: walk is 14 out of 40, which is 35 percent.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same graphs now, with fruit, halves and quarters, and a quick way to choose.

## Subtopic: Slicing the Pie

A class of 20 learners chose their favourite fruit: apple 10, banana 5, orange 5.

The whole circle is 360 degrees, and it stands for all 20 learners.

Apple is 10 out of 20 — half — so it gets half the circle: 180 degrees. Banana is 5 out of 20 — a quarter — so 90 degrees, a right angle. Orange is also a quarter: 90 degrees.

Check: 180 plus 90 plus 90 is 360. Perfect.

When the fractions are not so friendly, use the rule: frequency over total, times 360.

Your questions for this section are with you now: halves, quarters and slices.

## Subtopic: Gaps or No Gaps

Two graphs look alike, but they have one big difference.

A bar graph shows separate things, like fruit or sport. The bars stand apart with gaps, like people standing in a queue.

A histogram shows numbers in groups, like heights from 140 to 149, 150 to 159, and so on. The bars touch, like books on a shelf, because one group runs straight into the next.

So ask: separate things? Gaps. Number groups? Touching.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now: choose the right graph.
