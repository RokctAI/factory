# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers summarising data in short paragraphs, drawing conclusions and making predictions based on the data, identifying sources of error and bias, and choosing appropriate summary statistics to report. The anchor is the transport survey of 40 learners.

## Subtopic: Summarising and Concluding

A data report answers the original question in words, using numbers as evidence.

Our question: How do Grade 7 learners at our school travel to school? Our sample of 40 learners gave walk 14, taxi 11, bus 6, car 7 and bicycle 2.

A summary: Of the 40 learners asked, 14, or 35 percent, walk to school, making walking the most common way of travelling. Taxis are next with 11 learners, 27,5 percent. Only 2 learners cycle.

A conclusion that the data supports: In this sample, walking is the most common way to get to school.

A careful prediction: If the sample was chosen fairly, about a third of all 240 Grade 7 learners probably walk — roughly 80 to 90 learners.

Be careful not to claim more than the data shows. The data does not tell us why learners walk, or whether this is true at other schools.

## Subtopic: Sources of Bias and Error

Before trusting a conclusion, check how the data was collected.

Sampling bias: the sample does not represent the population. Surveying only learners on the school bus overstates bus travel.

Question bias: leading questions push answers in one direction.

Too small a sample: asking only five learners gives an unreliable picture; one unusual answer changes everything.

Recording errors: tallies miscounted, answers written in the wrong row. Checking that frequencies add up to the sample size catches many of these.

Timing: a survey taken on a rainy day might find more learners came by car than usual.

A good report mentions its limitations honestly, for example: This survey was done on one day in winter, so results may differ in summer.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Statistics to Report

A report usually includes summary statistics. Choose the ones that describe the data fairly.

For categories, such as transport, report frequencies, percentages and the mode: walking.

For numbers, report a measure of centre and the range. For the homework hours 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 13: mean 7 hours, median 6 hours, range 10 hours.

When there is an outlier, the median is fairer. For pocket money of R20, R25, R25, R30 and R200, reporting the mean of R60 would suggest learners typically get far more than they do. Report the median, R25, and mention the outlier.

Choose a graph that matches: a bar graph or pie chart for categories, a histogram or stem-and-leaf display for numbers.

A complete report: the question, how the data was collected, a graph, summary statistics, conclusions, and limitations.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same reporting ideas now, with a pizza survey and the whole data cycle from start to finish.

## Subtopic: Say Only What the Data Shows

Your class did a survey: 12 out of 30 learners said pizza is their favourite food.

You can say: pizza was the most popular choice in our class. Forty percent chose it.

You cannot say: everyone in South Africa loves pizza. You only asked your class!

And you cannot say why they like pizza, because you did not ask that.

Say what the data shows. Do not add extra stories.

## Subtopic: The Whole Data Cycle

Let's walk the full journey once.

Ask: Which sport do Grade 7 learners most like to play? Collect: a fair sample of 40 learners, a neutral questionnaire. Organise: tally and frequency table. Show: a bar graph or pie chart. Analyse: find the mode, percentages. Report: soccer was most popular, chosen by 15 of 40 learners, 37,5 percent. The survey was done in one week, in one school.

And often the report sparks a new question — like, does this change between girls and boys? — and the cycle starts again.
