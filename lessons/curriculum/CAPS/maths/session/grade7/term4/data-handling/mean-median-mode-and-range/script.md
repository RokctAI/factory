# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the measures of central tendency, which are the mean, median and mode, and the range as a measure of spread. The anchor is the weekly homework hours of seven learners: 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 13.

## Subtopic: The Mean

The mean is what most people call the average. Add all the values, then divide by how many values there are.

Seven learners did 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 13 hours of homework. The sum is 49. There are seven values. The mean is 49 divided by 7, which is 7 hours.

Think of the mean as sharing equally: if all 49 hours were shared evenly among the seven learners, each would get 7.

The mean does not have to be one of the data values, and it need not be a whole number. Marks of 6, 7, 8 and 10 have sum 31 and mean 31 divided by 4, which is 7,75.

The questions for this section are ready: sum, then divide by the count.

## Subtopic: The Median and the Mode

The median is the middle value when the data is in order.

Our homework hours are already ordered: 3, 5, 5, 6, 8, 9, 13. With seven values, the middle one is the fourth: 6. So the median is 6 hours.

With an even number of values there are two middle values; the median is halfway between them. For 4, 7, 9 and 12, the middle two are 7 and 9, so the median is 8.

The mode is the value that occurs most often. In our data, 5 appears twice and every other value once, so the mode is 5. A data set can have two modes, or no mode if every value appears once.

The mode is the only average that works for categories: in the transport survey, the modal way of travelling was walking.

Pause here for this section's questions: middle value and most common value.

## Subtopic: The Range and Choosing an Average

The range measures how spread out the data is: largest value minus smallest value. For our homework data, 13 minus 3 is 10 hours.

Two classes can have the same mean but very different ranges. Marks of 49, 50 and 51 and marks of 20, 50 and 80 both have mean 50, but ranges of 2 and 60.

Which average should you use? Look at this pocket money data: R20, R25, R25, R30 and R200. The mean is 300 divided by 5, R60 — but four of the five learners get far less than R60. The one large value, an outlier, pulls the mean up. The median, R25, describes a typical learner far better.

So: use the mean when values are fairly even, the median when there are outliers, and the mode for categories or the most popular choice.

Here come the questions for this section: spread, outliers and choosing wisely.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same four measures now, with five friends and a pile of sweets.

## Subtopic: Fair Share, Middle, Most

Five friends have 2, 4, 4, 5 and 10 sweets.

Mean — fair share. Put all the sweets in a pile: 25. Share among 5 friends: 5 each.

Median — the middle. Line them up in order: 2, 4, 4, 5, 10. The middle friend has 4.

Mode — the most common. Two friends have 4, so the mode is 4.

Range — the gap. The most is 10, the least is 2. Ten minus two is 8.

Notice the friend with 10 pulls the mean up to 5, while most friends have 4 or fewer. That is why the median is sometimes fairer.

Your questions for this section are with you now: share, middle, most, gap.

## Subtopic: Even Lists and Two Middles

What if there are six numbers? 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9.

There is no single middle. The two middle numbers are 4 and 6.

The median is halfway between them: add them and halve. Four plus six is ten, halved is five.

So the median is 5, even though 5 is not in the list. That is fine!

Quick check for the middle position: with 7 values, the 4th; with 9 values, the 5th; with an even count, the two in the centre.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now: find the middle.
