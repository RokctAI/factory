### subtopic_1 — Square Millimetres and Square Centimetres
**Assistant:** Why is the conversion factor for area one hundred when for length it is ten?

**Tutor:** Because area is length times length, so the length factor is used twice. A square centimetre is ten millimetres along one side and ten millimetres along the other, and you multiply those to get the area: ten times ten, one hundred square millimetres. Any time you convert an area, each of the two lengths is converted, so the factor is squared. That is why the little two in cm² is a useful reminder: square the length factor.

### subtopic_2 — Square Centimetres and Square Metres
**Assistant:** Is it better to convert the lengths before calculating the area, or to convert the area afterwards?

**Tutor:** Both give the same answer, so use whichever is safer for the problem — and the other as a check. Converting the lengths first, sixty centimetres to 0,6 metres and forty to 0,4, avoids the squared factor entirely because you only use length conversions you already know. Converting the area afterwards requires remembering that the factor is 10 000, not one hundred. When you are unsure, convert lengths first; when you already have an area, convert with the squared factor and check by the other route.

### subtopic_3 — Conversions in Problems
**Assistant:** How do I avoid getting lost in large numbers when converting areas?

**Tutor:** Choose units that keep the numbers sensible, and estimate first. For a wall, square metres keep the numbers small; for a tile, square centimetres do. When you must combine them, convert the smaller object into the larger unit, as with the tile becoming 0,0225 square metres. Then estimate: a tile about fifteen centimetres square means about six or seven tiles per metre each way, about forty per square metre, so ten square metres needs about four hundred tiles. If your answer is far from that, check the conversion factor.
