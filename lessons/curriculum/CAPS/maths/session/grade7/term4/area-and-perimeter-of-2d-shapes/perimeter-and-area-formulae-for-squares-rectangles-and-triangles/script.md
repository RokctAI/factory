# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers using formulae to calculate the perimeter and area of squares, rectangles and triangles, including the meaning of base and perpendicular height. The anchors are a classroom floor 8 m by 6 m and a triangular flag with base 40 cm and height 30 cm.

## Subtopic: Area of Squares and Rectangles

Area measures the amount of flat surface a shape covers. It is measured in square units: square millimetres, square centimetres, square metres. One square centimetre is the area of a square with sides of one centimetre.

A rectangle can be covered by rows of unit squares. The classroom floor is eight metres long and six metres wide. It holds six rows of eight one-metre squares: forty-eight square metres. That is why the formula is area equals length times breadth: A equals l times b.

A square has equal sides, so its area is side times side: A equals s squared. The notice board with sides of 1,2 metres has an area of 1,2 times 1,2, which is 1,44 square metres.

Perimeter formulas from the previous lesson: rectangle, P equals two l plus two b; square, P equals four s. The classroom's perimeter is two times eight plus two times six, twenty-eight metres.

Area and perimeter are different measures. A rectangle twelve by four has a perimeter of thirty-two and an area of forty-eight; a rectangle eight by eight has perimeter thirty-two and area sixty-four. Same perimeter, different areas.

Always write the correct unit: metres for perimeter, square metres for area.

The questions for this section are ready: use the formulas, and keep length units and square units apart.

## Subtopic: Area of Triangles

The area of a triangle is half the base times the perpendicular height: A equals one half times b times h.

Why? Draw the triangle inside a rectangle with the same base and the same height. The triangle fills exactly half of the rectangle: the parts of the rectangle outside the triangle can be cut and fitted together to make a second copy of the triangle. So the triangle's area is half of base times height.

The height must be PERPENDICULAR to the base: the straight-up distance from the base to the opposite vertex, meeting the base at a right angle. It is not the length of a sloping side.

The triangular flag has a base of forty centimetres and a perpendicular height of thirty centimetres. Area: half of forty times thirty, half of 1 200, 600 square centimetres.

Any side can be the base, as long as the height is measured perpendicular to that side. In a right-angled triangle, the two sides that form the right angle can serve as base and height. A right-angled triangle with shorter sides six and eight centimetres has an area of half of forty-eight, twenty-four square centimetres.

For an obtuse-angled triangle, the perpendicular height may fall outside the triangle, on the extension of the base. The formula still works.

Pause here for this section's questions: identify the base and perpendicular height, and apply the formula.

## Subtopic: Using Formulae Carefully

To use a formula reliably, follow the same steps each time.

One: write the formula. Two: substitute the values, with units consistent. Three: calculate. Four: write the answer with the correct unit.

Example: a rectangular garden is 7,5 metres by 4 metres. A equals l times b equals 7,5 times 4 equals thirty square metres. P equals two l plus two b equals fifteen plus eight equals twenty-three metres.

Mixed units: a rectangle is 2 metres by 50 centimetres. Convert first: 50 centimetres is 0,5 metres. Area: two times 0,5, one square metre. If you multiply two by fifty without converting, you get one hundred, which is wrong in any unit.

Working backwards with formulas: a rectangle has area forty-eight square metres and length eight metres. Breadth equals area divided by length: six metres. A triangle has area 600 square centimetres and base forty centimetres. Height: 600 equals half of forty times h, which is twenty h, so h is thirty centimetres.

Squares backwards: a square has area forty-nine square centimetres; its side is the square root, seven centimetres.

Here come the questions for this section: substitute carefully, convert first, and work backwards.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same classroom now: tiles on the floor and a triangular flag on the wall. Same formulae, things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Tiling the Classroom Floor

The classroom floor is eight metres long and six metres wide. How many one-metre square tiles would cover it?

Lay a row of tiles along the long wall: eight tiles. How many rows fit across the room? Six. So six rows of eight: forty-eight tiles.

Each tile is one square metre, so the floor's area is forty-eight square metres. That is where the rule comes from: area of a rectangle equals length times width.

A square is a rectangle with equal sides. The notice board is 1,2 metres on each side: 1,2 times 1,2 is 1,44 square metres.

Do not mix up area with perimeter. Perimeter is the skirting board running around the edge of the floor: eight plus six plus eight plus six, twenty-eight metres. Area is the tiles covering the floor: forty-eight square metres. One is a distance, the other is a surface.

Your questions for this section are with you now: rows times tiles in a row, and the right unit.

## Subtopic: Half a Rectangle

Now the triangular flag. Its bottom edge, the base, is forty centimetres, and it is thirty centimetres tall, measured straight up from the base to the top point.

Draw a rectangle around the flag, forty centimetres wide and thirty tall. The rectangle's area is forty times thirty, 1 200 square centimetres.

Look closely: the flag covers exactly half of that rectangle. If you cut off the two empty corner pieces, they fit together into a second flag! So the flag is half the rectangle: 1 200 divided by two, 600 square centimetres.

That is the rule for every triangle: half of base times height.

But be careful with the height. It must be measured STRAIGHT UP from the base, at a square corner — like the height of a person standing up straight, not the length of a slide. A sloping side is longer than the real height and gives the wrong answer.

You can now find the area of rectangles, squares and triangles. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: draw the rectangle in your head, then take half.
