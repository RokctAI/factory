### subtopic_1 — Perimeter of Irregular Polygons
**Assistant:** Why does an L-shaped figure have the same perimeter as the rectangle around it?

**Tutor:** Because the cut-out corner only moves two sides inward without changing their total length. Imagine pushing the top-right corner of the rectangle in: the top edge gets shorter by some amount, but an inner horizontal edge of exactly that amount appears lower down; the right edge gets shorter, but an inner vertical edge of the same amount appears further left. All the horizontal pieces still add up to twice the width, and all the vertical pieces to twice the height. So the perimeter is unchanged, even though the area is smaller.

### subtopic_2 — Perimeter of Regular Polygons
**Assistant:** Why can I multiply instead of adding for a regular polygon?

**Tutor:** Because all its sides are the same length, and adding the same number several times is exactly what multiplication means. A regular hexagon with sides of twelve centimetres has six sides of twelve, so twelve plus twelve plus twelve plus twelve plus twelve plus twelve is six times twelve. The formula P equals n times s is just a shortcut for that repeated addition. It only works when every side is equal; for irregular shapes you must add the sides individually.

### subtopic_3 — Missing Sides and Perimeter Problems
**Assistant:** Why does a closed loop need the same number of posts as gaps, but a straight fence needs one more?

**Tutor:** Because on a straight fence the first and last posts are at different ends, so you need one post at the start and one after every gap: four gaps of five metres along twenty metres need five posts. On a closed loop, the last gap ends back at the very first post, so that post does double duty and is not counted twice: the number of posts equals the number of gaps. Drawing a small example with three or four gaps is the quickest way to check which situation you are in.
