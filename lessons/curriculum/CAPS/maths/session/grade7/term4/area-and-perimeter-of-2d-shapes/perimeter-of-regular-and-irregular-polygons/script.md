# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers calculating the perimeter of regular and irregular polygons, including missing sides and working in consistent units. The anchor is an irregular pentagonal sports field with a perimeter of 320,5 m.

## Subtopic: Perimeter of Irregular Polygons

The perimeter of a closed figure is the total distance around its boundary. For a polygon, it is the sum of the lengths of all its sides.

The sports field is an irregular pentagon: five sides of different lengths, 85 metres, 62 metres, 70 metres, 48 metres and 55,5 metres. Perimeter: add them. 85 plus 62 is 147; plus 70 is 217; plus 48 is 265; plus 55,5 is 320,5 metres.

A good habit: start at one vertex and go round in order, ticking each side as you add it, so that no side is missed or counted twice.

All sides must be in the same unit before adding. If one side were given as 4 800 centimetres, convert it to 48 metres first.

Perimeter is a length, so its unit is a length unit — millimetres, centimetres, metres or kilometres — never square units.

Rectilinear shapes, made of horizontal and vertical sides, sometimes have unmarked sides. An L-shaped garden with outer sides twelve metres and eight metres, and an inner corner cut out five by three metres: the unmarked sides can be found by subtraction, and the perimeter of an L-shape equals the perimeter of the full rectangle around it: two times twelve plus two times eight, forty metres.

The questions for this section are ready: add every side, in one unit, and find unmarked sides.

## Subtopic: Perimeter of Regular Polygons

A regular polygon has all sides equal and all angles equal. Its perimeter is the number of sides times the length of one side.

An equilateral triangle with sides of nine centimetres: three times nine, twenty-seven centimetres. A square with sides of 6,5 metres: four times 6,5, twenty-six metres. A regular hexagon, like a honeycomb cell or a hexagonal paving stone, with sides of twelve centimetres: six times twelve, seventy-two centimetres. A regular octagon with sides of 2,5 metres: eight times 2,5, twenty metres.

In general: perimeter equals n times s, where n is the number of sides and s the side length.

Rectangles are not regular, but they have two pairs of equal sides: perimeter equals two times length plus two times width, or two times the sum of length and width. A rectangle fifteen metres by eight metres: two times twenty-three, forty-six metres.

Working backwards: a square has a perimeter of thirty-four centimetres; each side is thirty-four divided by four, 8,5 centimetres. A regular pentagon has a perimeter of 42 metres; each side is 8,4 metres.

Pause here for this section's questions: multiply for regular polygons and divide to work backwards.

## Subtopic: Missing Sides and Perimeter Problems

When the perimeter is known, a missing side can be found by subtracting the known sides from it.

The sports field has a perimeter of 320,5 metres. If the fifth side were unknown, it would be 320,5 minus the sum of the other four, 265, which gives 55,5 metres.

An isosceles triangle with a base of fourteen centimetres and perimeter of forty: the two equal sides share twenty-six centimetres, thirteen each.

Practical problems: fencing. To fence the sports field with three strands of wire, you need three times the perimeter: 961,5 metres of wire. If wire costs R4,50 per metre, the cost is about R4 327.

Posts: a fence around a closed figure with posts every five metres along a perimeter of 320 metres needs 320 divided by five, sixty-four posts. For a closed loop, the number of posts equals the number of gaps; for an open, straight fence, it is one more than the number of gaps.

Here come the questions for this section: missing sides, fencing and posts.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same sports field now, walked around and then fenced. Same perimeter, real-life jobs.

## Subtopic: Walking Around the Sports Field

Imagine walking all the way around the edge of the school sports field, starting at the gate. The field has five sides, all different lengths.

First side: 85 metres. Second: 62 metres. Third: 70. Fourth: 48. Fifth, back to the gate: 55,5 metres.

The total distance you walked is the perimeter: 85 plus 62 is 147; plus 70 is 217; plus 48 is 265; plus 55,5 is 320,5 metres.

The trick is to walk in order and tick off each side as you go, so you never miss one or count one twice.

Some shapes are easier. A square paving stone with sides of thirty centimetres: four equal sides, so four times thirty, 120 centimetres. A honeycomb-shaped tile with six equal sides of twelve centimetres: six times twelve, seventy-two centimetres. When all the sides are equal, just multiply.

Your questions for this section are with you now: walk around, add every side, or multiply when they are equal.

## Subtopic: Fences, Wire and Posts

The school wants to fence the sports field. They need to know how much wire to buy.

The perimeter is 320,5 metres. But the fence has three strands of wire, one above the other. So they need three times as much: 961,5 metres of wire.

Now the posts. They put a post every five metres. Around a closed field, imagine walking around: post, five metres, post, five metres, and so on, until you arrive back at the first post. The first post is also the last one! So there are as many posts as gaps: 320 metres divided by five is sixty-four posts — using 320 metres to keep it simple.

But a straight fence along one side is different. A fence twenty metres long with a post every five metres: post at the start, then at 5, 10, 15 and 20. That is FIVE posts for four gaps. Straight fences need one extra post at the end.

And if part of the perimeter is missing — say you know the total is 320,5 metres and four sides add to 265 — just take them away: 55,5 metres for the missing side.

You can now find perimeters and solve fencing problems. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: add, multiply, and count the posts carefully.
