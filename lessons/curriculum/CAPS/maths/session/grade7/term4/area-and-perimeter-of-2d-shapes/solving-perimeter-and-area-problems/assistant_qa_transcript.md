### subtopic_1 — Composite Shapes: Splitting Into Rectangles and Triangles
**Assistant:** Why is it useful to check a composite area by subtraction?

**Tutor:** Because the subtraction method uses different calculations from the splitting method, so if they agree, an error is unlikely. Splitting adds the areas of the pieces; subtracting starts with the big rectangle around the shape and takes away the missing part. A mistake in finding an unmarked length, like the 2,6 metres, would usually affect the two methods differently, so a disagreement tells you to look again. It also helps you notice when a shape is easier to handle as a big rectangle with a corner missing.

### subtopic_2 — Shapes With Holes and Practical Coverings
**Assistant:** Why must I round up when buying paint or tiles?

**Tutor:** Because rounding down would leave part of the job undone. If the calculation says 2,6 litres and you buy two litres, a strip of wall stays unpainted. With tiles, you also need extras because some will be cut to fit edges and some may break. Shops sell whole tins and whole boxes, so the practical answer is the next amount that covers the need. The mathematics gives the minimum; the context says you must have at least that much.

### subtopic_3 — Perimeter or Area? Choosing and Combining
**Assistant:** Why does a square give the biggest area for a fixed perimeter?

**Tutor:** Because for a fixed perimeter, making one side longer forces the other side shorter by the same amount, and the product of two numbers with a fixed sum is largest when they are equal. With a perimeter of twenty, length plus width is always ten: nine and one give nine, eight and two give sixteen, seven and three give twenty-one, six and four give twenty-four, five and five give twenty-five. As the sides become more equal the area grows, reaching its largest when the rectangle is a square.
