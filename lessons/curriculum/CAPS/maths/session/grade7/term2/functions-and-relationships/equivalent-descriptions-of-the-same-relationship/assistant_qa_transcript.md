### subtopic_1 — Five Ways to Describe a Relationship
**Assistant:** If all five forms describe the same thing, why do we need all of them?

**Tutor:** Because each one makes a different job easy. Words are the easiest to understand when you first meet a situation. A flow diagram makes the order of operations visible, so you can run it forwards and backwards. A table lets you see many values and spot patterns, such as the cost rising by R20 every ten minutes. A formula is compact and works for any number of minutes, even fractions. Number sentences show each calculation explicitly, which is useful for checking. Being able to switch forms lets you choose the best tool for each question.

### subtopic_2 — Checking That Descriptions Are Equivalent
**Assistant:** Why is testing just one input not enough to show two descriptions are equivalent?

**Tutor:** Because two different rules can agree at one input by coincidence. For example, y equals two x plus three and y equals three x plus two both give five when x is one, but at x equals two they give seven and eight. Testing several inputs makes coincidence unlikely, and testing an input like zero often reveals a different starting value. Best of all is a reason that covers every input, such as using the distributive property to show that two times, m plus twenty-five, is the same expression as two m plus fifty.

### subtopic_3 — Interpreting Relationships in Context
**Assistant:** How can a table help me decide between two phone plans?

**Tutor:** Put the inputs in one row and the outputs of both plans underneath, side by side. Then you can see at a glance which plan is cheaper for each number of minutes, and where the cheaper plan switches over. For plan A, two m plus fifty, and plan B, four m, the costs are seventy and forty at ten minutes, ninety and eighty at twenty, one hundred and one hundred at twenty-five, and 110 and 120 at thirty. The switch happens at twenty-five minutes, so your choice depends on how much you expect to talk.
