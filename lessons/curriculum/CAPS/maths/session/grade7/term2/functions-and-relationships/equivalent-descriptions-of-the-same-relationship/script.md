# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers determining, interpreting and justifying the equivalence of different descriptions of the same relationship, presented verbally, in flow diagrams, in tables, by formulae and by number sentences. The anchor is a phone plan: cost = 2 × minutes + 50.

## Subtopic: Five Ways to Describe a Relationship

A relationship connects input values to output values. The same relationship can be described in different forms, and each form is useful for a different job.

In words: the monthly cost is R50 plus R2 for every minute of calls.

As a flow diagram: minutes in, times two, plus fifty, cost out.

As a table: minutes zero, ten, twenty, thirty; cost R50, R70, R90, R110.

As a formula: c equals two times m plus fifty, where m is the minutes and c the cost in rand.

As number sentences, one for each input: two times zero plus fifty equals fifty; two times ten plus fifty equals seventy; two times twenty plus fifty equals ninety.

Words are easy to understand. The flow diagram shows the order of operations. The table shows many values at once and makes patterns visible. The formula is short and works for any input. Number sentences show each calculation explicitly.

The questions for this section are ready: recognise each form and translate between them.

## Subtopic: Checking That Descriptions Are Equivalent

Two descriptions are equivalent if they give the same output for every input. To check, test several inputs in both.

Is the table minutes zero, ten, twenty; cost fifty, seventy, ninety equivalent to the formula c equals two m plus fifty? Test: two times zero plus fifty is fifty; two times ten plus fifty is seventy; two times twenty plus fifty is ninety. All match. Equivalent.

Is the flow diagram "plus fifty, then times two" equivalent? Test m equals ten: ten plus fifty is sixty, times two is 120. The formula gives seventy. Not equivalent — the order of operations has changed the relationship.

Is the formula c equals two times, open bracket, m plus twenty-five, close bracket, equivalent? Test ten: thirty-five times two is seventy. Test zero: twenty-five times two is fifty. Test twenty: forty-five times two is ninety. All match. In fact, by the distributive property, two times m plus twenty-five equals two m plus fifty, so they are equivalent for every input. Different-looking descriptions can be the same relationship.

A single matching input is not enough: test several, including zero, and look for a reason, such as the distributive property, that explains why they match.

Pause here for this section's questions: test descriptions against each other and justify equivalence.

## Subtopic: Interpreting Relationships in Context

Each part of a description has a meaning in the context, and interpreting it is part of the skill.

In c equals two m plus fifty: the two is the rate, rand per minute; the fifty is the fixed monthly cost, the value of c when m is zero. In the table, the fifty appears as the cost for zero minutes, and the two appears as the cost increase of R20 for every ten minutes, which is two per minute.

Compare a second plan: no monthly fee, R4 per minute. Formula: c equals four m. Table: zero, ten, twenty, thirty minutes cost zero, forty, eighty, 120 rand.

Which plan is cheaper? At ten minutes: plan A seventy, plan B forty — B cheaper. At thirty minutes: A 110, B 120 — A cheaper. They cost the same where two m plus fifty equals four m: at twenty-five minutes, both cost R100. Below twenty-five minutes plan B is better; above, plan A.

Making tables of both relationships side by side is a powerful way to compare situations and make decisions.

Here come the questions for this section: interpret the numbers in a formula and compare two relationships.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same phone plan now, dressed in five different outfits, and an imposter to catch. Same rule, different forms.

## Subtopic: The Phone Plan in Five Outfits

Thabo's phone plan costs R50 every month, plus R2 for each minute he talks. That is the rule in words.

The same rule can wear different outfits.

As a machine — a flow diagram: minutes go in, times two, plus fifty, cost comes out.

As a table: zero minutes, R50. Ten minutes, R70. Twenty minutes, R90. Thirty minutes, R110.

As a formula: c equals two times m plus fifty. The letters are just short for cost and minutes.

As number sentences: two times ten plus fifty equals seventy. Two times twenty plus fifty equals ninety.

Different outfits, same person underneath. Whichever form you are given, you can always change it into another. If you have the words, you can build the machine. If you have the machine, you can fill in the table. If you have the table, you can find the formula.

Your questions for this section are with you now: spot the rule in each outfit.

## Subtopic: Spot the Imposter

Here are three descriptions. Which one is NOT the same as Thabo's plan?

One: a table — 0 minutes R50, 10 minutes R70, 20 minutes R90.

Two: a machine — add fifty, then times two.

Three: a formula — two times, open bracket, m plus twenty-five, close bracket.

Test them with ten minutes. The real plan gives R70. The table says R70 — good. The machine: ten plus fifty is sixty, times two is 120 — wrong! The formula: ten plus twenty-five is thirty-five, times two is seventy — good.

So the machine is the imposter. It has the right operations, but in the wrong order, and the order changes everything.

But be careful — one test is not always enough. Test a few inputs, like zero, ten and twenty. If they all match, the descriptions are probably the same. And here is the reason the formula works: two lots of twenty-five is fifty, so two times m plus twenty-five is the same as two m plus fifty.

You can now describe a relationship five ways and catch an imposter. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: test several inputs, and look for the reason.
