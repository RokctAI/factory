### subtopic_1 — Where Powers and Roots Fit in the Order of Operations
**Assistant:** Why do powers come before multiplication?

**Tutor:** Because a power is itself a multiplication that has been packed into a short form. Three squared means three times three, so it must be treated as one number, nine, before it is combined with anything else. If you multiplied first, you would be breaking that package apart and attaching another number to it, which changes its meaning: two times three squared would become six squared, thirty-six, instead of the eighteen that the expression actually describes. Doing powers and roots early keeps each package intact.

### subtopic_2 — Laws of Operations With Powers and Roots
**Assistant:** Why can I split a square root over multiplication but not over addition?

**Tutor:** Because squaring a product gives a product of squares: two times five, squared, is two squared times five squared, four times twenty-five, which is one hundred. So undoing it, the square root of four times twenty-five is two times five. But squaring a sum does NOT give a sum of squares: six plus eight squared is 196, not thirty-six plus sixty-four. Because squaring does not split over addition, its undoing, the square root, cannot either. Testing with small numbers is the quickest way to be sure.

### subtopic_3 — Multi-Step Calculations
**Assistant:** Why should I write each step on a new line?

**Tutor:** Because multi-step calculations have several places where a slip can happen, and a single line of mental work hides them. Writing each level on its own line — powers, then division, then subtraction — lets you see exactly what you did, check each line against the order of operations, and find an error quickly if your answer looks wrong. Examiners also award method marks for correct steps, even if the final answer slips, so showing the lines can earn marks you would otherwise lose.
