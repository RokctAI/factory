### subtopic_1 — The Commutative Property With Integers
**Assistant:** If subtraction is not commutative, how can I rearrange a calculation that has subtraction in it?

**Tutor:** Rewrite every subtraction as adding the opposite first. Five minus minus two becomes five plus two; ten minus three becomes ten plus minus three. Now the whole calculation is a sum, and addition is commutative, so you may move the terms around. The important thing is that each sign travels with its number: the minus three stays a minus three wherever it goes. Rearranging the original subtraction directly, without this step, swaps which number is being taken away and changes the answer.

### subtopic_2 — The Associative Property With Integers
**Assistant:** Why is grouping the positives and negatives allowed?

**Tutor:** Because addition of integers is both commutative and associative. The commutative property lets you move every positive term next to the other positives and every negative term next to the other negatives. The associative property lets you bracket them into two groups and add each group separately. Neither step changes the sum. The result is just two numbers to combine at the end, one positive and one negative, which is far less likely to go wrong than tracking the sign through every step.

### subtopic_3 — Using the Properties to Check and Simplify
**Assistant:** Why does adding minus twenty and then one give the same result as adding minus nineteen?

**Tutor:** Because minus nineteen is exactly minus twenty plus one: twenty steps left and one step back right lands nineteen steps left. So thirty-five plus minus nineteen equals thirty-five plus, open bracket minus twenty plus one close bracket. The associative property lets you regroup that as thirty-five plus minus twenty, which is fifteen, then plus one, sixteen. Splitting an awkward number into a friendly one and a small correction is a shortcut that rests directly on the associative property.
