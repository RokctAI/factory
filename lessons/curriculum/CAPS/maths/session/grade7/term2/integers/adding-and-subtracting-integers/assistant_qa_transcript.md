### subtopic_1 — Adding Integers on the Number Line
**Assistant:** When the signs are different, why do I subtract the sizes?

**Tutor:** Because the positive and negative parts cancel each other out. Minus twenty-five plus thirty is like owing twenty-five rand and then receiving thirty: twenty-five of the thirty pays off the debt, and five is left over. The amount that cancels is the smaller size, so you subtract it from the larger. The sign of the answer belongs to whichever side had more — here the positive thirty — so the result is positive five. On the number line, you first travel back to zero, which uses up the smaller size, and then continue with the rest.

### subtopic_2 — Subtracting Integers
**Assistant:** Why does subtracting a negative make the answer bigger?

**Tutor:** Because subtracting asks how far you must go from the second number to reach the first, or what happens when something is removed. Removing a debt makes you richer. If your account has a R4 fee charged by mistake — a minus four — and the bank removes it, your balance increases by R4. On the number line, three minus minus four asks for the distance from minus four up to three, which is seven steps. In every picture, taking away a negative has the same effect as adding the positive, which is why the rule is to add the opposite.

### subtopic_3 — Mixed Calculations and Checking
**Assistant:** Why is grouping the positives and negatives a good check?

**Tutor:** Because addition of integers is commutative and associative, so the order does not change the answer. Rewriting every subtraction as adding the opposite turns the calculation into one long sum, and you can then add all the positives together and all the negatives together. That gives two simple numbers to combine at the end. If this answer matches your left-to-right answer, you can be confident; if not, a sign was dropped somewhere along the way.
