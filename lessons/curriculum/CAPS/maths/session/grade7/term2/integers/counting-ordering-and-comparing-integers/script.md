# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces integers, counting forwards and backwards in integers for any interval, and recognising, ordering and comparing integers on a number line. The anchor temperatures are −6 °C, 3 °C and 14 °C.

## Subtopic: What Integers Are and Where They Live

Integers are the whole numbers together with their negatives: and so on, minus three, minus two, minus one, zero, one, two, three, and so on. Positive integers are greater than zero; negative integers are less than zero; zero itself is neither positive nor negative.

Negative numbers describe quantities below a reference point. Temperatures below freezing: Sutherland in the Northern Cape can reach minus six degrees Celsius on a winter morning. Depths below sea level. An overdrawn bank balance. Floors below ground level in a parking garage.

On a horizontal number line, zero sits in the middle, positive integers to the right and negative integers to the left. Each integer has an opposite at the same distance from zero on the other side: the opposite of six is minus six, and the opposite of minus three is three. Opposites are also called additive inverses, because together they make zero.

The distance of an integer from zero, ignoring its sign, is sometimes called its size or absolute value. Minus six and six are both six units from zero.

A minus sign written in front of a number is read as negative or minus: minus six degrees. Brackets are often used to make the sign clear in a calculation: open bracket minus six close bracket.

The questions for this section are ready: identify positive and negative integers, opposites and their positions on the number line.

## Subtopic: Counting Forwards and Backwards Across Zero

Counting forwards means moving right on the number line; counting backwards means moving left. Integers continue smoothly through zero.

Count forwards in ones from minus four: minus three, minus two, minus one, zero, one, two. Count backwards in threes from seven: four, one, minus two, minus five, minus eight. Notice that when you cross zero going backwards, the numbers keep getting further from zero on the negative side.

Count forwards in fives from minus twenty: minus fifteen, minus ten, minus five, zero, five. Count backwards in twenty-fives from fifty: twenty-five, zero, minus twenty-five, minus fifty.

The temperature rises from minus six to three degrees by midday in Sutherland. Counting forwards in ones: minus five, minus four, minus three, minus two, minus one, zero, one, two, three. That is nine steps, so the temperature rose by nine degrees.

The common error when counting forwards from a negative number is to make the numbers bigger in size: counting forwards from minus four as minus five, minus six. On the negative side, moving forwards means getting CLOSER to zero.

Pause here for this section's questions: count across zero in any interval and count the steps between integers.

## Subtopic: Ordering and Comparing Integers

On a horizontal number line, numbers increase from left to right. So of any two integers, the one further to the right is greater.

Any positive integer is greater than any negative integer: three is greater than minus six. Zero is greater than every negative integer and less than every positive integer.

Comparing two negative integers is where errors happen. Minus six and minus two: minus two is to the right of minus six, so minus two is greater. Written with the symbol: minus two is greater than minus six, or minus six is less than minus two. On a thermometer: minus two degrees is warmer than minus six.

Order the anchor temperatures together with Bloemfontein at minus two degrees, from coldest to warmest: minus six, minus two, three, fourteen. In ascending order, the most negative number comes first.

A useful rule: among negative integers, the one with the larger size is the smaller number. Minus 100 is less than minus one.

Here come the questions for this section: compare with the greater-than and less-than symbols and order integers in both directions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three towns now, on a thermometer you can picture. Same integers, warmer and colder.

## Subtopic: The Thermometer Number Line

A thermometer is a number line standing up. Zero degrees is where water freezes. Above zero, it gets warmer: one, two, three degrees. Below zero, it gets colder: minus one, minus two, minus three.

On a cold winter morning, Sutherland in the Northern Cape might be minus six degrees. Johannesburg might be three degrees. Durban, by the warm sea, might be fourteen degrees.

Where is each on the thermometer? Fourteen is high up. Three is just above zero. Minus six is six steps BELOW zero.

As the sun comes up, Sutherland warms up. Count up from minus six: minus five, minus four, minus three, minus two, minus one, zero, one, two, three. Nine steps. By midday Sutherland reaches three degrees, as warm as Johannesburg was in the morning.

Notice: as it warms up from minus six, the numbers after the minus sign get SMALLER — six, five, four — until zero, and then the positive numbers grow.

Your questions for this section are with you now: up is warmer, down is colder, and count the steps.

## Subtopic: Who Is Coldest?

Now let us put towns in order from coldest to warmest: Durban fourteen, Sutherland minus six, Johannesburg three, and Bloemfontein minus two.

The coldest is the one lowest on the thermometer. Sutherland, minus six, is the lowest. Next comes Bloemfontein at minus two — still below zero, but higher than minus six. Then Johannesburg at three, and Durban at fourteen at the top.

So: minus six, minus two, three, fourteen.

The tricky part is always two minus numbers. Which is warmer, minus two or minus six? Think about it like this: being two steps below zero is less cold than being six steps below zero. So minus two is warmer — it is the bigger number.

We write this with the same symbols as always: minus two is greater than minus six. The open mouth of the symbol faces the bigger number.

You can now place, count and compare integers. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: lower is smaller, higher is bigger.
