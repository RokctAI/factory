# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising and using the commutative and associative properties of addition for integers, and why they do not apply to subtraction unless it is rewritten as addition of the opposite. The anchor is the lift ride: start at four, add minus seven, nine, minus six and three, ending at three.

## Subtopic: The Commutative Property With Integers

The commutative property of addition says that the order of two numbers does not change their sum. It holds for integers exactly as it holds for whole numbers.

Minus seven plus nine is two. Nine plus minus seven is also two. On the number line: start at minus seven and move nine right, landing on two; or start at nine and move seven left, also landing on two. Same destination.

Minus three plus minus five is minus eight, and minus five plus minus three is minus eight.

In general, for any integers a and b, a plus b equals b plus a.

Subtraction is NOT commutative: five minus minus two is seven, but minus two minus five is minus seven. Swapping the numbers changes the answer, and here even its sign.

However, every subtraction can be rewritten as an addition of the opposite: five minus minus two is five plus two. Once it is written as addition, the terms can be swapped freely.

The questions for this section are ready: test the commutative property with integers and spot where it fails.

## Subtopic: The Associative Property With Integers

The associative property of addition says that when three or more numbers are added, the way they are grouped does not change the sum.

Group the first two: open bracket minus seven plus nine close bracket, plus minus six: two plus minus six is minus four. Group the last two: minus seven plus, open bracket nine plus minus six close bracket: minus seven plus three is minus four. Same sum.

In general, open bracket a plus b close bracket plus c equals a plus, open bracket b plus c close bracket.

Together, the commutative and associative properties mean that in a sum of integers you may add the terms in any order and any grouping. This allows clever strategies.

Strategy one: group positives and negatives. The lift: four plus minus seven plus nine plus minus six plus three. Positives: four plus nine plus three is sixteen. Negatives: minus seven plus minus six is minus thirteen. Sixteen plus minus thirteen is three.

Strategy two: look for opposites that cancel to zero, or pairs that make friendly numbers. In minus twenty-three plus fifty-eight plus twenty-three, minus twenty-three and twenty-three cancel, leaving fifty-eight.

Pause here for this section's questions: regroup sums, group by sign and cancel opposites.

## Subtopic: Using the Properties to Check and Simplify

The properties are also a checking tool. If you calculate a long sum left to right and then again by grouping, the two answers must agree.

Left to right for the lift: four plus minus seven is minus three; plus nine is six; plus minus six is zero; plus three is three. Grouped: sixteen plus minus thirteen is three. They agree.

With subtraction included: twelve minus fifteen plus eight minus minus four. Rewrite: twelve plus minus fifteen plus eight plus four. Positives: twenty-four. Negative: minus fifteen. Answer: nine.

The properties also explain mental shortcuts. To add minus nineteen, many people add minus twenty and then add one: thirty-five plus minus nineteen is thirty-five plus minus twenty plus one, which is sixteen. This works because minus nineteen equals minus twenty plus one, and the associative property allows the regrouping.

A common error is to move a number without its sign — for example moving the six in minus six as if it were positive. Each integer carries its sign with it.

Here come the questions for this section: rewrite, regroup and check two ways.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same lift ride now, floor by floor, and pairs of numbers that cancel each other out. Same answers, easy pictures.

## Subtopic: The Lift Ride

A lift in a tall building starts on floor four. It goes down seven floors, up nine, down six and up three. Where does it end up? Floor zero is the ground floor, and floors below ground are minus floors.

Way one: follow the lift step by step. Four, down seven, minus three — that is three floors below ground. Up nine: six. Down six: zero, the ground floor. Up three: floor three.

Way two: add up all the ups and all the downs. The ups are four, nine and three — we can count the starting floor four as up from ground — sixteen floors up. The downs are seven and six — thirteen floors down. Sixteen up and thirteen down leaves three up. Floor three again!

Both ways agree. That is because when you add numbers — even negative ones — you can add them in any order and group them any way you like. Ups with ups, downs with downs.

Your questions for this section are with you now: follow the steps, or group the ups and downs.

## Subtopic: Pairs That Cancel

Here is a neat trick that grouping lets you do. Look at minus twenty-three plus fifty-eight plus twenty-three.

Going left to right is messy. But notice minus twenty-three and twenty-three. They are opposites — going down twenty-three floors and up twenty-three floors brings you back where you started. Together they make zero!

Since order does not matter, pair them up first: minus twenty-three plus twenty-three is zero. What is left is just fifty-eight.

Look for other friendly pairs. Minus fifteen plus seven plus fifteen plus three: the fifteens cancel, leaving seven plus three, ten.

But be careful with subtraction! Ten minus three is not the same as three minus ten. Before you rearrange, turn every subtraction into adding a negative: ten plus minus three. Then every number keeps its own sign as you move it, and you can rearrange safely.

You now know why integer sums can be rearranged and how to use that to calculate faster. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: look for pairs that cancel, then add what is left.
