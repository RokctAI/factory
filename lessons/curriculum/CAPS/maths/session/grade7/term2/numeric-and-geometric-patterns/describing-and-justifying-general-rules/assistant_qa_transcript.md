### subtopic_1 — Term-to-Term Rules and Their Limits
**Assistant:** How do I know the rule starts with three times the position?

**Tutor:** Because the terms increase by three each time the position increases by one. If a rule is some number times the position plus or minus something, that number must be the constant difference: each step in position adds that many to the term. So a constant difference of three tells you the rule contains three times the position. Then compare: three times the position gives 3, 6, 9, 12, but the terms are 4, 7, 10, 13, always one more. So the rule is three times the position plus one.

### subtopic_2 — Justifying a Rule From the Structure
**Assistant:** Why are 3 × n + 1 and 4 + 3 × (n − 1) the same rule?

**Tutor:** Because they count the same matches in different ways. The first counts three matches for every square plus one closing match. The second counts four matches for the first square and three for each of the remaining squares. Working the second out: three times n minus one is three n minus three, plus four gives three n plus one. Testing with any number of squares gives the same answer from both, which is a good way to check that two descriptions are equivalent.

### subtopic_3 — Using General Rules to Solve Problems
**Assistant:** How do I work backwards from a term to its position?

**Tutor:** Undo the rule in reverse order. The rule three times n plus one first multiplies by three, then adds one. To undo it, first subtract one, then divide by three. For 151 matches: 151 minus one is 150, divided by three is fifty, so it is the fiftieth pattern. If the division does not give a whole number, the term is not in the sequence at all, because positions are always whole numbers. Checking by putting your position back into the rule confirms the answer.
