### subtopic_1 — Constant Difference, Constant Ratio and Neither
**Assistant:** If the differences are not constant, how can I still find the next term?

**Tutor:** Look at the differences as a new pattern of their own. For 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 the differences are 2, 3, 4, 5, and those go up by one each time. So the next difference will be six, and the next term is fifteen plus six, twenty-one. This works for many patterns: if the first differences are not constant, check the differences of the differences. Writing the differences underneath the terms, in a second row, makes the hidden pattern easy to see.

### subtopic_2 — Square Numbers and Other Growing Patterns
**Assistant:** Why are the differences between consecutive square numbers always odd?

**Tutor:** Because of the picture. To grow a square of dots from three by three to four by four, you add a new row of three along the bottom, a new column of three down the side, and one corner dot to fill the gap. That is three plus three plus one, seven. In general you add two equal strips plus one, and twice any whole number plus one is always odd. As the squares grow, the strips get one longer each time, which is why the odd differences go up by two.

### subtopic_3 — Patterns Built From Earlier Terms
**Assistant:** How do I decide what kind of rule an unusual pattern has?

**Tutor:** Try the common tests in order. First, are the differences constant? Second, are the ratios constant? Third, do the differences themselves make a pattern? Fourth, is each term made from earlier terms, like adding the two before? Fifth, does the pattern alternate between two rules, or repeat a cycle? Whichever test you use, check the rule on every given term, not just the first two. A rule that works for all of them is very likely the intended one.
