# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing and justifying, in your own words, the general rules for observed relationships between numbers, moving from term-to-term rules to position-to-term rules. The anchor is the matchstick pattern with rule 3 × n + 1.

## Subtopic: Term-to-Term Rules and Their Limits

A term-to-term rule says how to get from one term to the next: for 4, 7, 10, 13, the rule is add three. It is easy to find and to use for the next few terms.

But it has a limit. To find the hundredth term with a term-to-term rule, you must list all ninety-nine terms before it. That is slow and error-prone.

What we want is a position-to-term rule, also called a general rule: a rule that takes the position number and gives the term directly.

Look at the matchstick table again. Position one gives four, position two gives seven, position three gives ten, position four gives thirteen. The term goes up by three every time the position goes up by one, so the rule must involve three times the position. Three times one is three, but the term is four: one more. Three times two is six, but the term is seven: one more. Three times three is nine, term ten: one more. The rule is: multiply the position by three and add one.

Check with position four: three times four plus one is thirteen. Correct.

The questions for this section are ready: tell term-to-term and position-to-term rules apart, and find a position-to-term rule from a table.

## Subtopic: Justifying a Rule From the Structure

Finding a rule that fits the numbers is good; explaining why it fits is better. The curriculum asks you to describe AND justify rules.

Justify three times the number of squares plus one, using the matchstick picture. Think of each square as having three matches of its own: a top, a bottom and a right-hand side. With n squares that is three times n matches. But the very first square also needs a left-hand side, which no other square provides. So we add one: three times n, plus one.

A different but equally valid way of seeing it: the first square needs four matches, and each of the other n minus one squares adds three. Four plus three times n minus one. For ten squares: four plus three times nine, thirty-one. And three times ten plus one is also thirty-one. Two descriptions, one rule.

Describing rules in your own words is important. "Multiply the number of squares by three and add one" and "three matches for every square, plus one extra to close the first square" are both acceptable descriptions. A formula with a letter, such as three n plus one, is a short way of writing the same idea.

Pause here for this section's questions: justify rules from pictures and recognise equivalent descriptions.

## Subtopic: Using General Rules to Solve Problems

A general rule lets you answer two kinds of question quickly.

Forward: given the position, find the term. The hundredth matchstick pattern: three times one hundred plus one, 301 matches.

Backward: given the term, find the position. Which pattern uses 151 matches? Undo the rule: subtract one, 150; divide by three, fifty. So fifty squares. Check: three times fifty plus one is 151.

Can a pattern use exactly 200 matches? Subtract one: 199. Divide by three: 66 remainder one, not a whole number. So no pattern in this sequence uses exactly 200 matches — you would always have either 199 or 202.

General rules for other patterns: 5, 9, 13, 17 has difference four, so four times the position plus one. 2, 7, 12, 17 has difference five; five times the position is 5, 10, 15, 20, three more than the terms, so five times the position minus three. Square numbers: the position times itself.

Here come the questions for this section: find far terms, work backwards and decide whether a number belongs to a pattern.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same matchstick squares now: a rule you can explain by pointing at the picture, and then working backwards from the number of matches. Same rule, plain words.

## Subtopic: The 100th Pattern Without Building It

Imagine someone asks: how many matches do you need for a row of one hundred squares? You could build them all — but that would take ages, and you might run out of matches!

Instead, look at each square in a clever way. Every square owns three matches: its top, its bottom and its right-hand side. The only match left over is the very first left-hand side, which closes the first square.

So for any row: three matches per square, plus one. For one square: three plus one, four. For four squares: twelve plus one, thirteen. For one hundred squares: three hundred plus one, 301 matches. No building needed!

That sentence — "three matches per square, plus one" — is the general rule. Saying it in your own words is perfectly fine; you can also write it short as three times n plus one, where n is the number of squares.

The best part is that you can explain WHY it works, by pointing at the picture.

Your questions for this section are with you now: three per square, plus one, for any number of squares.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards

Now the question is the other way round. You have 151 matches and you use them all to make a row of squares. How many squares did you make?

The rule is: three per square, plus one. So first take away the one extra match: 150 left. Those 150 are shared out three per square: 150 divided by three is fifty. Fifty squares!

Check: three times fifty is 150, plus one is 151. Yes.

What if you had 200 matches? Take away one: 199. Can 199 be shared out in threes exactly? Three times sixty-six is 198 — one match left over. So 200 matches cannot make a perfect row; you would have a match left.

You can find rules for other patterns the same way. For 5, 9, 13, 17, the jump is four, so it is four per step; four times one is four, plus one makes five — the rule is four times n plus one.

You now know how to describe a pattern rule, explain why it works and use it in both directions. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: undo the plus, then undo the times.
