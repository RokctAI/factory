### subtopic_1 — Patterns in Diagrams and Sequences
**Assistant:** Why is it important to connect the number pattern to the diagram?

**Tutor:** Because the numbers alone only show what has happened so far, while the diagram shows why. From 4, 7, 10, 13 you might guess the rule add three, but there are other rules that also fit four terms. The matchstick picture proves it: each new square shares one side with the previous one, so it always adds exactly three matches. A rule justified by the structure of the pattern is certain to continue; a rule found only from the numbers is a guess that could fail later.

### subtopic_2 — Patterns in Tables
**Assistant:** Why does the 10th pattern need 4 + 9 × 3 matches and not 10 × 3?

**Tutor:** Because the first square is different: it needs all four of its sides, while every square after it needs only three new matches. So ten squares means one square of four matches plus nine more squares of three matches each. Ten times three, thirty, would treat the first square like the others and miss one match. Checking with a small case confirms it: two squares would be four plus one times three, seven, which matches the picture.

### subtopic_3 — Creating Your Own Patterns
**Assistant:** What makes a pattern rule clear enough for someone else to continue?

**Tutor:** It must say where to start and exactly what to do to get from one term to the next. Start at five and add four each time gives the same sequence to everyone who follows it. A vague description like it goes up by some amount could fit many different sequences. For geometric patterns, the rule should explain what is added each time in the picture, such as one new triangle sharing a side, so the number rule can be justified from the shape.
