# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating and extending numeric and geometric patterns represented in physical or diagram form, in tables, and of learners' own creation, by finding the relationship between consecutive terms. The anchor is the matchstick pattern 4, 7, 10, 13.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Diagrams and Sequences

A pattern is an arrangement of numbers or shapes that follows a rule. Each item in a number pattern is called a term, and its position is the term number: first term, second term, and so on.

The matchstick pattern: one square needs four matches. Two squares in a row share a side, so they need seven matches. Three squares need ten. Four squares need thirteen. The sequence is 4, 7, 10, 13.

To extend a pattern, find how each term relates to the one before. Here each term is three more than the previous: seven minus four is three, ten minus seven is three, thirteen minus ten is three. This constant difference is the rule between consecutive terms. The next terms are sixteen, nineteen and twenty-two.

The diagram explains the difference. Each new square shares one side with the previous square, so it needs only three new matches, not four. Connecting the number pattern to the picture is how you justify the rule rather than just guessing it.

Other patterns: 2, 6, 18, 54 — each term is three times the previous, so the next is 162. 1, 4, 9, 16 — the square numbers, so the next is twenty-five.

The questions for this section are ready: find the relationship between consecutive terms and extend.

## Subtopic: Patterns in Tables

A table organises a pattern by pairing each term number with its term, which makes it easier to see how the pattern grows and to predict terms further along.

The matchstick table: term number one, two, three, four, five in the top row; matches four, seven, ten, thirteen, sixteen in the bottom row.

Reading across the bottom row gives the relationship between consecutive terms, plus three. Reading down each column links the term number to the term, which leads to a general rule in the next lesson.

Tables help with larger positions. How many matches for ten squares? Continuing the pattern: nineteen, twenty-two, twenty-five, twenty-eight, thirty-one for squares six to ten. So ten squares need thirty-one matches.

Another way: the first square needs four matches, and each of the other nine squares adds three. Four plus nine times three is four plus twenty-seven, thirty-one. This method avoids writing every term.

Working backwards: if a row uses forty matches, how many squares? Subtract the first four: thirty-six. Thirty-six divided by three is twelve more squares, so thirteen squares in total.

Pause here for this section's questions: complete tables, predict later terms and work backwards.

## Subtopic: Creating Your Own Patterns

Creating patterns deepens understanding of how rules generate terms. You can create a pattern by choosing a starting number and a rule.

Start at five and add four each time: 5, 9, 13, 17, 21. Start at three and multiply by two: 3, 6, 12, 24, 48. Start at one hundred and subtract seven: 100, 93, 86, 79, 72.

Geometric patterns can be built too. Triangles from matches: one triangle needs three matches, a row of two triangles sharing a side needs five, three need seven. The rule is add two, because each new triangle shares a side.

When you create a pattern, describe the rule clearly enough that someone else can continue it without seeing your terms. "Start at five and add four each time" is clear. "It goes up" is not.

A good test of a created pattern is to swap with a partner: they extend your pattern and state your rule, and you check whether they reproduced it exactly.

Here come the questions for this section: generate terms from a rule, and describe the rule of a created pattern.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same matchstick squares now, built one at a time, and then patterns you make up yourself. Same rules, hands-on.

## Subtopic: Building With Matches

Grab some matches — or imagine them. Make one square: four matches. Now add a second square next to it. Do you need four more matches? No! The new square can use the side of the first square. You need only three more. Seven matches for two squares.

Add a third square: three more matches, ten altogether. A fourth: thirteen.

Write the numbers down: 4, 7, 10, 13. Each time we add three. So the next ones are 16, 19, 22.

The picture tells us WHY it is always three: every new square borrows one side from its neighbour.

How many matches for ten squares, without building them all? The first square needs four. The other nine squares need three each: nine times three is twenty-seven. Four plus twenty-seven is thirty-one matches.

Your questions for this section are with you now: look at the picture, find what gets added, and keep going.

## Subtopic: Make Up Your Own Pattern

Now it is your turn to be the pattern maker.

Choose a starting number — say five. Choose a rule — say add four. Write the pattern: 5, 9, 13, 17, 21. You made a pattern!

Try a different kind of rule: start at three and double each time: 3, 6, 12, 24, 48. This one grows much faster, because doubling adds more and more each time.

Or a shrinking pattern: start at one hundred and take away seven: 100, 93, 86, 79, 72.

You can make picture patterns too. Make triangles out of matches in a row, each sharing a side: 3, 5, 7, 9 matches. Every new triangle needs two more matches.

Here is a challenge to try with a friend: write the first four terms of your pattern, and see if they can tell you your rule and the next two terms. If they can, your pattern has a clear rule.

You have extended patterns and created your own. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: start, rule, and keep going.
