# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers numeric and geometric patterns that are not limited to sequences with a constant difference or a constant ratio, including increasing differences, square and triangular numbers, and patterns where each term depends on earlier terms. The anchor is the staircase 1, 3, 6, 10, 15.

## Subtopic: Constant Difference, Constant Ratio and Neither

Many patterns have a constant difference: add the same number each time, like 4, 7, 10, 13. Others have a constant ratio: multiply by the same number each time, like 2, 6, 18, 54. But many patterns have neither, and the curriculum expects you to work with these too.

Test any pattern in two steps. Step one: subtract consecutive terms. If the differences are all equal, it has a constant difference. Step two: divide consecutive terms. If the ratios are all equal, it has a constant ratio.

The staircase: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. Differences: two, three, four, five. Not constant. Ratios: three, two, 1,67 and so on. Not constant. So this pattern has neither — but the differences themselves form a pattern, increasing by one each time.

That gives the way to extend it: the next difference is six, so the next term is twenty-one; then seven, giving twenty-eight.

The staircase numbers are called triangular numbers, because they can be arranged as triangles of dots: one dot, then a row of two under it, then three, and so on.

The questions for this section are ready: test for constant difference and ratio, and use the pattern in the differences.

## Subtopic: Square Numbers and Other Growing Patterns

The square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 are another pattern with neither a constant difference nor a constant ratio. Their differences are the odd numbers: three, five, seven, nine. Each difference is two more than the last.

Geometrically, each square number is a square of dots. To go from a three by three square to a four by four square you add a row of three, a column of three and one corner dot: seven new dots. That explains why the differences are odd numbers.

Another pattern: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26. Differences three, five, seven, nine — the same odd numbers. Each term is one more than a square number: one squared plus one, two squared plus one, three squared plus one. So the next term is six squared plus one, thirty-seven.

Patterns can also decrease with growing gaps: 100, 99, 97, 94, 90 — subtract one, two, three, four. The next term subtracts five: eighty-five.

Pause here for this section's questions: square-based patterns and patterns with growing gaps.

## Subtopic: Patterns Built From Earlier Terms

Some patterns are built by combining earlier terms rather than by a fixed step.

The Fibonacci sequence: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Each term is the sum of the two terms before it: one plus one is two, one plus two is three, two plus three is five, and so on. The next terms are twenty-one and thirty-four. This sequence appears in nature, for example in the number of spirals on sunflower heads and pine cones.

Alternating patterns switch between two rules: 3, 6, 4, 8, 6, 12 — multiply by two, then subtract two, then multiply by two again. The next term is ten.

Repeating patterns cycle: red, blue, blue, red, blue, blue. To find the twentieth item, notice that the pattern repeats every three items. Twenty divided by three is six remainder two, so the twentieth item is the second item in the cycle: blue.

With unusual patterns, always test your rule on every given term, then extend. If a rule fits only some terms, it is not the rule.

Here come the questions for this section: Fibonacci-style patterns, alternating rules and repeating cycles.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 now, as a staircase of blocks, and then patterns from nature and necklaces. Same thinking, real-life pictures.

## Subtopic: The Growing Staircase

Build a staircase out of blocks. Step one is one block. Step two adds a column of two blocks: three blocks altogether. Step three adds a column of three: six blocks. Step four adds four: ten blocks. Step five adds five: fifteen.

The pattern is 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. It does not go up by the same amount each time. It goes up by two, then three, then four, then five. The jumps get one bigger each time.

So what comes next? The next jump is six: fifteen plus six is twenty-one. Then seven: twenty-one plus seven is twenty-eight.

The picture makes it obvious: each new step of the staircase is one block taller than the last one, so you add one more block than last time.

These are called triangular numbers, because the blocks — or dots — make triangles.

Your questions for this section are with you now: find the jumps, see how the jumps change, and keep going.

## Subtopic: Rabbits, Sunflowers and Repeating Beads

Some patterns use their own earlier numbers to make the next one.

Start with 1 and 1. Add them: 2. Now add the last two numbers, 1 and 2: 3. Then 2 and 3: 5. Then 3 and 5: 8. Then 13, 21, 34. This is the Fibonacci pattern, and it shows up in nature — count the spirals on a sunflower or a pine cone and you often find Fibonacci numbers!

Other patterns take turns. 3, 6, 4, 8, 6, 12: double, then take away two, then double, then take away two. Next: take away two from twelve, ten.

And some patterns just repeat, like beads on a necklace: red, blue, blue, red, blue, blue. Which colour is bead number twenty? The colours repeat every three beads. Three goes into twenty six times with two left over, so bead twenty is the second bead in the group: blue.

The secret with any strange pattern is to try an idea, then test it on every number you were given.

You have now met patterns that change their step. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: find the hidden rule, test it, and extend.
