### subtopic_1 — Common Denominators With Equivalent Fractions
**Assistant:** Why does the denominator not get added when I add fractions?

**Tutor:** Because the denominator is not a quantity you are counting; it is the name of the piece size. Two fifths plus one fifth is like two apples plus one apple: you get three apples, not three double-apples. The pieces are still fifths, so the denominator stays five. Adding denominators would change the size of the pieces in the middle of a sum, which makes no sense. That is why we first make the pieces the same size with a common denominator, then count how many pieces we have altogether.

### subtopic_2 — Adding Mixed Numbers
**Assistant:** When should I convert to improper fractions instead of adding wholes and fractions separately?

**Tutor:** Both methods always work, so it is about safety and size. Adding wholes and fractions separately keeps the numbers small and is quick for addition, but you must remember to carry a whole when the fraction part adds to more than one. Converting to improper fractions avoids all carrying and borrowing, so it is the safer choice for subtraction or when you feel unsure, but the numerators can get large. A good habit is to use one method and check with the other on important questions.

### subtopic_3 — Subtracting Mixed Numbers and Borrowing a Whole
**Assistant:** What exactly happens when I borrow a whole?

**Tutor:** You break one of the whole numbers into fraction pieces of the size you are working with. In twelfths, one whole is twelve twelfths. So two and eight twelfths becomes one and twenty twelfths: you took one whole away from the two and added its twelve twelfths to the eight twelfths already there. The value has not changed, only how it is written, but now the fraction part is big enough to subtract nine twelfths from. It is the same idea as borrowing a ten in column subtraction.
