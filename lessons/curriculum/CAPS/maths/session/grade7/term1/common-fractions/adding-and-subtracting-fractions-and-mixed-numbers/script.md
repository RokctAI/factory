# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers adding and subtracting common fractions and mixed numbers using equivalent fractions and the lowest common denominator, including denominators that are not multiples of each other. The anchor quantities are 2 3/4 and 1 2/3.

## Subtopic: Common Denominators With Equivalent Fractions

You can only add or subtract fractions when they count the same size of piece, that is, when they have the same denominator. Two fifths plus one fifth is three fifths: you are adding numerators of pieces that are all fifths. The denominator stays five, because the size of the pieces does not change.

When the denominators differ, first rewrite the fractions as equivalent fractions with a common denominator. If one denominator is a multiple of the other, the larger one works: one half plus three eighths becomes four eighths plus three eighths, seven eighths.

When neither denominator is a multiple of the other, use the lowest common multiple. Three quarters plus two thirds: four and three have LCM twelve. Three quarters is nine twelfths; two thirds is eight twelfths. Nine twelfths plus eight twelfths is seventeen twelfths, which is one and five twelfths.

Subtraction uses exactly the same preparation. Three quarters minus two thirds: nine twelfths minus eight twelfths is one twelfth.

The most common error is to add the denominators as well: three quarters plus two thirds is NOT five sevenths. Five sevenths is less than one, but three quarters plus two thirds is clearly more than one. A quick size check catches it.

The questions for this section are ready: find the common denominator, convert, then add or subtract the numerators.

## Subtopic: Adding Mixed Numbers

There are two reliable methods for adding mixed numbers.

Method one: add the whole numbers and the fractions separately. Two and three quarters plus one and two thirds. Whole numbers: two plus one is three. Fractions: three quarters plus two thirds is seventeen twelfths, which is one and five twelfths. Combine: three plus one and five twelfths is four and five twelfths. Notice that the fraction part added up to more than one, so a whole was carried into the whole numbers.

Method two: convert both to improper fractions. Two and three quarters is eleven quarters; one and two thirds is five thirds. With denominator twelve: thirty-three twelfths plus twenty twelfths is fifty-three twelfths. Fifty-three divided by twelve is four remainder five, so four and five twelfths — the same answer.

Method one keeps the numbers small; method two is very safe for subtraction. Either way, simplify the final answer and write it as a mixed number unless the question asks otherwise.

Estimate first: two and three quarters is nearly three, one and two thirds is nearly two, so the total should be nearly five. Four and five twelfths is a little less than four and a half — consistent, since both numbers were rounded up.

Pause here for this section's questions: add wholes and fractions separately, carry a whole when needed, or convert to improper fractions.

## Subtopic: Subtracting Mixed Numbers and Borrowing a Whole

Subtract two and three quarters minus one and two thirds. Wholes: two minus one is one. Fractions: nine twelfths minus eight twelfths is one twelfth. Answer: one and one twelfth. That was easy because the first fraction was the bigger one.

Now reverse the fractions: two and two thirds minus one and three quarters. Wholes: one. Fractions: eight twelfths minus nine twelfths — the top fraction is too small. Borrow one whole from the two: two and eight twelfths becomes one and twenty twelfths, because one whole is twelve twelfths. Now subtract: one and twenty twelfths minus one and nine twelfths is eleven twelfths.

The improper fraction method avoids borrowing altogether: two and two thirds is thirty-two twelfths; one and three quarters is twenty-one twelfths; thirty-two minus twenty-one is eleven twelfths. Same answer.

Check by adding back: eleven twelfths plus one and three quarters — one and nine twelfths plus eleven twelfths is one and twenty twelfths, which is two and eight twelfths, two and two thirds. Correct.

The error to avoid is subtracting the smaller fraction from the larger one regardless of order — writing one and one twelfth for the second problem. Subtraction is not commutative, and the borrowing step is what respects the order.

Here come the questions for this section: subtract with and without borrowing, and check by adding back.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same 2 3/4 cups of flour and 1 2/3 cups of milk now, in Gogo's kitchen. Same answers, measured out cup by cup.

## Subtopic: Mixing the Vetkoek Batter

Gogo is mixing vetkoek batter. She uses two and three quarters cups of flour and one and two thirds cups of milk. How many cups go into the bowl altogether?

Start with the easy part: the whole cups. Two cups plus one cup is three full cups.

Now the bits. Three quarters of a cup and two thirds of a cup. You cannot add quarters and thirds straight away — they are different size pieces. What size piece fits both? Twelfths, because four and three both go into twelve. Three quarters is nine twelfths. Two thirds is eight twelfths. Together: seventeen twelfths.

Seventeen twelfths is more than a whole cup! Twelve twelfths fill one cup, and five twelfths are left over. So the bits make one and five twelfths.

Add it to the three full cups: four and five twelfths cups altogether.

Does that make sense? Two and three quarters is almost three, and one and two thirds is almost two. Almost three plus almost two is almost five. Four and five twelfths is a bit less than four and a half. Yes, that fits.

Your questions for this section are with you now: whole cups first, then the bits in same-size pieces.

## Subtopic: How Much More Flour?

Now a different question: how much MORE flour than milk did Gogo use? That is a subtraction: two and three quarters minus one and two thirds.

Whole cups: two minus one, one cup. Bits: nine twelfths minus eight twelfths, one twelfth. So she used one and one twelfth cups more flour than milk.

But what if the numbers were the other way? Suppose she used two and two thirds cups of flour and one and three quarters cups of milk. Bits: eight twelfths minus nine twelfths — you cannot take nine from eight!

So borrow. Open up one of the two whole cups and pour it into twelfths: twelve twelfths. Now you have one whole cup and twenty twelfths. Take away one cup and nine twelfths: zero whole cups and eleven twelfths. The difference is eleven twelfths of a cup.

Check by adding back: eleven twelfths plus one and three quarters should give two and two thirds. Nine twelfths plus eleven twelfths is twenty twelfths, which is one and eight twelfths; add the one cup: two and eight twelfths, which is two and two thirds. It works.

You now know how to mix fractions and how to find the difference between them. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: same-size pieces, borrow when you must, add back to check.
