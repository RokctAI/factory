### subtopic_1 — Percentage of a Whole
**Assistant:** Why is the ten-percent method so useful?

**Tutor:** Because ten percent is easy to find — just divide by ten — and almost every common percentage can be built from it. Five percent is half of ten percent, twenty percent is double, fifteen percent is ten plus five, and thirty-five percent is three tens plus a five. It turns a fraction multiplication into a few simple steps you can do in your head, and it gives you a check on any answer you get by the multiplying method. If both methods agree, you can be confident.

### subtopic_2 — Writing a Part as a Percentage
**Assistant:** Why do we multiply by one hundred to get a percentage?

**Tutor:** Because a percentage is a fraction rewritten out of one hundred. The fraction three eighths tells you the part out of eight; you want to know the equivalent part out of one hundred. Multiplying by one hundred answers: if the whole were one hundred, how big would the part be? Three eighths of one hundred is 37,5. So 37,5 percent. You are not changing the amount, just expressing it on a scale where the whole is always one hundred, which makes comparisons easy.

### subtopic_3 — Percentage Increase and Decrease
**Assistant:** Why does a 25% increase followed by a 25% decrease not bring me back to the start?

**Tutor:** Because each percentage is calculated from a different starting amount. The increase is twenty-five percent of 640, which is 160, giving 800. The decrease is twenty-five percent of 800, which is 200 — a bigger amount, because 800 is bigger than 640. Taking away 200 from 800 leaves 600, which is less than where you began. Percentages are always of something, so whenever the something changes, the same percentage means a different number of learners, rand or kilograms.
