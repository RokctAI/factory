### subtopic_1 — Multiplying Common Fractions
**Assistant:** Why can I multiply fractions without a common denominator when I could not add them without one?

**Tutor:** Because addition and multiplication ask different questions. Adding counts pieces, so the pieces must be the same size. Multiplying takes a part of a part: two thirds of three quarters. Cutting a whole into thirds one way and quarters the other way automatically makes twelve equal pieces, which is the product of the denominators, and you take the product of the numerators of them. The piece size is created by the multiplication itself, so there is nothing to match beforehand.

### subtopic_2 — Simplifying Before You Multiply
**Assistant:** Why can I cancel the nine with the twelve when they are in different fractions?

**Tutor:** Because when you multiply, the two fractions become one fraction: nine times five over ten times twelve. In that single fraction, nine is a factor of the numerator and twelve is a factor of the denominator. Dividing a factor of the numerator and a factor of the denominator by the same number is just simplifying the fraction, and it does not matter which original fraction they came from. That is also why you cannot cancel when adding: two fractions joined by a plus sign are not one product.

### subtopic_3 — Multiplying Mixed Numbers
**Assistant:** Why is it wrong to multiply the whole numbers and the fractions separately?

**Tutor:** Because a mixed number is a sum: three and one third means three plus one third. Multiplying two sums needs every part of the first times every part of the second — three times two, three times one quarter, one third times two and one third times one quarter. Multiplying only wholes with wholes and fractions with fractions leaves out the two middle products. You can see it in the garden: a 3 1/3 by 2 1/4 rectangle has strips along the edges that the shortcut ignores. Converting to improper fractions first includes all the pieces automatically.
