### subtopic_1 — Fractions of Whole Numbers
**Assistant:** How do I find the whole when I am only told the size of a fraction of it?

**Tutor:** Work out the size of one part first. If seventy-two learners is one fifth of the school, then one fifth is seventy-two and five fifths — the whole — is five times seventy-two, which is 360. If you are told that three fifths is 216, divide by three to get one fifth, seventy-two, then multiply by five. This is the same thinking as ratio sharing: find one part, then scale up to the number of parts you need. Check by taking the fraction of your answer and seeing that you get back to the given amount.

### subtopic_2 — Grouping and Sharing With Fractions
**Assistant:** How do I tell a grouping problem from a sharing problem?

**Tutor:** Look at what you know and what you want. In a grouping problem you know the size of each group — two and a half litres per table — and you want how many groups. In a sharing problem you know how many groups there are — four learners — and you want the size of each share. Both are divisions, but the answer means something different: a number of tables versus an amount of pizza. Saying the answer in words with its unit, eight tables or three quarters of a pizza, is the best check that you solved the right problem.

### subtopic_3 — Percentages in Context
**Assistant:** Why is it important to know which whole a percentage refers to?

**Tutor:** Because the same percentage gives different amounts for different wholes. Forty percent of the budget is R480, but forty percent of the learners is 144 learners, and forty percent of a ticket price is six rand. In a long problem several wholes appear, and mixing them up is the most common source of wrong answers. Underlining the whole for each percentage, and checking that the parts of one whole add up to one hundred percent, keeps the calculation honest.
