# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies common fractions, mixed numbers and percentages to problems in context: fractions of whole numbers, grouping and sharing with fractions, and percentage problems. The anchor is a school fun day for 360 learners.

## Subtopic: Fractions of Whole Numbers

Many problems ask for a fraction of a quantity. To find a fraction of a whole number, divide by the denominator and multiply by the numerator. Three fifths of 360 learners: 360 divided by five is seventy-two, times three is 216 learners.

The order can be swapped — multiply first, then divide — but dividing first keeps the numbers small.

Problems often chain fractions. At the fun day, three fifths of the 360 learners take part in races. Of those, one quarter enter the long race. How many enter the long race? One quarter of 216 is fifty-four. Or in one step: one quarter of three fifths is three twentieths, and three twentieths of 360 is fifty-four.

Sometimes the remainder matters. If three fifths race, then two fifths do not: two fifths of 360 is 144. Check: 216 plus 144 is 360.

The reverse problem gives the part and asks for the whole. If 72 learners is one fifth of the school, the whole school is five times 72, which is 360. Finding the value of one part first is the key, exactly as in ratio sharing.

The questions for this section are ready: fractions of amounts, chained fractions and finding the whole.

## Subtopic: Grouping and Sharing With Fractions

Grouping asks how many groups of a certain size fit into an amount. Sharing asks how much each person gets when an amount is shared equally. Both use division, and with fractions they are often solved by repeated addition, by multiplication or by converting units.

Each table at the fun day needs two and a half litres of juice. The school has twenty litres. How many tables can be served? Group twenty litres into two-and-a-half-litre portions: two and a half fits into five litres twice, and into twenty litres four times as often, eight times. Eight tables. Check: eight times two and a half is twenty.

Sharing: three pizzas are shared equally among four learners. Each learner gets three quarters of a pizza. Sharing a whole number among a group gives a fraction: the amount over the number of people.

Mixed numbers in context: a learner runs three and three quarter laps in a fun run. How far is that if each lap is 400 metres? Three laps are 1 200 metres; three quarters of a lap is 300 metres; total 1 500 metres. Or as an improper fraction: fifteen quarters of 400 is fifteen times one hundred, 1 500.

The step that most often goes wrong is deciding whether the problem is grouping or sharing. Ask yourself: do I know the size of each group and want the number of groups, or do I know the number of groups and want the size of each?

Pause here for this section's questions: how many groups, how much each, and mixed-number distances.

## Subtopic: Percentages in Context

Percentage problems in context combine the skills from earlier in the term: percentage of an amount, a part as a percentage, and percentage increase or decrease.

The fun day has a budget of R1 200. Forty percent is spent on food, twenty-five percent on prizes, and the rest on decorations. Food: forty percent of 1 200 is R480. Prizes: twenty-five percent is R300. Decorations: the remaining thirty-five percent, which is R420. Check: 480 plus 300 plus 420 is R1 200, and forty plus twenty-five plus thirty-five is one hundred percent.

Tickets cost R15 each, and the school sells 288 tickets to the 360 learners. What percentage of learners bought a ticket? 288 over 360 is four fifths, eighty percent. Ticket income: 288 times R15, which is R4 320.

Next year, the ticket price is increased by twenty percent. Twenty percent of R15 is R3, so the new price is R18.

A good habit for context problems: underline the whole that each percentage refers to. Forty percent of the budget, eighty percent of the learners, twenty percent of the price — each percentage has its own whole.

Here come the questions for this section: budgets in percentages, a part as a percentage and a price increase.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same fun day now, from the committee's side: counting runners, filling tables, sharing pizza and spending the budget. Same answers, one step at a time.

## Subtopic: Planning the Fun Day

You are on the fun day committee. There are 360 learners. Three fifths of them want to run races. How many runners is that?

Break the learners into five equal groups: 360 divided by five is seventy-two in each group. Three of those groups want to run: three times seventy-two, 216 runners. The other two groups, 144 learners, will watch and cheer.

Now the juice. Each table needs two and a half litres, and you have twenty litres. How many tables can you fill? Two tables use five litres. Twenty litres is four lots of five litres, so four times two tables — eight tables.

And the pizzas. Three pizzas for every four helpers. If they share fairly, each helper gets three quarters of a pizza — three pizzas cut into quarters gives twelve quarters, and twelve quarters shared among four is three quarters each.

Notice the questions you asked: how many in a fraction of the group, how many portions fit, how much does each person get. Choosing the right question is half the work.

Your questions for this section are with you now: equal groups, portions that fit, fair shares.

## Subtopic: Spending the Budget

The committee has R1 200 to spend. The plan: forty percent on food, twenty-five percent on prizes, and the rest on decorations.

Start with ten percent of R1 200: R120. Forty percent is four of those: R480 for food. Twenty-five percent is a quarter of R1 200: R300 for prizes. What is left? R1 200 minus R480 minus R300 is R420 for decorations.

Does that make sense? Forty plus twenty-five is sixty-five percent, so decorations get thirty-five percent. Ten percent is R120, so thirty percent is R360 and five percent is R60: R420. It matches.

On the day, 288 of the 360 learners buy a R15 ticket. Is that a good turnout? 288 out of 360 — both divide by seventy-two: four out of five. Four fifths is eighty percent. Most learners came!

Next year the committee wants to raise the ticket price by twenty percent. Ten percent of R15 is R1,50, so twenty percent is R3. New price: R18.

You planned a whole fun day with fractions and percentages. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: ten percent first, then build, and check the parts add up to the whole.
