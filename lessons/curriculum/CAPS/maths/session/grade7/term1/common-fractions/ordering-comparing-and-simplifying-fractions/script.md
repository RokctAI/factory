# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers equivalent fractions, simplifying to lowest terms, and ordering and comparing common fractions, extended to thousandths. The anchor fractions are 3/4, 5/8 and 7/10.

## Subtopic: Equivalent Fractions and Simplest Form

A common fraction has a numerator on top and a denominator below. The denominator says how many equal parts make a whole; the numerator says how many of those parts we have. Three quarters means a whole cut into four equal parts, and three of them taken.

Equivalent fractions name the same amount. You make one by multiplying, or dividing, the numerator and the denominator by the same non-zero number. Three quarters times five over five is fifteen twentieths; times two hundred and fifty over two hundred and fifty, it is 750 thousandths. All three name the same point on the number line. Multiplying by a number over itself is multiplying by one, which is why the value does not change.

To simplify a fraction, divide the numerator and denominator by a common factor, ideally the highest common factor. Simplify 375 over 1 000. Both end in five or zero, so divide by five: 75 over 200. Again by five: 15 over 40. Again by five: 3 over 8. Three and eight share no factor, so three eighths is the simplest form. The HCF of 375 and 1 000 is 125, and dividing by 125 in one step gives the same three eighths.

A fraction is in simplest form, also called lowest terms, when the numerator and denominator have no common factor other than one.

## Subtopic: Ordering and Comparing Fractions

When fractions have the same denominator, compare the numerators: five eighths is more than three eighths. When they have the same numerator, the one with the SMALLER denominator is larger, because the pieces are bigger: one third is more than one quarter.

When neither matches, rewrite the fractions with a common denominator, best of all the lowest common denominator, which is the LCM of the denominators. Compare three quarters, five eighths and seven tenths. The denominators are four, eight and ten; their LCM is forty. Three quarters is thirty fortieths. Five eighths is twenty-five fortieths. Seven tenths is twenty-eight fortieths. Now compare numerators: twenty-five, twenty-eight, thirty. From smallest to largest: five eighths, seven tenths, three quarters. The learner who ate three quarters ate the most.

Another method uses thousandths. Three quarters is 750 thousandths, five eighths is 625 thousandths and seven tenths is 700 thousandths. The order is the same. Thousandths are useful because they connect fractions to decimals, which we meet later this term.

A quick check uses benchmarks. All three fractions are more than one half. Three quarters is a quarter less than one; five eighths is only an eighth more than a half. That already suggests five eighths is the smallest.

## Subtopic: Fractions on the Number Line and Mixed Numbers

Every fraction has a position on the number line. To place five eighths, divide the space from zero to one into eight equal parts and count five. Three quarters sits at six eighths, a little further along. The number line makes comparison visual: further right means larger.

Fractions greater than one can be written as improper fractions, where the numerator is bigger than the denominator, or as mixed numbers. Eleven quarters is two whole ones, which use eight quarters, plus three quarters left over: two and three quarters. To convert a mixed number back, multiply the whole number by the denominator and add the numerator: two times four plus three is eleven, so two and three quarters is eleven quarters.

To compare mixed numbers, compare the whole numbers first. Three and one tenth is larger than two and nine tenths, because three is larger than two, however big the fraction parts are. Only if the whole numbers are equal do you compare the fractions.

Simplifying works for mixed numbers too. Two and six eighths simplifies to two and three quarters, by simplifying only the fraction part.

## Subtopic: Same-Size Pieces

Three friends had the same size lunch box. Ayanda ate three quarters of hers, Ben ate five eighths of his, and Chloe ate seven tenths. Who ate the most?

The problem is that the pieces are different sizes — quarters, eighths and tenths. Comparing them is like comparing a price in rand with a price in dollars. So let us cut every lunch into the same size pieces.

What size works for all three? We need a number that four, eight and ten all go into. Forty works. Cut every lunch into forty tiny pieces.

Ayanda's three quarters: each quarter is ten of the tiny pieces, so three quarters is thirty pieces. Ben's five eighths: each eighth is five tiny pieces, so five eighths is twenty-five pieces. Chloe's seven tenths: each tenth is four tiny pieces, so seven tenths is twenty-eight pieces.

Now it is easy: thirty, twenty-five, twenty-eight. Ayanda ate the most, then Chloe, then Ben.

That is what a common denominator does. It makes all the pieces the same size so you can just count them.

## Subtopic: Making Fractions Simpler

Sometimes a fraction looks scarier than it is. Three hundred and seventy-five thousandths sounds complicated. But many fractions are just simpler fractions wearing a big coat.

To take the coat off, divide the top and the bottom by the same number. Both 375 and 1 000 end in five or zero, so both divide by five: 75 over 200. Still both divide by five: 15 over 40. Again: 3 over 8. Now three and eight have nothing in common, so we stop. Three hundred and seventy-five thousandths is just three eighths.

Why is that allowed? Imagine a chocolate slab with a thousand tiny squares. If you have 375 of them, you could group the squares into bundles of 125. You would have three bundles out of eight. Same chocolate, bigger bundles.

You can go the other way too. Three quarters can be dressed up as six eighths, fifteen twentieths or 750 thousandths, just by multiplying top and bottom by the same number.

And a mixed number like two and six eighths can be simplified in its fraction part: two and three quarters.

You started with a lunch-box puzzle and a scary fraction, and solved both. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: take the coat off, and check that nothing is left in common.
