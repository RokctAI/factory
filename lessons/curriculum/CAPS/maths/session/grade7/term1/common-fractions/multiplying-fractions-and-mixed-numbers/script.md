# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiplying common fractions and mixed numbers, including denominators that are not multiples of each other, converting mixed numbers to improper fractions, and simplifying by cancelling common factors before or after multiplying. The anchor is a garden 3 1/3 m by 2 1/4 m.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Common Fractions

To multiply two common fractions, multiply the numerators together and the denominators together. Two thirds times three quarters: two times three is six, three times four is twelve, so six twelfths, which simplifies to one half.

Why does this work? Two thirds times three quarters means two thirds OF three quarters. Draw a rectangle, shade three quarters of it, then take two thirds of the shaded part. The whole rectangle has been cut into three times four, twelve, small pieces, and the doubly-shaded region covers two times three, six, of them. Six twelfths.

The word of in a fraction problem usually means multiply. Three fifths of R40 is three fifths times forty over one: forty divided by five is eight, times three is R24.

Notice that multiplying by a proper fraction makes a number SMALLER. Two thirds of three quarters is less than three quarters. That surprises learners who expect multiplication always to make things bigger, but taking a part of something must give less than the whole thing.

You do not need a common denominator to multiply. Common denominators are for adding and subtracting. Mixing these rules up is one of the most frequent errors in this topic.

The questions for this section are ready: multiply tops and bottoms, use of as times, and predict the size of the answer.

## Subtopic: Simplifying Before You Multiply

You may cancel a factor that is common to any numerator and any denominator before you multiply. This keeps the numbers small and often removes the need to simplify at the end.

Take nine tenths times five twelfths. Multiplied directly, it is forty-five over 120, which then has to be simplified by fifteen to three eighths. Cancel first instead. Nine on top and twelve below share three: nine becomes three, twelve becomes four. Five on top and ten below share five: five becomes one, ten becomes two. Now multiply: three times one over two times four, three eighths. Same answer, smaller numbers.

Why is this allowed? Because the product is one big fraction, nine times five over ten times twelve, and dividing any numerator factor and any denominator factor by the same number is simplifying that big fraction.

Cancelling is ONLY for multiplication. In addition you cannot cancel across a plus sign, because the fractions are not one product.

After multiplying, always check if the answer can be simplified further. Cancelling first should leave nothing to simplify, but checking is quick.

Pause here for this section's questions: spot common factors across the multiplication and cancel.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Mixed Numbers

To multiply mixed numbers, first convert them to improper fractions. Never multiply the whole numbers and the fractions separately — that misses two cross products.

The garden plot is three and one third metres by two and a quarter metres. Area equals length times width. Three and one third is ten thirds. Two and a quarter is nine quarters. Now multiply: ten thirds times nine quarters. Cancel: ten and four share two, giving five and two; nine and three share three, giving three and one. Five times three over one times two: fifteen halves, which is seven and a half square metres.

Check with an estimate: about three times two is six, and both numbers were rounded down, so a bit more than six square metres is expected. Seven and a half is reasonable.

Now spinach is planted on two thirds of the plot: two thirds times fifteen halves. Cancel the twos and cancel three into fifteen: one times five over one times one — five square metres.

The error to avoid: three times two plus one third times one quarter gives six and one twelfth, which is wrong because the products three times one quarter and one third times two have been left out. Converting to improper fractions includes everything automatically.

Here come the questions for this section: convert, cancel, multiply, convert back, and estimate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same fractions now, as a chocolate slab and a school garden. Same rules, everyday pictures.

## Subtopic: A Part of a Part

Picture a chocolate slab. You have three quarters of it left, and you give two thirds of what you have to your brother. How much of the whole slab did he get?

That is two thirds OF three quarters. In maths, "of" means multiply.

Draw it. Shade three quarters of a rectangle by cutting it into four columns and shading three. Now cut the rectangle across into three rows, and take two of those rows from the shaded part. How many small blocks is the slab now? Four columns times three rows: twelve blocks. How many did your brother get? Three columns times two rows: six blocks. Six twelfths — half the slab.

So the rule is simple: multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms. Two times three is six; three times four is twelve; six twelfths, which is one half.

And notice: he got LESS than the three quarters you had. Taking a part of something always gives you less than the something.

Your questions for this section are with you now: of means times, tops times tops, bottoms times bottoms.

## Subtopic: How Big Is the Garden?

The school garden plot is three and a third metres long and two and a quarter metres wide. How much ground is that?

Area is length times width. But mixed numbers are awkward to multiply. So turn them into plain fractions first. Three and a third: three wholes are nine thirds, plus one third, ten thirds. Two and a quarter: two wholes are eight quarters, plus one, nine quarters.

Now ten thirds times nine quarters. Before multiplying, make the numbers smaller. Ten and four are both even: halve them to five and two. Nine and three both divide by three: three and one. Now it is just five times three over two times one — fifteen halves.

Fifteen halves is seven whole ones and one half left: seven and a half square metres.

Does that make sense? Three times two is six, and our numbers were a bit bigger, so a bit more than six. Seven and a half fits.

Spinach goes on two thirds of the garden. Two thirds of fifteen halves: the twos cancel, and three goes into fifteen five times. Five square metres of spinach.

You worked out the garden and the spinach patch with fractions. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: change to plain fractions, make the numbers smaller, multiply, and check with an estimate.
