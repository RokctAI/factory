### subtopic_1 — Dividing by Powers of Ten
**Assistant:** Why do zeros appear right after the comma when I divide by one hundred?

**Tutor:** Because the digits move two places to the right, and the places they leave behind must still be filled. 3,6 divided by one hundred: the three units become three hundredths, and the six tenths become six thousandths. There are now no units and no tenths, so those places hold zeros: 0,036. The zeros are placeholders, just as the zero in 405 shows there are no tens. Without them the digits would sit in the wrong places and the number would be ten times too big.

### subtopic_2 — Long and Short Division With Decimals
**Assistant:** Why is it allowed to add zeros to keep dividing?

**Tutor:** Because zeros at the end of the decimal part do not change the value. 1,85 means one unit, eight tenths and five hundredths, with no thousandths and no ten-thousandths. Writing 1,8500 says exactly the same thing, but it gives the division more places to work with. When a remainder is left over, the next zero lets you convert that remainder into smaller units — two hundredths become twenty thousandths — and carry on sharing. It is the decimal version of breaking a leftover rand into cents.

### subtopic_3 — Remainders, Rounding and Recurring Decimals
**Assistant:** How many decimal places should I divide to before rounding?

**Tutor:** One more than you need to keep. Rounding looks at the digit just after the last place you keep, so you must have that digit. To give 2 divided by seven to two decimal places you need the third decimal digit, five, which tells you to round 0,28 up to 0,29. If you stopped at two places and wrote 0,28, you would be truncating, not rounding, and the answer would be slightly wrong. Dividing to one extra place is a small effort that guarantees a correct rounding.
