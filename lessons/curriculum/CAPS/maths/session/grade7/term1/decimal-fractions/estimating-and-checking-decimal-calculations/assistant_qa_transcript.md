### subtopic_1 — Rounding to Estimate Decimal Calculations
**Assistant:** Why round to 40 and 25 instead of to the nearest whole numbers, 43 and 24?

**Tutor:** Both are valid; the choice is about speed. Forty times twenty-five is a fact many people know — one thousand — so it can be done instantly. Forty-three times twenty-four needs a little more work and gives 1 032, slightly closer to the exact answer. An estimate only has to be good enough to show whether an answer is sensible, so the friendlier numbers usually win. When you need a closer estimate, round less aggressively.

### subtopic_2 — Predicting the Number of Decimal Places
**Assistant:** If counting decimal places already places the comma, why do I need an estimate too?

**Tutor:** Because counting places only works if the whole-number multiplication was done correctly. If you drop a digit and get 101 601 instead of 1 016 010, counting three places gives 101,601 — the right number of decimals, but ten times too small. The estimate of about one thousand exposes that immediately. The two checks look at different things: the count looks at the end of the number, the estimate at its overall size. Together they catch almost every comma error.

### subtopic_3 — Checking With Inverse Operations and a Calculator
**Assistant:** If the calculator is more accurate than me, why not trust it when we disagree?

**Tutor:** Because a calculator is only as accurate as the keys you press. It cannot tell you that you typed 4,26 instead of 42,6, or 2 385 instead of 23,85. When your written answer and the calculator disagree, either could be wrong — you might have slipped in a column, or you might have mistyped. The estimate is the referee: whichever answer is close to about one thousand is the likely one. Then redo the other carefully. Two independent answers that agree are far more trustworthy than one answer from any source.
