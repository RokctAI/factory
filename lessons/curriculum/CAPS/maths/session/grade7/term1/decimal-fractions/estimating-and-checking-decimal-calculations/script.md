# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers using place value to estimate the number of decimal places in a result, rounding to estimate decimal calculations, and checking results with inverse operations and a calculator. The anchor is 42,6 litres at R23,85 per litre.

## Subtopic: Rounding to Estimate Decimal Calculations

An estimate replaces each number with a nearby friendly number, usually by rounding to the nearest whole number, ten or one significant digit, and then calculates mentally.

Estimate the fuel bill: 42,6 litres times R23,85. Round 42,6 to forty and 23,85 to twenty-five, because forty times twenty-five is easy: one thousand. Alternatively, round to forty-three and twenty-four: forty-three times twenty-four is 1 032. Both say the bill is about one thousand rand.

Estimates for addition and subtraction: R23,85 plus R18,40 plus R7,95 is about twenty-four plus eighteen plus eight, fifty. Rounding each number to the nearest rand makes the estimate quick and close.

Estimates for division: 98,4 divided by 3,9 is about one hundred divided by four, twenty-five. Choose compatible numbers that divide easily.

Knowing the direction of the error helps. If you round both numbers in a product up, the estimate is too big; if both down, too small. Forty is less than 42,6 but twenty-five is more than 23,85, so the errors partly cancel.

An estimate is not a guess. It is a calculation with simpler numbers, and you should be able to say how you got it.

The questions for this section are ready: round sensibly and estimate products, sums and quotients.

## Subtopic: Predicting the Number of Decimal Places

Place value lets you predict the number of decimal places in a result before calculating.

For multiplication, the number of decimal places in the product equals the total number in the factors: 42,6 has one, 23,85 has two, so the product will have three decimal places before any trailing zeros are removed. Calculate: 426 times 2 385 is 1 016 010. Three decimal places: 1 016,010, which is R1 016,01. The estimate of about one thousand confirms the comma's position.

For addition and subtraction, the result has as many decimal places as the number with the most: 23,85 plus 7,9 will have two decimal places.

For division by a whole number, the quotient may need more places than the dividend, as when 1,85 divided by four gave 0,4625. You decide how many places to keep and round.

Predicting places and estimating size are two independent checks on the comma. Places tell you how many digits come after the comma; the estimate tells you roughly how big the number is. If 1 016,010 had come out as 101,601, the places would be right but the size would be wrong — the estimate catches it.

Pause here for this section's questions: predict the decimal places and confirm the size.

## Subtopic: Checking With Inverse Operations and a Calculator

After calculating, check. The strongest checks are independent of the method you used.

Inverse operations: a subtraction is checked by adding, a division by multiplying. If the taxi driver pays R1 100 and gets R83,99 change, check: R1 016,01 plus R83,99 is R1 100,00. If 1 016,01 rand buys 42,6 litres, then dividing 1 016,01 by 42,6 should give back about 23,85.

Rounding checks: is the answer close to the estimate? Is it in the right direction? An answer of R10 160,10 for less than fifty litres of fuel at about twenty-four rand is plainly impossible.

Calculator checks: the curriculum says the calculator is used to check, after you have calculated. Type the calculation carefully; a calculator shows R1 016,01 only if you entered 42,6 and 23,85 correctly. If the calculator disagrees with your written answer, do not simply copy it — find the error, because it may be a typing slip.

A sense check for context: the units and size must make sense. Fuel bills in rand, distances in kilometres, and a share must be smaller than the whole.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same fuel stop now, from the driver's seat: a rough answer before paying and three checks afterwards. Same numbers, everyday thinking.

## Subtopic: About How Much Is the Fuel?

A taxi driver fills up with 42,6 litres of petrol at R23,85 a litre. Before he pays, he wants a rough idea of the bill.

He rounds the litres to forty — easy to work with. He rounds the price to twenty-five rand — also easy. Forty times twenty-five: four times twenty-five is a hundred, so forty times twenty-five is a thousand. About R1 000.

He rounded the litres down a bit and the price up a bit, so the two changes partly cancel out. The real bill should be close to a thousand.

The pump shows R1 016,01. That matches his rough answer — so he trusts it. But if the pump had shown R10 160,10 or R101,60, he would know immediately something was wrong, because those are ten times too big or ten times too small.

You can make rough answers for anything. Three items at R23,85, R18,40 and R7,95? About twenty-four plus eighteen plus eight — about R50.

Your questions for this section are with you now: round to friendly numbers, calculate in your head, and use it to judge an answer.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Check

The taxi driver pays with R1 100. The cashier gives him R83,99 change. Is that right?

Check one: add back. R1 016,01 plus R83,99. One cent plus ninety-nine cents is one rand. R1 016 plus R83 plus that one rand is R1 100. Perfect.

Check two: does it make sense? The bill was a bit more than R1 000, he paid R1 100, so the change should be a bit less than R100. R83,99 is a bit less than R100. Yes.

Check three: the calculator. Type 1 100 minus 1 016,01 and see 83,99. The calculator agrees with you — so now you have two answers that match.

Notice the order: first your own working, then the calculator. If you only use the calculator and you press a wrong key, you will never know. If you work it out yourself first, the calculator becomes your second opinion.

You now have three ways to check any answer: add or multiply back, ask if it makes sense, and confirm with a calculator. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: check, check and check.
