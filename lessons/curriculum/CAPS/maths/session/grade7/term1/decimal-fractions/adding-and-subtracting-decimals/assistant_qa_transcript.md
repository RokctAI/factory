### subtopic_1 — Adding Decimals by Place Value
**Assistant:** Why do I line up the commas and not the last digits?

**Tutor:** Because you can only add digits that have the same place value — tenths with tenths, hundredths with hundredths. The comma marks where the units end and the tenths begin, so lining up the commas automatically lines up every place. If you line up the last digits instead, the five tenths in 24,5 would sit under the nine hundredths in 16,99, and you would be adding tenths to hundredths, which is like adding rand to cents without converting. Filling empty places with zeros makes the columns obvious.

### subtopic_2 — Subtracting Decimals and Giving Change
**Assistant:** Why does the cashier's counting-on method give the same answer as column subtraction?

**Tutor:** Because subtraction asks how far it is from one number to another, and counting on measures that distance in friendly jumps. From R41,49 to R41,50 is one cent; to R42 is fifty cents; to R50 is eight rand. Adding the jumps, R8,51, gives the gap between the two amounts, which is exactly what fifty minus 41,49 means. Counting on avoids borrowing across zeros, which is where most column-subtraction errors happen, so it is also an excellent check.

### subtopic_3 — Mental Strategies and Mixed Calculations
**Assistant:** Why do I have to convert 350 g before adding it to 1,875 kg?

**Tutor:** Because addition only makes sense when you add the same kind of unit, just as you can only add tenths to tenths. 1,875 is measured in kilograms and 350 in grams, and a gram is a thousand times smaller than a kilogram. Adding the numbers directly would treat 350 grams as 350 kilograms. Converting 350 grams to 0,35 kilograms puts both quantities on the same scale, and then the place values line up correctly.
