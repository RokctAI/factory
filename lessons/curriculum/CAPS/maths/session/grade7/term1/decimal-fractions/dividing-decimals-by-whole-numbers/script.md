# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers dividing decimal fractions of at least three decimal places by whole numbers, including dividing by powers of ten, short and long division with the comma kept in place, and continuing the division by adding zeros. The anchors are 1,85 ÷ 4 and 7,875 ÷ 7.

## Subtopic: Dividing by Powers of Ten

Dividing by ten moves every digit one place to the right, so each digit becomes worth one tenth as much. 7,875 divided by ten is 0,7875. Divided by one hundred is 0,07875. Divided by one thousand is 0,007875. Zeros are placed in front as needed so that the digits sit in the correct places.

It looks as if the comma moves left one place for each zero in the divisor. That is a helpful shortcut, but the real reason is that dividing by ten undoes multiplying by ten.

Dividing by multiples of ten can be split into two steps. 8,4 divided by twenty: divide by two, which gives 4,2, then by ten, which gives 0,42. 7,5 divided by three hundred: divide by three, 2,5, then by one hundred, 0,025.

Check by multiplying back: 0,42 times twenty is 8,4. Multiplication and division are inverse operations, so the check always works.

A frequent error is to move the comma the wrong way, giving 78,75 for 7,875 divided by ten. A quick sense check catches it: dividing a number by ten must make it smaller.

The questions for this section are ready: divide by ten, a hundred and a thousand, and by multiples of ten.

## Subtopic: Long and Short Division With Decimals

To divide a decimal by a whole number, divide exactly as with whole numbers, and place the comma in the answer directly above the comma in the number being divided.

The relay team runs 1,85 km shared equally by four runners. 1,85 divided by four. Four into one does not go: write zero, and place the comma above the comma. Four into eighteen tenths goes four times, sixteen, remainder two. Bring down five: twenty-five hundredths. Four goes six times, twenty-four, remainder one. The division has not finished, so add a zero — 1,85 is the same as 1,850 — and bring it down: ten thousandths. Four goes twice, eight, remainder two. Add another zero: twenty. Four goes five times exactly. Answer: 0,4625 km per runner, which is 462,5 metres.

Second anchor: a rope of 7,875 m cut into seven equal pieces. Seven into seven goes once. Comma. Seven into eight goes once, remainder one. Bring down seven: seventeen, seven goes twice, remainder three. Bring down five: thirty-five, seven goes five times exactly. Answer: 1,125 m.

Check both by multiplying: 0,4625 times four is 1,85; 1,125 times seven is 7,875.

Adding zeros after the last decimal digit does not change the value, so you may keep dividing until the remainder is zero or until you have enough decimal places to round.

Pause here for this section's questions: comma above comma, add zeros, check by multiplying.

## Subtopic: Remainders, Rounding and Recurring Decimals

Some divisions never finish. 10 divided by three: three into ten is three, remainder one; add a zero, ten again, three again; and the pattern repeats forever: 3,333 and so on. This is a recurring decimal. For practical answers, round to the required number of places: 3,33 to two decimal places.

To round correctly you need one more digit than you keep. To give 2 divided by seven to two decimal places, divide to three: 0,285, then round to 0,29.

In money, answers are rounded to the nearest cent. Three friends share a R50 taxi fare: 50 divided by three is 16,666 and so on, rounded to R16,67. Notice that three times R16,67 is R50,01 — one cent too much. In real life, one friend pays R16,66. The context decides how to handle the rounding.

The context also decides what a remainder means. Seven metres of rope cut into pieces of two metres gives three pieces with one metre left over, not 3,5 pieces, because a half piece is not a usable two-metre length. But seven metres shared EQUALLY among two people gives 3,5 metres each.

Here come the questions for this section: recurring decimals, rounding to enough places, and deciding what a remainder means.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same relay and rope now, on the school field. Same divisions, with metres instead of kilometres to make the numbers feel friendly.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Relay

A relay team of four runners shares a 1,85 kilometre course equally. How far does each runner go?

Think of 1,85 kilometres as 1 850 metres — the same distance. 1 850 metres shared by four runners. Four into 1 800 is 450, and four into the last 50 is 12,5. So each runner runs 462,5 metres.

Now the same thing with the comma. Long division works exactly as usual; the only rule is: the comma in the answer sits right above the comma in the number you are dividing. 1,85 divided by four: four into one won't go, write a zero and the comma. Four into eighteen goes four, two left. Four into twenty-five goes six, one left. Run out of digits? Add a zero! Four into ten goes two, two left. Another zero: four into twenty goes five. Answer: 0,4625 kilometres — which is 462,5 metres. Same!

Check by multiplying: four runners times 0,4625 is 1,85. Perfect.

Your questions for this section are with you now: comma above comma, add zeros when you need to, and multiply back.

## Subtopic: Cutting the Rope

A rope is 7,875 metres long. You cut it into seven equal pieces. How long is each piece?

First a rough answer: seven metres into seven pieces is one metre each, and we have a bit more than seven metres, so a bit more than one metre.

Now divide. Seven into seven: one. Put the comma. Seven into eight: one, with one left over. That leftover joins the next digit: seventeen. Seven into seventeen: two, with three left. Three joins the five: thirty-five. Seven into thirty-five: five exactly. Each piece is 1,125 metres.

A bit more than one metre — just as we guessed.

Sometimes the sharing never ends. Ten metres into three pieces: 3,333 and the threes go on forever. Then we stop and round: about 3,33 metres each. And sometimes we have to think about what the leftover means. If you need pieces exactly two metres long, seven metres gives three pieces and one metre left over — you cannot use half a piece.

You now know how to divide decimals and what to do when the division keeps going. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: rough answer, divide, and decide what the leftover means.
