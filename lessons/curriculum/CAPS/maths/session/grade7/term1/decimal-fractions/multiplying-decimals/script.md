# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers multiplying decimal fractions of three decimal places by whole numbers, and decimal fractions of two decimal places by decimal fractions of one decimal place, including multiplying by powers of ten and using place value to position the decimal comma. The anchors are 1,375 kg × 6 and R12,45 × 2,5.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Decimals by Whole Numbers and Powers of Ten

Multiplying by ten moves every digit one place to the left, so each digit becomes worth ten times as much. 1,375 times ten is 13,75. Times one hundred moves two places: 137,5. Times one thousand moves three places: 1 375. It looks as if the comma moves right, and that is a useful way to remember it, but what really happens is that the digits move to bigger places.

To multiply a decimal by a whole number, multiply as if there were no comma, then put the comma back so the answer has the same number of decimal places as the decimal you started with. A market box weighs 1,375 kg. Six boxes weigh 1,375 times six. Ignore the comma: 1 375 times six is 8 250. The decimal had three decimal places, so the answer has three: 8,250 kg, which is 8,25 kg.

Why three places? Because 1,375 is 1 375 thousandths. Six times 1 375 thousandths is 8 250 thousandths, and 8 250 thousandths is 8,250.

Repeated addition confirms it for small multipliers: 1,375 plus 1,375 is 2,75, so two boxes weigh 2,75 kg, and six boxes are three times that, 8,25 kg.

Estimate as a check: 1,375 is a bit less than 1,5; six times 1,5 is nine; so a bit less than nine kilograms. 8,25 fits.

The questions for this section are ready: powers of ten, decimals times whole numbers, and estimates.

## Subtopic: Multiplying a Decimal by a Decimal

To multiply two decimals, multiply as whole numbers, then count the total number of decimal places in both numbers and give the answer that many decimal places.

Tomatoes cost R12,45 per kilogram, and the bag weighs 2,5 kg. Cost: 12,45 times 2,5. As whole numbers: 1 245 times 25. 1 245 times 20 is 24 900; 1 245 times 5 is 6 225; total 31 125. Decimal places: 12,45 has two, 2,5 has one, three altogether. So the answer is 31,125. In rand, rounded to the nearest cent: R31,13.

Why add the decimal places? 12,45 is 1 245 hundredths and 2,5 is 25 tenths. Hundredths times tenths gives thousandths, because one hundredth of one tenth is one thousandth. So the product is 31 125 thousandths, three decimal places.

Estimate: twelve rand per kilogram times two and a half kilograms is thirty rand. R31,13 is close.

Multiplying by a decimal less than one makes a number SMALLER. 0,5 times 12,45 is 6,225 — half of it. Multiplying by 0,1 is the same as dividing by ten.

A very common error is to line up the commas as in addition, or to give the answer the same number of decimal places as only one of the numbers. Count the places in BOTH factors.

Pause here for this section's questions: multiply as whole numbers, count places in both, estimate.

## Subtopic: Positioning the Comma With Estimation

Counting decimal places is reliable, but estimation gives an independent way to place the comma, and the two together make errors very unlikely.

Suppose you multiply 3,8 by 4,7 as whole numbers: 38 times 47 is 1 786. Where does the comma go? Count: one place plus one place, two places: 17,86. Estimate: about four times about five is twenty. Of the possible positions — 1 786, 178,6, 17,86 or 1,786 — only 17,86 is close to twenty. Both methods agree.

Estimation is especially useful when zeros appear at the end. 2,5 times 1,4: 25 times 14 is 350; two decimal places gives 3,50, which is 3,5. Someone who drops the zero too early and writes 35 then counts two places gets 0,35 — far from the estimate of about three. Count places on the full product, then remove any trailing zero.

The curriculum says: use place value to estimate the number of decimal places in a result, and use rounding and a calculator to check. A calculator check means you calculated first and the calculator only confirms. If it disagrees, find out why before trusting either.

Here come the questions for this section: place the comma by counting and by estimating, and handle trailing zeros.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same tomatoes now, in boxes and bags at the market. Same calculations, with a rough answer always beside the exact one.

## Subtopic: Six Boxes of Tomatoes

A farmer at the market packs tomatoes into boxes. Each box weighs 1,375 kilograms. How much do six boxes weigh?

Pretend the comma is not there for a moment. 1 375 times six: six times five is thirty, six times seventy is four hundred and twenty, six times three hundred is eighteen hundred, six times a thousand is six thousand — altogether 8 250.

Now bring the comma back. The box weight had three digits after the comma, so the answer gets three digits after the comma too: 8,250 kilograms. That is just 8,25 kilograms.

Does it make sense? A box weighs nearly one and a half kilograms. Six boxes of one and a half kilograms would be nine kilograms. Our boxes are a little lighter, so a little less than nine: 8,25. Yes!

Multiplying by ten, a hundred or a thousand is even easier. Ten boxes: every digit shifts one place bigger, 13,75 kilograms. A hundred boxes: two places, 137,5 kilograms.

## Subtopic: Paying for Tomatoes by Weight

Tomatoes cost R12,45 for each kilogram. Your bag weighs 2,5 kilograms. What do you pay?

First a rough answer: about R12 a kilogram, two and a half kilograms. Two kilograms is R24, and half a kilogram is R6. About R30.

Now exactly. Hide both commas: 1 245 times 25. 1 245 times 100 would be 124 500, and 25 is a quarter of 100, so a quarter of 124 500 — 31 125.

Bring the commas back. 12,45 has two digits after its comma. 2,5 has one. Two plus one is three. So the answer has three digits after the comma: 31,125. In money we round to cents: R31,13.

Compare with the rough answer of about R30. Very close — so the comma is in the right place.

And here is a surprise: if your bag weighed only 0,5 kilograms, you would pay HALF of R12,45 — less than R12,45. Multiplying by a number less than one makes the answer smaller.

You now know how to multiply decimals and put the comma in the right place. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: hide the commas, multiply, count the places, and compare with your rough answer.
