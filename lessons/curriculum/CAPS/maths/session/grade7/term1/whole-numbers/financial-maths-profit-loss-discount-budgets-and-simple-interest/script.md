# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies whole-number calculation to financial contexts: cost price, selling price, profit and loss; discount; budgets and accounts; and loans with simple interest. The anchor is Thabo's spaza shop and a R5 000 loan at 12% simple interest per year.

## Subtopic: Cost Price, Selling Price, Profit and Loss

The cost price is what a business pays for an item. The selling price is what the customer pays. If the selling price is higher than the cost price, the difference is a profit. If it is lower, the difference is a loss.

Profit equals selling price minus cost price. Thabo buys a case of twenty-four cooldrinks for R216 and sells each can for R14. Income from the case: twenty-four times R14 is R336. Profit: R336 minus R216 is R120.

Cost per can: R216 divided by twenty-four is R9. So each can makes R14 minus R9, which is R5 profit, and twenty-four cans make twenty-four times R5, R120 — the same answer by a different route, which is a good check.

Loss works the same way in reverse. If six cans go flat and Thabo can sell only eighteen, his income is eighteen times R14, R252. Still more than R216, so still a profit, but only R36. If he sold only fifteen, income would be R210, and he would make a loss of R6.

Businesses also express profit as a percentage of the cost price. A profit of R120 on a cost of R216 is about fifty-six percent, but at Grade 7 the essential skill is the rand amount, calculated carefully.

Break-even is the point where income exactly equals cost: no profit and no loss. For Thabo, R216 divided by R14 is about 15,4, so he must sell at least sixteen cans to make a profit.

The questions for this section are ready: profit, loss and break-even.

## Subtopic: Discount, Budgets and Accounts

A discount is an amount taken off the marked price. It may be given in rand or as a percentage. Thabo's supplier marks a fridge at R4 800 and offers ten percent discount for cash. Ten percent of R4 800 is R480, so the discount is R480 and the cash price is R4 800 minus R480, which is R4 320. A quick alternative: paying ninety percent of the price, nine tenths of R4 800, also gives R4 320.

A budget is a plan for income and expenses over a period, usually a month. Thabo expects income of R18 500. His planned expenses are stock R11 200, rent R2 500, electricity R900, transport R1 400 and wages R2 000. Total expenses: R18 000. Income minus expenses is R500, so the budget shows a surplus of R500. If expenses had been larger than income, it would show a deficit.

An account is a record of money in and money out, like a bank statement or a store account. Each transaction changes the balance. Thabo's account opens at R2 300. He deposits R1 500, pays R900 for electricity and pays R1 400 for transport. Balance: 2 300 plus 1 500 is 3 800, minus 900 is 2 900, minus 1 400 is R1 500. Work line by line, in order, and the closing balance is always the opening balance plus deposits minus payments.

Budgets look forward; accounts look back. Comparing them shows whether the plan was followed.

Pause here for this section's questions: discounts, surplus or deficit, and running balances.

## Subtopic: Loans and Simple Interest

When you borrow money, you pay back the amount borrowed — the principal — plus interest, which is the charge for using someone else's money. Interest is usually given as a percentage per year, called the interest rate.

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, the same amount every year. The formula: simple interest equals principal times rate times time, divided by one hundred, where the rate is a percentage per year and the time is in years.

Thabo borrows R5 000 at twelve percent simple interest per year for three years. Interest for one year: twelve percent of R5 000, which is R600. For three years: three times R600, R1 800. Using the formula: 5 000 times 12 times 3, divided by 100, is R1 800. The total amount he must repay is principal plus interest: R5 000 plus R1 800, R6 800.

If he repays in equal monthly instalments over three years, that is thirty-six months: R6 800 divided by 36 is about R188,89 a month.

Simple interest also applies to savings. If Thabo invests R5 000 at seven percent simple interest per year for three years, he earns 5 000 times 7 times 3 divided by 100, R1 050, and has R6 050 at the end.

Notice the difference: as a borrower, interest is a cost; as a saver, it is income. The same formula serves both.

Here come the questions for this section: interest for one year, for several years, and the total to repay.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same spaza shop now, told from behind the counter: what Thabo keeps on each can, when a good day turns into a loss, and what a loan really costs him. The numbers are the same; the pictures are everyday ones.

## Subtopic: Making Money at the Spaza Shop

Thabo runs a small spaza shop. He buys a box of twenty-four cooldrinks for R216 and sells each can for R14. Is he making money?

First, what did the box cost him per can? R216 shared over twenty-four cans is R9 a can. He sells each can for R14. So on every can he keeps R14 minus R9 — R5. That R5 is his profit per can.

Sell all twenty-four, and he makes twenty-four times R5, which is R120 profit. Check it another way: twenty-four cans at R14 brings in R336; take away the R216 he paid; R120. Same answer.

But what if only some cans sell? Each can he does not sell is R9 he spent and never got back. If he sells only fifteen cans, he takes in fifteen times R14, R210, which is less than the R216 he paid. Now he has a loss of R6.

So profit means you got back more than you spent; loss means you got back less. And a quick budget works the same way for a whole month: money coming in minus money going out. More in than out is a surplus; more out than in is a deficit.

Your questions for this section are with you now: profit per item, total profit, and when a profit turns into a loss.

## Subtopic: What a Loan Really Costs

Thabo wants a new fridge, so he borrows R5 000. The lender charges twelve percent interest per year, and he will take three years to pay it back. How much will he really pay?

Interest is the price of borrowing. Twelve percent means twelve rand for every hundred rand borrowed, every year. Thabo borrowed fifty hundreds, so each year the interest is fifty times twelve — R600.

Simple interest means it is the same R600 every year. Three years: three times R600, R1 800.

So the fridge money costs him the R5 000 he borrowed plus R1 800 interest — R6 800 altogether. That is the real cost of the loan.

Now flip it around. If Thabo instead SAVED R5 000 in a bank at seven percent simple interest, the bank would pay HIM interest: seven rand for every hundred, fifty hundreds, R350 a year, R1 050 over three years. Saving earns interest; borrowing costs interest.

One more money idea: discount. If the fridge is marked at R4 800 and the shop gives ten percent off for cash, ten percent of R4 800 is R480, so he pays R4 320. Sometimes saving up and paying cash is cheaper than borrowing.

You now understand what a loan really costs and how saving pays you back. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: interest per year, total interest, and the total to repay.
