# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises place value for whole numbers into the millions, ordering and comparing, and the properties of the four operations. The anchor numbers are 1 384 000, 1 241 000 and 1 108 000. Place value decides which number is larger; the properties decide which calculations may be rearranged and which may not.

## Subtopic: Place Value, Ordering and Comparing

Every digit in a whole number has a value that depends on its place. In 1 384 000 the one is worth one million, the three is worth three hundred thousand, the eight is worth eighty thousand and the four is worth four thousand. Written in expanded notation: one million plus three hundred thousand plus eighty thousand plus four thousand. Each place is worth ten times the place to its right, which is why our system is called a base-ten system.

To compare two whole numbers, first count the digits. A number with more digits is larger. If the digit counts are equal, compare digit by digit from the left, and the first place where the digits differ decides it. Compare 1 384 000 and 1 241 000: both have seven digits; the millions digits are both one; in the hundred thousands, three beats two. So 1 384 000 is greater than 1 241 000, written with the symbol greater than.

Now order all three anchor numbers from smallest to largest. Millions are equal. Hundred thousands: one, two and three. So the order is 1 108 000, then 1 241 000, then 1 384 000. Ascending means smallest first; descending means largest first. An examiner who asks for descending order and receives ascending order awards nothing, so read the instruction twice.

A common error is to compare the last digits, or to think that a number with a larger digit somewhere must be larger. 999 999 has many nines, but it has six digits, so it is smaller than 1 000 000. Count the digits first, every time.

Your questions for this section are ready: expanded notation, the greater-than symbol and putting numbers in order.

## Subtopic: Commutative, Associative and Distributive Properties

The properties of operations are the rules that say when you may rearrange a calculation. The commutative property says the order of two numbers does not matter for addition and multiplication: four plus nine equals nine plus four, and six times seven equals seven times six. The associative property says the grouping does not matter for addition and multiplication: in twenty-five plus thirty-seven plus seventy-five you may add twenty-five and seventy-five first to make one hundred, then add thirty-seven to get 137.

Subtraction and division are NOT commutative and NOT associative. Ten minus four is six, but four minus ten is not six. Twenty-four divided by six is four, but six divided by twenty-four is a quarter. And twenty minus eight minus two is ten if you work from the left, but twenty minus six is fourteen if you group the last two first. That is why subtraction and division are always worked from left to right.

The distributive property connects multiplication with addition and subtraction: a number multiplied by a sum equals the sum of the separate products. Seven times one hundred and three equals seven times one hundred plus seven times three, which is seven hundred plus twenty-one: 721. It also works backwards: twelve times forty-five plus twelve times fifty-five equals twelve times one hundred, which is 1 200.

These properties are not just names to memorise. They are why mental shortcuts are legal. When you regroup to make tens or hundreds, you are using commutativity and associativity; when you split a number to multiply, you are using distribution.

The questions for this section are with you now: name the property, and spot the operation where it fails.

## Subtopic: Zero, One and the Order of Operations

Two numbers behave specially. Zero is the additive identity: adding zero changes nothing, so 1 241 000 plus zero is still 1 241 000. One is the multiplicative identity: multiplying by one changes nothing. Zero also has a property of its own for multiplication: any number multiplied by zero gives zero. And division by zero is undefined — there is no number that, multiplied by zero, gives a non-zero answer, so the question has no answer at all.

When a calculation mixes operations, the order of operations decides what to do first. Brackets first. Then multiplication and division, from left to right. Then addition and subtraction, from left to right. Many learners remember this as BODMAS. Calculate eighteen plus six times four: multiplication first, six times four is twenty-four, then eighteen plus twenty-four is 42. Without the rule, someone would add first and get ninety-six, which is wrong.

Now combine everything. Calculate the difference between the largest and the smallest anchor numbers, then divide by one thousand. Brackets show the order: open bracket 1 384 000 minus 1 108 000, close bracket, divided by one thousand. Inside the bracket: 276 000. Divided by one thousand: 276. The bracket forces the subtraction to happen before the division.

The properties and the order of operations work together. Properties tell you which rearrangements are allowed; the order of operations tells you what the calculation means in the first place.

Here come the questions for this section: identities, division by zero and the order of operations.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three populations now, with a race, a few friendly numbers and some shortcuts you are allowed to use. No new rules — just a clearer picture of why the rules work.

## Subtopic: Which Town Is Bigger?

Imagine three towns with big populations written on a sign: 1 384 000, 1 241 000 and 1 108 000. Which town is the biggest? Your eyes want to look at the whole number at once, but there is a simple trick that never fails.

Step one: count the digits. All three have seven digits, so they all live in the millions. No winner yet.

Step two: start on the LEFT and move right, one place at a time, like reading a word. The first digit is one in every number — a tie. The next digit is three, two and one. Stop! The first place where they differ decides everything. Three is the biggest, so 1 384 000 is the biggest town. One is the smallest, so 1 108 000 is the smallest.

Think of it like a race where runners are judged at the first checkpoint where they are not level. Whatever happens after that checkpoint does not matter.

And here is the classic trap. Which is bigger, 999 999 or 1 000 000? The first one looks full of big nines! But count the digits: six against seven. The seven-digit number wins, because even the smallest million is bigger than the biggest six-digit number.

Your questions for this section are with you now: count the digits, start from the left, and stop at the first difference.

## Subtopic: Shortcuts That Are Allowed

Here are three shortcuts that clever calculators use, and why they are allowed.

Shortcut one: swap. Six times seven or seven times six — same answer. Adding or multiplying, you can swap the order. That is the commutative property, but you can just think of it as "swapping is fine for plus and times".

Shortcut two: make friendly numbers. Twenty-five plus thirty-seven plus seventy-five. Do not slog from left to right. Spot that twenty-five and seventy-five make a hundred, add those first, then add thirty-seven: 137. Grouping the friendly numbers first is the associative property.

Shortcut three: split and multiply. Seven times one hundred and three looks hard. Split one hundred and three into one hundred and three. Seven hundreds is seven hundred, seven threes is twenty-one, together 721. That splitting trick is the distributive property, and it is how most people do mental maths without knowing the name.

Now the warning. Swapping and regrouping do NOT work for minus and divide. Ten minus four is six, but four minus ten is not. So with minus and divide, go from left to right and do not swap.

And one last rule for mixed calculations: times and divide happen before plus and minus, unless brackets say otherwise. Eighteen plus six times four is eighteen plus twenty-four, which is 42.

You now have three legal shortcuts and one important warning. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: swap, group, split — and check the operation first.
