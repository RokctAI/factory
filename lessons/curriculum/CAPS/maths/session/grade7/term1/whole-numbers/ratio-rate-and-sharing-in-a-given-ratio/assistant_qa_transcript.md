### subtopic_1 — Ratio: Comparing Quantities of the Same Kind
**Assistant:** Why must the units be the same before I write a ratio?

**Tutor:** Because a ratio says how many times bigger one quantity is than another, and that only makes sense if you are counting the same thing. Forty minutes and two hours compared as 40 : 2 would suggest the first is twenty times longer, when it is actually three times shorter. Once both are in minutes, 40 : 120, the comparison is honest and simplifies to 1 : 3. That is also why a ratio has no units in the answer: the units cancel because they were the same.

### subtopic_2 — Sharing a Whole in a Given Ratio
**Assistant:** Why do we add the parts of the ratio before dividing?

**Tutor:** Because the whole amount is being split into equal-sized parts, and the ratio tells you how many of those parts each person gets. In 4 : 5, Lerato gets four parts and Sipho five, so there are nine equal parts altogether. Dividing R360 by nine tells you how much one of those equal parts is worth. If you divided by four or five instead, you would be pretending the whole only had four or five parts, and the shares would not add up to R360. Adding the parts first is what keeps the sharing fair and complete.

### subtopic_3 — Rate: Comparing Quantities of Different Kinds
**Assistant:** What is the real difference between a ratio and a rate?

**Tutor:** A ratio compares quantities of the same kind in the same unit, so the units cancel and the ratio has no unit — four hours to five hours is simply 4 : 5. A rate compares quantities of different kinds, so the units cannot cancel and must be written — rand per car, kilometres per hour. A good test is to ask whether you could say per. If it makes sense to say so much per one of something else, it is a rate. Both use division, and both can be simplified, but a rate is usually written as a unit rate, per one.
