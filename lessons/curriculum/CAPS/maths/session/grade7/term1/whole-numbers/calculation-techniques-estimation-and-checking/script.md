# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the column methods and long division for whole numbers, and the three habits that make them reliable: rounding to estimate, compensating to calculate mentally, and checking with an inverse operation or a calculator. The anchor calculations are 38 times R247 and 9 386 divided by 13.

## Subtopic: Rounding and Estimating Before You Calculate

An estimate is a quick, rounded answer that tells you roughly what the exact answer should be. You round each number to a convenient place value, calculate with the rounded numbers, and keep the result as a target. To round to the nearest ten, hundred or thousand, look at the digit just to the right of the place you are rounding to: five or more rounds up, four or less rounds down. 247 rounded to the nearest hundred is 200, because the tens digit is four. 247 rounded to the nearest ten is 250, because the units digit is seven.

Estimate 38 times R247. Round 38 to 40 and 247 to 250. Forty times two hundred and fifty is ten thousand. So the cost should be close to R10 000. Because both numbers were rounded up, the true answer will be a little less than ten thousand.

Estimate 9 386 divided by 13. Round 9 386 to 9 100, a number close to it that thirteen divides easily, because thirteen times seven hundred is 9 100. So the answer is a little more than seven hundred. Choosing compatible numbers like this is often better than rounding blindly.

Why estimate at all? Because written methods fail silently. A slipped carry or a misplaced digit produces an answer that looks perfectly normal. An estimate exposes the error instantly: if your exact answer for 38 times 247 is 1 186 or 93 860, the estimate of about ten thousand tells you something has gone wrong.

The questions for this section are ready: rounding to a place value, and estimating products and quotients.

## Subtopic: Column Methods and Long Division

The column methods line digits up by place value so that units sit under units, tens under tens, and so on. In addition, when a column adds to ten or more, you carry the extra ten to the next column. In subtraction, when the top digit is too small, you borrow a ten from the next column, a step also called decomposing or regrouping.

Long multiplication: 247 times 38. Multiply 247 by eight: 1 976. Multiply 247 by thirty — write a zero in the units place first, then multiply by three: 7 410. Add the two rows: 1 976 plus 7 410 is 9 386. So 38 boxes cost R9 386 — a little less than our estimate of ten thousand, exactly as predicted.

Long division: 9 386 divided by 13. Thirteen into nine does not go, so take ninety-three: thirteen goes seven times, seven times thirteen is ninety-one, remainder two. Bring down the eight: twenty-eight. Thirteen goes twice, twenty-six, remainder two. Bring down the six: twenty-six. Thirteen goes twice exactly. The answer is 722 with no remainder. Our estimate said a little more than seven hundred — confirmed.

The pattern of long division is always the same: divide, multiply, subtract, bring down, and repeat. Many learners remember it as Dad, Mom, Sister, Brother. When a division does not come out exactly, the leftover is the remainder, and the context decides what to do with it: round up if every item needs a place, round down if only complete groups count.

Here come the questions for this section: the two rows of long multiplication and the steps of long division.

## Subtopic: Compensating and Checking With Inverse Operations

Compensating is a mental strategy: you change a number to make the calculation easy, then adjust the answer to make up for the change. To add 247 plus 198, add two hundred instead — 447 — then take away the two you added too many: 445. To multiply 247 by 38 mentally you could compensate as well: 247 times 40 is 9 880, but that is two boxes too many, and two boxes cost 494, so subtract: 9 880 minus 494 is 9 386. The same answer as the column method, reached a different way.

Every calculation can be checked with its inverse operation. Addition and subtraction undo each other; multiplication and division undo each other. Check 9 386 divided by 13 equals 722 by multiplying: 722 times 13. Seven hundred and twenty-two times ten is 7 220, times three is 2 166, total 9 386. The division is confirmed.

The curriculum is clear about the calculator: use it only to check the correctness of answers you have already worked out. The calculator is not the method; it is the second opinion. And a calculator can only check what you typed — if you press 274 instead of 247, it will confidently give you the wrong answer. That is why the estimate remains your first line of defence, the inverse operation your second, and the calculator your third.

The questions for this section are with you now: compensate, check by the inverse, and decide when the calculator belongs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same exercise books and the same rand amounts now, seen from the school office: a rough answer first, a fair share second, and a check at the end. No new methods — the same steps in everyday language.

## Subtopic: The Rough Answer That Saves You

Picture the school secretary ordering 38 boxes of exercise books at R247 a box. Before she touches a calculator, she asks: roughly how much will this be?

Forty boxes is close to thirty-eight. Two hundred and fifty rand is close to two hundred and forty-seven. Forty times two hundred and fifty: four times twenty-five is a hundred, so forty times two hundred and fifty is ten thousand. About R10 000.

Now she works it out exactly and gets R9 386. Is that believable? Yes — it is a bit under ten thousand, and she rounded both numbers up, so a bit under is exactly what she expected.

But suppose she had made a slip and got R93 860. Her rough answer shouts at her: that is nearly ten times too big! Or R1 186: far too small. The rough answer did not give her the exact price, but it guarded the exact price.

That is the whole idea of estimating. Rounding to the nearest ten means looking at the units digit: five or more, go up; four or less, stay. Rounding to the nearest hundred means looking at the tens digit. And sometimes you choose friendly numbers instead, like rounding 9 386 to 9 100 because thirteen goes into 9 100 exactly.

Your questions for this section are with you now: round, estimate, and use the estimate to judge an answer.

## Subtopic: Sharing Books the Long Way

Now the school has 9 386 exercise books to share equally among 13 classes. How many does each class get?

Think of long division as sharing in stages, biggest first. Can each of the thirteen classes get a thousand books? No — that would need 13 000. Can each class get hundreds? Look at the first two digits: ninety-three hundreds. Thirteen times seven is ninety-one, so each class gets seven hundred books, and two hundred are left over.

Those two leftover hundreds become twenty tens, plus the eight tens we already had: twenty-eight tens. Thirteen goes into twenty-eight twice, so each class gets two more tens — twenty books — and two tens are left.

Two tens become twenty units, plus the six units: twenty-six. Thirteen goes in twice exactly. Each class gets two more books, and nothing is left over.

Add up what each class received: seven hundred plus twenty plus two — 722 books each.

Now check it the opposite way. If each of 13 classes has 722 books, the total is 722 times 13: 7 220 plus 2 166, which is 9 386. It matches, so the sharing was fair and complete. Only after all that would you pick up a calculator, and only to see it agree with you.

You started with a pile of books and ended with a fair share and a check that proves it. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: share in stages, then multiply back.
