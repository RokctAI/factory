### subtopic_1 — Primes and Prime Factorisation
**Assistant:** Why must every factor on the ladder be prime?

**Tutor:** Because the whole point of prime factorisation is to break a number into pieces that cannot be broken any further. If you write four on the side of the ladder, you are hiding two twos inside it, and when you compare your factorisation with another number's you will miscount the twos. Prime factorisation is unique — every learner who does it correctly gets the same primes — but a factorisation with composite numbers in it is not unique: 84 could be four times twenty-one or six times fourteen. Only the all-prime version can be compared reliably, which is what HCF and LCM depend on.

### subtopic_2 — The HCF by Inspection and by Factorisation
**Assistant:** How do I know the common factor I found is the highest one?

**Tutor:** Divide both numbers by it and look at the answers. For 84 and 120 divided by twelve, you get seven and ten. Seven and ten share no factor except one. If they did share a factor, say two, then twice your answer would also divide both numbers and your answer would not be the highest. When the two quotients have nothing in common, nothing bigger can work. A second quick check: the HCF can never be bigger than the smaller number, so any answer over 84 is impossible.

### subtopic_3 — The LCM and Choosing Between HCF and LCM
**Assistant:** Why does the LCM take the greater number of each prime?

**Tutor:** Because the LCM must be divisible by both numbers, so it must contain everything each number demands. 120 contains three twos, so any multiple of 120 must contain at least three twos. 84 contains a seven, so any multiple of 84 must contain a seven. If you took fewer of any prime, one of the numbers would not divide into your answer. Taking exactly the greater count, and no more, gives the smallest number that satisfies both, which is why it is the lowest common multiple.
