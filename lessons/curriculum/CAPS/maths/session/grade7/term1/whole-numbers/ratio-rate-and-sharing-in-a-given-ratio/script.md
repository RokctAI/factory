# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers ratio as a comparison of quantities of the same kind, simplifying ratios, rate as a comparison of quantities of different kinds, and sharing a given whole in a given ratio. The anchor is R360 shared between Lerato and Sipho, who worked 4 hours and 5 hours.

## Subtopic: Ratio: Comparing Quantities of the Same Kind

A ratio compares two or more quantities of the same kind, measured in the same units. If Lerato works four hours and Sipho works five hours, the ratio of their hours is four to five, written 4 : 5. The order matters: the ratio of Sipho's hours to Lerato's is 5 : 4, which is a different statement.

Ratios have no units, but the quantities must be in the SAME unit before you write the ratio. Forty minutes to two hours is NOT 40 : 2. Convert first: two hours is 120 minutes, so the ratio is 40 : 120.

Ratios are simplified like fractions, by dividing every part by the highest common factor. 40 : 120 — the HCF is forty — simplifies to 1 : 3. 12 : 18 has HCF six and simplifies to 2 : 3. A ratio is in simplest form when its parts are whole numbers with no common factor other than one. Equivalent ratios, like 2 : 3, 4 : 6 and 20 : 30, all describe the same comparison, just as equivalent fractions describe the same part of a whole.

A ratio can also compare more than two quantities. A concrete mix of one part cement, two parts sand and three parts stone is written 1 : 2 : 3, and every part is multiplied or divided by the same number to scale it.

The questions for this section are ready: write ratios in the right order, convert units first, and simplify.

## Subtopic: Sharing a Whole in a Given Ratio

To share a whole amount in a given ratio, find the total number of parts, find the value of one part, then multiply.

Lerato and Sipho earned R360 and agree to share it in the ratio of their hours, 4 : 5. Step one: total parts — four plus five is nine. Step two: value of one part — R360 divided by nine is R40. Step three: multiply — Lerato gets four parts, four times R40 is R160; Sipho gets five parts, five times R40 is R200.

Always check that the shares add up to the whole: R160 plus R200 is R360. And check the ratio: 160 : 200 simplifies, dividing by forty, to 4 : 5. Both checks pass.

The method works for three parts as well. A grandmother leaves R7 200 to three grandchildren in the ratio 2 : 3 : 5. Total parts: ten. One part: R720. Shares: R1 440, R2 160 and R3 600. Check: these add to R7 200.

Another way to see the same calculation uses fractions. Lerato receives four ninths of the total and Sipho five ninths. Four ninths of 360 is 360 divided by nine, times four — the same R160. The fraction of the whole for each person is their part over the total parts.

A common error is to divide by one of the ratio numbers instead of the total, for example dividing R360 by four. That would give Lerato R90 and makes no sense, because then the shares no longer add to R360.

## Subtopic: Rate: Comparing Quantities of Different Kinds

A rate compares two quantities of DIFFERENT kinds, so unlike a ratio it always has units. Speed is a rate: kilometres per hour. Price is a rate: rand per kilogram. Earnings can be a rate: rand per car washed.

A rate is usually given per one unit, called a unit rate. If the car wash earned R360 for washing twelve cars, the rate is R360 divided by twelve, which is R30 per car. If a taxi travels 240 kilometres in three hours, its average speed is 240 divided by three, 80 kilometres per hour.

Unit rates let you compare best buys. A two-kilogram bag of maize meal costs R38 and a five-kilogram bag costs R85. Per kilogram: R38 divided by two is R19; R85 divided by five is R17. The five-kilogram bag is cheaper per kilogram, so it is the better buy, provided you will use it all.

Rates also let you predict. At R30 per car, washing twenty cars earns twenty times thirty, R600. At eighty kilometres per hour, a 400-kilometre trip takes 400 divided by eighty, five hours.

The units tell you what to do. Kilometres per hour means kilometres divided by hours; to find kilometres, multiply the rate by hours; to find hours, divide kilometres by the rate. Write the units at every step and they will keep the calculation honest.

## Subtopic: A Fair Split at the Car Wash

Lerato and Sipho washed cars all Saturday and earned R360. Lerato worked four hours, Sipho worked five. Is splitting it in half fair? Not really — Sipho worked longer. A fairer way is to split it in the ratio of their hours, 4 : 5.

Picture the money cut into equal slices. Lerato gets four slices, Sipho gets five. How many slices altogether? Four plus five, nine slices.

How big is one slice? R360 divided into nine equal slices: R40 each.

Now hand out the slices. Lerato: four slices of R40 is R160. Sipho: five slices of R40 is R200.

Did all the money get handed out? R160 plus R200 is R360. Yes! Is it still 4 : 5? R160 and R200 — divide both by forty and you get four and five. Yes!

So the recipe for sharing in a ratio is: add the parts to get the number of slices, divide to find one slice, multiply for each person, and check that it all adds back.

## Subtopic: Per One: Speeds and Best Buys

A rate tells you how much of one thing you get for ONE of another thing. "Per" just means "for each".

At the car wash, twelve cars earned R360. How much for each car? R360 shared among twelve cars is R30 per car. Now any question is easy: twenty cars? Twenty times R30, R600.

A taxi drives 240 kilometres in three hours. How far in one hour? 240 divided by three, 80 kilometres. So the speed is eighty kilometres per hour.

Best buys work the same way. A small bag of maize meal, two kilograms, costs R38. A big bag, five kilograms, costs R85. The big bag costs more, but you get more. Which is better value? Find the price for ONE kilogram. Small bag: R38 for two kilograms is R19 per kilogram. Big bag: R85 for five kilograms is R17 per kilogram. The big bag gives you each kilogram two rand cheaper.

So whenever you need to compare, ask: what is it for one? Then the comparison is fair.
