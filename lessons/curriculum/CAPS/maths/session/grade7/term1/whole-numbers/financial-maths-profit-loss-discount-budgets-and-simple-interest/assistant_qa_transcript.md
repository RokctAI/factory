### subtopic_1 — Cost Price, Selling Price, Profit and Loss
**Assistant:** Why calculate the profit per item as well as the total profit?

**Tutor:** Because it gives you an independent check and a tool for decisions. Total profit is income minus total cost; profit per item is selling price minus cost per item, multiplied by the number sold. If both routes give the same answer, you can trust it. Per-item profit also shows quickly what happens if sales change: each extra can sold adds R5, each can not sold costs Thabo R9 he already paid. That is how a business owner decides prices and how many items he needs to sell to break even.

### subtopic_2 — Discount, Budgets and Accounts
**Assistant:** Why is paying 90% of the price the same as taking off a 10% discount?

**Tutor:** Because the whole price is one hundred percent. Taking away ten percent leaves ninety percent, so whatever is left after the discount must be ninety percent of the original. Calculating it directly, nine tenths of R4 800, is one step instead of two, and it avoids the common slip of forgetting to subtract the discount. Using both methods is also a good check: R4 800 minus R480 and nine tenths of R4 800 should agree, and here both give R4 320.

### subtopic_3 — Loans and Simple Interest
**Assistant:** Why is it called simple interest?

**Tutor:** Because the interest is worked out only on the original principal, so it is the same amount every year. Thabo pays R600 interest in year one, R600 in year two and R600 in year three, no matter how much he still owes. In later grades you will meet compound interest, where each year's interest is added to the amount and the next year's interest is calculated on the bigger total, so it grows faster. Simple interest is the straightforward version, which is why the formula is just principal times rate times time divided by one hundred.
