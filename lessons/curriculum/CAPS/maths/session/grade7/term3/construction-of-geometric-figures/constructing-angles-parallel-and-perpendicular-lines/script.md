# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers constructing angles to one degree of accuracy, and constructing parallel and perpendicular lines, with a compass, ruler and protractor. The anchor is a garden plan with a 65° path, a perpendicular bench line and a parallel fence.

## Subtopic: Constructing Angles With a Protractor

To construct an angle of a given size, for example 65 degrees, follow four steps.

One: draw a straight line segment with a ruler; this is one arm. Mark a point on it to be the vertex. Two: place the protractor's centre on the vertex with the baseline exactly along the arm. Three: find sixty-five on the scale that starts at zero on your arm, and make a small dot at that mark. Four: remove the protractor and use the ruler to join the vertex to the dot. Label the angle sixty-five degrees.

Check by classifying: sixty-five is acute, so the angle you drew must look smaller than a right angle. If it looks obtuse, you used the wrong scale and drew 115 degrees.

To construct a reflex angle, such as 250 degrees, construct its partner, 360 minus 250, which is 110 degrees, and mark the outside angle as the reflex angle. Or extend the arm and add seventy degrees beyond the straight line, since 180 plus seventy is 250.

Accuracy matters: the curriculum asks for one degree of accuracy. Use a sharp pencil, small dots and a careful line through the exact centre.

The questions for this section are ready: construct and check angles, including reflex angles.

## Subtopic: Constructing Perpendicular Lines

Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle, ninety degrees. The symbol for perpendicular is an upside-down T.

With a protractor: at the point on the line where you want the perpendicular, place the centre, line up the baseline, mark ninety and join. This is quick and accurate.

With a compass and ruler, to construct the perpendicular bisector of a line segment AB — the line that cuts AB in half at right angles: open the compass to more than half of AB. With the point on A, draw an arc above and below AB. Keep the same opening, put the point on B, and draw arcs crossing the first two. Join the two crossing points with a ruler. This line is perpendicular to AB and passes through its midpoint.

To construct a perpendicular from a point P on a line: with the compass point on P, draw two arcs cutting the line at equal distances on either side. From each of those points, with a wider opening, draw arcs above the line that cross. Join P to the crossing point.

Check any perpendicular with a protractor or the corner of a set square.

Pause here for this section's questions: perpendicular with a protractor and with compass and ruler.

## Subtopic: Constructing Parallel Lines

Parallel lines are always the same distance apart and never meet, however far they are extended. The symbol is two short slanting lines, and on diagrams matching arrowheads show which lines are parallel.

Method one, using perpendiculars: construct a perpendicular to the first line at one point, and another perpendicular at a second point. Measure the same distance, say four centimetres, along each perpendicular. Join the two marks. The new line is parallel to the first, because it is the same distance from it at both points.

Method two, using equal angles: draw a line crossing the first line, called a transversal. Measure the angle it makes with the first line, for example sixty-five degrees. Further along the transversal, construct the same angle, sixty-five degrees, in the same position. The new arm is parallel to the first line.

Method three, sliding a set square along a ruler: hold the ruler still and slide a set square along it, drawing along the same edge at two positions.

Check by measuring the distance between the lines at two different places: if both distances are equal, the lines are parallel.

Here come the questions for this section: construct parallel lines and check them.

## Subtopic: Planning the Garden Path

The school wants a new garden. The plan says: the path must leave the fence at an angle of sixty-five degrees. Let us draw it.

First, draw the fence as a straight line with a ruler. Choose the point where the path starts and put a dot.

Put the middle of the protractor exactly on the dot. Line up the flat edge of the protractor with the fence. Find sixty-five on the row that starts at zero along the fence. Make a tiny dot there.

Take the protractor away and join your two dots with a ruler. That is your path at sixty-five degrees!

Now look: sixty-five degrees is less than a square corner, so your path should lean over, not stand up straight or lean back. If it looks wider than a square corner, you read the wrong row and drew 115 degrees. Rub it out and try again.

Your questions for this section are with you now: dot, line up, mark, join, and check with your eyes.

## Subtopic: Square Corners and Railway Tracks

Now a bench must stand at a square corner — perpendicular — to the path. And a second fence must run parallel to the first fence, like railway tracks.

Perpendicular is easy with a protractor: put it on the path where the bench starts and mark ninety. Join. Or use a compass: from the two ends of a piece of path, draw arcs that cross above and below, then join the crossings. That line cuts the path in half at a perfect square corner.

Parallel lines are like railway tracks: they never meet, and they stay the same distance apart all the way. To draw one, make two square corners on the first fence, a little apart. Measure four centimetres up each one and make a dot. Join the dots. Your new fence is parallel — the same four centimetres away at both places.

To check parallel lines, measure the gap in two different places. If the gaps match, the tracks will never crash.

You can now construct angles, square corners and railway tracks. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: mark, join and check.
