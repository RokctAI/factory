# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers using a protractor to measure angles accurately and classifying angles as acute, right, obtuse, straight and reflex. The anchor angles are the clock-hand angles 60°, 90°, 150°, 180° and the reflex angle 300°.

## Subtopic: Angles and Their Types

An angle is formed where two rays meet at a common endpoint called the vertex. The size of an angle measures the amount of turn from one arm to the other, in degrees. A full turn is 360 degrees.

Angles are classified by size. An acute angle is more than zero and less than 90 degrees. A right angle is exactly 90 degrees, often marked with a small square at the vertex. An obtuse angle is more than 90 and less than 180 degrees. A straight angle is exactly 180 degrees: its arms form a straight line. A reflex angle is more than 180 and less than 360 degrees. A revolution is exactly 360 degrees.

The clock helps. Each hour mark is thirty degrees apart, because 360 divided by twelve is thirty. At two o'clock the hands are two hours apart: sixty degrees, acute. At three o'clock: ninety degrees, a right angle. At five o'clock: 150 degrees, obtuse. At six o'clock: 180 degrees, straight. And the outside angle at two o'clock, going the long way round, is 360 minus sixty, 300 degrees, reflex.

Classifying first, before measuring, is a strong habit: it tells you roughly what reading to expect.

The questions for this section are ready: name the type of angle from its size or from a clock.

## Subtopic: Measuring With a Protractor

A protractor measures angles in degrees. It has a centre mark, a baseline and two scales, an inner and an outer, running in opposite directions from zero to 180.

To measure an angle: one, place the centre mark exactly on the vertex. Two, line up the baseline with one arm of the angle, so that arm passes through zero on one of the scales. Three, follow THAT scale — the one that starts at zero on your arm — round to where the other arm crosses it. Read the number.

The most common error is reading the wrong scale. If an angle is clearly acute but you read 120 degrees, you have used the scale that starts at 180 on your arm. The cure is to estimate first: classify the angle, and the reading must agree. Sixty and 120 are on the same tick mark on opposite scales; only one of them matches an acute angle.

If the arms are too short to reach the scale, extend them with a ruler first, which does not change the angle.

Measure to the nearest degree. Small errors come from not placing the centre exactly on the vertex, so take care with that step.

Pause here for this section's questions: centre on the vertex, zero on an arm, read the matching scale.

## Subtopic: Measuring Reflex Angles

A protractor only goes up to 180 degrees, so reflex angles need an extra step.

Method one: measure the non-reflex angle on the inside and subtract it from 360. If the inside angle is sixty degrees, the reflex angle is 360 minus sixty, 300 degrees. This works because the two angles together make a full turn.

Method two: extend one arm to make a straight line. The reflex angle is then 180 degrees plus the angle between the extended line and the other arm. If that extra angle is 120 degrees, the reflex angle is 180 plus 120, 300 degrees.

Both methods should agree. Using one as a check on the other is good practice.

When an angle is marked on a diagram with a curved arc, check whether the arc is on the small side or the large side: a big arc on the outside indicates the reflex angle, not the smaller angle.

Every angle less than 360 degrees has a partner that makes up a full turn: acute angles partner reflex angles above 270; obtuse angles partner reflex angles between 180 and 270.

Here come the questions for this section: measure reflex angles two ways and read diagram markings carefully.

## Subtopic: Angles on a Clock

A clock face is a perfect angle teacher. The hands go all the way round in a full turn, which is 360 degrees. There are twelve numbers, so each gap between numbers is 360 divided by twelve, thirty degrees.

At two o'clock, the hands are two gaps apart: sixty degrees. That is smaller than a square corner, so it is an acute angle.

At three o'clock, three gaps: ninety degrees. That is exactly a square corner — a right angle.

At five o'clock, five gaps: 150 degrees. Bigger than a corner but less than a straight line — obtuse.

At six o'clock, the hands point in opposite directions and make a straight line: 180 degrees, a straight angle.

And if you go the LONG way round at two o'clock, from the minute hand all the way round to the hour hand, that is 360 minus sixty, 300 degrees. More than a straight line — that is a reflex angle.

Your questions for this section are with you now: count the gaps, times thirty, and name the angle.

## Subtopic: Measuring Like a Pro

A protractor looks like half a circle with two rows of numbers. Here is how to use it without mistakes.

First, put the little centre hole or mark right on the corner of the angle — the vertex.

Second, turn the protractor so its flat bottom line lies along one arm of the angle. That arm should now point at a zero.

Third, find which row of numbers starts at zero on that arm. Follow that row round until you reach the other arm. Read the number.

Before you read it, ask: does my angle look smaller than a square corner, or bigger? If it is small, your number must be less than ninety. Many people read sixty when it should be 120, or 120 when it should be sixty, because the two rows share the same marks. Your eyes are the check.

For a reflex angle — bigger than a straight line — measure the small angle inside instead, then take it away from 360.

You can now measure and name any angle. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: centre, line up, read the right row, and check with your eyes.
