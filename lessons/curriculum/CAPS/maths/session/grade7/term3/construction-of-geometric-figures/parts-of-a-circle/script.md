# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing and naming the parts of a circle: centre, radius, diameter, circumference, chord, arc, sector and segment, together with constructing circles with a compass. The anchor is a bicycle wheel of radius 30 cm.

## Subtopic: Centre, Radius, Diameter and Circumference

A circle is the set of all points in a plane that are the same distance from a fixed point. That fixed point is the centre.

The radius is a line segment from the centre to any point on the circle; its length is the fixed distance. All radii of a circle are equal. On a bicycle wheel, each spoke from the hub to the rim is like a radius. If the radius is thirty centimetres, every spoke is thirty centimetres long.

The diameter is a line segment through the centre with both endpoints on the circle. It is made of two radii in a straight line, so the diameter is twice the radius: sixty centimetres for the wheel. The diameter is the longest chord of a circle.

The circumference is the boundary of the circle — the curved line itself — and also its length, the distance all the way round. On the wheel, the circumference is the rim, the part that touches the road.

To construct a circle of radius thirty millimetres, open the compass so the distance between its point and pencil is thirty millimetres on a ruler, place the point on the centre, and turn the compass once without changing the opening.

The questions for this section are ready: name these parts and connect radius and diameter.

## Subtopic: Chords and Arcs

A chord is a straight line segment whose two endpoints both lie on the circle. A diameter is a special chord that passes through the centre. Every other chord is shorter than the diameter.

An arc is part of the circumference between two points on the circle. Any two points divide the circle into two arcs: the shorter one is the minor arc and the longer one is the major arc. If the two points are the ends of a diameter, both arcs are semicircles, half of the circle each.

On the bicycle, imagine a straight bar joining two points on the rim without passing through the hub: that is a chord. The curved piece of rim between those two points is an arc.

Chords and arcs come in pairs: every chord cuts off an arc on each side.

Pause here for this section's questions: tell chords from diameters and arcs from chords.

## Subtopic: Sectors and Segments

Parts of a circle can also be regions — areas inside the circle.

A sector is a region bounded by two radii and the arc between them. It looks like a slice of pizza or a slice of a pie chart. On the bicycle wheel, the region between two neighbouring spokes and the rim is a sector.

A segment is a region bounded by a chord and the arc it cuts off. It looks like the shape of an orange slice cut off with a straight knife, or the region between a chord and the rim.

Do not confuse them: a sector always has the centre as a corner, because it is made of two radii. A segment does not include the centre unless the chord is a diameter, in which case the segment is a semicircle.

A semicircle is both a sector — with two radii in a straight line — and a segment — with a diameter as its chord.

These parts are needed later for pie charts in data handling, and in Grade 8 and beyond for circumference and area calculations.

Here come the questions for this section: identify sectors and segments and explain how they differ.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same circle now, as a bicycle wheel and a pizza. Same parts, things you can hold.

## Subtopic: The Bicycle Wheel

Look at a bicycle wheel. It is full of circle words.

In the middle is the hub. In maths, that is the centre of the circle.

Spokes go from the hub out to the rim. Each spoke is a radius. On our wheel each spoke is thirty centimetres, and every spoke is the same length — that is what makes it a circle.

Now imagine a long stick going from the rim, straight through the hub, to the rim on the other side. That is a diameter. It is two spokes in a straight line: thirty plus thirty, sixty centimetres.

The rim itself, the round edge that touches the road, is the circumference.

And how do you draw a circle like this? Use a compass. Open it to the length of the radius, put the sharp point on the centre, and swing the pencil all the way round without changing the opening.

Your questions for this section are with you now: hub, spoke, two spokes, rim.

## Subtopic: Slices, Chords and Curves

Let us cut up a round pizza to meet the rest of the circle family.

Cut from the centre out to the edge, twice. You get a pizza slice. In maths, a region with two radii and a curved edge is a sector.

The curved crust edge of that slice is an arc — just a piece of the circumference.

Now take a fresh pizza and make one straight cut that does NOT go through the middle, from one edge to another edge. That straight cut is a chord. The small piece you cut off — a straight edge and a curved edge — is a segment.

If your straight cut goes right through the middle, it is a diameter, and you have cut the pizza into two halves called semicircles.

So: sectors are slices from the middle; segments are pieces cut off by one straight cut. Arcs are curved edges; chords are straight cuts.

You can now name every part of a circle. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: slice, cut, curve and line.
