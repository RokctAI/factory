# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising, describing and performing translations, reflections and rotations with geometric figures on squared paper, and understanding that these transformations keep shape and size unchanged. The anchor is a flag shape on a grid.

## Subtopic: Translations: Sliding a Figure

A transformation moves a figure to a new position. The original figure is called the object and the moved figure is the image. Translations, reflections and rotations do not change the shape or size of the figure, so the image is always congruent to the object.

A translation slides every point of a figure the same distance in the same direction. Nothing turns or flips.

On squared paper, describe a translation by the number of squares moved right or left, and up or down. Translate the flag five squares right and two squares up: move each vertex five right and two up, then join the new vertices in the same order.

If the flag's pole runs from the square at three across, one up to three across, five up, then after the translation it runs from eight across, three up to eight across, seven up.

Check a translation: every vertex must have moved by the same amount, and the image must face the same way as the object. Lines joining each vertex to its image are all parallel and equal in length.

A common error is to move the shape so that its image starts five squares from where the object ENDS, instead of moving each point five squares.

The questions for this section are ready: describe and perform translations on a grid.

## Subtopic: Reflections: Flipping in a Mirror Line

A reflection flips a figure over a line called the mirror line or line of reflection. Each point of the image is the same distance from the mirror line as the matching point of the object, but on the opposite side, and the line joining them is perpendicular to the mirror line.

On squared paper, reflect a vertex by counting the squares from the vertex straight across to the mirror line, then counting the same number on the other side. If the flag's tip is two squares to the left of a vertical mirror line, its image is two squares to the right.

Points ON the mirror line stay where they are.

The image is congruent to the object, but it is reversed, like your reflection in a mirror: a flag pointing right becomes a flag pointing left in a vertical mirror line. This reversal is what distinguishes a reflection from a translation.

For a horizontal mirror line, count up and down instead: a point three squares above the line goes to three squares below.

Check a reflection by folding the paper along the mirror line: the object and image should match exactly.

Pause here for this section's questions: reflect points and figures in vertical and horizontal mirror lines.

## Subtopic: Rotations: Turning About a Point

A rotation turns a figure about a fixed point called the centre of rotation, through a given angle, in a given direction: clockwise, the way clock hands move, or anticlockwise.

Each point of the image is the same distance from the centre as the matching point of the object. The centre itself does not move.

On squared paper, the common rotations are quarter turns of ninety degrees, half turns of 180 degrees and three-quarter turns of 270 degrees. Rotate the flag ninety degrees clockwise about the bottom of its pole: the pole, which pointed up, now points right; the flag, which pointed right from the top of the pole, now points down from the end.

A useful method: trace the figure and the centre on thin paper, put a pencil point on the centre, and turn the paper a quarter turn. Then copy the image.

A half turn of 180 degrees gives the same result clockwise or anticlockwise. A ninety-degree clockwise turn is the same as a 270-degree anticlockwise turn.

Like translations and reflections, rotations keep shape and size: the image is congruent to the object.

Here come the questions for this section: describe rotations by centre, angle and direction, and perform quarter and half turns.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same flag now, slid, flipped in a mirror and turned around a pin. Same transformations, hands-on.

## Subtopic: Slide and Flip

Draw a little flag on squared paper: a pole three squares tall with a triangle flag at the top pointing right.

A slide — a translation — moves the whole flag without turning it. "Five right, two up" means every corner moves five squares right and two squares up. Count it for each corner, put dots, and join them. The new flag looks exactly the same and still points right. It has only changed place.

A flip — a reflection — is like a mirror. Draw a mirror line down the page. If the tip of the flag is two squares from the mirror, its reflection is two squares from the mirror on the other side. Do this for every corner. Now the flag points LEFT — mirrors reverse things, just like when you lift your right hand in a mirror and the reflection lifts its left.

Check a flip by folding the paper on the mirror line. The two flags should land exactly on top of each other.

Your questions for this section are with you now: slide every corner the same way, or flip every corner the same distance.

## Subtopic: Turning the Flag

The third move is a turn — a rotation. Push a pin through the bottom of the flagpole into the paper, and turn the page around the pin.

A quarter turn clockwise — the way clock hands go — turns the pole from pointing up to pointing right. The flag, which was at the top of the pole, is now at the end, pointing down.

A half turn turns the pole upside down: it now points straight down, and the flag points left.

To draw a turn neatly, trace your flag and the pin point on see-through paper. Hold your pencil on the pin point, turn the tracing a quarter turn, and copy where the flag lands.

For every move — slide, flip or turn — the flag stays the same size and the same shape. Only its position, or which way it faces, changes. That is why the new flag is always congruent to the old one.

You can now slide, flip and turn shapes on a grid. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: which move, which direction, and how far?
