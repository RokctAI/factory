### subtopic_1 — What a Line of Symmetry Is
**Assistant:** Why is the diagonal of a rectangle not a line of symmetry?

**Tutor:** Because folding along it does not make the halves match. Picture an A4 sheet folded corner to corner: the long side lands on the short side, and the corners stick out past each other. For a line to be a line of symmetry, every point on one side must land exactly on a point of the figure on the other side. The diagonal does divide the rectangle into two congruent triangles, which is why it looks tempting, but the triangles are rotations of each other, not mirror images across the diagonal. Only in a square, where the sides are equal, does the diagonal fold work.

### subtopic_2 — Symmetry in Triangles, Quadrilaterals and Regular Polygons
**Assistant:** Why does a regular polygon have exactly as many lines of symmetry as sides?

**Tutor:** Because all its sides and angles are equal, so it looks the same from every vertex. For each vertex there is a line of symmetry through it and the centre. In a polygon with an odd number of sides, like a pentagon, each of those lines also passes through the midpoint of the opposite side, giving five lines. In a polygon with an even number of sides, like an octagon, half the lines join opposite vertices and half join midpoints of opposite sides: four plus four, eight. Either way the count equals the number of sides.

### subtopic_3 — Completing Symmetrical Figures
**Assistant:** Why must I count straight across to the mirror line, not diagonally?

**Tutor:** Because a reflection moves each point along a line perpendicular to the mirror line. The image is directly opposite the object, the same distance away, on a line that meets the mirror at a right angle. Counting diagonally would move the point sideways along the mirror as well, which places the image in the wrong spot and makes the halves fail to match when folded. On squared paper, straight across means along a row for a vertical mirror and along a column for a horizontal mirror.
