# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers identifying and drawing lines of symmetry in geometric figures, including triangles, quadrilaterals, regular polygons and circles. The anchors are an equilateral yield sign, a regular octagon stop sign and a rectangle.

## Subtopic: What a Line of Symmetry Is

A figure has line symmetry if a line can be drawn through it so that one half is the exact mirror image of the other. That line is a line of symmetry, also called an axis of symmetry or mirror line.

The test: if you fold the figure along the line, the two halves match exactly, with no overlap and no gaps. Equivalently, reflecting the figure in the line produces the same figure.

A rectangle has two lines of symmetry: one vertical, through the midpoints of the top and bottom sides, and one horizontal, through the midpoints of the left and right sides.

A rectangle does NOT have symmetry along its diagonals. Folding along a diagonal makes the corners miss each other, unless the rectangle is a square. This is the most common error in this topic.

A figure may have no lines of symmetry, one, or several. A scalene triangle has none. An isosceles triangle has exactly one, from the top vertex to the midpoint of the base.

To draw a line of symmetry, use a ruler, extend the line slightly beyond the figure, and draw it as a dashed or clearly marked line.

The questions for this section are ready: test lines by folding and count the lines of symmetry.

## Subtopic: Symmetry in Triangles, Quadrilaterals and Regular Polygons

The number of lines of symmetry depends on the figure's equal sides and angles.

Triangles: equilateral, three lines, each from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. Isosceles, one line. Scalene, none.

Quadrilaterals: square, four lines — two through midpoints of opposite sides and two along the diagonals. Rectangle, two. Rhombus, two, along its diagonals. Kite, one, along the diagonal between the equal-side vertices. Isosceles trapezium, one, through the midpoints of the parallel sides. Parallelogram that is not a rectangle or rhombus, none. General trapezium, none.

Regular polygons, with all sides and all angles equal, have as many lines of symmetry as they have sides. A regular pentagon has five; a regular hexagon six; a regular octagon, like a stop sign's outline, eight. The yield sign, an equilateral triangle, has three.

A circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry: every diameter is one.

Pause here for this section's questions: count lines of symmetry for triangles, quadrilaterals and regular polygons.

## Subtopic: Completing Symmetrical Figures

Lines of symmetry let you complete a figure when only half is drawn.

On squared paper, half a shape is drawn on one side of a vertical mirror line. For each vertex, count the squares from the vertex straight across to the mirror line, then count the same number on the other side and mark the image vertex. Join the new vertices in order. The result is symmetrical about the line.

With a horizontal mirror line, count squares up and down instead.

With two lines of symmetry, such as a vertical and a horizontal line, reflect the quarter-figure in one line, then reflect the result in the other, completing all four quarters.

Designs in nature and culture use symmetry: butterfly wings, many leaves, Ndebele wall paintings and beadwork patterns often show line symmetry. Recognising the lines helps you both describe and create such designs.

Common errors: counting diagonally instead of straight across to the mirror line, and forgetting to reflect a vertex that lies away from the main body of the shape.

Here come the questions for this section: complete figures using one or two mirror lines.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same shapes now, cut from paper and folded: road signs, a sheet of paper and a butterfly. Same lines of symmetry, found by folding.

## Subtopic: Folding Road Signs

Imagine cutting road signs out of paper and folding them.

A yield sign is a triangle with all three sides equal. Fold it from any corner down to the middle of the opposite side: the halves match! There are three ways to do this, one from each corner. Three lines of symmetry.

A stop sign is a shape with eight equal sides — an octagon. You can fold it in half eight different ways: four folds go corner to corner, and four go from the middle of one side to the middle of the opposite side. Eight lines of symmetry.

A rectangle, like a sheet of paper, folds in half top to bottom and side to side. Two lines of symmetry. But try folding it corner to corner — the corners stick out! A diagonal is NOT a line of symmetry for a rectangle. Only a square can fold along its diagonals.

A circle can fold in half along any line through its centre. It has more lines of symmetry than you could ever draw.

Your questions for this section are with you now: fold it in your mind, and check if the halves match.

## Subtopic: Finishing the Butterfly

On squared paper, half a butterfly is drawn on the left of a straight dotted line down the middle. Your job: draw the other half.

Take one corner of the wing. Count how many squares it is from the dotted line — say three squares. Now count three squares on the OTHER side of the line, straight across, and put a dot. Do the same for every corner of the wing. Then join the dots in the same order. The butterfly is complete — and if you fold along the dotted line, the wings match perfectly.

Always count straight across, along the row, not on a slant.

Some patterns have two lines, one up and one across, like many Ndebele wall designs. Draw the first quarter, flip it across the up-and-down line, then flip both halves across the side-to-side line. Four matching quarters!

And watch out for shapes that look balanced but are not symmetrical. A parallelogram, pushed over sideways, has no fold that works, even though it looks neat.

You can now find, draw and use lines of symmetry. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: count across, mark, join, and fold to check.
