# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing enlargements and reductions of figures on squared paper and comparing them with the original in terms of shape and size, using a scale factor. The anchor is a house shape 4 squares wide and 6 squares tall.

## Subtopic: Scale Factors

An enlargement makes a figure bigger; a reduction makes it smaller. Both keep the shape the same: every length is multiplied by the same number, called the scale factor, and every angle stays the same. The image is therefore similar to the original, but not congruent unless the scale factor is one.

A scale factor greater than one gives an enlargement: a factor of two doubles every length. A scale factor between zero and one gives a reduction: a factor of one half halves every length.

The house is four squares wide and six squares tall, with a roof rising two squares above the walls. Enlarged by a scale factor of two: eight squares wide, twelve squares tall, roof rising four squares. Reduced by a scale factor of one half: two squares wide, three squares tall, roof rising one square.

To find the scale factor from an object and its image, divide an image length by the matching object length. If a side of three squares becomes a side of nine squares, the scale factor is nine divided by three, three.

The scale factor must be the same for EVERY length. If one length doubles and another triples, it is not an enlargement — the shape is distorted.

The questions for this section are ready: apply scale factors and find them from drawings.

## Subtopic: Drawing Enlargements and Reductions on Squared Paper

Squared paper makes enlargements straightforward: multiply the number of squares for every horizontal and vertical length.

To enlarge the house by a factor of two: the base of four squares becomes eight; each wall of four squares becomes eight; the roof, which rises two squares above the walls to a point in the middle, now rises four squares to a point in the middle of the new base. Draw the walls and base first, then find the roof peak by counting from the middle.

Sloping lines need care. Do not measure along a slope; instead, count its horizontal and vertical steps. A roof edge that goes two squares across and two squares up becomes four across and four up.

To reduce by a factor of one half, halve every count. Odd counts give half squares: a length of five squares becomes two and a half squares.

A useful check: the image should look exactly like the original, just bigger or smaller. If it looks stretched or squashed, a length has been scaled wrongly.

Pause here for this section's questions: enlarge and reduce shapes by counting squares, including sloping sides.

## Subtopic: Comparing Perimeter and Area

How do the perimeter and the area of an image compare with the original?

Perimeter is a length, so it is multiplied by the scale factor. A rectangle four by six squares has a perimeter of twenty. Enlarged by a factor of two, it is eight by twelve with a perimeter of forty — doubled.

Area behaves differently. The original rectangle covers four times six, twenty-four squares. The enlargement covers eight times twelve, ninety-six squares. That is four times as many, not twice as many. Both the length AND the width doubled, so the area is multiplied by two times two, four.

For a reduction by a factor of one half: the two by three rectangle has perimeter ten, half of twenty, and area six, a quarter of twenty-four.

You can see the area effect by drawing the enlarged rectangle and fitting copies of the original inside it: exactly four copies fit.

This is a key comparison: in an enlargement, lengths and perimeters scale by the factor, but areas scale by the factor times itself.

Here come the questions for this section: compare perimeters and areas of objects and images.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same house now, drawn bigger and smaller, and painted. Same scale factors, things you can count.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Bigger House

On squared paper, draw a simple house: a square-ish body four squares wide and four squares tall, with a pointed roof that goes up two more squares to a peak in the middle. Six squares tall altogether.

Now draw it twice as big. Every length doubles. The base: four squares becomes eight. The walls: four squares becomes eight. The roof: it went two across and two up to the peak, so now it goes four across and four up.

Look at your two houses side by side. The big one is twice as wide and twice as tall. But the shape is exactly the same — the roof slopes at the same angle, the corners are the same square corners. It looks like you zoomed in.

Now draw it half as big: base two squares, walls two squares, roof one across and one up. A tiny house, the same shape.

That multiplying number — two for bigger, a half for smaller — is the scale factor.

Your questions for this section are with you now: multiply every length by the same number.

## Subtopic: How Much More Paint?

Your big house is twice as wide and twice as tall as the small one. If you painted the front of each, would the big one need twice as much paint?

Count the squares. The small house's body is four by four: sixteen squares. The big house's body is eight by eight: sixty-four squares. Sixty-four is FOUR times sixteen, not two times!

Here is why. Fit small houses inside the big one: two across and two up. Four small houses fill the big one. Doubling the width AND the height means two times two, four times the area.

But the fence around the outside — the perimeter — only doubles. The small body has a fence of four plus four plus four plus four, sixteen squares long. The big one: thirty-two. Twice as long.

So when you make something twice as big: lengths and fences double, but areas and paint go up four times. Half as big: lengths halve, but areas become only a quarter.

You can now enlarge and reduce shapes and compare their size. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: lengths times the factor, areas times the factor twice.
