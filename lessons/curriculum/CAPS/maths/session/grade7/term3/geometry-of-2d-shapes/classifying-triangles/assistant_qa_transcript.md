### subtopic_1 — Classifying Triangles by Their Sides
**Assistant:** Is an equilateral triangle also isosceles?

**Tutor:** Yes, with the usual definition. An isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides, and an equilateral triangle has three equal sides, which certainly includes two. So every equilateral triangle is a special kind of isosceles triangle, just as every square is a special kind of rectangle. Some books use the narrower definition exactly two equal sides; when a question asks you to classify, give the most specific name — equilateral — but understand that it also satisfies the isosceles condition.

### subtopic_2 — Classifying Triangles by Their Angles
**Assistant:** Why do I only need to look at the largest angle to classify a triangle by its angles?

**Tutor:** Because a triangle can have at most one angle that is ninety degrees or more. If the largest angle is less than ninety, then all three angles are less than ninety, so the triangle is acute-angled. If the largest is exactly ninety, it is right-angled, and the other two must share the remaining ninety, so they are both acute. If the largest is more than ninety, it is obtuse-angled, and the other two are again acute. The largest angle decides everything.

### subtopic_3 — Combining Both Classifications
**Assistant:** Why can there be no right-angled equilateral triangle?

**Tutor:** Because an equilateral triangle has three equal angles that add up to 180 degrees, so each must be exactly sixty degrees. There is no room for a ninety-degree angle. If one angle were ninety, the other two would have to share ninety degrees, and they could not both be ninety as well. So the angles could not all be equal, and the sides could not all be equal either. Equal sides force equal angles, and equal angles in a triangle force sixty degrees each.
