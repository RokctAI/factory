# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting, naming and comparing triangles according to their sides and angles: equilateral, isosceles, scalene, and acute-angled, right-angled and obtuse-angled triangles. The anchor is a roof truss made of different triangles.

## Subtopic: Classifying Triangles by Their Sides

A triangle is a closed figure with three straight sides and three angles. Triangles are named by their vertices, for example triangle ABC.

By sides, there are three types. An equilateral triangle has all three sides equal. An isosceles triangle has at least two sides equal. A scalene triangle has no sides equal.

Equal sides are shown on diagrams by matching small dashes on the sides: one dash on each of two equal sides, or two dashes on a different pair.

Properties follow from the sides. In an equilateral triangle all three angles are equal too, each sixty degrees. In an isosceles triangle, the two angles opposite the equal sides are equal; these are called the base angles. In a scalene triangle all three angles are different.

In the roof truss, the central triangle has sides of two metres, two metres and two metres: equilateral. The outer triangles have two sides of 2,5 metres and a base of three metres: isosceles. A brace with sides of 1,2 metres, 1,6 metres and two metres is scalene.

To classify by sides, measure each side with a ruler, or read the markings, and count how many are equal.

The questions for this section are ready: classify triangles by their sides and use the markings.

## Subtopic: Classifying Triangles by Their Angles

By angles, there are also three types. An acute-angled triangle has all three angles acute, less than ninety degrees. A right-angled triangle has one right angle. An obtuse-angled triangle has one obtuse angle, more than ninety degrees.

The angles of any triangle add up to 180 degrees. This means a triangle can have at most one right angle or one obtuse angle: two right angles would already use 180 degrees, leaving nothing for the third angle.

The side opposite the right angle in a right-angled triangle is the longest side; in Grade 8 it is called the hypotenuse.

In the roof truss, the equilateral triangle is acute-angled, since all its angles are sixty degrees. Where a vertical post meets the horizontal beam, the triangles formed are right-angled. A low, wide triangle at the eaves, with an angle of 120 degrees at the top, is obtuse-angled.

To classify by angles, find the LARGEST angle. If it is less than ninety, the triangle is acute-angled; equal to ninety, right-angled; more than ninety, obtuse-angled. You only need to check one angle.

Pause here for this section's questions: classify triangles by angles using the largest angle.

## Subtopic: Combining Both Classifications

Every triangle has a name by sides AND a name by angles. Using both gives a full description.

A right-angled isosceles triangle has a ninety-degree angle and two equal sides. Its other two angles are equal and add to ninety, so each is forty-five degrees. A set square often has this shape.

A right-angled scalene triangle: for example sides three, four and five centimetres, with angles of ninety, about fifty-three and about thirty-seven degrees.

An obtuse-angled isosceles triangle: for example angles of thirty, thirty and 120 degrees.

An acute-angled scalene triangle: for example angles fifty, sixty and seventy.

An equilateral triangle is always acute-angled, because every angle is sixty degrees. So "right-angled equilateral" and "obtuse-angled equilateral" are impossible combinations.

When comparing triangles, list both classifications and the evidence: which sides are equal, and the size of the largest angle.

Here come the questions for this section: give full descriptions and decide which combinations are possible.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same roof truss now, seen by a builder: counting equal sides and checking corners. Same names, real triangles.

## Subtopic: Triangles in the Roof

Look up at the frame of a roof before the tiles go on. It is full of triangles, because triangles are strong — they do not wobble.

In the middle there is a triangle with all three sides two metres long. All sides equal: equilateral. Its corners are all the same too — sixty degrees each.

On each side there are triangles with two long sloping sides of 2,5 metres and a flat bottom of three metres. Two sides equal: isosceles. Its two bottom corners match each other.

There is also a little brace with sides of 1,2, 1,6 and two metres. No sides equal: scalene.

To name a triangle by its sides, just count the equal sides. Three equal: equilateral. Two equal: isosceles. None equal: scalene.

On drawings, little dashes on the sides show which sides are equal.

Your questions for this section are with you now: count the equal sides.

## Subtopic: Sharp, Square and Wide Corners

Triangles also get names from their corners.

If every corner is sharp — smaller than a square corner — the triangle is acute-angled.

If one corner is a perfect square corner, ninety degrees, it is right-angled. Where a straight upright post meets the flat beam in the roof, you get right-angled triangles.

If one corner is wide — bigger than a square corner — it is obtuse-angled.

Here is a shortcut: just look at the biggest corner. If even the biggest is sharp, they are all sharp. A triangle can only ever have ONE square or wide corner, because the three corners always add up to 180 degrees.

And every triangle gets two names, one for its sides and one for its corners. A set square, with a square corner and two equal sides, is a right-angled isosceles triangle. But you will never find a right-angled equilateral triangle — equilateral corners are always sixty degrees.

You can now give any triangle its full name. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: count the sides, check the biggest corner.
