# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers describing, sorting, naming and comparing quadrilaterals in terms of the length of sides, parallel and perpendicular sides, and the size of angles: square, rectangle, parallelogram, rhombus, trapezium and kite. The anchor is a kite-making workshop.

## Subtopic: Parallelograms, Rectangles, Rhombi and Squares

A quadrilateral is a closed figure with four straight sides and four angles. Its angles always add up to 360 degrees.

A parallelogram has both pairs of opposite sides parallel. As a result, its opposite sides are equal and its opposite angles are equal.

A rectangle is a parallelogram with four right angles. Opposite sides are parallel and equal, and adjacent sides are perpendicular.

A rhombus is a parallelogram with all four sides equal. Its opposite angles are equal, but its angles need not be right angles — it looks like a pushed-over square.

A square has all four sides equal AND four right angles. It is a parallelogram, a rectangle and a rhombus all at once, because it satisfies every one of their definitions.

On diagrams, parallel sides are marked with matching arrows, equal sides with matching dashes and right angles with small squares.

In the workshop, a sheet of A4 paper is a rectangle, about 21 by 29,7 centimetres; folding and cutting it can make a square of 21 by 21 centimetres.

The questions for this section are ready: name these quadrilaterals from their properties and markings.

## Subtopic: Trapeziums and Kites

Two more quadrilaterals complete the set.

A trapezium has at least one pair of opposite sides parallel. A typical trapezium looks like a table seen from the side, with a long base, a shorter parallel top and two slanting legs. If the two non-parallel sides are equal, it is an isosceles trapezium, and its base angles are equal.

A kite has two pairs of adjacent sides equal: two short sides next to each other at the top and two long sides next to each other at the bottom. One diagonal is a line of symmetry, and the angles on either side of that line are equal. The diagonals of a kite are perpendicular.

In the workshop, a kite is built from two sticks crossing at right angles, with the cross-stick nearer the top. The frame of sides joining the stick ends gives two short equal sides and two long equal sides.

Note the difference between opposite and adjacent sides: opposite sides do not touch; adjacent sides share a vertex. A parallelogram has equal opposite sides; a kite has equal adjacent sides.

Pause here for this section's questions: trapeziums, kites and the difference between opposite and adjacent.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Comparing Quadrilaterals

Quadrilaterals can be organised into a family tree by their properties.

At the top: all quadrilaterals. Below that, trapeziums, which have at least one pair of parallel sides, and kites, which have two pairs of equal adjacent sides. Below trapeziums, parallelograms, with two pairs of parallel sides. Below parallelograms, rectangles and rhombi. At the bottom, squares, which belong to both the rectangle and the rhombus branches — and, because a rhombus is a kite, to the kite branch too.

Comparing properties in a table helps. Opposite sides parallel: parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square. All sides equal: rhombus, square. All angles ninety degrees: rectangle, square. Diagonals perpendicular: rhombus, square, kite.

Sorting questions: a quadrilateral has opposite sides equal and one angle of ninety degrees. Opposite sides equal means a parallelogram; a parallelogram with one right angle has all right angles, because co-interior angles add to 180; so it is a rectangle — and if its sides were all equal it would be a square.

Here come the questions for this section: place quadrilaterals in the family tree and deduce names from properties.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same shapes now, cut from A4 paper and built into a kite. Same properties, hands-on.

## Subtopic: Cutting Shapes at the Kite Workshop

At the kite workshop, everyone gets a sheet of A4 paper. It has four square corners and opposite sides that are the same length. That is a rectangle.

Fold one corner down to the opposite edge and cut off the extra strip. Now all four sides are equal AND all corners are square. That is a square — the most special quadrilateral of all.

Take a square and push it over sideways, like squashing a box. The sides stay equal but the corners are no longer square. That is a rhombus.

Take a rectangle and push it over sideways. Opposite sides are still equal and parallel, but the corners are no longer square. That is a parallelogram.

So squares, rectangles and rhombi are all special parallelograms — they all have two pairs of parallel sides. A square is so special that it is a rectangle AND a rhombus at the same time.

Your questions for this section are with you now: parallel sides, equal sides, square corners.

## Subtopic: Building the Kite

Now we build a real kite. Take two sticks. Cross them at a square corner, with the short stick near the top of the long stick. Join the four ends with string.

Look at the shape: the two short sides at the top are equal to each other, and the two long sides at the bottom are equal to each other. The equal sides are NEXT to each other, touching at a corner. That is what makes it a kite — two pairs of equal sides that are neighbours.

The two sticks are the diagonals, and they cross at a square corner. Fold the kite along the long stick, and the two halves match exactly.

One more shape: a lampshade or a table seen from the side often has a long bottom, a shorter top parallel to it, and slanting sides. One pair of parallel sides makes it a trapezium.

So the family is: square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, kite and trapezium. Look for parallel sides, equal sides and square corners, and you can name any of them.

You can now sort and name every quadrilateral. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: which sides are parallel, which are equal, which corners are square?
