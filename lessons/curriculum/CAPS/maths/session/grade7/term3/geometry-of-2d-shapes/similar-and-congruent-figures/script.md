# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers recognising and describing similar and congruent figures by comparing shape and size, including corresponding sides and angles. The anchor is a 10 × 15 cm photo and its copies.

## Subtopic: Congruent Figures

Two figures are congruent if they have exactly the same shape AND exactly the same size. One could be placed on top of the other and fit exactly, possibly after sliding, turning or flipping it.

In congruent figures, all corresponding sides are equal and all corresponding angles are equal. Corresponding means matching: the side or angle in the same position in each figure.

Two prints of the same ten by fifteen centimetre photograph are congruent: sides ten and ten, fifteen and fifteen, and all angles ninety degrees.

A triangle with sides three, four and five centimetres is congruent to another three, four, five triangle, even if the second is drawn upside down or as a mirror image. Turning or flipping does not change shape or size.

The symbol for congruent is an equals sign with a wavy line above it. Triangle ABC congruent to triangle PQR means A matches P, B matches Q and C matches R — the order of the letters shows the correspondence.

Everyday congruent objects: the tiles on a floor, coins of the same value, pages of the same book.

The questions for this section are ready: decide whether figures are congruent and identify corresponding parts.

## Subtopic: Similar Figures

Two figures are similar if they have exactly the same shape but not necessarily the same size. One is an enlargement or reduction of the other.

In similar figures, all corresponding angles are equal, and all corresponding sides are in the same ratio — every side is multiplied by the same scale factor.

The ten by fifteen centimetre photo and its twenty by thirty centimetre enlargement are similar. Ten times two is twenty, and fifteen times two is thirty: the same scale factor, two, for both sides. All angles are ninety degrees in both.

The stretched version, ten by twenty-five centimetres, is NOT similar. The width is multiplied by one, but the length by five thirds. Different scale factors mean the shape has changed — faces in the photo would look stretched.

All squares are similar to each other, and all circles are similar to each other, because their shape is fixed and only size varies. All equilateral triangles are similar. But not all rectangles are similar: a long thin rectangle is a different shape from a nearly square one.

Congruent figures are a special case of similar figures, with a scale factor of one.

Pause here for this section's questions: check the scale factor for every pair of sides.

## Subtopic: Comparing Figures Systematically

To decide whether two figures are congruent, similar or neither, follow a routine.

Step one: match corresponding vertices — usually the smallest angle with the smallest angle, the shortest side with the shortest side.

Step two: compare the corresponding angles. If any pair differs, the figures are neither similar nor congruent.

Step three: compare the corresponding sides. Divide each side in the second figure by the matching side in the first. If all the ratios are one, the figures are congruent. If all the ratios are equal but not one, they are similar, and that ratio is the scale factor. If the ratios differ, they are not similar.

Example: triangle one has sides three, four, five; triangle two has sides six, eight, ten. Ratios: six over three is two, eight over four is two, ten over five is two. All equal: similar, scale factor two. Not congruent, because the factor is not one.

Example: triangle one sides three, four, five; triangle three sides four, five, six. Ratios: four thirds, five quarters, six fifths — all different. Not similar.

Here come the questions for this section: match, compare angles, compare side ratios.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same photographs now, at the print shop, and the same triangles checked like a detective. Same ideas, everyday pictures.

## Subtopic: Copies, Enlargements and Stretches

You have a favourite photo, ten centimetres wide and fifteen centimetres long. You go to the print shop.

First you ask for a second copy, the same size. The copy has exactly the same shape and the same size — it could sit perfectly on top of the original. These two photos are congruent.

Next you ask for an enlargement, twenty by thirty centimetres. Everything is doubled: ten to twenty, fifteen to thirty. The picture looks exactly the same, just bigger. Same shape, different size. These two photos are similar.

Then the printer makes a mistake: the photo comes out ten by twenty-five. The width stayed the same but the length grew. Everyone's face looks stretched and long! The shape has changed. This photo is NOT similar to the original.

So: congruent means a perfect twin. Similar means a bigger or smaller twin, with every side changed by the same amount. Anything else is a stretched or squashed shape.

Your questions for this section are with you now: twin, bigger twin, or stretched?

## Subtopic: Spot the Twins

Let us check some triangles like detectives.

Triangle A has sides 3, 4 and 5 centimetres. Triangle B has sides 6, 8 and 10. Is B a bigger twin? Compare: 3 to 6 is doubled, 4 to 8 is doubled, 5 to 10 is doubled. Every side doubled. Yes — similar!

Triangle C has sides 4, 5 and 6. Compare with A: 3 to 4 is a bit more, 4 to 5 is a bit more, 5 to 6 is a bit more. But "a bit more" is not the same multiplying number each time. Not similar — C is a different shape.

Triangle D has sides 5, 3 and 4, and is drawn upside down. Is it a twin of A? Match the sides: 3 with 3, 4 with 4, 5 with 5. Exactly the same. Turning it over does not matter. D is congruent to A.

Some shapes are always twins of each other in shape: every square looks like every other square, and every circle looks like every other circle, just bigger or smaller. So all squares are similar, and all circles are similar.

You can now tell congruent, similar and different shapes apart. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: match the sides, check the multiplying number.
