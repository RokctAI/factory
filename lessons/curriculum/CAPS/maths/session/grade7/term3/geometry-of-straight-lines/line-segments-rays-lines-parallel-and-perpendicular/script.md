# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers defining and recognising line segments, rays, straight lines, parallel lines and perpendicular lines, together with their notation. The anchor is a street map with a main road, a taxi route and side streets.

## Subtopic: Line Segments, Rays and Straight Lines

A point marks an exact position and has no size. It is named with a capital letter, such as point A.

A line segment is a straight path between two endpoints. It has a definite length that can be measured. Segment AB starts at A and ends at B, and is written AB with a short bar above it, or simply as AB in words. The road between two traffic lights is like a line segment.

A ray starts at one endpoint and continues in one direction forever. It is named starting with its endpoint: ray AB starts at A, passes through B and carries on. It cannot be measured, because it has no end. A beam of light from a torch is a good model of a ray. Ray AB and ray BA are different rays: they start at different points and go in opposite directions.

A straight line continues forever in both directions. It has no endpoints and no measurable length. On diagrams, arrowheads on both ends show that it continues. Line AB is the same as line BA.

The difference is in the endpoints: a segment has two, a ray has one, a line has none.

The questions for this section are ready: name and recognise segments, rays and lines.

## Subtopic: Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Two lines in the same plane either meet at one point, called the point of intersection, or never meet.

Parallel lines are lines in the same plane that never meet, however far they are extended. They stay the same distance apart everywhere. AB parallel to CD is written AB, two slanting bars, CD. On diagrams, matching arrowheads on the lines show that they are parallel.

Perpendicular lines meet at a right angle, ninety degrees. AB perpendicular to CD is written AB, an upside-down T, CD. On diagrams, a small square at the intersection shows the right angle.

Intersecting lines that are not perpendicular meet at angles other than ninety degrees.

In the street map: two streets that run alongside each other without ever meeting, like the lanes of a highway, are parallel. A side street that crosses the main road at a perfect square corner is perpendicular to it. A street that crosses at a slant simply intersects.

Segments and rays can also be parallel or perpendicular, when the lines they lie on are.

## Subtopic: Using the Language of Lines

Accurate language and notation let you describe diagrams precisely, which matters in every later geometry topic.

Describing a rectangle ABCD: opposite sides are parallel — AB parallel to DC and AD parallel to BC. Adjacent sides are perpendicular — AB perpendicular to BC. Each side is a line segment with a measurable length.

Describing a road diagram: the main road is drawn as line PQ with arrows at both ends, because the road continues off the map. The taxi route starts at the rank R and goes on through S: ray RS. A side street from T to U is a segment TU, because it has a start and an end on the map.

When drawing, use a ruler for all straight lines, label points with capital letters, put arrows on lines and rays to show where they continue, and use the correct markings for parallel and perpendicular.

A common error is to call a segment a line. In everyday speech we say "draw a line", but in geometry a line has no ends. If your figure has endpoints, it is a segment.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same street map now: roads that go on, routes that start somewhere, streets that stop, and streets that meet or never meet. Same definitions, a town you can picture.

## Subtopic: Roads, Routes and Streets

Picture a map of your town drawn with a ruler.

The main road runs right across the map and keeps going off both edges — it does not stop. In geometry, something straight that goes on forever both ways is a line. We draw arrows at both ends.

The taxi route starts at the rank and heads off in one direction, out of town and on forever. It has a start but no end. That is a ray. We put a dot at the start and an arrow at the other end.

A short side street goes from the shop on one corner to the school on the other. It starts and stops. That is a line segment. It has two endpoints, and you can measure how long it is — say 250 metres.

So: line — no ends. Ray — one end. Segment — two ends.

## Subtopic: Streets That Meet and Streets That Never Do

Some streets never meet. Two lanes of a long straight highway run side by side, always the same distance apart. However far you drive, they never cross. Those are parallel lines — like railway tracks.

Some streets meet at a perfect square corner, like a T-junction or a neat crossroads. Those are perpendicular lines. We draw a little square in the corner to show the ninety-degree angle.

And some streets cross at a slant, not a square corner. They intersect, but they are not perpendicular.

Here is a quick test on paper: if two lines are parallel, the gap between them is the same everywhere — measure it in two places. If two lines are perpendicular, the corner of a book fits exactly into the corner where they meet.

Look around the classroom: the top and bottom edges of the board are parallel; the side edge and the bottom edge are perpendicular.

You can now name every kind of straight line and spot parallel and perpendicular ones. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: never meet, square corner, or slanted crossing?
