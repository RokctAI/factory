# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the description and justification of general rules for observed relationships, in words and in algebraic language. A rule is justified when its structure is explained by the diagram or situation that produced it, and equivalent rules — such as two n plus two and two bracket n plus one bracket — are recognised as the same relationship. The anchor situations are chairs around a row of n tables, with rule two n plus two; the border of an n-by-n grid, with rule four n minus four; and the matchstick squares, three n plus one.

## Subtopic: Describing a Rule in Words and in Symbols

A rule for a pattern can be described in two languages, and you must be fluent in both. In words: the number of sticks is one more than three times the number of squares. In symbols: s equals three n plus one, where n is the number of squares and s the number of sticks. The word description and the symbolic description say the same thing, and a question may ask for either or for the translation between them.

Precision matters in the word description. Add three each time describes how to get from one term to the next — the recursive rule. Three times the position plus one describes how to get any term from its position — the general rule. Both are correct descriptions of the matchstick pattern, but they answer different questions: the first tells you how the pattern grows, the second tells you how to jump. An examiner who asks for a rule in terms of n wants the general rule.

Translating from symbols to words: two n plus two reads as double the number of tables and add two. Four n minus four reads as four times the side length minus four. N squared plus one reads as the position squared, plus one. Translating from words to symbols: three more than five times the position is five n plus three; the position multiplied by the next position is n times bracket n plus one bracket.

Define your letters. A rule in symbols is incomplete without a statement of what each letter stands for: let n be the number of tables and c the number of chairs. Marks are awarded for the definition, and without it the formula is ambiguous — n could be the number of chairs for all the reader knows.

Finally, a rule should be stated for the whole pattern, not for one instance. The number of chairs for five tables is twelve is a fact; the number of chairs is two times the number of tables plus two is a rule. Questions on describing rules follow now.

## Subtopic: Justifying a Rule from the Diagram

A rule is justified when you can explain where each part of it comes from. Take the matchstick squares. The rule is three n plus one. Justification: the left-hand edge of the first square is one stick that stands alone — that is the plus one. Every square, including the first, then contributes its top, bottom and right-hand edge — three sticks each — that is the three n. The parts of the formula correspond to parts of the picture, and that correspondence is the justification.

Now the anchor: chairs around a row of tables. One square table seats four. Two tables pushed together in a row seat six, because the two touching sides lose their chairs. Three tables seat eight. The pattern is four, six, eight, with difference two, so the rule is two n plus two. Justification from the diagram: each table has a chair on its long top side and a chair on its bottom side — two chairs per table, two n — and the row has two ends, one chair each — plus two. Every term in the formula is visible in the picture.

A third: the border of an n-by-n grid of dots. A five-by-five grid has sixteen dots on its border. The rule is four n minus four. Justification: four sides of n dots each would be four n, but each corner dot has been counted twice, once for each side it lies on, and there are four corners, so subtract four. Alternative justification: the top and bottom rows have n dots each, two n, and the left and right columns have n minus two dots each once the corners are removed, two bracket n minus two bracket, which totals four n minus four.

That last example shows something important: two correct justifications can produce two differently written rules, four n minus four and two n plus two bracket n minus two bracket, and both are right because they describe the same count. The next section makes this equivalence precise.

In an examination, a justification is a sentence that links each part of the formula to the diagram. Three sticks per square because each new square adds a top, a bottom and a side; plus one for the first left edge. Write it that way. Questions on justifying from diagrams follow.

## Subtopic: Equivalent Rules — Same Relationship, Different Expression

Two learners describe the chairs. One says two chairs per table plus two end chairs: two n plus two. The other says imagine one extra table on the end and give every table two chairs: two bracket n plus one bracket. Are they both right? Test with numbers. Three tables: two times three plus two is eight; two times four is eight. Five tables: twelve and twelve. The rules agree at every position, so they describe the same relationship. They are equivalent.

Why are they equivalent? Expand the bracket using the distributive property from the integers topic: two bracket n plus one bracket is two n plus two. The algebra shows that the two expressions are the same expression written differently, which is what equivalent means. Any two rules that simplify to the same expression are equivalent, and any two rules that agree for every value of n are equivalent.

The grid border gave four n minus four and two n plus two bracket n minus two bracket. Expand the second: two n plus two n minus four, which is four n minus four. Equivalent. A third description: four bracket n minus one bracket, from counting each side as n minus one dots so that each corner belongs to exactly one side. Expand: four n minus four. Three descriptions, three justifications, one relationship.

Equivalence is tested in two ways. Numerically: substitute two or three values of n into both expressions and compare — necessary but not a proof, because two different expressions can agree at a few points. Algebraically: expand and simplify both expressions to the same form — this is the proof. In Grade 8, do both: the numbers to be convinced, the algebra to be sure.

Non-equivalent rules that look similar are the examiner's favourite trap. Two n plus two and two n plus one differ at every position. Three n plus one and three bracket n plus one bracket differ, because the second is three n plus three. Four n minus four and four n minus one differ. A bracket moves the constant through the multiplication, and that changes the rule. Questions on equivalence follow.

## Subtopic: Justifying from a Situation and Examination Technique

Not every pattern comes with a diagram; some come from a situation, and the justification then explains the situation. A taxi charges a flag fee of twelve rand plus eight rand per kilometre. The cost for k kilometres is eight k plus twelve. Justification: eight rand for each of the k kilometres gives eight k; the twelve is paid once regardless of distance. A phone contract of ninety-nine rand a month plus one rand fifty per minute: one comma five m plus ninety-nine for m minutes. A hall hire of five hundred rand plus forty rand per guest: forty g plus five hundred. In every case, the per-item rate is the coefficient of the variable and the once-off amount is the constant — the same structure as difference and position-zero value in a number pattern.

Justifying a rule also means checking that it predicts correctly. For the chairs, two n plus two predicts twenty-two chairs for ten tables; draw or imagine ten tables in a row and count — twenty along the tops and bottoms and two at the ends. Twenty-two. The prediction and the count agree, which is further evidence the rule is right.

A rule is wrong when it fails for any case. If a learner proposes four n for the chairs because one table seats four, the second table refutes it: four n gives eight, but two tables seat six. One counterexample is enough to reject a rule, and in an examination, the way to reject a rule is to exhibit the counterexample explicitly: n equals two gives eight, but the diagram shows six.

Examination technique for describe-and-justify questions. One: state the rule in words. Two: state the rule in symbols, with letters defined. Three: justify each part of the symbolic rule from the diagram or situation. Four: verify with at least two positions. Five: if asked whether another rule is equivalent, test numerically and then expand algebraically. Six: if asked to reject a rule, give a counterexample.

Distinction-level takeaway: a rule is justified when each part of it is explained by the picture or situation; the coefficient is the per-item amount and the constant is the once-off amount; equivalent rules expand to the same expression; and one counterexample refutes a rule. Questions on situations and technique follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rules told as stories at a wedding, on a chessboard and on a building site, where two people describe the same pattern in different words and discover they were both right. Justifying a rule is just explaining your counting out loud.

## Subtopic: Chairs at the Wedding — Two People, One Rule

At a wedding the tables are small squares, one chair per side, and they get pushed together into long rows. One table on its own: four chairs. Push two together: the sides that touch lose their chairs, so six. Three in a row: eight. Four: ten. The numbers go up by two each time, and now the question is why — because once you can say why, you can say how many chairs for any row without pushing tables around.

Here is how one guest explains it. Every table has a chair on its top side and a chair on its bottom side, so two chairs for each table — that is two times the number of tables. Then the row has two ends, one chair at each — add two. Two n plus two. Ten tables: twenty plus two, twenty-two chairs.

Here is how another guest explains it. Pretend there is one more table at the end of the row, so n plus one tables. Give every one of those tables two chairs, top and bottom: two times bracket n plus one bracket. The pretend table's two chairs are really the two end chairs of the actual row, moved round. Ten tables: two times eleven, twenty-two.

Both guests got twenty-two. Try three tables: two times three plus two is eight; two times four is eight. Try one: four and four. They agree every time, because they are counting the same chairs in two different orders. Two n plus two and two bracket n plus one bracket are the same rule — and if you multiply out the bracket, two times n and two times one, you get two n plus two, which is exactly the first guest's version.

That is what justifying a rule means. Not just the numbers go up by two, but every table gives two chairs and the ends give two more — a sentence that points at the picture. And when two people justify differently and get the same answer, that is not a problem; that is the best check there is. The questions are wedding questions; say where each chair comes from before you count.

## Subtopic: The Chessboard Border — Three Ways to Count

Take a square grid of dots, like the corners of a chessboard's squares, n dots along each side. How many dots are on the border? For a five-by-five grid, count: sixteen. For four-by-four: twelve. For three-by-three: eight. Up by four each time. Why?

First way. Four sides, n dots on each: four n. But look at a corner dot — it is on the top side and on the left side, so it got counted twice. Four corners, four double-counts, so take four off: four n minus four. Five-by-five: twenty minus four, sixteen.

Second way. The top row has n dots and the bottom row has n dots: two n. The left and right columns have n dots each, but their top and bottom dots are already in the rows, so each column contributes n minus two new dots: two bracket n minus two bracket. Total: two n plus two bracket n minus two bracket. Five-by-five: ten plus six, sixteen.

Third way. Walk around the border and give each side its starting corner but not its ending corner, so each side owns n minus one dots: four bracket n minus one bracket. Five-by-five: four times four, sixteen.

Three descriptions, all giving sixteen for five, all giving twelve for four. Are they really the same? Multiply out. Two bracket n minus two bracket is two n minus four, so the second way is two n plus two n minus four, four n minus four. Four bracket n minus one bracket is four n minus four. All three are four n minus four in different clothes. The counting was different; the dots were the same; the algebra proves it.

Now the trap. Someone says the border is four n, because four sides of n. Test it: three-by-three would have twelve border dots, but the picture shows eight. One wrong case and the rule is dead — that is what a counterexample does. Say the counterexample out loud: for n equals three, four n gives twelve, but there are eight. Rule rejected, and you have justified the rejection. The questions are chessboard questions; find where the corners went before you choose.

## Subtopic: The Builder's Quote — The Takeaway

A builder quotes for paving: a call-out fee of five hundred rand, then forty rand per square metre. For g square metres the cost is forty g plus five hundred. Justify it: the forty is per square metre, so it gets multiplied by how many square metres; the five hundred is paid once, whatever the size. The coefficient is the per-thing amount and the constant is the once-off amount. That is the same structure as the matchsticks — three per square, one to start — and the chairs — two per table, two for the ends. Every linear rule has a per-thing part and a once-off part, and justifying the rule means saying which is which and why.

A second builder quotes forty bracket g plus twelve point five bracket. Is that the same price? Multiply out: forty g plus five hundred. Yes — the second builder has just folded the call-out fee into twelve and a half extra square metres' worth. Same quote, different presentation. You check by expanding, and you confirm by trying a number: twenty square metres, eight hundred plus five hundred is one thousand three hundred; forty times thirty-two and a half is one thousand three hundred.

A third builder quotes forty g plus fifty, having dropped a zero. For twenty square metres that is eight hundred and fifty. Not the same — and one case is enough to say so.

Here is the whole lesson. Describe the rule in words and in symbols, and say what the letters mean. Justify it by pointing at the picture or the situation: this part is per item, this part is once-off, this part is the corners counted twice. Check it with two or three values. If someone else has a different-looking rule, expand both — same expression, same rule. If a rule fails for even one value, show that value and reject it.

You came into this topic spotting the next number, and you are leaving able to write a rule, explain where every piece of it comes from, prove that two rules are the same, and knock down a wrong one with a single counterexample — that is real mathematical reasoning, and it is exactly what the algebra of Term 2 will ask of you. The final questions mix wedding, chessboard and builder; justify before you choose.
