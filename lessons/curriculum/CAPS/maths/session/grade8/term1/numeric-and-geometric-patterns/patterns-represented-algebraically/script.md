# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the algebraic representation of patterns: the general term, written as an expression in the position n, from which any term can be calculated directly. For constant-difference patterns the general term is the common difference times n plus a constant found from the first term; the anchor is four n minus one for three, seven, eleven, fifteen, with hundredth term three hundred and ninety-nine. The general term for the matchstick squares, three n plus one, and for the square numbers, n squared, are also established, and the reverse problem of finding the position of a given term is solved.

## Subtopic: From Rule to Formula — The General Term

Last lesson the rule for three, seven, eleven, fifteen was add four. That rule gets you from one term to the next, but to reach the hundredth term you would have to add four ninety-nine times. A formula does better: it gives any term directly from its position. Call the position n, so the first term has n equal to one, the second n equal to two, and so on. The general term, written T subscript n, is an expression in n whose value is the nth term.

For a constant-difference pattern, the general term is the common difference times n, plus or minus a constant. Reason: the terms go up by the difference each time the position goes up by one, exactly as four n does. Four n gives four, eight, twelve, sixteen. Our pattern is three, seven, eleven, fifteen — each term one less. So the general term is four n minus one. Check: n equal to one gives three; n equal to two gives seven; n equal to three gives eleven. Correct.

The method, then. Step one: find the common difference d; it is the coefficient of n. Step two: compare d times one with the first term; the gap is the constant. For five, eight, eleven, fourteen: d is three; three times one is three; the first term is five, two more; so T n equals three n plus two. Check n equal to four: twelve plus two is fourteen. Correct. For twenty, seventeen, fourteen, eleven: d is negative three; negative three times one is negative three; the first term is twenty, twenty-three more; T n equals negative three n plus twenty-three. Check n equal to two: negative six plus twenty-three is seventeen. Correct.

The constant is also what you get by going one step backwards from the first term — the term at position zero. For three, seven, eleven, going back four gives negative one, and the formula is four n minus one. Either way of finding the constant is acceptable; the check with two positions is not optional.

The letter n stands for a natural number, one, two, three and so on, because positions are counted. The formula is the rule said once. Questions on finding the general term follow now.

## Subtopic: Using the Formula — Any Term, and Which Position

With the general term in hand, any term is one substitution away. The hundredth term of three, seven, eleven, fifteen: T one hundred equals four times one hundred minus one, three hundred and ninety-nine. The twentieth term: eighty minus one, seventy-nine. The fiftieth term of five, eight, eleven: three times fifty plus two, one hundred and fifty-two. Write the substitution line — four times one hundred minus one — before the answer, because it is the method mark.

The reverse question: which term is seventy-nine? Set the general term equal to seventy-nine and solve for n. Four n minus one equals seventy-nine; four n equals eighty; n equals twenty. Seventy-nine is the twentieth term. This is the inverse method from the integers topic — undo the minus one by adding one, undo the times four by dividing by four — and it is the first real use of equation solving in Grade 8.

A related question: is one hundred a term of this pattern? Four n minus one equals one hundred gives four n equals one hundred and one, n equals twenty-five and a quarter. Positions are whole numbers, so no — one hundred is not a term. The formula answers yes-or-no questions by whether n comes out whole.

Matchstick squares: four, seven, ten, thirteen. Difference three; three times one is three; first term four, one more; T n equals three n plus one. Shape one hundred uses three hundred and one sticks. Which shape uses sixty-one sticks? Three n plus one equals sixty-one; three n equals sixty; n equals twenty. Shape twenty. Both answers in one line each, which is the point of the formula.

Tables with positions that jump are the same skill. A table shows positions one, two, three, ten and fifty with terms three, seven, eleven, blank and blank. From the first three, T n equals four n minus one, so the tenth is thirty-nine and the fiftieth is one hundred and ninety-nine. Learners who try to continue the pattern step by step all the way to the fiftieth term waste time and invite error; the formula is the tool. Questions on using the formula follow.

## Subtopic: Formulas for Other Patterns — Squares, Multiples and Ratios

Not every pattern is linear, and the general term shows the difference. The square numbers one, four, nine, sixteen have T n equals n squared: the tenth term is one hundred, the twentieth is four hundred. The cubes one, eight, twenty-seven have T n equals n cubed. The odd numbers one, three, five, seven have T n equals two n minus one, and the even numbers two, four, six have T n equals two n. The multiples of seven, seven, fourteen, twenty-one, have T n equals seven n — a constant-difference pattern whose constant adjustment happens to be zero.

Patterns built from squares are common. Two, five, ten, seventeen: compare with the squares one, four, nine, sixteen; each term is one more, so T n equals n squared plus one. Zero, three, eight, fifteen: each one less, T n equals n squared minus one. Two, eight, eighteen, thirty-two: each double, T n equals two n squared. When the first differences are not constant, line the pattern up against n squared and look for the adjustment.

A constant-ratio pattern has a general term with n in the exponent. Two, six, eighteen, fifty-four: each term is two times a power of three. The first term is two times three to the power zero, the second two times three to the power one, so T n equals two times three to the power n minus one. Check n equal to four: two times twenty-seven is fifty-four. Correct. The fifth term is two times eighty-one, one hundred and sixty-two. The exponent n minus one, not n, is the detail that is most often wrong; check it against the first term, where the power must be zero.

Write T n and T n plus one carefully. For four n minus one, T n plus one is four bracket n plus one bracket minus one, which is four n plus three. The difference T n plus one minus T n is four, the common difference — the algebra confirms the arithmetic. This is a preview of the algebraic expressions topic next term; for now, know that a bracket around n plus one is essential.

Questions on non-linear general terms follow.

## Subtopic: Checking, Verifying and Examination Technique

A general term is a claim, and a claim must be checked. Substitute at least two positions — the first and one other — and confirm the terms. For four n minus one: n equal to one gives three and n equal to four gives fifteen; both match. For three n plus one: n equal to one gives four, n equal to three gives ten; both match. A formula that fits the first term only is not verified, because many formulas share a first term.

Here is a classic error and its detection. A learner finds the general term of five, eight, eleven as three n plus five, because the first term is five. Check n equal to one: eight. Wrong — the first term is five. The constant is not the first term; it is the first term minus the difference. Three n plus two. Check: five. Correct. The constant equals the first term only when the difference happens to be zero.

A second error: writing n plus four for a pattern that adds four. Check: n equal to one gives five, n equal to two gives six — a difference of one, not four. The difference must be the coefficient of n, not the constant. Four n, adjusted.

Examination layout for a general-term question. Line one: the sequence with positions. Line two: the differences, and the statement constant difference d equals four. Line three: T n equals four n plus c, find c from T one: four plus c equals three, c equals negative one. Line four: T n equals four n minus one. Line five: check with a second position. Then any requested term by substitution on its own line, and any requested position by solving on its own lines.

For a table with missing entries, find the general term from the given consecutive entries, then fill every blank from the formula, and show at least one substitution.

Distinction-level takeaway: the general term is the difference times n plus a constant; find the constant from the first term; verify with two positions; substitute for a term and solve for a position; and for squares and ratios, compare with n squared or write a power with exponent n minus one. Questions on checking and technique follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same formulas as shortcuts you would actually want: the number of sticks in a hundred squares without building them, the day a rumour reaches a town, and a quick way to find which pattern a number belongs to. The letter n is just the position, and the formula is the rule said once instead of a hundred times.

## Subtopic: A Hundred Squares Without Building Them

Last lesson you built matchstick squares: four sticks, seven, ten, thirteen, adding three each time. Now imagine a hundred squares in a row. Nobody is going to add three ninety-nine times, and nobody has that many matches. So let us find a shortcut by looking at how the row is built.

Here is the picture. Lay down one stick standing up — that is the left edge. Now every square you add needs three sticks: a top, a bottom and a right edge. So a row of n squares uses one stick to start, plus three sticks for each square: one plus three times n. Three n plus one. Check it against what you built: one square, three plus one, four. Two squares, six plus one, seven. Three squares, nine plus one, ten. It works, and it works because the picture said so.

Now the hundred squares: three times one hundred plus one, three hundred and one sticks. One line. That is what a formula is for — it is the rule said once, with a letter for the position, so you can jump to any position without visiting the ones in between.

Let us read the formula backwards too. Someone used sixty-one sticks; how many squares? Three n plus one equals sixty-one. Take off the starting stick: three n equals sixty. Each square is three sticks: n equals twenty. Twenty squares. You just undid the formula with the two undo buttons from the integers topic — subtract the one, divide by the three.

Now the number pattern from last lesson: three, seven, eleven, fifteen, adding four. The same trick: four n would be four, eight, twelve, sixteen, and ours are each one less, so four n minus one. The hundredth term: three hundred and ninety-nine. Which term is seventy-nine? Add one, eighty; divide by four, twenty. The twentieth.

So: the letter n is the position, the number in front of n is what gets added each time, and the constant fixes the start. The questions are matchstick questions; find the formula, then jump.

## Subtopic: Finding the Constant — Where Does the Pattern Start?

The part of the formula that trips people is the constant — the plus one in three n plus one, the minus one in four n minus one. Here is a way to find it that never fails: ask where the pattern would be at position zero.

Three, seven, eleven, fifteen goes up by four. Walk one step backwards from the first term: three minus four is negative one. That is the position-zero value, and it is the constant. Four n minus one. Try it on five, eight, eleven, fourteen: up by three, so one step back from five is two. Three n plus two. Try twenty, seventeen, fourteen: down by three, so one step back from twenty is twenty-three. Negative three n plus twenty-three. The constant is where the pattern would start if you began counting at zero.

Why does this work? Because at position zero, the n part of the formula is zero, so the formula gives just the constant. And the position-zero term is one step before the first term, which is the first term minus the difference.

Now the other way round. Someone hands you the formula two n plus five and asks for the pattern. Position one: seven. Position two: nine. Position three: eleven. The pattern goes up by two, the number in front of n, and it starts at seven, which is the constant five plus one step of two. Both views agree.

Here is the mistake to avoid, said plainly. The constant is not the first term. For five, eight, eleven, the formula is not three n plus five; that would start at eight. It is three n plus two. Always check by putting in n equals one and seeing whether the first term comes out.

And a last check that costs nothing: put in n equals two as well. Three times two plus two is eight, the second term. Two checks, both pass, formula confirmed. The questions are about the constant; walk one step back from the first term before you choose.

## Subtopic: The Rumour Formula and the Takeaway

One more kind of formula, for the rumour that tripled: two, six, eighteen, fifty-four. Here n in front does not work, because the pattern multiplies. Look at the terms as two times something: two times one, two times three, two times nine, two times twenty-seven. The somethings are powers of three: three to the zero, three to the one, three to the two, three to the three. So the nth term is two times three to the power n minus one. Day five: two times three to the fourth, two times eighty-one, one hundred and sixty-two. Day ten: two times three to the ninth, thirty-nine thousand three hundred and sixty-six. The power is n minus one because on day one nobody has tripled yet — the exponent must be zero at the start.

The square numbers have the simplest formula of all: n squared. Position ten, one hundred. And patterns that sit near the squares borrow it: two, five, ten, seventeen is each one more than a square, so n squared plus one.

Here is the lesson in one breath. Adding patterns: the formula is the difference times n plus the position-zero value. Multiplying patterns: a starting value times the ratio to the power n minus one. Square-like patterns: n squared, adjusted. Always check with n equals one and n equals two. To find a term, substitute. To find a position, set the formula equal to the term and undo.

The letter n frightens people, and it should not. It is just the position — a placeholder for one, then two, then three. The formula is the rule you already understood, written once so that you never have to say it a hundred times.

You came in able to add four, four, four until you reached the term you wanted. You are leaving able to write four n minus one and jump straight to the hundredth term, and to work backwards from a term to its position — that is the first real algebra of Grade 8, and a genuine step up. The final questions mix formulas of all three kinds; check with n equals one before you choose.
