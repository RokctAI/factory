# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the investigation and extension of numeric and geometric patterns, including patterns with a constant difference, a constant ratio, and neither. Patterns are presented as number sequences, as diagrams, and as tables of position against term, and the method is to find the rule that takes each term to the next and to test it. The anchor sequences are three, seven, eleven, fifteen; two, six, eighteen, fifty-four; one, four, nine, sixteen; and the matchstick-square sequence four, seven, ten.

## Subtopic: Constant Difference — Adding the Same Amount Each Time

A number pattern, or sequence, is a list of numbers in a fixed order, each with a position: the first term, the second term, and so on. To investigate a pattern, look at how each term is obtained from the one before. The simplest rule is a constant difference: the same amount is added every time.

Three, seven, eleven, fifteen. From three to seven is plus four; from seven to eleven is plus four; from eleven to fifteen is plus four. The difference is constant at four, so the rule from one term to the next is add four. Extend: nineteen, twenty-three, twenty-seven. Write the differences under the sequence, between the terms, as the first move in every investigation; if they are all equal, you have a constant-difference pattern.

The difference can be negative. Twenty, seventeen, fourteen, eleven: the difference is negative three, and the next terms are eight, five, two, negative one. The pattern continues through zero into the negatives without any change of rule.

Record the pattern in a table of position against term. Position one, term three; position two, term seven; position three, term eleven; position four, term fifteen. The table makes the position explicit, and the position is what the next lesson will use to write a formula. For now, use the table to answer questions such as what is the eighth term: continue adding four from the fourth term four more times — nineteen, twenty-three, twenty-seven, thirty-one — so the eighth term is thirty-one.

A constant difference pattern is called linear, because its points lie on a straight line when plotted, and you will meet that picture in the graphs topic. Linear patterns grow steadily — the same step every time — and that steadiness is what distinguishes them from the patterns in the next section.

Finally, be precise in language. The difference is the amount added; the term is the number in the sequence; the position is where it sits. Three is the first term, not the first difference. Questions on constant-difference patterns follow now.

## Subtopic: Constant Ratio — Multiplying by the Same Amount Each Time

Not every pattern adds. Two, six, eighteen, fifty-four. The differences are four, twelve, thirty-six — not constant, so this is not a linear pattern. Try the ratios instead: six divided by two is three; eighteen divided by six is three; fifty-four divided by eighteen is three. Each term is three times the one before. The ratio is constant at three, and the rule is multiply by three. Extend: one hundred and sixty-two, four hundred and eighty-six.

A constant-ratio pattern grows much faster than a constant-difference pattern. Compare: three, seven, eleven, fifteen reaches thirty-one by the eighth term; two, six, eighteen, fifty-four reaches four thousand three hundred and seventy-four by the eighth term. Multiplication compounds. This is the pattern behind compound interest and population growth, and recognising it is the start of the exponents topic next term.

The ratio can be a fraction, which makes the pattern shrink. Eighty, forty, twenty, ten: each term is half the one before, ratio one half, next terms five, two comma five, one comma two five. The terms never reach zero but get ever closer. The ratio can also be negative: three, negative six, twelve, negative twenty-four has ratio negative two, and the signs alternate.

The investigation method, then, has two tests. First, the differences: equal differences mean a constant-difference pattern. Second, if the differences are not equal, the ratios: equal ratios mean a constant-ratio pattern. Always test the differences first, because they are easier to see, and write both tests in your working when the question says investigate.

Table the geometric pattern as before: position one, term two; position two, term six; position three, term eighteen; position four, term fifty-four. The fifth and sixth terms, from the rule, are one hundred and sixty-two and four hundred and eighty-six. Questions on constant-ratio patterns follow.

## Subtopic: Neither Difference nor Ratio — Patterns in the Differences

The syllabus is explicit: patterns are not limited to constant difference or constant ratio. One, four, nine, sixteen. Differences: three, five, seven — not constant. Ratios: four, two and a quarter, one and seven ninths — not constant. Yet there is clearly a pattern, because the differences themselves form a pattern: three, five, seven are the odd numbers, increasing by two each time. The next difference is nine, so the next term is twenty-five, and then eleven gives thirty-six.

You may also recognise these as the square numbers: one squared, two squared, three squared, four squared. Both descriptions are valid, and the second one — term equals position squared — is more powerful because it gives any term directly. Position ten, term one hundred. The differences method needs you to walk all the way there.

Another: one, three, six, ten, fifteen. Differences: two, three, four, five — increasing by one. Next difference six, next term twenty-one. These are the triangular numbers, and the diagram section will show why. Another: one, one, two, three, five, eight. Differences: zero, one, one, two, three — the sequence itself, shifted. Each term is the sum of the two before it. Next: thirteen, twenty-one.

The method extends naturally. First differences not constant? Find the differences of the differences — the second differences. For the squares, the second differences are two, two, two: constant. A constant second difference is the signature of a quadratic pattern, which you will study properly in Grade 9 and Grade 10; for now, use it to find the next first difference and hence the next term.

Record the differences in rows beneath the sequence: the sequence, the first differences, the second differences. The layout makes the structure visible and earns the investigation mark. When a pattern fits none of the three types, describe the rule in words — each term is the sum of the previous two — and extend from the description. Questions on patterns with neither follow.

## Subtopic: Geometric Patterns from Diagrams and Tables — Examination Technique

A geometric pattern is a sequence of shapes, and the investigation counts something in each shape — matchsticks, dots, tiles — to produce a number sequence. Squares made of matchsticks in a row: one square uses four sticks; two squares joined use seven, because the shared side is counted once; three squares use ten. The sequence is four, seven, ten, with constant difference three, because each new square adds three sticks. The fourth shape uses thirteen, the fifth sixteen.

Table it with the shape number as the position: shape one, four sticks; shape two, seven; shape three, ten; shape four, thirteen. From the table, shape ten uses thirty-one sticks, found by adding three six more times from shape four — or, next lesson, by a formula.

Dots in a triangle: one dot, then a row of two below it, then a row of three. Shape one has one dot, shape two has three, shape three has six, shape four has ten. These are the triangular numbers from the previous section, and the diagram explains the differences: each new shape adds a row with one more dot than the last row.

Tiles in a square: one tile, four tiles, nine tiles — the square numbers, with the diagram showing a two-by-two, then a three-by-three square. The differences three, five, seven are the L-shaped borders added to turn each square into the next.

Examination technique for pattern questions. One: write the sequence with positions above or beside it. Two: write the first differences underneath; if constant, state the rule add such-and-such. Three: if not constant, test ratios, then second differences. Four: extend by applying the rule, showing each step. Five: for a diagram, say what is added each time and why — one new square adds three sticks because one side is shared. Six: complete any table given, including positions that skip ahead, by repeated application of the rule. A learner who states the rule, shows the differences and explains the diagram has done everything the question asks.

Distinction-level takeaway: investigate by differences, then ratios, then second differences; a diagram explains the rule; and a table of position against term is the bridge to the formula in the next lesson. Questions on diagrams and technique follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same patterns as things you can build: matchsticks on a desk, tiles on a floor, a stack of cans. When you can see what gets added each time, the next term is never a guess, and the table writes itself.

## Subtopic: Build It — Matchsticks on the Desk

Get some matchsticks, or imagine them. Make one square: four sticks. Now make a second square right next to it, sharing a side. How many sticks did you add? Not four — three, because the shared side is already there. Seven sticks for two squares. A third square: three more, ten. A fourth: thirteen. Write the numbers down: four, seven, ten, thirteen. Look at the gaps: three, three, three. Every square you add costs three sticks, and now you can see why, because you built it.

This is the whole secret of patterns. If you can see what gets added each time, you can extend the pattern forever without guessing. Five squares: sixteen. Six: nineteen. Ten squares? Start at four and add three nine more times — that is twenty-seven more — thirty-one sticks. Or, more cleverly: ten squares is one stick to start and then three for each square, one plus thirty, thirty-one. Next lesson that cleverness becomes a formula.

Now a different build. Stack cans in a triangle: one can on top, two below it, three below that. One shape: one can. Two rows: three cans. Three rows: six. Four rows: ten. The gaps are two, three, four — not the same, because each new row is one can longer than the last. That is a pattern too; it just lives in the differences. Five rows adds five: fifteen cans.

And a third. Tiles in a square: one tile; a two-by-two square, four tiles; three-by-three, nine. To get from one square to the next you add an L-shape around two sides: three tiles, then five, then seven. Four-by-four is sixteen. The gaps are the odd numbers, and the squares are, well, squares: position times position.

So, three builds, three kinds of pattern. Squares in a row: add the same every time. Cans: the additions grow by one. Tiles: the additions are the odd numbers. In every case you found the rule by building one more and watching what you added. The questions are build questions; picture adding one more before you count.

## Subtopic: Doubling, Tripling and the Pattern That Explodes

Here is a pattern that starts small and gets out of hand. A rumour: on day one, two people know it. Each person tells two new people a day, so on day two, six people know — the original two and four more. Hmm, let us keep it simpler and say the number who know triples each day: two, six, eighteen, fifty-four. Look at the gaps: four, twelve, thirty-six — they are growing, and growing fast, so this is not an add-the-same pattern. Look at the ratios: each number is three times the one before. Multiply by three is the rule. Day five: one hundred and sixty-two. Day six: four hundred and eighty-six. Day ten: thirty-nine thousand three hundred and sixty-six. A whole town, from two people, in ten days.

Compare that with adding. Three, seven, eleven, fifteen adds four each time, and after ten terms it has only reached thirty-nine. The multiplying pattern passed it on day four and never looked back. Adding grows in a straight line; multiplying grows in a curve that bends upward, and the bend is what makes savings grow and rumours spread.

Multiplying can shrink too. A ball bounces to half its previous height: eighty centimetres, forty, twenty, ten, five, two comma five. Each bounce is half the last. The heights never quite reach zero, but they get tiny fast. The ratio is a half, and a ratio under one means the pattern dies away.

So you now have two tests to run on any pattern. Test one: are the gaps the same? If yes, it is an adding pattern, and the gap is the rule. Test two: if not, is each term the same multiple of the one before? If yes, it is a multiplying pattern, and the multiple is the rule. And if neither — like the cans and the tiles — look for a pattern in the gaps themselves.

The questions are rumour and bounce questions; run the two tests before you extend.

## Subtopic: Make Your Own — The Takeaway

The syllabus asks you to create your own patterns, and that is the best test of whether you understand them. Pick a starting number and a rule, and write five terms. Start at five, add six: five, eleven, seventeen, twenty-three, twenty-nine. Start at one, multiply by four: one, four, sixteen, sixty-four, two hundred and fifty-six. Start at two, and add one more each time than you added before: two, three, five, eight, twelve — gaps of one, two, three, four. Each of those is a legitimate pattern, and each would be investigated by the two tests you now own.

Now swap with a friend. Give them your five terms, not your rule, and see whether they can find it. They write the gaps. If the gaps are equal, done. If not, they try the ratios. If those are not equal either, they look at the gaps of the gaps. That is exactly what you will do in a test, with a pattern the examiner made up, so practising on each other's patterns is practising for the test.

Then draw one. Make a shape pattern — a row of hexagons, a staircase of blocks, a cross that grows an arm on each end — and count something in each shape. Write the sequence, write the gaps, and say in words what gets added each time and why the diagram makes it so. If you can explain the gap from the picture, you have understood the pattern; if you can only see it in the numbers, you have only half of it.

Here is the whole lesson in three moves. Write the gaps; equal gaps mean add. Not equal, try the ratios; equal ratios mean multiply. Neither, look at the gaps of the gaps, or at the picture, and describe the rule in words. Then extend, and put position and term in a table, because the table is what the next lesson turns into a formula.

You came in able to spot the next number when the gap was obvious, and you are leaving with a method that handles adding, multiplying and growing-gap patterns, a way of reading rules off a diagram, and your own patterns to test friends with — that is a real step up. The final questions mix all three kinds; write the gaps before you choose.
