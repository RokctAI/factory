### subtopic_1 — From Rule to Formula — The General Term
**Assistant:** Why is the common difference the coefficient of n in the general term?

**Tutor:** Because the coefficient of n is how much the expression changes each time n increases by one, and that is exactly what the common difference measures. If the general term is four n minus one, then moving from position n to position n plus one changes the term by four, since four bracket n plus one bracket minus one is four n plus three, which is four more than four n minus one. So a pattern that goes up by four every step must have four n in its formula. The constant does not affect the step; it only shifts every term by the same amount, which is why it is found separately from the first term. Learners who write n plus four for a pattern with difference four have put the difference in the wrong place, and the check with n equal to one and two exposes it immediately, since n plus four gives five and six, a difference of one.

### subtopic_2 — Using the Formula — Any Term, and Which Position
**Assistant:** What does it mean when solving for n gives a fraction?

**Tutor:** It means the number you were given is not a term of the pattern. Positions are counted — first, second, third — so n must be a natural number, and a general term only produces pattern values at those positions. Setting four n minus one equal to one hundred gives n equal to twenty-five and a quarter, and there is no twenty-five-and-a-quarter-th term, so one hundred does not appear in three, seven, eleven, fifteen and so on. You can confirm by checking the neighbours: the twenty-fifth term is ninety-nine and the twenty-sixth is one hundred and three, and one hundred falls between them. This is a standard examination question — is such-and-such a term of the pattern — and the method is always the same: set the general term equal to the number, solve, and answer yes with the position if n is a natural number, or no with the reason if it is not.

### subtopic_3 — Formulas for Other Patterns — Squares, Multiples and Ratios
**Assistant:** Why is the exponent in the general term of a constant-ratio pattern n minus one and not n?

**Tutor:** Because the first term has not yet been multiplied by the ratio, so its power of the ratio must be zero, and n minus one is zero when n is one. In two, six, eighteen, fifty-four the terms are two times three to the zero, two times three to the one, two times three to the two and two times three to the three, so the nth term is two times three to the power n minus one. Writing two times three to the power n would give six as the first term, which is wrong. The quickest check is to substitute n equal to one and insist on getting the first term; if you get the second term instead, the exponent is one too high. The same reasoning explains the constant-difference case: four n minus one works because at position one the n part contributes exactly one difference, and the constant fixes the first term. In both cases the first term is the anchor that fixes the formula.

### subtopic_6 — Finding the Constant — Where Does the Pattern Start?
**Assistant:** Is stepping back to position zero always a valid way to find the constant?

**Tutor:** Yes, for any constant-difference pattern, because the general term is a linear expression d n plus c and at n equal to zero it is just c. Position zero is not a real position in the pattern — positions start at one — but the formula does not know that, and extending the pattern backwards by one step gives the value the formula would produce there. For three, seven, eleven with difference four, one step back from three is negative one, so c is negative one and the general term is four n minus one. For a decreasing pattern such as twenty, seventeen, fourteen with difference negative three, stepping back means adding three, giving twenty-three. The method is equivalent to the rule constant equals first term minus difference, and both are equivalent to substituting n equal to one and solving for c. Use whichever you find most natural, but always finish by checking with two positions.
