### subtopic_1 — Describing a Rule in Words and in Symbols
**Assistant:** What is the difference between describing a rule and justifying it?

**Tutor:** Describing a rule states what it is — in words, one more than three times the number of squares, or in symbols, s equals three n plus one with the letters defined. Justifying a rule explains why it is true by linking each part of it to the diagram or situation that produced the pattern: three sticks per square because every square contributes a top, a bottom and a right-hand edge, and one more for the left-hand edge of the first square, which belongs to no square's count. A description can be checked against a few terms; a justification explains why it will hold for every term, which is why it is worth more. Examiners signal which they want with the words describe, write a rule, explain or justify, and a complete answer to a justify question has three parts: the rule in symbols, the definition of the letters, and the sentence that points each part of the symbols at the picture.

### subtopic_2 — Justifying a Rule from the Diagram
**Assistant:** Why does the chair rule have a constant of two and the matchstick rule a constant of one?

**Tutor:** Because the constant counts whatever is in the pattern once and does not grow with n, and that once-off part is different in the two situations. In the row of tables, the once-off part is the pair of end chairs: however long the row, it has exactly two ends, each with one chair, so the constant is two, and the two n counts the top and bottom chairs that every table contributes. In the row of matchstick squares, the once-off part is the single left-hand edge of the first square: every square contributes a top, a bottom and a right-hand edge, three n, but the first square's left edge is shared with nothing and is counted once, so the constant is one. In both cases the constant is also the value the formula gives at position zero — a row of no tables still has its two end chairs in the formula's imagination, and a row of no squares still has its one starting stick.

### subtopic_3 — Equivalent Rules — Same Relationship, Different Expression
**Assistant:** If two rules agree for the first three values of n, are they definitely equivalent?

**Tutor:** No. Agreement at a few values is strong evidence but not proof, because it is possible to write two different expressions that coincide at a handful of points and then diverge. For the rules you meet in Grade 8, which are linear in n, agreement at two values does in fact force equivalence, but you are not expected to know that, and the examiner wants the algebraic argument: expand each expression and simplify, and if both reduce to the same form — two n plus two and two bracket n plus one bracket both become two n plus two — they are equivalent for every n. The numerical check is still worth doing first, because it is quick and because a disagreement at any single value proves the rules are not equivalent, which saves you the algebra. The habit to build is numbers to be convinced, algebra to be sure.

### subtopic_6 — The Chessboard Border — Three Ways to Count
**Assistant:** How can three different ways of counting the border all be correct?

**Tutor:** Because each way counts the same set of dots, just in a different order, and the total of a fixed set of dots does not depend on the order you count them in. Four n minus four counts four full sides and then removes the four corners that were counted twice. Two n plus two bracket n minus two bracket counts the top and bottom rows in full and then the two columns without their corner dots. Four bracket n minus one bracket gives each side its starting corner and not its finishing corner, so every dot is counted exactly once. The three expressions look different, but expanding the brackets turns each of them into four n minus four, which is the algebraic way of saying they count the same thing. Only a rule that counts some dots twice or misses some — such as four n, which double-counts the corners — is wrong, and a single grid, three-by-three, exposes it.
