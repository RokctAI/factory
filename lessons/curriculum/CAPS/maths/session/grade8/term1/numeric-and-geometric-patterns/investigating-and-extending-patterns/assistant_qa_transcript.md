### subtopic_1 — Constant Difference — Adding the Same Amount Each Time
**Assistant:** Why write the differences before anything else when investigating a pattern?

**Tutor:** Because the differences are the quickest diagnostic, and they tell you immediately which kind of pattern you have or that you need another test. Equal differences mean a constant-difference, or linear, pattern, and the common difference is the rule for getting from one term to the next — three, seven, eleven, fifteen has differences of four, so the rule is add four and the next term is nineteen. If the differences are not equal you have learnt something too: the pattern is not linear, and you move to the ratios, and if those fail, to the differences of the differences. Writing them down also shows the marker your method, which earns the investigation mark even if a later step goes wrong. Keep the vocabulary straight while you do it: the terms are the numbers in the sequence, the differences are the gaps between consecutive terms, and the position says where a term sits.

### subtopic_2 — Constant Ratio — Multiplying by the Same Amount Each Time
**Assistant:** How can a multiplying pattern overtake an adding pattern that starts ahead of it?

**Tutor:** Because adding the same amount gives steady growth while multiplying by the same factor gives growth that accelerates, since each step is applied to an ever larger term. The pattern three, seven, eleven, fifteen gains four per term, so after ten terms it has gained thirty-six and sits at thirty-nine. The pattern two, six, eighteen, fifty-four triples each time; its gains are four, twelve, thirty-six and so on, each gain three times the previous one, and by the tenth term it has reached thirty-nine thousand three hundred and sixty-six. The adding pattern was ahead at the first term and was passed at the fourth. This is the difference between simple and compound growth, between a straight-line graph and one that curves upwards, and it is why savings with compound interest, populations and rumours all behave in the same explosive way. A ratio less than one reverses the effect and the terms shrink towards zero.

### subtopic_3 — Neither Difference nor Ratio — Patterns in the Differences
**Assistant:** What does a constant second difference tell me about a pattern?

**Tutor:** It tells you that the first differences themselves form a constant-difference pattern, so the sequence grows by amounts that increase steadily, and that the sequence is quadratic — related to squares. One, four, nine, sixteen has first differences three, five, seven, which increase by two each time, so the second differences are two, two, two. Recognising that lets you extend: the next first difference is nine, so the next term is twenty-five. The sequence is in fact the square numbers, where the term is the position squared, and the constant second difference of two is a fingerprint of that. The triangular numbers one, three, six, ten have first differences two, three, four and second differences one, one, one, so they are also quadratic, which is why the formula for them involves a position squared. In Grade 9 and Grade 10 you will use the second difference to find the formula directly; for now it is a reliable way to extend a pattern that neither adds nor multiplies.

### subtopic_5 — Build It — Matchsticks on the Desk
**Assistant:** Why is building one more shape better than just looking at the numbers?

**Tutor:** Because the shape explains the rule, and an explained rule can be trusted and extended without doubt. The numbers four, seven, ten tell you that three is added each time, but the diagram tells you why: a new square shares one side with the previous square, so only three new sticks are needed. That explanation guarantees the pattern will continue — the eleventh square will also share one side and also need three sticks — whereas a rule guessed from three numbers might fail at the fourth. The diagram also offers a shortcut: ten squares is one starting stick plus three sticks per square, thirty-one, which is the formula of the next lesson appearing before its time. Examiners ask you to explain the pattern from the diagram precisely because it separates understanding from number-spotting, and the explanation is usually worth a mark on its own.
