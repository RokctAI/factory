## Subtopic: Why Counting Decimal Places Works

Start with the simplest product: zero comma six times zero comma four. Write each as a fraction: six tenths times four tenths. The denominators multiplied — ten times ten is one hundred — and that is why the decimal places add.

Generalise. A number with m decimal places is a whole number over ten to the m. A number with n decimal places is a whole number over ten to the n. Their product is the product of the whole numbers over ten to the m plus n. So the product has m plus n decimal places. This is the rule: multiply the digits ignoring the commas, then place the comma so that the answer has as many decimal places as the factors have in total.

The zero after the comma is a placeholder that keeps the fifteen in the thousandths; leaving it out would give zero comma one five, ten times too big.

## Subtopic: Where Does the Comma Go? The Takeaway

Everything in this lesson comes down to one question: where does the comma go? And there are two ways to answer it, and they must agree. Way one is counting: add up the decimal places in the two numbers you multiplied, and the answer has that many. Way two is estimating: round each number to something friendly, multiply roughly, and the answer must be near that. If counting says two comma eight two and estimating says about three, you are done.
