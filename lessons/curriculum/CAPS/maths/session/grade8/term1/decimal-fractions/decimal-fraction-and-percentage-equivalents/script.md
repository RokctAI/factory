## Subtopic: Terminating or Recurring? Look at the Denominator

A fraction converts to a decimal by division, and the decimal either stops or repeats forever. Which happens is decided entirely by the denominator in simplest form. A decimal that terminates is a fraction over a power of ten, and ten is two times five, so a fraction terminates exactly when its simplified denominator has no prime factors other than two and five. Any other prime factor — three, seven, eleven — forces the decimal to recur.

One sixth: six is two times three, and the three spoils it: zero comma one six six six, recurring six. Two thirds: zero comma six recurring. Five twelfths: twelve is two squared times three, recurs: zero comma four one six recurring, with the dot over the six only.

Simplify before you decide. Six fifteenths has denominator fifteen, three times five, which looks like it should recur, but six fifteenths simplifies to two fifths, whose denominator is five, so it terminates at zero comma four. The rule applies to the simplest form.

Recurring decimals are written with a dot over the repeating digit, or a bar over a repeating block. Zero comma one six recurring has the dot on the six only, because the one does not repeat. One seventh is zero comma one four two eight five seven, all six digits recurring, written with a bar across the block. Why does this matter beyond neatness? Because it tells you which form to calculate in. A fraction that terminates can be used as a decimal with no loss; a fraction that recurs must be kept as a fraction for exact work and only turned into a rounded decimal at the very end.
