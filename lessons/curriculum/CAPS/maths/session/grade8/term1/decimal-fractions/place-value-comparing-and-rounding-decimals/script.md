## Subtopic: Place Value After the Comma

A decimal fraction is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten, written without the denominator. In South Africa we separate the whole part from the fraction part with a decimal comma, and we separate thousands with a space, never a comma. So three thousand four hundred and seven comma zero six five is written three, space, four zero seven, comma, zero six five.

Each position after the comma has a value one tenth of the position before it. Take the number three comma four zero seven. The three is three units. The four is in the first decimal place, the tenths: four tenths. The zero is in the second decimal place, the hundredths: zero hundredths. The seven is in the third decimal place, the thousandths: seven thousandths. So three comma four zero seven is three plus four tenths plus seven thousandths, which as a single fraction is three and four hundred and seven thousandths.

Read the name of the last place to find the denominator. Zero comma four is four tenths. Zero comma four zero is forty hundredths, the same number — a zero on the end of a decimal changes nothing. Zero comma zero four is four hundredths, a different and much smaller number — a zero between the comma and the digit changes everything. Learners who treat zero comma four and zero comma zero four as the same lose marks every year, so say the place name aloud: tenths, hundredths, thousandths.

Expanded notation is the examiner's way of checking this. Write twelve comma three zero eight in expanded form: ten plus two plus three tenths plus eight thousandths. Each term is a digit times a place value, and the zero hundredths term is simply omitted.

Finally, the value of a digit depends on its position, not its size. In zero comma five eight, the five is worth five tenths, which is more than the eight, worth eight hundredths. The digit furthest left after the comma always carries the most weight, and that fact is the whole secret of comparing decimals in the next section. Questions on place value follow now.

## Subtopic: Comparing Decimals — Pad, Then Read Left to Right

To compare two decimals, compare the whole parts first; if they are equal, compare the tenths; if those are equal, the hundredths; and so on, moving right. Zero comma eight against zero comma seven five. Whole parts equal. Tenths: eight against seven. Eight is bigger, so zero comma eight is bigger than zero comma seven five — even though seventy-five looks bigger than eight. A longer decimal is not a bigger decimal.

The safest technique is to pad with zeros so both numbers have the same number of decimal places, and then compare as if they were whole numbers. Zero comma eight becomes zero comma eight zero; now eighty hundredths against seventy-five hundredths, and eighty wins. Padding changes nothing, because trailing zeros add nothing, and it removes the illusion that more digits means more value.

## Subtopic: Rounding to a Given Number of Decimal Places

Rounding replaces a number with a nearby number that has fewer decimal places. To round to two decimal places, look at the third decimal place. If it is five or more, increase the second decimal place by one; if it is four or less, leave the second place as it is; then drop everything after the second place.

Three comma four five six seven to two decimal places. The third decimal digit is six, which is five or more, so the five becomes six: three comma four six. Twelve comma three four five to one decimal place: look at the second decimal digit, four, which is less than five, so twelve comma three. Zero comma zero four four nine to two decimal places: the third digit is four, so zero comma zero four. Only the digit immediately after the cut matters — the nine further along has no vote.

The anchor: seven comma nine nine five to two decimal places. The third decimal digit is five, so round up the second place. Nine plus one is ten, so write zero and carry: the first decimal place becomes nine plus one, ten again, write zero and carry; the units become eight. Seven comma nine nine five rounds to eight comma zero zero. Keep both zeros — they show that the answer is correct to two decimal places. Writing eight alone would be rounding to the nearest whole number, which is a different instruction.

Rounding to the nearest whole number, tenth, or thousandth follows the same rule at a different cut. Three comma four five six seven to the nearest whole number is three; to one decimal place is three comma five; to three decimal places is three comma four five seven.

Two conventions to state in an answer. First, use the approximately-equal sign, or write the words correct to two decimal places, so the marker knows you rounded deliberately. Second, round only at the end of a calculation, never in the middle; rounding intermediate values accumulates error and loses accuracy marks. Questions on rounding follow.

## Subtopic: Decimals and Fractions on the Number Line — Examination Technique

Every decimal is a common fraction with a power-of-ten denominator, and converting in both directions is assumed knowledge in this topic. Zero comma three seven five is three hundred and seventy-five thousandths, which simplifies to three eighths. Two comma zero three five is two and thirty-five thousandths, which is two and seven two-hundredths. Conversely, three fifths is six tenths, zero comma six; and seven twentieths is thirty-five hundredths, zero comma three five.

This conversion is what lets you place decimals on a number line. Between zero and one, mark tenths; zero comma three seven five lies between zero comma three and zero comma four, three quarters of the way along — because zero comma zero seven five is three quarters of a tenth. Between two and three, two comma zero three five lies just past two, a little more than a third of the way from two comma zero to two comma one. Number-line questions test whether you can read a scale whose divisions are tenths or hundredths, and the method is always the same: find the two marked values the number lies between, then the fraction of the gap.

Examination technique for this topic comes down to four habits. One: say the place name of every digit you compare or round — tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Two: pad to equal decimal places before comparing, and write the padded forms in your working so the marker sees the method. Three: when rounding, underline the deciding digit, apply the rule, and keep trailing zeros that show the required accuracy. Four: for ordering questions, give the answer in the original forms the question used, in the direction asked — ascending or descending — and check you have included every number.

A common examination question combines all of these: write the following in descending order, then round the largest to one decimal place. Pad, order, translate back, then round. Each step is a mark, and each is lost by skipping the step, not by failing it.

Distinction-level takeaway: a decimal is a fraction over a power of ten; the leftmost decimal digit carries the most weight; pad before you compare; round using the next digit only and keep the zeros that show accuracy. Questions on number lines and technique follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same decimals as money, petrol-pump readings and race times, where tenths, hundredths and thousandths are cents, millilitres and the difference between gold and silver. Place value stops being a column heading and becomes something you can hold.

## Subtopic: Cents, Millilitres and Hundredths of a Second

Let us make place value something you can hold. Money first. Twelve rand and thirty-five cents is written twelve comma three five. The three is thirty cents — three tenths of a rand. The five is five cents — five hundredths of a rand. So the first decimal place is tens of cents and the second is single cents. That is why prices always have exactly two decimal places: there is nothing smaller than a cent to put in the third place.

Now the petrol pump. It shows forty-two comma three zero five litres. The three is three tenths of a litre, three hundred millilitres. The zero means no extra hundredths. The five is five thousandths of a litre, five millilitres — about a teaspoon. The pump measures to the teaspoon, which is why it has three decimal places. Say each digit with its unit and the place value becomes obvious.

And the stopwatch. Two runners finish a hundred metres in eleven comma zero eight seconds and eleven comma eight seconds. Who won? It looks close — both have an eight — but eleven comma eight is eleven and eight tenths, while eleven comma zero eight is eleven and eight hundredths. Pad the shorter one: eleven comma eight zero. Eighty hundredths against eight hundredths. The runner on eleven comma zero eight was almost three quarters of a second faster. The zero after the comma pushed the eight into a much smaller place.

This is the trap in every comparing question: zero comma four looks like zero comma zero four, and eleven comma eight looks like eleven comma zero eight, but a zero right after the comma shoves everything to a place ten times smaller. Say the place: eight tenths versus eight hundredths. Tenths are bigger.

So: first place is tens of cents or hundreds of millilitres or tenths of a second; second place is cents or tens of millilitres or hundredths of a second; third place is teaspoons. The questions now are tills, pumps and stopwatches; say the unit of each digit before you compare.

## Subtopic: Rounding at the Till — The Takeaway

Rounding is something every shop does. Petrol comes to seven hundred and forty-three rand, nine nine five cents — the pump works in thousandths. The till cannot charge nine comma five cents, so it rounds to two decimal places. Look at the third decimal digit: five. Five rounds up. The second place is a nine; nine up one is ten, so it becomes zero and carries. The first place is also a nine; up one, ten, zero, carry. The units go from three to four. Seven hundred and forty-four comma zero zero. Keep the two zeros — they say the amount is in whole cents.

Now the easy direction. A cool drink is priced at twelve comma three four five rand per litre at some wholesaler; what is that to the nearest cent? Third digit five, round up: twelve comma three five. A packet of sweets at zero comma zero four four nine rand per gram — to two decimal places, the third digit is four, so zero comma zero four. The nine at the end does not get a say; only the digit right after the cut.

Why not look at all the digits? Because the question is which cent is nearest, and the digit immediately after the cut already decides whether you are above or below the halfway point. Zero comma zero four four nine is less than zero comma zero four five, so it is nearer zero comma zero four, however many nines follow.

One more: a test average works out to seven comma nine nine five. To two decimal places, that is the carry case again: eight comma zero zero. Learners write eight and lose a mark, because eight on its own says nearest whole number, not two decimal places. The zeros are not decoration; they report how accurately you rounded.

Here is the whole lesson in four lines. Say the place name of every digit: tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Pad to equal length before you compare, then order the decimal parts as whole numbers. To round, look only at the next digit; five or more goes up, and carries ripple leftward. Keep the trailing zeros that show your decimal places. You walked in reading decimals as strings of digits, and you are leaving reading them as amounts you could pay, pour or time — that is a real step up, and it is what multiplying and dividing decimals in the next lessons will build on. The final questions mix comparing and rounding; say the place name first.
