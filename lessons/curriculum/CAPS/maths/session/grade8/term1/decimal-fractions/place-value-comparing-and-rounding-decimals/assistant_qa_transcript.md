### subtopic_1 — Place Value After the Comma
**Assistant:** Why does a zero at the end of a decimal change nothing while a zero after the comma changes everything?

**Tutor:** Because of what each zero does to the positions of the other digits. A zero added at the end of a decimal occupies a new, smaller place and contributes zero of that place: zero comma four zero is four tenths and zero hundredths, still four tenths. Nothing else moved. A zero inserted directly after the comma pushes every following digit one place to the right, into a position worth one tenth as much: zero comma zero four puts the four in the hundredths, so it is worth four hundredths instead of four tenths, ten times smaller. The same thing happens in whole numbers — forty and four hundred differ because a zero shifted the four — but in whole numbers the zero goes on the right, so learners expect it; in decimals the dangerous zero goes on the left of the digits, which feels backwards. Saying the place name of the first non-zero digit, tenths or hundredths, settles it every time.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Decimals — Pad, Then Read Left to Right
**Assistant:** Is padding with zeros a trick, or is it mathematically sound?

**Tutor:** It is sound, because a trailing zero in a decimal adds zero of a smaller place and so leaves the value unchanged: zero comma eight and zero comma eight zero are the same number, eight tenths or eighty hundredths. Padding is simply rewriting every number in the list over the same denominator — thousandths, say — which is exactly what you do when you add or compare common fractions over a common denominator. Once all the numbers are in thousandths, the decimal parts can be compared as whole numbers of thousandths, and whole-number comparison is reliable. What padding prevents is the illusion that a longer string of digits is a bigger number. Three hundred and five thousandths is less than three hundred and fifty thousandths even though three zero five has as many digits as three five zero. In an examination, write the padded forms in your working; markers accept them as the method, and they make the comparison visible.

### subtopic_3 — Rounding to a Given Number of Decimal Places
**Assistant:** When rounding, why does only the digit immediately after the cut matter?

**Tutor:** Because that digit alone determines whether the number is below or at least halfway to the next value at the required accuracy. Rounding zero comma zero four four nine to two decimal places asks whether the number is nearer zero comma zero four or zero comma zero five, and the halfway mark is zero comma zero four five. The third decimal digit is four, so the number is below zero comma zero four five regardless of what follows — the nine in the fourth place cannot lift it past the halfway point, because even zero comma zero four four nine nine nine is still less than zero comma zero four five. Learners who round in stages, first to three places and then to two, can get this wrong: zero comma zero four four nine rounds to zero comma zero four five at three places and then wrongly to zero comma zero five at two. Round once, from the original number, using the next digit only.

### subtopic_7 — Rounding at the Till — The Takeaway
**Assistant:** Why must I keep the zeros in 8,00 after rounding 7,995?

**Tutor:** Because the zeros report the accuracy of the answer, and the question specified that accuracy. Writing eight says the value is correct to the nearest whole number, which could mean anything from seven comma five to eight comma five. Writing eight comma zero zero says the value is correct to two decimal places, so the true value lies between seven comma nine nine five and eight comma zero zero five — a much stronger statement, and the one the question asked for. In money the zeros are obviously necessary: a till shows seven hundred and forty-four rand zero zero cents, not seven hundred and forty-four with the cents missing. In science and later mathematics these zeros are called significant figures, and dropping them is treated as a loss of information. In a Grade 8 examination, the mark for rounding to two decimal places is awarded for an answer with two decimal places, so eight alone loses it even though its value is right.
