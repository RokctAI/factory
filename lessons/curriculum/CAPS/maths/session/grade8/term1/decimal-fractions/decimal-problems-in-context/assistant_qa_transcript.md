### subtopic_1 — Money — Totals, Change and Budgets
**Assistant:** Why write the budget as one bracketed expression instead of working step by step?

**Tutor:** Because the expression records the structure of the problem, and the structure is what the marker is checking before any arithmetic. Five hundred minus bracket three times forty-five comma five zero plus one hundred and twenty-three comma eight zero bracket says, in one line, that three identical items form a product, that the product and the single item form a total, and that the total is taken from the budget. The brackets force the right order of operations, and the expression can be estimated as a whole before anything is calculated. Working step by step gives the same answer when every step is right, but it hides the structure, and a wrong step — subtracting the shirts before adding the shoes, say — is harder to spot. In examinations the expression line usually carries a method mark on its own, and it protects the final answer because the estimate about two hundred and forty can be compared with it directly.

### subtopic_2 — Perimeter and Area with Decimal Dimensions
**Assistant:** How do I keep perimeter and area apart in a problem that asks for both?

**Tutor:** By their units and their formulas, written down before substituting. Perimeter is a length — the distance around the shape — so it is found by adding the sides, two times bracket length plus breadth bracket for a rectangle, and it is measured in metres. Area is a covering — how much surface the shape has — so it is found by multiplying length by breadth and is measured in square metres. In the vegetable-bed problem the fence follows the perimeter, ten comma one metres, and the compost covers the area, five comma eight five square metres; the estimate for each is different, about ten and about six, which is a further check that the two have not been swapped. Writing the unit with every answer is not decoration: a fencing cost calculated from square metres or a compost cost from metres is wrong, and the unit is what reveals it.

### subtopic_3 — Averages, Speed and Fuel Consumption
**Assistant:** Why must an average lie between the smallest and largest values?

**Tutor:** Because an average is the value every item would have if the total were shared out equally, and sharing equally cannot give each item more than the biggest item or less than the smallest. If all four race times were replaced by twelve comma four seconds, the total would still be forty-nine comma six, so twelve comma four is the fair share; it must be at least the fastest time, eleven comma eight, and at most the slowest, twelve comma nine. This gives a check that costs nothing. An average of forty-nine comma six seconds would mean the division by four was forgotten; an average of three comma one would mean the total was divided by sixteen or the commas were misaligned when adding. In general, if your average falls outside the range of the data, the error is in the adding or the dividing, and you look there first.

### subtopic_7 — Saturday Evening — The Race, and the Takeaway
**Assistant:** Why is 0,75 of an hour forty-five minutes and not seventy-five minutes?

**Tutor:** Because the decimal part of a measurement is a fraction of the unit, and an hour has sixty minutes, not one hundred. Two comma seven five hours means two whole hours plus seventy-five hundredths of an hour, and seventy-five hundredths of sixty minutes is forty-five minutes. Reading the digits after the comma as minutes treats an hour as if it had one hundred minutes, which is the same error as reading two comma seven five metres as two metres and seventy-five millimetres. The conversion rule is to multiply the decimal part by the number of smaller units in one larger unit: times sixty for hours to minutes and minutes to seconds, times one hundred for metres to centimetres, times one thousand for kilograms to grams. Going the other way, forty-five minutes is forty-five divided by sixty, zero comma seven five of an hour. Speed and time questions test this conversion constantly.
