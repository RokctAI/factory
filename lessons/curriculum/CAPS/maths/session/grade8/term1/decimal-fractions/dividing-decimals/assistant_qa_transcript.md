### subtopic_1 — Make the Divisor a Whole Number
**Assistant:** Why am I allowed to multiply both numbers in a division by ten without changing the answer?

**Tutor:** Because a division is a fraction, and a fraction's value depends only on the ratio of its numerator to its denominator. Four comma eight divided by zero comma six is the fraction four comma eight over zero comma six; multiplying the top and the bottom by ten gives forty-eight over six, which is the same fraction in a different form, just as two thirds and four sixths are the same fraction. Multiplying top and bottom by the same number is multiplying by one. In the language of the division itself, you have changed the unit you count in — from units to tenths — and the number of times six tenths fits into forty-eight tenths is the same as the number of times zero comma six fits into four comma eight. What you may not do is scale only one of the numbers, because that multiplies the fraction by ten rather than by one and gives an answer ten times too large or too small.

### subtopic_2 — The Anchor and Dividing by Whole Numbers
**Assistant:** Why is the comma in the quotient placed above the comma in the dividend, rather than by counting places?

**Tutor:** Because once the divisor is a whole number, dividing is a place-by-place process that respects the place values of the dividend. When you divide seventy-three comma five by five, you first find how many fives fit into the seventy-three units, which is fourteen units, and only then move into the tenths, where five fits into thirty-five tenths seven times. The quotient digits therefore have the same place values as the dividend digits they were produced from, which is why the comma sits directly above. Counting decimal places is a rule for multiplication, where the denominators multiply and the places add; in division the places would subtract, and that can even go negative, which is why the scaling method is taught instead. If you ever doubt the position of the quotient's comma, the inverse check settles it: fourteen comma seven times zero comma five is seven comma three five, the original dividend.

### subtopic_3 — Dividing by Powers of Ten and Why Small Divisors Give Big Answers
**Assistant:** How can dividing make a number bigger when division is supposed to share things out?

**Tutor:** Sharing is only one meaning of division, and it applies when the divisor is a whole number of people or groups. The other meaning is grouping: how many pieces of a given size fit into the amount. When the divisor is a decimal between zero and one, grouping is the meaning that makes sense, and small pieces fit many times. Seven comma three five divided by zero comma five asks how many halves there are in seven comma three five, and there are fourteen comma seven of them — more than seven comma three five, because every unit contains two halves. Dividing by zero comma one is the extreme case: there are ten tenths in every unit, so dividing by zero comma one multiplies by ten. The reverse holds when the divisor is greater than one, where sharing shrinks the result. Asking which way the answer should go before dividing is the quickest guard against scaling only one of the two numbers.

### subtopic_6 — The Unit Price and the Petrol Bill
**Assistant:** In the juice comparison, why is the bottle with the higher price the better buy?

**Tutor:** Because the right comparison is the price per litre, not the price per bottle, and finding a price per litre is a decimal division. Brand A gives zero comma seven five litres for twelve rand, so its unit price is twelve divided by zero comma seven five; scaling both by one hundred gives one thousand two hundred divided by seventy-five, sixteen rand per litre. Brand B gives one comma two litres for eighteen rand; scaling both by ten gives one hundred and eighty divided by twelve, fifteen rand per litre. The bottle that costs six rand more contains sixty percent more juice, and per litre it is a rand cheaper. The direction checks also make sense here: dividing twelve by a number less than one gave an answer above twelve, and dividing eighteen by a number above one gave an answer below eighteen. Unit pricing is the most practical use of decimal division, and supermarkets print it on the shelf label for exactly this reason.
