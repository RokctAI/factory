# Part 1 — Expert

This session works through the financial contexts of the whole-numbers topic: profit and loss, discount, VAT, budgets, simple interest, hire purchase and exchange rates. Two anchors carry the arithmetic — a cooldrink bought for R80 a case and sold for R100, and a R2 000 loan at eight percent per annum simple interest for three years. Every quantity is defined precisely, every calculation laid out in the form the memorandum credits.

## Subtopic: Profit, Loss and Percentage Profit

Definitions first, because every financial question is marked on the definitions. The cost price is what the seller pays for an item. The selling price is what the seller charges. If the selling price exceeds the cost price, the difference is PROFIT; if the cost price exceeds the selling price, the difference is LOSS. Profit equals selling price minus cost price.

The spaza anchor. A case of cooldrink is bought for R80 and sold for R100. Profit: R100 minus R80 is R20. Now the quantity the examiner really wants: percentage profit. Percentage profit is the profit expressed as a percentage of the COST price — always the cost price, because profit is measured against what the seller invested. Twenty over eighty, times one hundred: twenty eightieths is a quarter, and a quarter is 25 percent. The shop made 25 percent profit on the cooldrink.

Watch the denominator. A learner who divides by the selling price gets twenty over one hundred, 20 percent, and loses the marks. Profit percent is on cost. Write the formula line every time: percentage profit equals profit over cost price, times one hundred.

Loss runs the same way. A phone bought for R2 500 and sold second-hand for R2 000 makes a loss of R500. Percentage loss: five hundred over two thousand five hundred, times one hundred, which is 20 percent. Again on the cost price.

Working forwards from a target: a shop wants 30 percent profit on an item costing R150. Profit is 30 percent of R150, which is R45, so the selling price is R150 plus R45, R195. Equivalently, the selling price is 130 percent of the cost: one point three times R150 is R195. The single multiplier one point three is the professional shortcut, and you will use it constantly from here to matric.

Pause here for this section's questions: profit from the two prices, the percentage on the right denominator, and the target selling price.

## Subtopic: Discount and VAT

A discount is a reduction in the marked price, usually stated as a percentage of the marked price. A jacket marked at R450 is offered at 20 percent off. The discount is 20 percent of R450: twenty hundredths times 450 is R90. The sale price is R450 minus R90, which is R360. Equivalently, paying 80 percent of the marked price: nought comma eight times 450 is R360. Both routes are acceptable; the multiplier route is faster.

Now VAT. Value-added tax in South Africa is 15 percent, added to the price of most goods and services. A price EXCLUDING VAT is the price before the tax; a price INCLUDING VAT is the price after. Take a pair of school shoes priced at R460 excluding VAT. VAT is 15 percent of R460: fifteen hundredths times 460 is R69. The price including VAT is R460 plus R69, which is R529. The multiplier version: 115 percent of R460 is one comma one five times 460, which is R529.

The direction that catches everyone: finding the VAT inside a price that already includes it. A till slip shows R529 including VAT. The VAT is NOT 15 percent of R529. The R529 is 115 percent of the original price, so the original price is 529 divided by one comma one five, which is R460, and the VAT is the difference, R69. The rule: to remove VAT, divide by one comma one five; never take 15 percent of the inclusive price. Fifteen percent of R529 would give R79,35, which is wrong, and the examiner sets this trap deliberately.

Discount and VAT together, in the order a shop applies them: discount first on the marked price, then VAT on the discounted amount. A R450 jacket at 20 percent off is R360; if that price excludes VAT, the till adds 15 percent: one comma one five times 360 is R414.

Here come this section's questions: the discount two ways, VAT added, and VAT removed by division.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest, Loans and Hire Purchase

Interest is the price of borrowing money, or the reward for lending it. Simple interest is calculated on the original amount only, every year, at a fixed rate. The formula: simple interest equals P times i times n — the principal, the rate per annum as a decimal, and the number of years. The total amount owed or saved at the end is A equals P plus the interest.

The loan anchor. R2 000 is borrowed at 8 percent per annum simple interest for three years. Formula line: I equals P i n. Data line: P is 2 000, i is eight over one hundred which is nought comma nought eight, n is three. Substitution: 2 000 times nought comma nought eight times three. Compute: 2 000 times nought comma nought eight is 160, the interest for one year; times three years is R480. Total repaid: R2 000 plus R480, which is R2 480.

The rate must be a decimal. Eight percent enters as nought comma nought eight, never as eight; substituting eight inflates the interest a hundredfold. Convert the rate the moment you read it.

Hire purchase is how most furniture and appliances are bought in South Africa: a deposit, then the balance plus simple interest, paid in equal monthly instalments. A fridge is priced at R6 000 on hire purchase with a 10 percent deposit, the balance charged at 12 percent per annum simple interest over two years. Deposit: 10 percent of R6 000 is R600. Balance: R5 400. Interest on the balance: 5 400 times nought comma one two times two is R1 296. Total still to pay: R5 400 plus R1 296, which is R6 696. Monthly instalment over 24 months: 6 696 divided by 24 is R279. And the real cost of the fridge: R600 deposit plus R6 696, which is R7 296 — R1 296 more than the cash price. That final comparison is often the last mark of the question, and it is the point of the whole topic: credit has a price, and you can calculate it.

An account or loan statement reads the same way: opening balance, charges and interest added, payments subtracted, closing balance. Read it line by line and the arithmetic is Term 1 arithmetic.

Pause for this section's questions: the formula with a decimal rate, the total repaid, and the hire-purchase chain.

## Subtopic: Budgets, Exchange Rates and the Full Method

A budget is a plan that sets income against expenses. Income is money coming in; expenses are money going out; the difference is the surplus if positive and the shortfall if negative. A learner earns R600 a month from a weekend job. Expenses: R180 transport, R150 airtime and data, R200 saving for a phone. Total expenses: R530. Surplus: R600 minus R530 is R70. A budget question is marked on the totals and on the correct label — surplus or shortfall. If expenses rose to R650, the shortfall would be R50, and the budget would need an adjustment.

Exchange rates convert one currency to another, and the conversion is a rate — rand per dollar. Suppose one US dollar is R18. To convert dollars to rand, multiply by eighteen: a fifty-dollar game costs 50 times 18, which is R900. To convert rand to dollars, divide by eighteen: R1 800 is 1 800 divided by 18, which is 100 dollars. The direction rule: going INTO rand, multiply; going OUT of rand, divide — because one dollar is worth many rand, so a rand amount must be the bigger number. If your dollar answer came out larger than your rand amount, you multiplied where you should have divided.

The method, compressed for the examination. One: identify the quantity — profit, discount, VAT, interest, instalment, surplus, conversion. Two: write the formula or rule in words or symbols. Three: convert every percentage to a decimal. Four: substitute visibly and calculate. Five: state the answer in rand with the correct label, to two decimal places where cents arise. Six: check the direction — a discount lowers the price, VAT raises it, interest increases what is owed, an increase in a rand amount is a larger number.

The error museum. Exhibit one: percentage profit on the selling price. Exhibit two: VAT removed by taking 15 percent of the inclusive price. Exhibit three: the rate substituted as eight instead of nought comma nought eight. Exhibit four: dividing rand by the rate when converting to rand. Every exhibit is a direction or a denominator error, and every one is caught by asking whether the answer makes sense.

The distinction-level takeaway: every financial calculation here is one of two moves — a percentage of an amount, or a multiplier applied to an amount. Master the multiplier, one comma one five for VAT, nought comma eight for a 20 percent discount, one comma three for a 30 percent mark-up, and Grade 9's compound interest and Grade 10's growth formulae become the same move repeated.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the budget line, the conversion direction, and the exhibits.

## Subtopic: Counting the Profit at the Spaza Counter

Picture the spaza shop. The owner drives to the wholesaler and buys a case of cooldrink for R80. That is her cost price — what it cost HER. Back at the shop she sells the whole case, bottle by bottle, and the money in the tin comes to R100. That is her selling price — what the customers paid. Take away what she paid from what she got: R100 minus R80 leaves R20 in her hand. That R20 is profit. If the tin had held only R70, she would have been R10 down — that is a loss.

Now the question that trips people: how good a profit is R20? It depends what she put in. She put in R80, and she got R20 back on top of it. Twenty out of eighty is a quarter — one rand back for every four she spent. A quarter is 25 percent. So she made 25 percent profit, and that 25 percent is measured against the R80 she risked, because that is her money on the line.

Why not measure it against the R100? Because the R100 includes the profit itself — measuring profit against a number that already contains the profit is like measuring how much you grew by using your new height. Always compare the profit with the cost price, the money that went out first.

Turn it around. Suppose she decides she wants 30 percent profit on a packet of sweets that costs her R150 a box. Thirty percent of R150: ten percent is R15, so thirty percent is R45. She must sell the box for R150 plus R45, R195. Or in one move: she wants 130 percent of what she paid, and one comma three times 150 is 195. Same R195, one multiplication.

That one-move multiplier is the trick of the whole lesson, so look at it closely. One hundred percent is the thing itself. Adding 30 percent gives 130 percent, which is one comma three as a decimal. Multiply by one comma three and the mark-up is already included.

The questions for this section are with you now: find the profit, measure it against the cost, and build the selling price from a target percentage.

## Subtopic: Sale Prices and the Tax on the Till Slip

Two things happen to a price in a shop. A sale takes something OFF, and VAT puts something ON. Both are percentages, and both are easier than they look.

The sale first. A jacket is marked R450 and the sign says 20 percent off. Ten percent of 450 is 45, so twenty percent is 90. You save R90 and pay R450 minus R90, which is R360. Or think of it this way: if 20 percent comes off, you are paying the other 80 percent, and 80 percent of 450 is nought comma eight times 450, R360. Same answer, one multiplication.

Now VAT. In South Africa the government adds 15 percent to most things you buy — that is value-added tax, and it is already inside the price on the shelf. Say school shoes cost R460 BEFORE VAT. Ten percent of 460 is 46; five percent is half of that, 23; so fifteen percent is 69. The VAT is R69 and the shelf price is R460 plus R69, R529. One move: you pay 115 percent, and one comma one five times 460 is 529.

Here is the one that fools almost everyone, including adults. Your till slip says R529 including VAT, and you want to know how much of that was tax. The tempting move is to take 15 percent of R529. Do not. The R529 is already the bigger number — it is 115 percent of the real price — so to get back to the real price you divide by one comma one five: 529 divided by one comma one five is 460. The tax was the gap, R69. Taking 15 percent of 529 would give you R79,35, which is too much, because you would be taxing the tax.

Think of it as a staircase. Going UP to the shelf price, you multiply by one comma one five. Coming back DOWN to the price without tax, you divide by one comma one five. Never come down by subtracting 15 percent — that is a different staircase.

If a shop does both, the order is: sale first, then VAT on what is left. The R450 jacket at 20 percent off is R360; add VAT and it is one comma one five times 360, R414.

Well done — you can now read a sale sign and a till slip better than most of the people in the queue. The questions for this section are with you now: take the discount off, put VAT on, and come back down the staircase by dividing.

## Subtopic: What the Fridge Really Costs

Here is the money lesson that matters most in this whole topic. A family wants a fridge. The cash price is R6 000, but they do not have R6 000, so the shop offers hire purchase: pay a deposit now, and pay the rest off monthly, with interest added. Let us find out what the fridge really costs them.

Step one, the deposit. The shop asks for 10 percent up front. Ten percent of R6 000 is R600. They pay R600 and still owe R5 400.

Step two, the interest. The shop charges 12 percent per year on that R5 400, simple interest, for two years. Simple interest means the same charge every year, worked out on the original R5 400. Twelve percent of 5 400: ten percent is 540, two percent is 108, together 648 a year. Two years: R1 296. That is the formula P times i times n without the letters — 5 400 times nought comma one two times two.

Step three, the instalments. They owe the R5 400 plus the R1 296 interest: R6 696. Spread over 24 months: 6 696 divided by 24 is R279 a month. That sounds manageable — which is exactly why hire purchase sells.

Step four, the truth. Add it all up: R600 deposit plus 24 payments of R279, which is R6 696, giving R7 296. The R6 000 fridge cost R7 296. The extra R1 296 is the price of not having the cash today. Not wrong, not a trick — but a price, and now you can see it.

The same arithmetic runs any simple-interest loan. Borrow R2 000 at 8 percent for three years: eight percent of 2 000 is 160 a year, three years is R480, pay back R2 480. The only rule to respect is that eight percent goes into the sum as nought comma nought eight — eight hundredths — never as eight.

And one more everyday conversion: if one dollar costs R18, a fifty-dollar game costs 50 times 18, R900. Going into rand makes the number bigger, because rand are smaller than dollars; coming out of rand, divide. R1 800 is 100 dollars.

You have just priced a fridge, a loan and a game in two currencies — that is real adult maths, done. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: the deposit, the interest for one year then two, the instalment, and the true cost.
