# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers ratio, rate, sharing in a given ratio, and increasing or decreasing a number in a given ratio. The anchor problem shares R1 800 in the ratio four to five; the method — total the parts, find one part, multiply out — then extends to three-part ratios, to scaling a number up or down by a ratio, and to the distinction between a ratio and a rate. Every step is laid out the way the memorandum awards it.

## Subtopic: Ratio — Comparing Parts of the Same Kind

A ratio compares two or more quantities of the SAME kind, written with a colon. A class with twelve boys and eighteen girls has a boys-to-girls ratio of twelve to eighteen. Order matters: twelve to eighteen is not eighteen to twelve, and the examiner reads the order from the wording of the question — the quantity named first is written first.

Ratios simplify exactly like fractions: divide every term by the same number. Twelve to eighteen, divide both by six: two to three. In simplest form the terms share no common factor other than one — the HCF from last lesson tells you what to divide by. Two to three is the simplest form, and it says that for every two boys there are three girls. A ratio carries no units: twelve boys to eighteen girls, twelve rand to eighteen rand, twelve metres to eighteen metres — all simplify to two to three, and the units vanish because they were the same on both sides.

Two technical points that earn or lose marks. First, before comparing, the quantities must be in the SAME units. Fifty centimetres to two metres is not fifty to two; convert two metres to two hundred centimetres, so the ratio is fifty to two hundred, which simplifies to one to four. Second, a ratio can also be written as a fraction of the whole. In the ratio two to three there are five parts altogether, so boys are two fifths of the class and girls are three fifths. Ratio of part to part; fraction of part to whole — know which one the question wants.

Equivalent ratios are produced by multiplying every term by the same number: two to three, four to six, six to nine, twelve to eighteen are all the same comparison. A recipe scales this way: if two cups of flour need three cups of water, then six cups of flour need nine cups of water. Multiply both terms by three.

The questions for this section are with you now: simplest form, matching units, and the difference between part-to-part and part-to-whole.

## Subtopic: Sharing in a Given Ratio — The Parts Method

The anchor. Share R1 800 between two people in the ratio four to five. The method has three lines, and each line carries a mark.

Line one: total the parts. Four plus five is nine parts. Line two: find the value of one part. R1 800 divided by nine is R200 per part. Line three: multiply out. The first person receives four parts: four times R200 is R800. The second receives five parts: five times R200 is R1 000.

Line four is the check, and a distinction learner never skips it: R800 plus R1 000 is R1 800, the original amount. If the shares do not add back to the total, a part was miscounted or a division slipped.

The method extends to three or more parts without change. Share sixty sweets among three children in the ratio one to two to three. Total parts: six. One part: sixty divided by six is ten. Shares: ten, twenty and thirty. Check: ten plus twenty plus thirty is sixty.

Two reading errors the examiner sets traps for. First, the order of the names must match the order of the ratio: if the question says Sipho and Lerato share in the ratio four to five, Sipho takes the four parts. Second, when a question gives the DIFFERENCE instead of the total — Lerato receives R200 more than Sipho — the difference corresponds to the difference in parts, five minus four, which is one part. So one part is R200 and the shares follow: R800 and R1 000. Read whether you are given the whole or the gap before you divide.

A note on the form of the answer: state each share with its unit and its owner. R800 and R1 000 alone is not a complete answer; Sipho receives R800 and Lerato receives R1 000 is.

Pause for this section's questions now: total the parts, find one part, multiply out, and check the sum.

## Subtopic: Increasing and Decreasing a Number in a Given Ratio

A ratio can also act as a scale. To increase or decrease a number in a given ratio, multiply the number by the fraction formed from the ratio — new over old.

Increase 240 in the ratio five to three. The new value relates to the old as five to three, so the multiplier is five over three. Two hundred and forty times five over three: divide 240 by three to get 80, then multiply by five: 400. The number increased from 240 to 400. Check the direction: five over three is greater than one, so the result must be larger — it is.

Decrease 240 in the ratio three to five. Multiplier three over five: 240 divided by five is 48, times three is 144. Decreased from 240 to 144. Three over five is less than one, so the result must be smaller — it is.

The structural rule: an INCREASE uses a fraction greater than one, with the larger number on top; a DECREASE uses a fraction less than one, with the smaller number on top. If you are asked to increase and your fraction is less than one, you have inverted it. Reading the direction of the result against the word in the question is a free check.

The same idea scales recipes and maps. A recipe for four people is scaled to ten people in the ratio ten to four, which simplifies to five to two: every ingredient is multiplied by five over two. Two hundred grams of rice becomes five hundred grams. A photograph enlarged in the ratio three to two has every length multiplied by three over two; a reduction in the ratio two to three multiplies every length by two over three. You will meet exactly this in Term 4 under enlargements and reductions, where the multiplier is called the scale factor.

A unitary variation: a price goes from R150 to R180. In what ratio was it increased? Write new to old, one hundred and eighty to one hundred and fifty, and simplify by dividing by thirty: six to five. The price was increased in the ratio six to five. Always write the ratio in the order the question names — new to old for an increase — and simplify.

Here come this section's questions: build the fraction from the ratio, check its direction, and find the ratio from two values.

## Subtopic: Rate — Comparing Different Kinds, and the Full Method

A rate compares quantities of DIFFERENT kinds, and because the kinds differ, a rate keeps its units. A taxi travels 240 kilometres in three hours: the rate is 240 divided by three, which is 80 kilometres per hour. Petrol costs R45 for five litres... no — read carefully — R45 for two litres would be a different rate; take R45 for five litres: R9 per litre. The word per signals a rate: kilometres per hour, rand per litre, words per minute. The unit after per is the one you divide BY.

Rates are compared by reducing to a unit rate — the amount per one. Shop A sells six eggs for R21; Shop B sells eighteen eggs for R54. Shop A: R21 divided by six is R3,50 per egg. Shop B: R54 divided by eighteen is R3 per egg. Shop B is cheaper per egg, even though its box costs more. Unit rates make unequal packs comparable, and a question that asks which is the better buy is a unit-rate question.

A rate also predicts. At 80 kilometres per hour, five hours covers 80 times five, 400 kilometres. At R9 per litre, 30 litres cost R270. Multiply the rate by the quantity, and the units multiply too: kilometres per hour times hours gives kilometres.

The method, compressed. For ratio: same kind, same units, simplest form, order as named. For sharing: total the parts, one part, multiply out, check the sum. For scaling: multiply by new over old, check the direction. For rate: different kinds, divide to the unit rate, keep the units, compare or predict.

The error museum. Exhibit one: dividing R1 800 by the number of people instead of the number of parts — two people, R900 each — which ignores the ratio entirely. Exhibit two: writing a ratio with mixed units, fifty to two for fifty centimetres to two metres. Exhibit three: inverting the scaling fraction, so an increase shrinks the number. Exhibit four: dropping the units from a rate, so 80 means nothing — 80 kilometres per hour is the answer.

The distinction-level takeaway: a ratio is a comparison with no units; a rate is a comparison with units attached. In Grade 9 you will formalise direct proportion — rate as a constant — and in Grade 10 the gradient of a straight line is a rate. The parts method and the unit rate carry straight through.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: unit rates, the better buy, and the exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same R1 800 now, split on a kitchen table with real notes, and the same ratios read as recipes and taxi fares. Nothing changes about the answers — R800 and R1 000 — but this time you will see the parts before you see the method.

## Subtopic: Splitting R1 800 on the Kitchen Table

Two cousins did a weekend job together and were paid R1 800 in cash. One worked four days, the other worked five. Splitting it down the middle would not be fair — so they agree to share in the ratio four to five. How do you actually do that with notes on a kitchen table?

Here is the slow, honest way. Think of the money as a pile of equal stacks. How many stacks? One cousin gets four, the other gets five — so nine stacks altogether. Now divide the R1 800 into nine equal stacks. R1 800 divided by nine is R200 a stack. Push four stacks to the first cousin: four times R200 is R800. Push five stacks to the second: five times R200 is R1 000. Count what is left on the table: nothing. R800 plus R1 000 is R1 800. Fair, and nobody argues.

That is the whole method, and it never changes: count the stacks, find the value of one stack, hand out the stacks. Total the parts, one part, multiply out. Check that it all adds back.

Three children, sixty sweets, ratio one to two to three — because the oldest did most of the chores. Stacks: one plus two plus three is six. Each stack: sixty divided by six is ten sweets. Hand out: ten, twenty, thirty. Count: sixty. Done.

Watch the one slip everybody makes at least once. Two cousins, so people divide R1 800 by two and get R900 each. But the ratio was never two; it was four to five, nine stacks. Divide by the number of STACKS, never by the number of PEOPLE.

And read the names in order. If the question says Sipho and Lerato share in the ratio four to five, Sipho is the four. Swap them and both answers are wrong even though the numbers are right.

Your questions for this section are coming now: count the stacks, value a stack, hand them out, and count the table.

## Subtopic: Scaling a Recipe Up and Down

A ratio is also a recipe for making something bigger or smaller. Say a recipe feeds three people and you have five coming. You want to scale everything up in the ratio five to three — five people instead of three.

Take 240 grams of rice. The honest way: find out how much rice ONE person needs, then multiply by the new number of people. For three people, 240 grams; so one person needs 240 divided by three, which is 80 grams. Five people need five times 80, which is 400 grams. You went from 240 to 400. That is increasing 240 in the ratio five to three.

Now the other way. The recipe feeds five and only three are coming. Scale down in the ratio three to five. One person: 240 divided by five is 48 grams. Three people: three times 48 is 144 grams. You went from 240 down to 144. That is decreasing 240 in the ratio three to five.

Look at what the two calculations really did. The first multiplied 240 by five over three — divide by three, multiply by five. The second multiplied 240 by three over five — divide by five, multiply by three. New number on top, old number underneath, and the fraction does the scaling.

A quick check that catches the most common slip. If you are making MORE, the answer must be bigger than you started with, so the fraction you multiply by must be bigger than one — larger number on top. If you are making LESS, the fraction must be smaller than one. If you were told to increase and your answer shrank, you turned the fraction upside down. Just flip it.

One more everyday version. The price of a loaf went from R15 to R18. In what ratio did it increase? New to old: eighteen to fifteen. Both divide by three: six to five. The price went up in the ratio six to five — for every five rand it used to cost, it now costs six.

Well done — you have scaled a recipe both ways and read a price rise as a ratio. The questions for this section are with you now: find one person's share, multiply up or down, and check the direction.

## Subtopic: Taxi Fares and the Better Buy

Last idea, and it is the one that saves you money for the rest of your life: the difference between a ratio and a rate.

A ratio compares two things of the same kind — rand to rand, boys to girls — and the units cancel, so a ratio is just numbers: four to five. A rate compares two DIFFERENT things — kilometres and hours, rand and litres — and because they are different, the units stay. A taxi that covers 240 kilometres in three hours is doing 240 divided by three, which is 80 kilometres per hour. That little word per is the sign of a rate, and the thing after per is what you divided by.

The most useful rate is the rate for ONE — the unit rate. Here is why. Shop A sells six eggs for R21. Shop B sells eighteen eggs for R54. Which is the better buy? You cannot compare R21 with R54 directly, because the boxes are different sizes. So bring both down to one egg. Shop A: R21 divided by six is R3,50 an egg. Shop B: R54 divided by eighteen is R3 an egg. Shop B is cheaper, even though its box costs more. Price per one is the only fair comparison, and the shops know most people will not do it.

Rates also tell you what happens next. If the taxi keeps up 80 kilometres per hour for five hours, it covers five times 80, which is 400 kilometres. If petrol is R9 a litre and you put in thirty litres, that is thirty times R9, R270. Rate times amount gives the total.

And keep the units on. If somebody asks the taxi's speed and you say eighty, eighty what? Eighty kilometres per hour is the answer; eighty is a number with no meaning. Ratios lose their units; rates keep them. That one sentence is the whole difference.

You now have the three moves of this lesson: share by stacks, scale by a fraction, compare by the rate for one. Every one of them is a kitchen-table skill with an examination name. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: find the unit rate, pick the better buy, and keep the units on.
