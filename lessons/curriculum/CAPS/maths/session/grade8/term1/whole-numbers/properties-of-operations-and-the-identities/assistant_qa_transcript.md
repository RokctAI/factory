### subtopic_1 — Commutative and Associative — The Laws of Order and Grouping
**Assistant:** Why do we bother naming the laws when the answer is right anyway?

**Tutor:** Because the examiner is marking the reasoning, not only the result, and the name of the law is the proof that your rearrangement was legal. A learner who writes four times thirty-seven times twenty-five equals three thousand seven hundred with no justification earns the answer mark and nothing else. A learner who writes commutative beside the swap and associative beside the regrouping has shown that every step was permitted, and collects the method marks as well. There is a second, deeper reason. In Term 2 the numbers become letters, and you can no longer check a step by working it out — the only thing that tells you a manipulation is allowed is whether a law permits it. Learning to name the law now, while the numbers can still confirm you, is how you build the habit before the safety net disappears.

### subtopic_1 — Commutative and Associative — The Laws of Order and Grouping
**Assistant:** Subtraction is not commutative — but can we ever reorder a sum that has minus signs in it?

**Tutor:** Yes, provided each number keeps the sign that is attached to it. Fifty minus eighteen plus thirty can be read as fifty, plus negative eighteen, plus thirty, and once every term carries its own sign, the whole thing is an addition — and additions commute. So you may add fifty and thirty first to get eighty, then take away the eighteen to land on sixty-two. What you may never do is swap the numbers across a minus sign while leaving the sign where it was, turning fifty minus eighteen into eighteen minus fifty. The rule of thumb: the sign travels with its number. When the Integers topic arrives in week three, this exact idea is what makes adding negative numbers feel natural rather than frightening.

### subtopic_2 — The Distributive Law — Multiplication Reaches Into the Bracket
**Assistant:** When is it better to distribute over subtraction rather than addition?

**Tutor:** Whenever the number you are multiplying sits just BELOW a round number. Ninety-eight is two below one hundred, so seven times ninety-eight is seven hundred minus fourteen. Nine hundred and ninety-nine is one below a thousand, so six times nine hundred and ninety-nine is six thousand minus six. If instead the number sits just ABOVE a round number — one hundred and two, say — distribute over addition: seven times one hundred and two is seven hundred plus fourteen. The law is the same; you simply choose the split that produces a round number and a small correction. Train your eye to ask, before any multiplication, which round number is closest, and the distributive law will do the rest.

### subtopic_3 — The Identities — 0 for Addition, 1 for Multiplication
**Assistant:** Why is zero the identity for addition but not for multiplication?

**Tutor:** An identity must leave the other number exactly as it was. Add zero to forty and forty is still forty, so zero qualifies for addition. Multiply forty by zero and you get zero — the forty has been wiped out, not preserved — so zero fails the test for multiplication. The number that leaves a product unchanged is one: forty times one is forty. Zero's behaviour under multiplication is important enough to have its own name, the zero property, and it is the reason that, later in algebra, if a product of two factors equals zero then one of the factors must itself be zero. Keep the two roles separate: zero preserves under addition and annihilates under multiplication; one preserves under multiplication.
