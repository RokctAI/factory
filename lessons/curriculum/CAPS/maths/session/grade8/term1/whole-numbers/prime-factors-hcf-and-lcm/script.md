# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises prime factorisation and uses it to find the highest common factor and the lowest common multiple of three-digit numbers. The anchor pair is 180 and 300: factorised by the ladder method, written in index form, and read for HCF and LCM without listing a single multiple. Small numbers are done by inspection; large numbers are done by factorisation, and the examiner expects you to know which tool to reach for.

## Subtopic: Prime Factorisation — The Ladder and Index Form

A prime number has exactly two factors: one and itself. Two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen — and two is the only even prime. Every whole number greater than one is either prime or can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way. That unique product is the number's prime factorisation, and it is the DNA of the number: everything about its factors and multiples can be read from it.

The method the examiner expects is the ladder. Divide by the smallest prime that goes in, write the quotient underneath, and repeat until you reach one. Anchor number one: 180. Two goes into 180: 90. Two goes into 90: 45. Two does not go into 45, so move to three: 15. Three again: 5. Five is prime: 1. The primes down the side of the ladder are two, two, three, three, five. So 180 equals two times two times three times three times five.

Now write it in index form, which is the form that earns the mark: 180 equals two squared, times three squared, times five. Index form groups repeated primes as powers, and it is the form you will read HCF and LCM from in the next section.

Anchor number two: 300. Two: 150. Two: 75. Three: 25. Five: 5. Five: 1. So 300 equals two squared, times three, times five squared. Check by multiplying back: four times three is twelve, twelve times twenty-five is 300. Always multiply back — it costs ten seconds and catches a slipped division.

Two disciplines. First, divisibility tests speed the ladder: a number is divisible by two if it ends in an even digit, by three if its digit sum is divisible by three, by five if it ends in zero or five. For 180 the digits sum to nine, so three divides it; for 300 the digit sum is three, so three divides it once. Second, the ladder must end at one, and every number on the side must be prime — writing six on the side is a method error even if the final product is right.

The questions for this section are with you now: run the ladder, write index form, and multiply back to check.

## Subtopic: The HCF — Shared Primes, Lowest Powers

The highest common factor of two numbers is the largest number that divides into both of them. For small numbers you find it by inspection: the factors of twelve are one, two, three, four, six and twelve; the factors of eighteen are one, two, three, six, nine and eighteen; the largest number in both lists is six. HCF of twelve and eighteen: 6. Inspection is fine up to about fifty. For three-digit numbers listing factors is slow and error-prone, and the examiner expects factorisation.

Here is the rule, read directly from index form. The HCF takes every prime that appears in BOTH factorisations, each raised to the LOWER of its two powers. Set the anchor pair side by side. 180 is two squared, three squared, five. 300 is two squared, three, five squared. Prime two appears in both: powers two and two, lower is two, take two squared. Prime three appears in both: powers two and one, lower is one, take three. Prime five appears in both: powers one and two, lower is one, take five. HCF equals two squared times three times five, which is four times fifteen: 60.

Why the lower power? Because a factor of both numbers can only contain as many copies of each prime as the stingier number provides. 300 has only one three, so the common factor may hold at most one three, however many 180 has. The HCF collects everything the two numbers share and nothing more.

Verify, as always: 180 divided by 60 is three; 300 divided by 60 is five. Both divisions are exact, and the quotients three and five share no factor, which confirms that 60 is the HIGHEST common factor and not merely a common factor.

What an HCF question sounds like in words: the largest square tile that fits a floor exactly, the greatest number of identical gift packs that can be made from two quantities, the biggest group size that divides two classes evenly. Whenever a problem wants the LARGEST thing that goes INTO both quantities, it is an HCF problem.

Pause here for this section's questions: the lower-power rule, the verification, and the word problem that hides an HCF.

## Subtopic: The LCM — Every Prime, Highest Powers

The lowest common multiple of two numbers is the smallest number that both of them divide into. By inspection for small numbers: multiples of twelve are twelve, twenty-four, thirty-six; multiples of eighteen are eighteen, thirty-six; the first number in both lists is thirty-six. LCM of twelve and eighteen: 36. Listing multiples of three-digit numbers is hopeless, so again the examiner expects factorisation.

The rule is the mirror image of the HCF rule. The LCM takes EVERY prime that appears in EITHER factorisation, each raised to the HIGHER of its powers. The anchor pair: 180 is two squared, three squared, five; 300 is two squared, three, five squared. Prime two: higher power is two, take two squared. Prime three: powers two and one, higher is two, take three squared. Prime five: powers one and two, higher is two, take five squared. LCM equals two squared times three squared times five squared, which is four times nine times twenty-five: 900.

Why the higher power? A multiple of both numbers must be divisible by each, so it must contain at least as many copies of each prime as the GREEDIER number demands. 180 demands two threes, so the LCM must carry two threes; 300 demands two fives, so the LCM must carry two fives. Take the maximum of each and the result is the smallest number that satisfies both.

Verify: 900 divided by 180 is five; 900 divided by 300 is three. Both exact. And here is a relationship worth knowing: HCF times LCM equals the product of the two numbers. Sixty times nine hundred is fifty-four thousand; one hundred and eighty times three hundred is fifty-four thousand. This identity is a free check on both answers at once, and it lets you find the LCM from the HCF by division.

What an LCM question sounds like: two events that repeat and you want the first time they coincide; the smallest length that can be cut into pieces of either size; the common denominator when adding fractions in week five. Whenever a problem wants the SMALLEST thing that both quantities go INTO, it is an LCM problem.

Here come the questions for this section: the higher-power rule, the product check, and the taxis that leave together.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Tool and Reading the Word Problem

The method, compressed for the examination. One: decide whether the question wants a factor or a multiple. A factor goes INTO the numbers — largest group, largest tile, greatest number of equal packs — HCF. A multiple is what the numbers go INTO — first time together again, smallest common length, common denominator — LCM. Two: for numbers under about fifty, work by inspection and show the lists. Three: for larger numbers, ladder each number to index form. Four: HCF takes shared primes at lower powers; LCM takes all primes at higher powers. Five: verify by division, and if time allows, by the product identity HCF times LCM equals the product of the numbers.

Two full word problems. First: a school has 180 Grade 8 learners and 300 Grade 9 learners to be divided into groups of equal size, with no learner left over and every group from a single grade. What is the largest possible group? Largest thing going into both: HCF, 60. Then 180 divided by 60 gives three groups and 300 divided by 60 gives five groups — eight groups in all. Second: a fence needs posts every 180 centimetres and a decorative light every 300 centimetres, starting together at the corner. How far along does a post next coincide with a light? Smallest thing both go into: LCM, 900 centimetres, which is nine metres.

The error museum, three exhibits. Exhibit one: swapping the rules — taking higher powers for the HCF — which produces 900 as a factor, and 900 cannot divide 180. The sanity check catches it: an HCF can never exceed the smaller number, and an LCM can never be less than the larger number. Exhibit two: forgetting a prime that appears in only one number when building the LCM — every prime must be present. Exhibit three: an unfinished ladder, stopping at a composite such as fifteen, so the index form is wrong before the rules are even applied.

Where this leads. Week five adds fractions with unlike denominators, and the lowest common denominator is the LCM of the denominators — this exact method. Simplifying a fraction to lowest terms divides top and bottom by their HCF. In Grade 9 you factorise algebraic expressions by taking out the highest common factor of their terms, and in Grade 10 you add algebraic fractions over a lowest common denominator. The ladder you ran today never stops being useful.

The distinction-level takeaway: the HCF is what the numbers share; the LCM is what they jointly need. Lower powers collect the shared part; higher powers cover every demand. Hold that sentence and the two rules can never be swapped.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: classify each problem, apply the right rule, and run the sanity check.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same 180 and 300 now, with Lego bricks, tiles and two taxis leaving a rank. No new numbers, no new answers — the HCF is still 60 and the LCM is still 900 — but this time you will see what those two numbers are FOR before you see how to find them.

## Subtopic: Breaking a Number Into Bricks

Think of every whole number as a tower built from bricks, and the bricks are prime numbers — two, three, five, seven and so on. Primes are the bricks you cannot break any smaller. Six is a two-brick and a three-brick. Twelve is two two-bricks and a three-brick. Every number has exactly one set of bricks, and once you know the bricks you know everything about the number.

How do you find the bricks? Keep splitting. Take 180. Is it even? Yes, so pull off a two-brick: 90 left. Even again: another two-brick, 45 left. Forty-five is not even, so try a three-brick — four plus five is nine, and nine is in the three times table, so yes: 15 left. Another three-brick: 5 left. Five is itself a brick. Stop. The bricks of 180 are two, two, three, three, five. Count them up the short way: two twos, two threes, one five — two squared times three squared times five.

Now 300. Even: two-brick, 150. Even: two-brick, 75. Not even; three plus zero is three, so a three-brick: 25. Five-brick: 5. Five-brick: done. The bricks of 300 are two, two, three, five, five — two squared times three times five squared.

Put the two piles side by side and look. Both piles have two two-bricks. Both have at least one three-brick, but 180 has a spare three. Both have at least one five-brick, but 300 has a spare five. That little comparison — what do they share, where does one have extra — is the whole of the next two sections. Everything about HCF and LCM is just reading the two brick piles.

Quick check: multiply the bricks of 300 back together. Two times two is four, times three is twelve, times five is sixty, times five is three hundred. The tower rebuilds, so the bricks are right. Do that check every time — it takes seconds and saves marks.

Your questions for this section are with you now: find the bricks, count them up in powers, and rebuild the tower to check.

## Subtopic: The Biggest Tile — What the Piles Share

Here is a real job. A bathroom floor is 180 centimetres one way and 300 centimetres the other, and you want to tile it with square tiles, all the same size, no cutting. What is the biggest tile you can use?

The tile must fit into 180 exactly AND into 300 exactly. So its size must be a factor of both numbers — a common factor — and you want the biggest one. Go back to the brick piles. A tile that fits both can only be built from bricks that BOTH piles have. Both have two two-bricks: take them. Both have at least one three: take one — not two, because 300 only has one. Both have at least one five: take one — not two, because 180 only has one. Multiply: two times two is four, times three is twelve, times five is sixty. The biggest tile is sixty centimetres.

Check it like a tiler would: 180 divided by 60 is three tiles along one wall, 300 divided by 60 is five tiles along the other. Three by five, fifteen tiles, no cutting. And notice three and five share nothing, which tells you that 60 really is the biggest — if the counts still shared a factor, a bigger tile would have been possible.

That biggest shared size has a name: the highest common factor, HCF. Highest because it is the biggest, common because both numbers have it, factor because it goes into them. And the rule you just used without noticing: take only the bricks both piles have, and for each brick type take the SMALLER count. The shared part, nothing extra.

For small numbers you do not need bricks at all. Twelve and eighteen: twelve is one, two, three, four, six, twelve; eighteen is one, two, three, six, nine, eighteen; the biggest in both lists is six. That is called inspection, and for small numbers it is quicker than the ladder. For 180 and 300 the lists are too long — that is when the bricks earn their keep.

One sanity check to keep forever: the HCF can never be bigger than the smaller of the two numbers. If you ever get an HCF of 900 for 180 and 300, something swapped, because 900 cannot go into 180.

Well done — you have just found an HCF by thinking like a tiler. The questions for this section are with you now: the biggest tile, the tile count, and the sanity check.

## Subtopic: When the Taxis Meet Again — What the Piles Need

Second real job. Two taxis leave the rank at the same moment. One comes back and leaves again every 18 minutes; the other every 24 minutes. When do they next leave together?

List it the slow way first, because the slow way shows what you are looking for. The first taxi leaves at 18, 36, 54, 72, 90 minutes. The second leaves at 24, 48, 72, 96. The first time both lists hold the same number: 72 minutes. That shared number is a common multiple — both 18 and 24 go into it — and the first one is the lowest common multiple, the LCM.

Now with bricks, because listing multiples of 180 and 300 would take all afternoon. Eighteen is two, three, three. Twenty-four is two, two, two, three. A number that BOTH go into must have enough bricks to satisfy each of them. Twenty-four demands three two-bricks, so you need three twos. Eighteen demands two three-bricks, so you need two threes. Take the BIGGER count of each brick: two times two times two times three times three — eight times nine — 72. Same answer as the lists, in a fraction of the time.

Back to 180 and 300. Two-bricks: both want two, take two. Three-bricks: 180 wants two, 300 wants one — take two. Five-bricks: 300 wants two, 180 wants one — take two. Multiply: four times nine is thirty-six, times twenty-five is nine hundred. The LCM of 180 and 300 is 900. If a fence has a post every 180 centimetres and a light every 300 centimetres, starting together, a post and a light next land in the same place nine metres along.

So the two rules sit side by side in your head as two pictures. The HCF is the biggest TILE — what the piles share, smaller count of each brick. The LCM is the first time the taxis MEET — what the piles jointly need, bigger count of each brick. Share versus need; smaller versus bigger. And one more check for free: multiply the HCF by the LCM — sixty times nine hundred is fifty-four thousand — and you get 180 times 300, also fifty-four thousand. If that check fails, one of your answers is wrong.

You started this lesson with two numbers and ended it with tiles, taxis and a check that catches your own mistakes. That is a real win. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: list, then brick, then check.
