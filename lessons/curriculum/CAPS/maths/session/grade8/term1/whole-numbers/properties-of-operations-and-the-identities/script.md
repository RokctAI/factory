# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the properties of operations on whole numbers: the commutative, associative and distributive laws, and the identity elements zero and one. The anchor calculations are four times thirty-seven times twenty-five, and twenty-five times twelve — both done without a calculator, every step justified by the law that permits it. The laws are not decoration: in Term 2 they become the rules of algebra, so learn their names now.

## Subtopic: Commutative and Associative — The Laws of Order and Grouping

Formal language first, because the examiner awards the mark for the name as well as the number. The commutative property says that the ORDER of two numbers does not change the result: a plus b equals b plus a, and a times b equals b times a. Three plus eight is eleven; eight plus three is eleven. Six times four is twenty-four; four times six is twenty-four. The word comes from commute — to swap places.

The associative property says that the GROUPING of three numbers does not change the result: the quantity a plus b, plus c, equals a plus the quantity b plus c. The same holds for multiplication. Brackets tell you which operation to do first, and the associative law says that for addition alone, or for multiplication alone, it does not matter where the brackets sit.

Now the two exceptions, which examiners love. Subtraction is NOT commutative: eight minus three is five, but three minus eight is negative five. Division is NOT commutative: twelve divided by four is three, but four divided by twelve is a fraction, one third. Neither is associative either. The laws belong to addition and to multiplication only. Write that boundary in your notes — a learner who applies the commutative law to subtraction loses the mark and the answer.

The anchor calculation. Four times thirty-seven times twenty-five. Worked left to right it is ugly: four times thirty-seven is one hundred and forty-eight, and one hundred and forty-eight times twenty-five is a long multiplication. Instead, apply the commutative law to swap thirty-seven and twenty-five, then the associative law to group four with twenty-five. Four times twenty-five is one hundred. One hundred times thirty-seven is three thousand seven hundred. Two laws, two seconds, and the answer is 3 700. In the examination you write the law beside each step: commutative, then associative. That annotation is the method mark.

A second drill with addition: seventeen plus fifty-nine plus eighty-three. Regroup seventeen with eighty-three: that pair makes one hundred. Add fifty-nine: 159. The partner that completes a hundred is always the pair to find first.

The questions for this section are ready for you now: they ask you to name the law in use, and to catch the two operations the laws do not cover.

## Subtopic: The Distributive Law — Multiplication Reaches Into the Bracket

The third law is the one that does the most work in your mathematical life. The distributive property says that multiplication distributes over addition: a times the quantity b plus c, equals a times b, plus a times c. The multiplier outside the bracket reaches in and multiplies EVERY term inside. It also distributes over subtraction: a times the quantity b minus c equals a b minus a c.

Second anchor: twenty-five times twelve. Split twelve into ten plus two. Twenty-five times ten is two hundred and fifty; twenty-five times two is fifty; the sum is three hundred. Written formally: twenty-five times the quantity ten plus two, equals twenty-five times ten plus twenty-five times two, which gives 300. The law let us trade one hard multiplication for two easy ones and an addition.

Now the subtraction version, which is the sharper tool. Seven times ninety-eight. Ninety-eight is one hundred minus two. Seven times one hundred is seven hundred; seven times two is fourteen; subtract: 686. Any number just below a round number is an invitation to distribute over subtraction.

The law runs in reverse as well, and the reverse is called factorising — the word you will meet constantly from Grade 9 onwards. Consider thirty-six times eight, plus sixty-four times eight. Both products share the factor eight, so pull it out: the quantity thirty-six plus sixty-four, times eight. That bracket is one hundred, so the answer is 800. Forwards, the law expands a bracket; backwards, it collects a common factor. Same law, two directions, and both earn marks.

The error that costs the most marks in this topic: distributing to only the first term. Three times the quantity four plus five is NOT twelve plus five. The three must reach both terms: twelve plus fifteen, which is twenty-seven — and check it against the bracket done first, three times nine, twenty-seven. Agreement. Whenever you distribute, count the terms inside the bracket and confirm that every one of them received the multiplier.

One more boundary: multiplication does not distribute over multiplication. Two times the quantity three times four is two times twelve, twenty-four — not six times eight. Distribution is strictly a multiplication-over-addition-or-subtraction law.

Your questions for this section are with you now: expand a bracket correctly, pull a common factor out, and spot the learner who forgot the second term.

## Subtopic: The Identities — 0 for Addition, 1 for Multiplication

An identity element is a number that leaves every other number unchanged under an operation. For addition that number is zero: a plus zero equals a, and zero plus a equals a. Add nothing, and the value keeps its identity. Zero is called the additive identity.

For multiplication the identity is one: a times one equals a, and one times a equals a. One lot of a number is that number. One is the multiplicative identity. Notice that zero is NOT the multiplicative identity — in fact zero does the opposite job in multiplication: any number times zero is zero. That is called the zero property of multiplication, and it must never be confused with the identity. Zero preserves a number under addition and annihilates it under multiplication. Two different roles, two different names.

Why does this matter beyond definitions? Because the multiplicative identity in disguise is one of the most used tools in the whole syllabus. Three quarters equals six eighths — how? Multiply by two over two, which is one. Multiplying by one changes nothing about the VALUE, only the appearance. Every equivalent fraction you will write in week five is the multiplicative identity at work. Every time you write an unknown x as one x, you are using the identity to make the coefficient visible.

The additive identity is just as busy. When you solve an equation in Term 2 by adding negative five to both sides, you are using the fact that five plus negative five makes zero, and zero added to the unknown leaves it standing alone. The identity is the destination of every inverse operation.

Examination technique: when a question asks you to name the property, read the shape. A number added to zero or multiplied by one — identity. Two numbers swapping places — commutative. Brackets moving — associative. A multiplier reaching into a bracket — distributive. Quote the full name: additive identity or multiplicative identity, never just identity. Precision in naming is a mark.

Here come this section's questions: identify which number is the identity for which operation, and separate the zero property from the identity property.

## Subtopic: Calculating Smartly and Naming Every Step

Now the laws together, as an examiner expects to see them. Eight times one hundred and twenty-five times seven. Commutative: swap one hundred and twenty-five and seven. Associative: group eight with one hundred and twenty-five, which makes one thousand. One thousand times seven: 7 000. Two laws named, answer earned.

Nine hundred and ninety-nine times six. Distributive over subtraction: six times one thousand is six thousand; six times one is six; subtract: 5 994. Fifteen times twenty-four: write twenty-four as four times six, associate fifteen with four to get sixty, then sixty times six: 360. Read every calculation for a partner that makes ten, a hundred or a thousand — that is what the commutative and associative laws are for. Read every number near a round number as an invitation to distribute.

The error museum has three exhibits. Exhibit one: a learner applies the commutative law to subtraction, writing fifty minus eighteen as eighteen minus fifty. Exhibit two: a learner distributes over multiplication, turning two times the quantity three times four into six times eight, forty-eight, instead of twenty-four. Exhibit three: a learner names zero as the multiplicative identity because zero feels like nothing. All three are naming errors, and naming is where the method marks live.

Where does this go? Straight into Term 2. Collecting like terms — three x plus five x is eight x — is the distributive law run backwards: the quantity three plus five, times x. Expanding three times the quantity x plus two to get three x plus six is the distributive law run forwards with a letter inside. Simplifying two x times three y to six x y is the commutative and associative laws letting the numbers meet. In Grade 9 you will multiply two brackets together, and that is the distributive law applied twice. Every algebraic manipulation you will ever perform is one of these laws wearing letters. Learn the names now and Term 2 becomes translation, not discovery.

The distinction-level takeaway: the laws tell you what you are ALLOWED to rearrange. If a step can be justified by commutative, associative or distributive, it is legal. If it cannot, it is wrong — however convenient it looks.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the smart regroupings, the three exhibits, and the law hiding inside an algebraic step.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three laws now, from a spaza shop counter and a taxi rank — no formal language until the idea is already in your hands. Nothing new is coming: the answers will land exactly where the first part landed. What changes is that this time you will see why the laws are obvious.

## Subtopic: Swapping and Regrouping at the Till

Picture a spaza shop counter. You put down three cooldrinks and then two packets of chips. The owner rings up the chips first, then the cooldrinks. Does the total change? Of course not. That is the whole commutative law: the ORDER you add things in makes no difference to the total. Same for multiplying — four packets of six eggs is the same number of eggs as six packets of four. Twenty-four eggs either way.

Now the associative law, with coins. You have a pile of R20 notes, a pile of R50 notes and a pile of R10 coins. Count the twenties and fifties together first and then add the tens, or count the fifties and tens first and then add the twenties — the total in your hand is the same. GROUPING does not matter. Brackets in maths are just a way of saying which pile you counted first.

Here is why the laws are useful, not just true. Seventeen plus fifty-nine plus eighty-three. In the order given it is clumsy. But look for friends: seventeen and eighty-three are friends, because together they make one hundred. Put them together first — the law says you may — and then add fifty-nine. One hundred and fifty-nine. You just did a hard sum in your head.

The anchor from the first part: four times thirty-seven times twenty-five. Twenty-five and four are friends — like four twenty-five-cent coins making a rand. Pair them: one hundred. Then one hundred times thirty-seven is three thousand seven hundred. Nobody needs a calculator when they know who the friends are.

One warning, and it is the one that catches people. The till does NOT let you swap the order of taking away. If you have fifty rand and spend eighteen, you have thirty-two. You cannot swap that round into eighteen minus fifty and expect the same thing. Subtraction and division care about order. Adding and multiplying do not. That is the whole boundary.

Your questions for this section are with you now: find the friends that make a hundred, and remember which two operations refuse to swap.

## Subtopic: Breaking a Number Into Easy Pieces

The distributive law sounds grand, but you have used it since primary school every time you did a times table in your head. Twenty-five times twelve. Nobody knows that straight off. But everybody knows twenty-five times ten — two hundred and fifty — and twenty-five times two — fifty. Twelve is just ten plus two, so twenty-five times twelve is those two pieces added together: three hundred. You broke twelve into easy pieces, multiplied each piece, and added. That IS the distributive law.

Here is the picture. Twelve crates, each holding twenty-five oranges. Push ten crates to one side: two hundred and fifty oranges. The other two crates: fifty oranges. Total: three hundred. The multiplication reached into the whole group of crates, every crate, not just some of them.

Now the trick that feels like magic. Seven packets at ninety-eight rand each. Ninety-eight is awkward — but it is just two rand short of a hundred. Pretend each packet costs a hundred: seven hundred rand. Then give back the two rand on each packet: seven times two is fourteen rand back. Seven hundred minus fourteen is six hundred and eighty-six rand. You distributed over subtraction, and you did it faster than the till.

The law also runs backwards, and backwards is where your Grade 9 self will live. Thirty-six times eight plus sixty-four times eight. Both have an eight in them — thirty-six lots of eight and sixty-four lots of eight. That is just one hundred lots of eight: eight hundred. Pulling the shared piece out is called factorising, and you just did it on a shop counter.

The mistake to watch: when you break a number into pieces, EVERY piece must get multiplied. Three times the quantity four plus five — if you multiply the four and forget the five, you get twelve plus five, seventeen, and it is wrong. Three times four AND three times five: twelve plus fifteen, twenty-seven. Check it the slow way: four plus five is nine, three nines are twenty-seven. Both roads must meet.

That is the distributive law, and you already owned it. The questions for this section are ready: break a number into pieces, multiply every piece, and spot the piece a learner left behind.

## Subtopic: The Do-Nothing Numbers

Two numbers in maths have a special job: they do nothing. Not nothing useful — nothing to the number they meet.

The first is zero, for adding. You have forty rand. Somebody gives you nothing. You still have forty rand. Forty plus zero is forty. Zero is the do-nothing number for addition, and its formal name is the additive identity — identity because the number keeps its identity, stays itself.

The second is one, for multiplying. One packet of twelve eggs is twelve eggs. One lot of anything is that thing. Twelve times one is twelve. One is the do-nothing number for multiplication: the multiplicative identity.

Now here is where people slip, so go slowly. Zero is NOT a do-nothing number for multiplication. Zero packets of twelve eggs is zero eggs. Twelve times zero is zero. Zero wipes the whole thing out when you multiply — that is a different job entirely, and it has its own name, the zero property. So keep them apart in your head: adding zero does nothing; multiplying by zero destroys everything; multiplying by one does nothing.

Why would anybody bother naming a number that does nothing? Because doing nothing in disguise is one of the most useful moves you own. Half a loaf of bread and two quarters of a loaf are the same amount. One half becomes two quarters when you multiply top and bottom by two — and two over two is just one. You multiplied by one, so the value did not move; only the way it looks changed. Every equivalent fraction you have ever written is the do-nothing number one, dressed up.

And in Term 2, when you solve an equation, your whole aim is to get the letter standing alone. You add the opposite of whatever is attached to it, so that it becomes zero — and zero does nothing, so the letter is left by itself. The do-nothing numbers are where every solving step is heading.

Well done — you have just revised all three laws and both identities with a shop counter and a loaf of bread. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: name the do-nothing number for each operation, and keep the zero property away from the identities.
