### subtopic_1 — Profit, Loss and Percentage Profit
**Assistant:** Why is percentage profit worked out on the cost price and not the selling price?

**Tutor:** Because profit is a return on what was invested, and the investment is the cost price. The seller put R80 at risk and received R20 on top of it, so the fair question is R20 as a share of R80 — twenty-five percent. The selling price of R100 already contains the R20 profit, so measuring profit against it compares the gain with a figure that includes the gain, which understates the return and gives 20 percent. The convention is universal in business and in every memorandum: percentage profit and percentage loss are both expressed on the cost price. A useful habit is to write the formula line in full every time — profit over cost price times one hundred — so the denominator is chosen before the numbers go in. The question will sometimes give the selling price first to tempt you; the definition, not the order of the numbers, decides the denominator.

### subtopic_2 — Discount and VAT
**Assistant:** If VAT is fifteen percent, why can I not take fifteen percent off the till total to find the price without VAT?

**Tutor:** Because the till total is not one hundred percent of anything — it is one hundred and fifteen percent of the price before tax. The fifteen percent was added to the smaller, original price, so fifteen percent of the larger, inclusive price is a bigger number than the actual tax. On a R529 slip, fifteen percent of 529 is R79,35, but the real VAT was R69, charged on R460. The only way back is to undo the multiplication that built the price: the shop multiplied by one comma one five, so you divide by one comma one five, and 529 divided by one comma one five returns R460. Think of it as a staircase with a fixed step — you go up by multiplying and come down by dividing, and subtracting a percentage is a different staircase that lands you in the wrong place. Examiners set this trap in almost every financial paper, and the dividing line is usually a full mark.

### subtopic_3 — Simple Interest, Loans and Hire Purchase
**Assistant:** What is the difference between simple interest and the compound interest I have heard about?

**Tutor:** Simple interest is calculated on the original amount only, every year, so the charge is the same each year — on R2 000 at eight percent it is R160 in year one, R160 in year two and R160 in year three, R480 in total. Compound interest, which you will meet formally in Grade 9, adds each year's interest to the balance, so the next year's interest is calculated on a larger amount and the charges grow year by year. For short periods and modest rates the two are close; over many years compound interest grows far faster. In Grade 8 every interest question is simple interest unless the question says otherwise, and hire purchase in particular is always simple interest by the syllabus convention. The formula P times i times n, with the rate as a decimal, is the only one you need now — but notice that the idea of a multiplier, one plus the rate, is exactly what compound interest will build on.

### subtopic_4 — Budgets, Exchange Rates and the Full Method
**Assistant:** How do I remember which way to multiply or divide with an exchange rate?

**Tutor:** Anchor on the size of the units. One dollar is worth many rand — eighteen of them at the rate used today — so any amount expressed in rand must be a larger number than the same value expressed in dollars. Going into rand therefore multiplies, and coming out of rand divides. Fifty dollars becomes fifty times eighteen, R900, a bigger number; R1 800 becomes 1 800 divided by 18, one hundred dollars, a smaller number. After every conversion, compare the two numbers: if your rand figure is smaller than your dollar figure, you went the wrong way. The same size test works for any strong currency against the rand — pounds and euros are each worth even more rand than a dollar. Rates do change daily, so the question will always give you the rate; your job is only the direction and the arithmetic.
