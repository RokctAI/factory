# Part 1 — Expert

This session establishes the division property of zero: zero divided by any non-zero number is zero, and any number divided by zero is undefined. The anchor pair is twelve divided by zero against zero divided by twelve — two expressions that look like twins and behave like opposites. The reasoning rests entirely on the fact that division is the inverse of multiplication, and that reasoning is what the examiner wants written down.

## Subtopic: Division Is Multiplication Asked Backwards

Start with what division MEANS, because every argument in this lesson comes from it. Twelve divided by three is four. Why four? Because four times three is twelve. Division asks a question about multiplication: twelve divided by three asks, what number multiplied by three gives twelve? The answer is the number that makes the multiplication true. Division is the inverse of multiplication, exactly as subtraction is the inverse of addition.

Write the general statement: a divided by b equals c means that c times b equals a. Every division fact can be turned around into a multiplication fact, and every multiplication fact can be turned around into two division facts. Three times four is twelve; so twelve divided by three is four, and twelve divided by four is three. This is the fact family you met in primary school, and today it does serious work.

Now test the definition on the easy case. Zero divided by twelve. The question is: what number times twelve gives zero? The answer is zero, because zero times twelve is zero — the zero property of multiplication from last lesson. So zero divided by twelve equals zero. More generally, zero divided by any non-zero number is zero. Share nothing among twelve people and each receives nothing.

Keep the definition in front of you, because the next section applies it to twelve divided by zero, and the definition is what breaks. Everything you need to answer the examiner's favourite question — explain why division by zero is undefined — is already on the board: division is a multiplication question, and the answer to a division is the number that makes the multiplication true.

Take the questions for this section now: they turn divisions into multiplications and back, and they check the zero-on-top case.

## Subtopic: Twelve Divided by Zero — Why No Answer Exists

Now the anchor. Twelve divided by zero. Apply the definition without flinching: what number, multiplied by zero, gives twelve? Try four: four times zero is zero, not twelve. Try one hundred: one hundred times zero is zero. Try a million. Every whole number, every fraction, every number you will ever meet, multiplied by zero, gives zero. Nothing times zero gives twelve. The multiplication question has NO answer — and therefore the division has no answer. Twelve divided by zero is undefined.

Undefined is a precise technical word. It does not mean the answer is zero. It does not mean the answer is infinity. It means that no number satisfies the definition, so the expression has not been given a value — it is literally not defined. In an examination, write the word undefined, and write the reason beside it: no number multiplied by zero gives twelve. That one sentence is the full-marks explanation.

Compare the twins. Zero divided by twelve asks, what times twelve gives zero — answer zero, perfectly defined. Twelve divided by zero asks, what times zero gives twelve — no answer, undefined. Same two numbers, opposite behaviour, and the position of the zero decides everything. Zero on top is harmless. Zero underneath is forbidden.

There is a second way to see it, through sharing. Twelve sweets shared among three people is four each. Twelve sweets shared among zero people — how many does each person get? The question collapses: there is nobody to receive anything, so the quantity each receives does not exist. The sharing picture agrees with the multiplication picture, and you may use either in an explanation.

One subtle case the syllabus mentions and the examiner may probe: zero divided by zero. What times zero gives zero? EVERY number does — four, seven, a thousand. Now the problem is the opposite: too many answers instead of none. Division must produce one value, so zero divided by zero is also not defined; mathematicians call it indeterminate. For Grade 8 the rule is clean: any division with zero as the divisor is undefined, whatever is on top.

This section's questions are in front of you now: they ask you to state the result and, more importantly, to choose the correct reason.

## Subtopic: Watching the Quotient Run Away

A second line of evidence, because a distinction-level learner can argue a point two ways. Hold the dividend at twelve and shrink the divisor towards zero, watching the quotient. Twelve divided by four is three. Twelve divided by two is six. Twelve divided by one is twelve. Twelve divided by a half is twenty-four — dividing by a half doubles. Twelve divided by a tenth is one hundred and twenty. Twelve divided by a hundredth is one thousand two hundred. Twelve divided by a thousandth is twelve thousand.

Read the pattern. As the divisor gets closer to zero, the quotient does not settle on a number — it grows without any limit. Every time the divisor shrinks by a factor of ten, the quotient grows by a factor of ten. Arriving at zero itself, the quotient would have to be larger than every number, which is not a number. The runaway confirms the definition: there is no value to assign to twelve divided by zero.

Now the trap this pattern lays. Learners who see the quotient racing upwards want to write infinity as the answer. Do not. Infinity is not a number in the number system you work with, and twelve divided by zero does not EQUAL anything. The correct statement is undefined, and the runaway is an illustration of why, not a replacement for the answer.

Your calculator agrees. Enter twelve, divide, zero, equals. A scientific calculator displays Math Error or Error. It does not display zero and it does not display a very large number. The machine is applying the exact rule you have just learned, and in a test the words Math Error are a reminder, not an answer: you must write undefined and give the reason.

Contrast once more with the harmless twin, because the examiner will set them side by side. Zero divided by four, zero divided by two, zero divided by a half, zero divided by a hundredth: zero, zero, zero, zero. Nothing shared among any group is still nothing. Zero on top is calm; zero underneath runs away.

Here come the questions for this section: continue the pattern, interpret the calculator, and refuse the word infinity.

## Subtopic: Where the Zero Divisor Hides in Later Work

The rule is short; its consequences run through the whole syllabus. In Term 2 you meet algebraic expressions with letters in the denominator, such as six over x. The moment a letter sits underneath, you must ask what value of the letter would make the denominator zero — here, x equals zero — and that value is forbidden. In Grade 9 and Grade 10 this becomes a formal step: state the restriction, x is not equal to zero, before you simplify. The marks for that line come directly from today.

The rule also governs rates. Speed is distance divided by time. A journey of one hundred and twenty kilometres in two hours is sixty kilometres per hour. A journey of one hundred and twenty kilometres in zero hours is not an infinitely fast journey — it is a journey that has not happened, and the rate is undefined. Cost per item when there are zero items, average mark when nobody wrote the test: all undefined, all for the same reason.

Now the method, compressed. One: identify the divisor — the number you are dividing BY, the number underneath. Two: if the divisor is zero, the expression is undefined; write the word and the reason: no number multiplied by zero gives the dividend. Three: if the dividend is zero and the divisor is not, the answer is zero. Four: never write infinity, never write zero for an undefined expression, and never let a calculator's error message stand as your answer.

The error museum, three exhibits. Exhibit one: a learner writes twelve divided by zero equals zero, confusing it with zero divided by twelve. Exhibit two: a learner writes infinity, mistaking the runaway for a value. Exhibit three: a learner cancels an x from the top and bottom of an algebraic fraction without noting that x may not be zero — a Grade 9 preview of why today matters.

The distinction-level takeaway: division is multiplication asked backwards, and zero as a multiplier destroys information — it sends every number to the same place. Because the information is destroyed, it cannot be recovered by dividing, and that is the real reason the operation is undefined. Say it that way and the examiner knows you understand, not merely remember.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: restrictions on denominators, rates with zero time, and the three exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same rule now, with sweets, friends and a calculator that says Math Error. Nothing changes about the answers — zero divided by anything is still zero and anything divided by zero still has no answer — but this time you will feel why, instead of memorising it.

## Subtopic: Sharing Sweets With Nobody

Forget symbols for a minute. You have twelve sweets. Three friends. Share them out: four each. Everybody understands twelve divided by three.

Now you have twelve sweets and zero friends. Share them out. Go on — how many does each friend get? You cannot answer, and not because the answer is a hard number. You cannot answer because there is no friend to hand a sweet to. The question itself has nowhere to land. That is what mathematicians mean by undefined: not zero, not a huge number — the question has no answer to give.

Turn it around. Zero sweets, twelve friends. Share them out. Each friend gets nothing. Zero each. That question has a perfectly good answer, and the answer is zero. So the two situations that look so alike — twelve and zero, zero and twelve — are completely different. Nothing shared among people is nothing each. Something shared among nobody has no answer at all.

Here is a second picture, in case the first one slips. Twelve divided by three asks: how many threes fit inside twelve? Four of them. Twelve divided by two: six twos fit. Twelve divided by one: twelve ones. Now, twelve divided by zero asks: how many zeros fit inside twelve? Put one zero in — you have covered nothing. Put a hundred zeros in — still nothing. You can keep putting zeros in forever and never fill twelve up. The question never finishes, so it never has an answer.

Both pictures say the same thing, and you can use either one when a test asks you to explain. Sharing among nobody: no one to receive. Fitting zeros into twelve: never finishes. Either way, the word to write is undefined.

Your questions for this section are coming now — they are the sweets and the friends, in different numbers. Decide each time: is the zero the number of sweets, or the number of friends?

## Subtopic: The Calculator That Says Math Error

Take out a calculator, or picture one. Type twelve, divide, three, equals: four. Twelve divide two: six. Twelve divide one: twelve. Now go smaller than one. Twelve divide a half: twenty-four. Dividing by a half means asking how many halves fit in twelve — and halves are small, so lots of them fit. Twelve divide a tenth: one hundred and twenty. Twelve divide a hundredth: one thousand two hundred.

Do you see what the divisor is doing? It is sneaking towards zero — a half, a tenth, a hundredth — and every step it takes, the answer leaps up ten times bigger. The answer is running away. If you could actually reach zero, the answer would have to be bigger than every number there is, and there is no such number.

So type it: twelve, divide, zero, equals. The screen says Math Error. Not zero. Not a big number. The calculator is a very honest machine here: it is telling you that there is no number to show. Some learners think the calculator is broken. It is not. It is doing exactly what you have just worked out — refusing a question that has no answer.

Now the trap, and it catches clever people. Because the answers were running away upwards, some learners want to say the answer is infinity. Be careful. Infinity is a direction, not a number you can write down and check. Twelve divided by zero is not infinity; it is undefined. If you write infinity in a test you lose the mark, because you have claimed a value for something that has none.

And the honest twin again, on the calculator: zero divide four, zero divide a half, zero divide a hundredth. Zero, zero, zero. The calculator is calm, because nothing shared out is always nothing.

That is a real win: you now understand something your calculator can only grunt about. The questions for this section are with you now — the runaway pattern, the error message, and the one word you must write instead of infinity.

## Subtopic: Spotting a Zero Underneath

Last skill, and it is the one you will actually use for years: spotting a zero underneath before it bites.

Division hides in lots of places that do not use the division sign. A fraction is a division: six over x means six divided by x. A rate is a division: speed is distance divided by time, price per litre is money divided by litres, average is total divided by how many. Every one of those has a number underneath, and the question to ask every time is: could the number underneath be zero?

Take speed. You travel one hundred and twenty kilometres in two hours — sixty kilometres per hour. One hundred and twenty kilometres in one hour — one hundred and twenty kilometres per hour. One hundred and twenty kilometres in zero hours? That is not a fast trip; that is a trip that never took any time, which means it never happened. There is no speed to report. Undefined.

Take an average. Your class writes a test, the marks are added up and divided by the number of learners. If nobody wrote the test, there is nothing to divide by and no average exists. Not zero percent — no average. A spreadsheet will show an error in that cell, for the same reason your calculator did.

Now the Term 2 preview, because this is where the rule earns its keep. You will meet expressions like six over x. The x underneath is a stand-in for a number, and if that number is zero the whole expression falls apart. So before you do anything with six over x, you write a small note: x may not be zero. In Grade 9 that note becomes a required step with a mark attached, and the reason behind it is today's lesson, word for word.

The habit, in one line: find the number underneath, and make sure it is not zero. Zero on top is fine — it just gives zero. Zero underneath has no answer. Say it out loud until it is boring: zero on top, zero; zero underneath, undefined.

You have just taken a rule most people only memorise and turned it into something you understand from three directions — sweets, a calculator, and a journey that never happened. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: find the zero underneath in each situation, and say what it does.
