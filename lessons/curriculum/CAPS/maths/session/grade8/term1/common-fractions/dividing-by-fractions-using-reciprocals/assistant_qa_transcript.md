### subtopic_1 — What Dividing by a Fraction Asks
**Assistant:** Why does dividing by a fraction give a bigger answer when division is supposed to make things smaller?

**Tutor:** Division makes things smaller only when the divisor is bigger than one, because then you are sharing into several large portions. When the divisor is a fraction between zero and one, division asks how many of those small pieces fit into the amount, and small pieces fit many times. Six divided by one half asks how many halves fit into six, and since every whole contains two halves the answer is twelve, which is larger than six. The amount of juice has not changed; it is being measured in smaller cups, so the count goes up. The general rule is that dividing by a number greater than one shrinks the result and dividing by a number between zero and one enlarges it, and dividing by exactly one leaves it alone. Using this as a sense check before and after flipping is the fastest way to catch the common error of flipping the wrong fraction.

### subtopic_2 — Why Flip and Multiply Works — The Reciprocal
**Assistant:** Why is only the second fraction flipped and not the first?

**Tutor:** Because the flip comes from making the divisor's denominator equal to one, and only the divisor needs that treatment. Write three quarters divided by two thirds as a big fraction with three quarters on top and two thirds underneath. To clear the two thirds from the bottom, multiply top and bottom by its reciprocal, three halves; that is multiplying by one, so nothing changes in value. The bottom becomes two thirds times three halves, which is one, and the top becomes three quarters times three halves. The first fraction was never inverted — it simply acquired a new factor, the reciprocal of the divisor. Flipping the first fraction instead would compute two thirds divided by three quarters, the opposite division, and the answer would land on the wrong side of one. Flipping both gives a product of reciprocals that has no meaning for this question at all.

### subtopic_3 — Dividing with Mixed Numbers and Whole Numbers
**Assistant:** What is the reciprocal of a mixed number, and why do learners get it wrong?

**Tutor:** The reciprocal of a mixed number is found only after converting it to an improper fraction, because a mixed number is a sum of a whole and a fraction, and a sum cannot be flipped part by part. Two and a half is five halves, so its reciprocal is two fifths. Learners who flip the fraction part alone write two and two, which is nonsense: two and a half times two and two is nowhere near one. The check is always the definition — a number times its reciprocal must give one — and five halves times two fifths does give one. The same conversion is needed for whole numbers, which are written over one before flipping, so the reciprocal of six is one sixth. Every division of fractions therefore begins with the same line: rewrite every mixed number and whole number as a single fraction, and only then flip the divisor.

### subtopic_5 — Juice in Cups — How Many Fit?
**Assistant:** In the juice example, what does the leftover one eighth of a cup mean?

**Tutor:** It is the fraction of a second cup that the remaining juice would fill. Three quarters of a litre is nine twelfths; a cup holding two thirds of a litre is eight twelfths. The first cup takes eight twelfths and leaves one twelfth of a litre. That twelfth is measured against the size of a cup, not against a litre: one twelfth of a litre is one eighth of an eight-twelfths cup, because one divided by eight is one eighth. So the division three quarters divided by two thirds gives one and one eighth cups, meaning one full cup and an eighth of another. The answer to a division by a fraction is always counted in units of the divisor — cups, not litres — which is why the fractional part of the answer is a fraction of a cup. Flip and multiply produces nine eighths directly, and the picture tells you what the eighths are eighths of.
