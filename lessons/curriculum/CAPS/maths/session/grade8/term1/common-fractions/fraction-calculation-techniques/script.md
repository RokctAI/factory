# Part 1 — Expert

This session consolidates the calculation techniques that underpin all work with common fractions: converting mixed numbers to improper fractions and back, simplifying using the highest common factor, building equivalent fractions using the lowest common multiple, comparing and ordering fractions, and cancelling common factors in products. The anchor results are that thirty-six over forty-eight simplifies to three quarters and that fourteen fifteenths times twenty-five twenty-firsts equals ten ninths.

## Subtopic: Mixed Numbers and Improper Fractions — Both Directions

A mixed number and an improper fraction are two names for the same amount, and you must move between them fluently in both directions. Mixed to improper: multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, and keep the denominator. Four and two fifths: four times five is twenty, plus two is twenty-two, over five. Twenty-two fifths. The reason: each whole is five fifths, so four wholes are twenty fifths, and with the two extra fifths you have twenty-two.

More: two and three quarters is eleven quarters. Seven and one third is twenty-two thirds. Five and five sixths is thirty-five sixths. Notice the denominator never changes; it names the piece size, and the piece size is not affected by how many pieces you have.

Improper to mixed: divide the numerator by the denominator; the quotient is the whole number, the remainder is the new numerator, the denominator stays. Twenty-nine sixths: twenty-nine divided by six is four, remainder five. Four and five sixths. Seventeen fourths: four remainder one, so four and a quarter. Forty over seven: five remainder five, so five and five sevenths.

When to use which form. Improper fractions for multiplying, dividing, squaring and rooting, where the methods need single fractions. Mixed numbers for final answers and for estimating size, because seven and a half tells you the size instantly while fifteen halves does not. A good habit: convert to improper to calculate, convert back to mixed to answer.

A check that costs nothing: the whole-number part of the mixed form must match the rough size of the improper fraction. Twenty-nine sixths is a bit less than thirty sixths, which is five, so the mixed number should be a bit less than five. Four and five sixths fits. Questions on converting follow now.

## Subtopic: Simplifying with the Highest Common Factor

A fraction is in simplest form when the numerator and denominator share no factor other than one. To simplify, divide both by their highest common factor. Thirty-six over forty-eight: the HCF of thirty-six and forty-eight is twelve, so divide both by twelve: three quarters. One move. If you do not spot the HCF, simplify in stages — divide by two to get eighteen over twenty-four, by two again to get nine over twelve, by three to get three quarters. Three moves, same answer. The HCF route is faster; the staged route is safer when the numbers are unfamiliar. Both are correct.

Finding the HCF uses the prime-factor method from the whole numbers topic. Thirty-six is two squared times three squared; forty-eight is two to the fourth times three. The common factors are two squared and three, product twelve. For smaller numbers, inspection is enough: forty-two over fifty-six, both in the seven times table, forty-two is six sevens and fifty-six is eight sevens, so six over eight, and then three quarters.

Why simplify at all? Three reasons. Examiners require simplest form for the final mark. Smaller numbers mean fewer arithmetic errors in the next step. And equivalence is easier to see: eighteen twenty-fourths and fifteen twentieths look different, but both simplify to three quarters.

Simplify mixed numbers by simplifying the fraction part only: four and ten fifteenths is four and two thirds. And a warning that recurs throughout algebra: you may only divide the whole numerator and the whole denominator. In three plus six over nine, you cannot cancel the six with the nine and leave three plus two over three; the numerator is nine as a whole, and nine over nine is one. Cancelling is for factors, never for terms.

Final answers should also be checked for hidden simplification: fifteen over thirty-five is three sevenths; twenty-eight over sixty-three is four ninths. If both numbers end in zero or five, or both are even, the fraction is not yet simplified. Questions on simplifying follow.

## Subtopic: Equivalent Fractions, Comparing and Ordering

Building an equivalent fraction is the reverse of simplifying: multiply numerator and denominator by the same number. Three eighths with denominator forty: forty is eight times five, so multiply the three by five as well: fifteen fortieths. The question always tells you the target denominator or lets you choose it, and the multiplier is the target divided by the current denominator.

Comparing fractions needs a common denominator, and the lowest common denominator is the LCM of the denominators. Order two thirds, five eighths and seven twelfths from largest to smallest. The LCM of three, eight and twelve is twenty-four. Two thirds is sixteen twenty-fourths; five eighths is fifteen twenty-fourths; seven twelfths is fourteen twenty-fourths. Largest to smallest: two thirds, five eighths, seven twelfths. Without the common denominator these three are almost impossible to order by eye — they are all a little above a half.

For just two fractions, cross-multiplication is quicker. Five eighths against seven elevenths: multiply five by eleven, fifty-five, and seven by eight, fifty-six. The larger product belongs to the larger fraction: fifty-six came from seven elevenths, so seven elevenths is bigger. This works because you are secretly writing both over the common denominator eighty-eight and comparing the numerators.

Why the lowest common denominator rather than any common denominator? Any common denominator works; the product of the denominators always does. But five twelfths plus seven eighteenths over the product two hundred and sixteen gives ninety plus eighty-four, one hundred and seventy-four over two hundred and sixteen, which then has to be simplified to twenty-nine thirty-sixths. Over the LCM thirty-six it is fifteen plus fourteen, twenty-nine thirty-sixths, already simplest. The LCM saves a simplification and keeps the numbers small.

Equivalent fractions on a number line: between zero and one, mark thirds, then rewrite as twelfths, and you see that one third sits exactly on four twelfths. Equivalence is the same point with a different label. Questions on equivalence and ordering follow.

## Subtopic: Cancelling in Products and Examination Technique

Cancelling before multiplying is the technique that separates clean working from a page of large numbers. The anchor: fourteen fifteenths times twenty-five twenty-firsts. Multiplying straight across gives three hundred and fifty over three hundred and fifteen, which must then be simplified by thirty-five to ten ninths — correct, but slow and risky. Instead, cancel first. Any numerator may be cancelled against any denominator, because in a product the numerators are all multiplied together and so are the denominators. Fourteen and twenty-one share seven: fourteen becomes two, twenty-one becomes three. Twenty-five and fifteen share five: twenty-five becomes five, fifteen becomes three. The product is now two thirds times five thirds, ten ninths, one and one ninth. No number bigger than twenty-five was ever written.

Cancelling applies to any string of multiplications, including whole numbers over one and converted mixed numbers. Two and two fifths times one and seven eighths: twelve fifths times fifteen eighths. Twelve and eight share four; fifteen and five share five. Three halves times three over two... careful. Twelve over eight after dividing by four is three over two; fifteen over five is three over one. Three halves times three over one — but the twelve was over the five and the fifteen over the eight; cancelling across the product is allowed because the arrangement does not matter in a product. The result is three times three over two, nine halves, four and a half.

Cancelling is not allowed in addition or subtraction. Two thirds plus three fifths cannot have anything cancelled; the fractions must be written over fifteen and added. Nor is it allowed across a division sign until the division has been rewritten as multiplication by the reciprocal.

Technique for marks: show cancelling by striking through the original number and writing the new factor beside it, so the marker can see the common factor used. Keep the final answer in simplest form and, if improper, as a mixed number unless the question says otherwise.

Distinction-level takeaway: convert mixed numbers to calculate and back to answer; simplify with the HCF; build equivalents and compare with the LCM; and cancel freely in products, never in sums. These are the moves that will make algebraic fractions in Grade 9 routine. Questions on cancelling follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same tools used the way a careful shopper or a cook uses them: changing the size of the slices without changing the amount, finding the biggest share, and cutting numbers down before they get big. The tools are the same; the pictures make them stick.

## Subtopic: Same Cake, Different Slices

Here is the picture behind every technique in this lesson. A cake. Cut it into four and take three slices: three quarters. Now cut every slice in half, so there are eight slices and you hold six: six eighths. Same cake in your hands, different label. Cut each of those in half again: twelve sixteenths. Equivalent fractions are the same amount of cake with a different number of cuts, and that is why multiplying the top and bottom by the same number changes nothing that matters.

Simplifying is going the other way: gluing slices back together. Thirty-six slices out of forty-eight sounds like a lot of bookkeeping. Glue them in groups of twelve and you have three big slices out of four. Three quarters. Simplest form is the fewest slices that describe the same amount, and the highest common factor is the biggest group you can glue.

Mixed numbers in cake terms. Twenty-nine sixths: twenty-nine slices, six to a cake. Put them into whole cakes: six, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four — four whole cakes — with five slices over. Four and five sixths. And the other way, four and two fifths: four whole cakes cut into fifths is twenty slices, plus two more, twenty-two fifths. The denominator stays five all the way, because you never changed the knife.

Now the thing that makes cakes better than rules: comparing. Is five eighths of a cake more or less than seven twelfths? You cannot tell by looking at the slices because the knives were different. Re-cut both into twenty-fourths — fifteen twenty-fourths and fourteen twenty-fourths — and now you can see: five eighths wins by one slice. Common denominators are just agreeing on one knife.

So: equivalent fractions re-cut the same cake, simplifying glues slices, mixed numbers count whole cakes, and comparing means one knife for everyone. The questions now are cake questions; picture the knife before you answer.

## Subtopic: Cut It Down Before It Gets Big

Cancelling is the lazy person's best friend, and laziness here is a virtue. Fourteen fifteenths times twenty-five twenty-firsts. If you multiply straight away you get three hundred and fifty over three hundred and fifteen and then you have to find what divides both — a horrible job. Instead, look for shared factors before you multiply anything.

Think of it like a market. The numerators are what you are buying, the denominators are what you are paying with, and any coin on top can pay off a matching coin on the bottom. Fourteen on top and twenty-one on the bottom are both sevens: two sevens and three sevens. Cancel the seven: two on top, three on the bottom. Twenty-five on top and fifteen on the bottom are both fives: five fives and three fives. Cancel the five: five on top, three on the bottom. Now you have two thirds times five thirds, which is ten ninths. One and one ninth. Nothing bigger than twenty-five was ever touched.

Why is any top allowed to cancel with any bottom? Because in a product, all the tops get multiplied together and all the bottoms get multiplied together. Fourteen times twenty-five over fifteen times twenty-one is one big fraction, and a factor of seven anywhere on top can cancel a factor of seven anywhere on the bottom. Which fraction the seven came from does not matter once they are all being multiplied.

The one place this is forbidden: adding. Two thirds plus three fifths has a three on top and a three on the bottom, and it is very tempting to cancel them. Do not. In a sum the fractions are separate cakes; you cannot pay for one cake with a slice of another. Write both over fifteen and add: ten plus nine, nineteen fifteenths.

So: before multiplying, hunt for shared factors between any top and any bottom, cross them out, and multiply what is left. Small numbers, no big simplification at the end, fewer mistakes. The questions now are products to cut down; find the shared factors first.

## Subtopic: The Toolkit — When to Reach for Which Tool

Let us close by laying the tools out on the bench and saying when to pick each one up, because knowing the technique is half the job and choosing it is the other half.

Tool one, convert. Reach for it the moment you see a mixed number and the operation is multiply, divide, square or root. Four and two fifths becomes twenty-two fifths before anything else happens. Reach for the reverse at the end: twenty-nine sixths becomes four and five sixths for the final answer, because that is how an examiner wants it and how a human reads it.

Tool two, simplify. Reach for it at the end of every calculation, and in the middle whenever a fraction looks heavy. The highest common factor does it in one move; dividing by small primes does it in several. Both end at three quarters for thirty-six forty-eighths.

Tool three, equivalent fractions with the lowest common multiple. Reach for it whenever you add, subtract, compare or order. Two thirds, five eighths and seven twelfths become sixteen, fifteen and fourteen twenty-fourths, and suddenly they line up.

Tool four, cancel. Reach for it before any multiplication, and before any division once it has been flipped into a multiplication. Fourteen fifteenths times twenty-five twenty-firsts collapses to ten ninths with nothing big ever written. Never reach for it in a sum.

And the check that comes free with all of them: does the size make sense? Four and two fifths should be a bit more than four, and twenty-two fifths is. Thirty-six forty-eighths is three quarters because thirty-six is three quarters of forty-eight. Ten ninths is a bit more than one, and fourteen fifteenths times twenty-five twenty-firsts is just under one times just over one. If the size is wrong, the tool was misused.

You started this lesson with a bag of half-remembered tricks, and you are leaving with four named tools and a rule for when to use each — that is a real step up, and it is exactly what the next lesson's word problems will demand. The final questions mix all four tools; name the tool before you use it.
