### subtopic_1 — Recognising the Operation — Grouping, Sharing and Of
**Assistant:** How do I tell a grouping problem from a sharing problem when both are divisions?

**Tutor:** By what the answer counts. A grouping problem asks how many pieces of a known size can be made from an amount, so the answer is a number of pieces and you divide by the size of one piece — seven and a half metres divided by three quarters of a metre gives ten pieces, a count with no unit. A sharing problem asks how much each of a known number of people or containers receives, so the answer is an amount with a unit and you divide by the number of shares — three and three quarter kilograms divided by five gives three quarters of a kilogram each. The signature words help: how many bags, cups or lengths points to grouping; shared equally, each or per person points to sharing. Both are divisions, but the divisor is a size in one and a count in the other, and getting that right is what makes the units of the answer come out sensibly.

### subtopic_2 — Fractions of a Remainder
**Assistant:** Why does the fraction route give the same answer as following the rands step by step?

**Tutor:** Because each step multiplies the current amount by a fraction, and multiplying by one fraction and then by another is the same as multiplying by their product. Spending a third leaves two thirds of the money; spending a quarter of that remainder leaves three quarters of two thirds, which is one half of the original. The rand route computes two hundred, then four hundred, then one hundred, then three hundred, arriving at half of six hundred. The fraction route multiplies the fractions and arrives at one half directly, then applies it to six hundred once. The fraction route has two advantages: it works even when the starting amount is unknown, and it produces the fraction of the original spent or left, which examiners often ask for. Showing both routes, or using one to check the other, is a strong habit that catches the common error of taking the second fraction of the original amount.

### subtopic_3 — Finding the Whole from a Part
**Assistant:** In the pocket-money problem, why does the R150 go with five sevenths and not two sevenths?

**Tutor:** Because one hundred and fifty rand is the money left, and the fraction left is five sevenths, since two sevenths was spent. Matching the given amount to the fraction it actually represents is the whole of this skill. If you pair one hundred and fifty with the two sevenths that was spent, you find one seventh as seventy-five and a total of five hundred and twenty-five rand, and the check fails: two sevenths of that is one hundred and fifty spent, leaving three hundred and seventy-five, not one hundred and fifty. With the correct pairing, five sevenths equals one hundred and fifty, one seventh is thirty, and the whole is two hundred and ten. The forward check then works: two sevenths of two hundred and ten is sixty, and two hundred and ten minus sixty is one hundred and fifty. Always ask whether the amount given is the part used or the part remaining, and write the matching fraction beside it.

### subtopic_6 — Of What? Following the Money
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a quarter of the tank and a quarter of the water?

**Tutor:** They are fractions of different things, and the phrase after of decides which. A quarter of the tank means a quarter of the full capacity, so with a tank two thirds full you subtract one quarter from two thirds: eight twelfths minus three twelfths leaves five twelfths of the tank. A quarter of the water means a quarter of what is currently in the tank, which is two thirds of the capacity; a quarter of two thirds is one sixth of the capacity, so two thirds minus one sixth leaves one half of the tank. The arithmetic in both is simple, and the entire difficulty is reading which quantity the fraction belongs to. The habit to build is to say of what aloud for every fraction in a problem and write the quantity it refers to beside it before multiplying. This single habit prevents more lost marks in fraction problems than any other.
