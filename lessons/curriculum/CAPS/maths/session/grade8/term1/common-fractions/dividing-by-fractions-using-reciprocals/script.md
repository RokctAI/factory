# Part 1 — Expert

This session establishes division of whole numbers and common fractions by common fractions through the reciprocal relationship: dividing by a number is multiplying by its multiplicative inverse. The anchor results are that six divided by one half is twelve and that three quarters divided by two thirds is nine eighths, written as one and one eighth. The method is justified before it is practised, and mixed numbers are included.

## Subtopic: What Dividing by a Fraction Asks

Division answers the question how many of this fit into that. Twelve divided by three asks how many threes fit into twelve: four. Keep that meaning fixed and apply it to a fraction divisor. Six divided by one half asks how many halves fit into six. Each whole contains two halves, so six wholes contain twelve halves. Six divided by one half is twelve. Notice the answer is bigger than six. Dividing by a number smaller than one gives a result larger than the starting number, because small pieces fit many times. That sense check will protect you all lesson.

Try four divided by two thirds. How many two-thirds fit into four? Each whole contains three thirds, so four wholes contain twelve thirds; two-thirds pieces are pairs of thirds, so twelve thirds make six pairs. Four divided by two thirds is six. Check by multiplying back: six times two thirds is twelve thirds, which is four. Correct.

Now a fraction divided by a fraction. Three quarters divided by one eighth asks how many eighths fit into three quarters. Three quarters is six eighths, so six eighths fit. Three quarters divided by one eighth is six. Again, dividing by a small piece gives a large count.

Three quarters divided by two thirds is harder to picture, because two thirds does not fit a whole number of times, and that is where the method of the next section comes in. But the meaning is unchanged: the answer is the number of two-thirds in three quarters, and since three quarters is slightly bigger than two thirds, the answer is slightly more than one. Keep that estimate; the exact answer will be nine eighths, which is one and one eighth.

Two facts round off the section. Dividing by one leaves a number unchanged. Dividing a fraction by itself gives one: two thirds divided by two thirds is one, because two thirds fits into itself exactly once. Both will be useful checks. Questions on the meaning of dividing by a fraction follow now.

## Subtopic: Why Flip and Multiply Works — The Reciprocal

From the Integers topic: the multiplicative inverse, or reciprocal, of a number is what you multiply it by to get one. The reciprocal of two thirds is three halves, because two thirds times three halves is six sixths, one. To find a reciprocal, swap the numerator and the denominator. The reciprocal of five is one fifth, since five is five over one. The reciprocal of one eighth is eight.

Now the key statement: dividing by a number is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal. You already use this with whole numbers — dividing by two is halving, which is multiplying by one half. Here is why it holds in general. Write three quarters divided by two thirds as a fraction with three quarters on top and two thirds underneath. Multiply the top and the bottom by three halves, the reciprocal of the denominator; this is multiplying by one, so the value is unchanged. The bottom becomes two thirds times three halves, which is one. The top becomes three quarters times three halves. A fraction with denominator one is just its numerator, so three quarters divided by two thirds equals three quarters times three halves. The flip is forced by the goal of making the denominator one.

Compute it: three quarters times three halves is nine eighths, which is one and one eighth. Our estimate was slightly more than one. Correct. Check by multiplying back: nine eighths times two thirds is eighteen twenty-fourths, which is three quarters.

Confirm the method on the earlier examples. Six divided by one half: six times two over one, twelve. Four divided by two thirds: four times three halves, twelve halves, six. Three quarters divided by one eighth: three quarters times eight, twenty-four quarters, six. Every answer from the counting method is reproduced by flip and multiply, which is how you know the rule is not arbitrary.

The rule in words, for your notes: keep the first fraction, change the division to multiplication, flip the second fraction. Only the divisor is flipped — never the first fraction, and never both. Questions on reciprocals and the method follow.

## Subtopic: Dividing with Mixed Numbers and Whole Numbers

Mixed numbers must become improper fractions before any division, for the same reason as in multiplication: the method works on single fractions. Two and a half divided by one and a quarter. Two and a half is five halves. One and a quarter is five quarters. Five halves divided by five quarters is five halves times four fifths. Cancel the fives; cancel the two into the four leaving two. Result: two. Check: one and a quarter fits into two and a half exactly twice, since two lots of one and a quarter is two and a half. Correct.

A whole number divided by a fraction: write the whole number over one. Five divided by two thirds is five over one times three over two, fifteen halves, seven and a half. Sense check: two thirds is less than one, so the answer should exceed five. It does.

A fraction divided by a whole number: the reciprocal of the whole number is one over it. Five sixths divided by five is five sixths times one fifth, which is five thirtieths, one sixth. Sense check: dividing by five should make the amount much smaller. It did. Learners often reach for flipping the first fraction here; keep the first fraction as it is, and flip the five into one fifth.

Two thirds divided by four: two thirds times one quarter is two twelfths, one sixth. And a mixed number divided by a whole number: three and three fifths divided by six. Three and three fifths is eighteen fifths. Eighteen fifths times one sixth: cancel six into eighteen leaving three, giving three fifths. Check: six lots of three fifths is eighteen fifths, which is three and three fifths. Correct.

Examination layout, five lines. Line one, the question. Line two, mixed numbers and whole numbers as improper fractions. Line three, the division rewritten as multiplication by the reciprocal. Line four, cancelling shown, then the product. Line five, the simplified answer as a mixed number if improper. Omitting line three is the most common way to lose the method mark, because the marker cannot see the flip. Questions on mixed and whole numbers follow.

## Subtopic: Mixed Operations and Examination Technique

Division sits with multiplication in the order of operations: after brackets, before addition and subtraction, and worked left to right with multiplication. One half plus three quarters divided by two thirds. Division first: three quarters divided by two thirds is nine eighths. Then one half plus nine eighths is four eighths plus nine eighths, thirteen eighths, one and five eighths. Adding first would give five quarters divided by two thirds, fifteen eighths — a different and wrong answer.

A bracketed example. Bracket two thirds plus one sixth bracket divided by five twelfths. Bracket first: four sixths plus one sixth is five sixths. Then five sixths divided by five twelfths is five sixths times twelve fifths. Cancel the fives; cancel six into twelve leaving two. Result two. Sense check: five twelfths is about zero comma four, five sixths is about zero comma eight, so about two. Correct.

A chain with both operations. Three fifths times one and two thirds divided by three quarters. Convert: one and two thirds is five thirds. Left to right: three fifths times five thirds is one. Then one divided by three quarters is four thirds, one and one third. Chains of multiplications and divisions are done in order from the left, or all divisions can be converted to multiplications first and then everything multiplied together, cancelling freely. Three fifths times five thirds times four thirds: the three and five cancel in pairs, leaving four thirds.

Three errors to avoid. Flipping the first fraction instead of the second. Flipping before converting a mixed number — the reciprocal of two and a half is not two and two, it is two fifths, because two and a half is five halves. And cancelling across a division sign; cancelling is for multiplication only, so rewrite as a multiplication before you cancel anything.

Distinction-level takeaway: division by a fraction is multiplication by its reciprocal, justified by making the denominator one; convert mixed numbers first; flip only the divisor; cancel only after the division has become a multiplication; and estimate — dividing by a proper fraction makes things bigger. # Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same division with juice poured into cups, ribbon cut into lengths and a chocolate bar shared out. Dividing by a fraction will stop being a rule you obey and become a question you can picture: how many of these fit into that?

## Subtopic: Juice in Cups — How Many Fit?

Let us pour. You have three litres of juice and cups that hold a quarter of a litre each. How many cups can you fill? That is three divided by one quarter, and you can just count: each litre fills four cups, so three litres fill twelve. Three divided by a quarter is twelve. Dividing by a quarter gave a bigger number than three, because quarter-litre cups are small and lots of them fit.

Now bigger cups: two thirds of a litre each, and you have four litres. How many cups? Each litre is three thirds, so four litres is twelve thirds. Each cup takes two thirds, so twelve thirds fill six cups. Four divided by two thirds is six. And now look at what you actually did with the numbers: four times three to get thirds, then divided by two to make pairs. Four times three over two. That is four times the flipped fraction. Flip and multiply is just count the small pieces, then group them.

Here is the awkward one, where the picture needs a little help. You have three quarters of a litre of juice and cups of two thirds of a litre. How many cups? Three quarters is a bit more than two thirds, so you can fill one cup and have a bit left over — the answer is one and a bit. Exactly how much of a second cup? Cut everything into twelfths: three quarters is nine twelfths, a cup is eight twelfths. Nine twelfths fills one cup, with one twelfth of a litre left, and one twelfth is one eighth of a cup. One and one eighth cups. Flip and multiply says three quarters times three halves is nine eighths, which is one and one eighth. Same answer, and now you know what the eighth means: an eighth of a cup.

So whenever you divide by a fraction, say the sentence: how many of these fit into that? If the cup is small, expect a big answer. If the cup is nearly as big as the juice, expect an answer near one. Then flip and multiply to get it exactly. The questions now are all juice and cups; say the sentence before you calculate.

## Subtopic: Ribbon and Chocolate — Dividing a Fraction by a Whole Number

Now the other direction: a fraction shared among people. You have five sixths of a slab of chocolate and five friends share it equally. Each gets five sixths divided by five. Picture the slab cut into six pieces, five of them yours. Five friends, five pieces: one piece each. One sixth of the slab each. Flip and multiply: five sixths times one fifth, five thirtieths, one sixth. Dividing by a whole number made the share smaller, as sharing should.

Try two thirds of a metre of ribbon cut into four equal pieces. Two thirds divided by four. Picture: two thirds is two pieces of a third each. Cutting two pieces into four means each third is halved: four pieces of one sixth. Two thirds divided by four is one sixth. Flip and multiply: two thirds times one quarter is two twelfths, one sixth. Same.

Here is the thing to notice. Dividing by two thirds made numbers bigger; dividing by four made them smaller. Both are division. The difference is whether the thing you divide by is bigger or smaller than one. Divide by something bigger than one and the answer shrinks; divide by something smaller than one and it grows. Once you have that sense, you can catch almost any flip-and-multiply mistake just by looking at the size of the answer.

A mixed number shared. Three and three fifths metres of ribbon cut into six equal bows. Convert: three and three fifths is eighteen fifths. Eighteen fifths divided by six is eighteen fifths times one sixth: six goes into eighteen three times, so three fifths. Each bow uses three fifths of a metre. Check: six bows at three fifths is eighteen fifths, which is three and three fifths. All the ribbon used, none left. Correct.

The questions now share chocolate and cut ribbon; decide first whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than what you started with.

## Subtopic: The Takeaway — Three Questions Before Every Division

Let us finish with a routine. Before you divide by a fraction, ask three questions. One: what is the sentence? How many of the second thing fit into the first? Two: should the answer be bigger or smaller than the first number? Bigger if the divisor is less than one, smaller if it is more than one. Three: have I converted every mixed number and whole number into a single fraction? Only then: keep, change, flip, cancel, multiply, simplify.

Run it on the anchor. Three quarters divided by two thirds. Sentence: how many two-thirds in three quarters? Size: two thirds is less than one, so the answer is bigger than three quarters; and two thirds is nearly as big as three quarters, so the answer is only a bit more than one. Conversion: both are already single fractions. Keep three quarters, change to times, flip to three halves, multiply: nine eighths. One and one eighth. Bigger than three quarters, a bit more than one. Every check passes.

Run it on two and a half divided by one and a quarter. Sentence: how many one-and-a-quarters in two and a half? Size: one and a quarter is bigger than one, so the answer is smaller than two and a half; and one and a quarter is half of two and a half, so expect two. Conversion: five halves and five quarters. Keep, change, flip: five halves times four fifths. Cancel: two. Exactly as expected.

The mistakes this routine catches: flipping the wrong fraction, which usually gives an answer on the wrong side of one; forgetting to convert a mixed number, which gives a nonsense reciprocal; and cancelling across the division sign before the flip. All three show up as an answer that fails the size question.

You started this lesson able to flip and multiply because someone told you to; you are leaving able to say why it works, to picture it with juice and cups, and to predict the size of the answer before you start — that is a real step up, and it is the last piece you need before fractions go into powers and roots next lesson. The final questions mix every type from the session; ask the three questions out loud before each one.
