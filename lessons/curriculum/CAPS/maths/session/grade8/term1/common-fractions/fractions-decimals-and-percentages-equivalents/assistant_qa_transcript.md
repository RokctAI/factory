### subtopic_1 — Fraction to Decimal to Percentage
**Assistant:** Why do some fractions give decimals that stop and others give decimals that recur?

**Tutor:** It depends on the prime factors of the denominator once the fraction is in simplest form. A decimal that stops is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten, and ten is two times five, so a fraction terminates exactly when its simplified denominator has no prime factors other than two and five. Three eighths has denominator eight, which is two cubed, so it can be scaled to three hundred and seventy-five over one thousand and terminates at zero comma three seven five. Seven twentieths has denominator twenty, which is two squared times five, and terminates at zero comma three five. One third has denominator three, which can never be scaled to a power of ten, so the division never ends and the digit three recurs forever. One sixth has a factor of three in the denominator as well, so it recurs after the first digit. Knowing this lets you predict before dividing whether to expect a recurring dot.

### subtopic_2 — Percentage to Decimal to Fraction
**Assistant:** How do I convert a percentage that contains a fraction, such as twelve and a half percent, to a common fraction?

**Tutor:** Write it over one hundred as usual and then clear the fraction inside by multiplying top and bottom by the same number. Twelve and a half over one hundred has a half in the numerator; multiplying top and bottom by two gives twenty-five over two hundred, and simplifying by twenty-five gives one eighth. The same approach handles thirty-three and a third percent: multiplying top and bottom by three gives one hundred over three hundred, which is one third, and sixty-six and two thirds percent gives two hundred over three hundred, two thirds. An alternative is to convert the fractional percentage to a decimal first — twelve and a half percent is zero comma one two five — and then read the place value, one hundred and twenty-five thousandths, which simplifies to one eighth. Both routes are acceptable in an examination, and both end in the same simplest fraction, which is the only form that earns the final mark.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing and Ordering Across Forms
**Assistant:** When ordering a mixed list, why convert to decimals rather than to fractions?

**Tutor:** Either works, but decimals give a direct comparison by place value with no further work, whereas fractions require a common denominator that can become large. In the list three eighths, zero comma four, thirty-five percent and one third, the lowest common denominator of eight, five, twenty and three is one hundred and twenty, which is manageable but slow. As decimals the four values are zero comma three seven five, zero comma four, zero comma three five and zero comma three recurring, and once each is padded to the same number of places the order is read off digit by digit. Percentages are a good alternative when every number converts to a whole or simple percentage, which is common when the fractions have denominators that divide one hundred. Whatever form you choose, every number must be in that form before you compare, and the final answer must list the numbers in their original forms, since that is what the question asked you to order.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing the Right Form and Examination Technique
**Assistant:** If all three forms are the same number, why does the examiner care which form I use?

**Tutor:** Because the forms differ in exactness and in convenience, and choosing well is part of mathematical skill. A fraction is exact: one third is precisely one third, while zero comma three three is an approximation that would make one third of ninety come out as twenty-nine comma seven instead of thirty. A decimal is the natural language of measurement and money, where instruments and tills read in tenths and hundredths. A percentage communicates a comparison instantly, because every percentage is out of the same hundred, which is why marks and discounts are quoted that way. Examiners also test the conversions themselves and the conventions that go with each form: a recurring decimal needs its dot, a fraction must be simplified, and a percentage needs its sign. When a question specifies a form, use it; when it does not, work in the form that makes the current step easiest and convert the final answer to whatever form the context suggests.
