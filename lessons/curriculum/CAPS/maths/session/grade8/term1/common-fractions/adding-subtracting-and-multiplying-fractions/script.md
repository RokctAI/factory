# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises addition, subtraction and multiplication of common fractions and mixed numbers, and the calculation of a fraction of a whole number. The anchor results are that two thirds plus three quarters equals seventeen twelfths, written as one and five twelfths, and that one and a half times two and two thirds equals four. Every method is justified by equivalent fractions, and the examination layout is modelled.

## Subtopic: Equivalent Fractions and the Common Denominator

A fraction has two parts: the denominator says how many equal pieces make the whole, and the numerator says how many of those pieces you have. Two fractions are equivalent when they name the same amount with different pieces: one half, two quarters and six twelfths are all the same point on the number line. You make an equivalent fraction by multiplying the numerator and the denominator by the same non-zero number, which is multiplying by one in disguise. Two thirds times four over four is eight twelfths; the value has not changed.

Equivalent fractions are the whole secret of adding and subtracting. You can only add pieces that are the same size. Two thirds plus three quarters cannot be added as they stand, because thirds and quarters are different sizes. Find a denominator that both three and four divide into: the lowest common multiple of three and four is twelve. Rewrite each fraction in twelfths. Two thirds is eight twelfths. Three quarters is nine twelfths. Now the pieces match, and eight twelfths plus nine twelfths is seventeen twelfths.

Seventeen twelfths is an improper fraction — the numerator is bigger than the denominator, so the value is more than one. Convert to a mixed number by dividing: seventeen divided by twelve is one remainder five, so seventeen twelfths is one and five twelfths. Both forms are correct; the examination usually asks for the mixed number in its simplest form, and five twelfths cannot be simplified because five and twelve share no factor.

The lowest common denominator is found exactly as the LCM was found in the whole numbers topic: by listing multiples or by prime factors. For five sixths and one quarter, the LCM of six and four is twelve, so five sixths is ten twelfths and one quarter is three twelfths. For denominators with no common factor, such as three and five, the LCD is simply the product, fifteen.

Always simplify a final answer. Divide numerator and denominator by their HCF. Six twentieths: the HCF of six and twenty is two, so three tenths. An unsimplified answer loses the final mark even when the arithmetic is right. Questions on equivalent fractions and common denominators are with you now.

## Subtopic: Adding and Subtracting Fractions and Mixed Numbers

With a common denominator in hand, addition and subtraction are one line each. Five sixths minus one quarter: LCD twelve; ten twelfths minus three twelfths is seven twelfths. Two thirds plus three quarters: eight twelfths plus nine twelfths is seventeen twelfths, which is one and five twelfths. The denominator is never added or subtracted — it names the piece size, and the piece size does not change when you count pieces.

Mixed numbers can be handled two ways, and you should know both. Method one, whole parts and fraction parts separately. Two and one third plus one and one half: wholes two plus one is three; fractions one third plus one half is two sixths plus three sixths, five sixths; total three and five sixths. This is fast when the fraction parts do not overflow one.

Method two, convert to improper fractions first. Three and a half minus one and two thirds. Three and a half is seven halves — three times two plus one over two. One and two thirds is five thirds. LCD six: twenty-one sixths minus ten sixths is eleven sixths, which is one and five sixths. Method two is safer for subtraction, because it avoids the awkward moment when the fraction part being subtracted is larger than the one you subtract from — a half minus two thirds — which otherwise requires borrowing from the whole.

Check the subtraction by estimation. Three and a half minus one and two thirds: roughly three and a half minus one and a bit more than a half, so a bit less than two. One and five sixths fits. Estimation is quick and catches the common error of subtracting denominators.

Examination layout: line one, the question. Line two, mixed numbers as improper fractions if you are using method two. Line three, both fractions over the LCD. Line four, the single fraction. Line five, the simplified mixed number. A marker can follow every step, and each line carries a mark. Questions on adding and subtracting follow.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Fractions and Mixed Numbers

Multiplication needs no common denominator. To multiply two fractions, multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators. Two fifths times three quarters is six twentieths, which simplifies to three tenths. The reason: a fraction of a fraction. Three quarters of a chocolate bar, and you take two fifths of that piece. Cutting the quarters into fifths makes twentieths, and you have two rows of three, six twentieths.

Simplify before multiplying whenever you can — it is called cancelling, and it keeps numbers small. Two fifths times three quarters: the two in the numerator and the four in the denominator share a factor of two, so divide both by two, leaving one fifth times three halves, which is three tenths directly. Cancelling is dividing a numerator and a denominator by the same number, and it is only allowed across a multiplication sign, never across an addition.

Mixed numbers must be converted to improper fractions before multiplying. The anchor: one and a half times two and two thirds. One and a half is three halves. Two and two thirds is eight thirds. Three halves times eight thirds: cancel the three with the three, cancel the two with the eight leaving four, giving one times four over one times one, which is four. Learners who multiply the wholes and the fractions separately — one times two, a half times two thirds — get two and a third, which is wrong. There is no shortcut through the whole parts for multiplication.

A whole number is a fraction over one. Four times two thirds is four over one times two over three, eight thirds, two and two thirds. And multiplying by a proper fraction makes a number smaller, since you are taking a part of it — a useful sense check. Two fifths times three quarters should be less than three quarters, and three tenths is.

Layout for marks: convert, cancel with the cancellation shown by striking through and writing the new factor, multiply, simplify. Questions on multiplication follow.

## Subtopic: Fractions of Whole Numbers and Mixed Operations

Finding a fraction of a whole number is multiplication. Three eighths of two hundred and forty is three eighths times two hundred and forty. The efficient route: divide by the denominator, then multiply by the numerator. Two hundred and forty divided by eight is thirty; thirty times three is ninety. Three eighths of two hundred and forty is ninety. Dividing first keeps the numbers small; multiplying first gives seven hundred and twenty divided by eight, also ninety, but with more work.

Two fifths of a class of thirty-five learners: thirty-five divided by five is seven, times two is fourteen learners. Five sixths of an hour: sixty divided by six is ten, times five, fifty minutes. The pattern is always the same, and the word of means multiply.

Mixed operations with fractions follow the usual order: brackets, then multiplication, then addition and subtraction. One half plus one third times three quarters. Multiplication first: one third times three quarters is three twelfths, which is one quarter. Then one half plus one quarter is two quarters plus one quarter, three quarters. A learner who adds first gets five sixths times three quarters, which is five eighths — wrong, because the order of operations was ignored.

A worked example with brackets. Bracket two thirds minus one quarter bracket times one and one fifth. Bracket first: eight twelfths minus three twelfths is five twelfths. One and one fifth is six fifths. Five twelfths times six fifths: cancel five with five, cancel six with twelve leaving two, giving one over two. One half.

Distinction-level takeaway: add and subtract over a common denominator and never touch the denominators; multiply straight across after converting mixed numbers and cancelling; find a fraction of a whole by dividing by the bottom and multiplying by the top; and simplify every final answer. Questions on fractions of wholes and mixed operations follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same fractions with pizza slices, a shared taxi fare and a recipe that gets scaled up. The methods do not change; you will just see the common denominator as cutting slices the same size, and multiplying as taking a part of a part.

## Subtopic: Pizza Slices — Why You Need the Same Size Pieces

Let us put the common denominator on a table. Two pizzas, same size. The first is cut into three slices and you take two of them: two thirds. The second is cut into four slices and you take three: three quarters. How much pizza do you have? You cannot say five slices of anything, because the slices are different sizes — a third of a pizza is bigger than a quarter. To add them you need to cut both pizzas into the same size slice.

Which slice size works? One that both thirds and quarters can be cut into exactly. Twelfths. Cut each third into four pieces: two thirds becomes eight twelfths. Cut each quarter into three pieces: three quarters becomes nine twelfths. Now every piece is a twelfth, and eight plus nine is seventeen twelfths. Twelve twelfths make one whole pizza, so you have one whole pizza and five more slices: one and five twelfths. That is the entire method for adding fractions — find a slice size that fits both, cut, count.

Subtracting is the same table. You have five sixths of a pizza and your brother takes a quarter. Cut everything into twelfths: five sixths is ten slices, a quarter is three slices, ten minus three is seven. Seven twelfths left. Notice the denominator was never added or subtracted — you just counted slices of the same size.

Now a mixed number. You have three and a half pizzas; a friend takes one and two thirds. Turn everything into slices: three and a half pizzas is seven halves, and one and two thirds is five thirds. Common slice: sixths. Seven halves is twenty-one sixths; five thirds is ten sixths; twenty-one minus ten is eleven sixths, which is one whole pizza and five sixths. The reason to turn mixed numbers into slices first is that you never have to borrow a whole pizza halfway through.

So: same-size slices, then count. Every fraction addition and subtraction you will ever do is this. The questions now are pizza questions; find the slice size that fits both before you count.

## Subtopic: A Part of a Part — Multiplying Made Visible

Multiplying fractions feels like magic until you see it as taking a part of a part. You have three quarters of a slab of chocolate. You give two fifths of what you have to a friend. How much of the whole slab does the friend get? Two fifths of three quarters.

Draw the slab. Cut it into quarters the long way and shade three — that is your three quarters. Now cut the whole slab into fifths the other way. The slab is now in twenty little blocks, four by five. Your three quarters covers three columns of five, fifteen blocks. Two fifths of those fifteen blocks is two rows of three, six blocks. Six out of twenty, which is three tenths. And look at the numbers: two times three on top, five times four on the bottom. Top times top, bottom times bottom — the rule is just a description of the grid.

Notice the answer is smaller than three quarters. Of course: you took a part of it. Whenever you multiply by a proper fraction, the result shrinks. If your answer after multiplying by two fifths is bigger than what you started with, something went wrong.

Mixed numbers in a recipe. A recipe needs two and two thirds cups of flour and you want to make one and a half times the recipe. One and a half times two and two thirds. Turn both into improper fractions first — three halves and eight thirds — because you cannot take one and a half of two and then one and a half of two thirds and glue them together; well, you can, but you will get the whole parts right and the fraction parts in a tangle. Three halves times eight thirds: the threes cancel, the two goes into the eight four times, and you are left with four. Four cups of flour exactly. Check: one and a half of about two and a bit is about four. Good.

So: multiplying is a part of a part, top times top and bottom times bottom, mixed numbers converted first, and the answer shrinks when you multiply by a proper fraction. The questions now use chocolate slabs and recipes; convert first, then multiply.

## Subtopic: Fractions of Money and Time — The Takeaway

The most useful fraction skill in daily life is finding a fraction of an amount. A shop takes three eighths off a jacket that costs two hundred and forty rand. How much is the discount? Three eighths of two hundred and forty. Do not multiply three by two hundred and forty and then divide — it works, but the numbers get big. Divide first: two hundred and forty divided by eight is thirty, so one eighth is thirty rand. Then three eighths is ninety rand. The discount is ninety rand and the jacket costs one hundred and fifty.

The same move for time. You spend five sixths of an hour on homework. Sixty minutes divided by six is ten, so one sixth is ten minutes; five sixths is fifty minutes. For a class: two fifths of thirty-five learners walk to school. Thirty-five divided by five is seven; two fifths is fourteen learners. Bottom first, then top. Every time.

Now let us finish the shared taxi story with everything at once. Three friends split a taxi fare. One pays one half, one pays one third. What fraction does the third friend pay? Add the first two: one half plus one third, same-size slices are sixths, three sixths plus two sixths is five sixths. The third friend pays the rest: one whole minus five sixths, which is six sixths minus five sixths, one sixth. If the fare was ninety rand, the third friend pays one sixth of ninety: ninety divided by six, fifteen rand. Adding, subtracting and a fraction of an amount in one story.

Here is your takeaway in three lines. Adding and subtracting: same-size slices, count, never touch the bottom. Multiplying: convert mixed numbers, cancel, top times top and bottom times bottom. Fraction of an amount: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top. And simplify at the end, always.

You came into this lesson able to add fractions when someone gave you the denominator; you are leaving able to find the denominator yourself, handle mixed numbers both ways, and work out a discount in your head — that is a real step up, and it is exactly what the next lesson on dividing fractions builds on. The final questions mix money, time and taxis; say which of the three lines you are using before you start.
