### subtopic_1 — Mixed Numbers and Improper Fractions — Both Directions
**Assistant:** Why keep the denominator the same when converting a mixed number to an improper fraction?

**Tutor:** Because the denominator names the size of the pieces, and converting only changes how the pieces are counted, not how big they are. Four and two fifths means four whole units and two more fifths. Each whole unit is five fifths, so four wholes contribute twenty fifths, and adding the two extra fifths gives twenty-two fifths in total. Every piece involved was a fifth, so the denominator is still five. The multiply-then-add rule — whole number times denominator plus numerator — is just a fast way of counting all the fifths. Learners who change the denominator have usually confused this with building an equivalent fraction, which does change the piece size but then changes the numerator to match. Checking the size also helps: twenty-two fifths should be a little more than four, and it is, whereas a wrong denominator typically gives a value that is obviously too big or too small.

### subtopic_2 — Simplifying with the Highest Common Factor
**Assistant:** If I cannot see the highest common factor, is simplifying in stages acceptable?

**Tutor:** Entirely acceptable, and often wise. Dividing numerator and denominator by any common factor is a correct step, so repeating it with small primes — twos, threes, fives — until nothing further divides both reaches the same simplest form as a single division by the HCF. Thirty-six over forty-eight halves to eighteen over twenty-four, halves again to nine over twelve, and dividing by three gives three quarters, exactly the result of dividing by twelve at once. The staged route takes longer and gives more opportunities for an arithmetic slip, which is why the HCF is preferred when the numbers are familiar, but a correct slow answer beats a wrong fast one. Whichever route you take, the final check is the same: the numerator and denominator must share no factor other than one. If both are even or both end in zero or five, you are not finished.

### subtopic_3 — Equivalent Fractions, Comparing and Ordering
**Assistant:** Why does cross-multiplication tell me which of two fractions is larger?

**Tutor:** Because it compares the numerators the two fractions would have over a common denominator, without writing that denominator down. For five eighths and seven elevenths, the common denominator eighty-eight is the product of eight and eleven. Five eighths over eighty-eight has numerator five times eleven, fifty-five, and seven elevenths over eighty-eight has numerator seven times eight, fifty-six. Since fifty-six is larger, seven elevenths is larger. Cross-multiplying simply skips writing the eighty-eights. Two cautions: the method works only for positive fractions as taught here, and each product must be matched with the fraction whose numerator it came from, not the fraction whose denominator it used. For three or more fractions, cross-multiplication becomes clumsy, and rewriting all of them over the lowest common multiple of the denominators is the cleaner method.

### subtopic_4 — Cancelling in Products and Examination Technique
**Assistant:** Why is cancelling allowed in a product but not in a sum?

**Tutor:** Because a product of fractions is a single fraction whose numerator is the product of all the tops and whose denominator is the product of all the bottoms. Fourteen fifteenths times twenty-five twenty-firsts is fourteen times twenty-five over fifteen times twenty-one, one fraction, and dividing its top and bottom by the same factor is legitimate wherever that factor sits. A sum is different: two thirds plus three fifths is two separate fractions that must be rewritten over a common denominator before they can be combined, and the three in one numerator has nothing to do with the three in the other denominator. Cancelling them would be dividing one fraction by three and multiplying the other by three, which changes the total. The same rule follows you into algebra: common factors of the whole numerator and the whole denominator may be cancelled, but individual terms in a sum may not.
