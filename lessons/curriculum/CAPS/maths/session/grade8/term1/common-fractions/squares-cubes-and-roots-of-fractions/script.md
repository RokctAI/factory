# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers squares, cubes, square roots and cube roots of common fractions and mixed numbers. The governing rule is that a power or root of a fraction is the power or root of the numerator over the power or root of the denominator. The anchor results are that two thirds squared equals four ninths, the square root of nine sixteenths equals three quarters, and one and a half squared equals two and a quarter. Mixed numbers are converted before any power is taken.

## Subtopic: Squaring a Fraction — Top Squared Over Bottom Squared

To square a fraction, multiply it by itself, and multiplication of fractions is top times top over bottom times bottom. Two thirds squared is two thirds times two thirds, which is two times two over three times three, four ninths. So the square of a fraction is the square of the numerator over the square of the denominator. Write it generally: a over b, all squared, equals a squared over b squared.

More examples to make it automatic. Three quarters squared is nine sixteenths. One fifth squared is one twenty-fifth. Five halves squared is twenty-five quarters, which is six and a quarter. Notice that the rule needs the whole fraction to be squared — the brackets around the fraction matter. Two over three squared, with only the three squared, would be two ninths, a different number. In an examination the brackets tell you which is meant; in your own writing, always bracket the fraction before applying the power.

Here is the fact that surprises learners. Two thirds is about zero comma six seven. Four ninths is about zero comma four four. The square is smaller than the fraction. For whole numbers greater than one, squaring makes things bigger; for fractions between zero and one, squaring makes things smaller, because you are taking a part of a part. Two thirds of two thirds is less than two thirds. Keep this as a sense check: if you square a proper fraction and the answer is bigger, you have made an error.

Mixed numbers must be converted first, exactly as for multiplication. One and a half squared. One and a half is three halves; three halves squared is nine quarters, two and a quarter. The common error is to square the whole part and the fraction part separately, giving one and a quarter — wrong by a whole unit. There are no shortcuts through the whole part for powers.

Negative fractions follow the integer rules. Negative three quarters, all squared, is nine sixteenths, since two negatives multiply to a positive. Negative three quarters cubed is negative twenty-seven over sixty-four, since three negatives multiply to a negative. Questions on squaring fractions follow now.

## Subtopic: Cubing a Fraction

Cubing is the same rule with three copies. A over b, all cubed, equals a cubed over b cubed. One half cubed is one over eight. Two thirds cubed is eight over twenty-seven. Three fifths cubed is twenty-seven over one hundred and twenty-five. The perfect cubes from the integers lesson — one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and sixteen, three hundred and forty-three, five hundred and twelve, seven hundred and twenty-nine, one thousand — now appear in both numerators and denominators, so keep them fresh.

The shrinking effect is stronger for cubes. One half is zero comma five; a half squared is a quarter, zero comma two five; a half cubed is an eighth, zero comma one two five. Each extra power on a proper fraction takes a further part of a part. Compare with a fraction bigger than one: three halves cubed is twenty-seven eighths, three and three eighths, which grows with each power. Whether a power makes a number grow or shrink depends only on whether the base is above or below one.

Mixed numbers convert first, as always. One and one third cubed. One and one third is four thirds; four thirds cubed is sixty-four over twenty-seven, which is two and ten twenty-sevenths. Check against the error of cubing the parts separately: one cubed plus one third cubed would be one and one twenty-seventh, far too small. The improper fraction route is the only route.

A mixed example with a square and a cube together. Two thirds squared plus one half cubed. Four ninths plus one eighth. Common denominator seventy-two: thirty-two seventy-seconds plus nine seventy-seconds is forty-one seventy-seconds. Powers first, then the addition over a common denominator — the order of operations is unchanged by fractions.

Negative bases again: negative one half cubed is negative one eighth, because an odd power keeps the sign. Negative one half to the power four is one sixteenth, because an even power loses it. Questions on cubing follow.

## Subtopic: Square Roots of Fractions

The square root undoes the square, so it also works on the numerator and denominator separately: the square root of a over b equals the square root of a over the square root of b. The square root of nine sixteenths is root nine over root sixteen, three over four. Check: three quarters squared is nine sixteenths. The square root of four ninths is two thirds. The square root of one over twenty-five is one fifth. For this to produce a neat answer, both numerator and denominator must be perfect squares, and that is how examination questions are set.

Notice the direction of size. Three quarters is bigger than nine sixteenths. Taking a square root of a proper fraction makes it bigger, exactly reversing the shrink of squaring. Root of a quarter is a half; root of one hundredth is one tenth. If a root of a proper fraction comes out smaller than the fraction, something is wrong.

Mixed numbers convert first. The square root of two and a quarter. Two and a quarter is nine quarters; root of nine quarters is three halves, one and a half. Check: one and a half squared is two and a quarter — the anchor from the first section, run backwards. The error to avoid is rooting the parts separately, root two plus root a quarter, which is not even a rational number.

Sometimes a fraction must be simplified before its root is visible. Root of eight over eighteen. Neither eight nor eighteen is a perfect square, but eight over eighteen simplifies to four ninths, whose root is two thirds. Always simplify first and then look for perfect squares.

As with integers, the root sign means the non-negative root, and a negative under a square root has no real value. Root of negative four ninths is not a real number; the negative of root four ninths is negative two thirds. Position of the sign decides, as before. Questions on square roots follow.

## Subtopic: Cube Roots and Mixed Calculations

The cube root works the same way: cube root of a over b is cube root of a over cube root of b. Cube root of eight over twenty-seven is two thirds. Cube root of one over one hundred and twenty-five is one fifth. Cube root of sixty-four over one thousand is four tenths, which simplifies to two fifths. And because cubing keeps the sign, cube roots of negative fractions are fine: cube root of negative one eighth is negative one half, since negative one half cubed is negative one eighth.

Mixed numbers convert first. Cube root of three and three eighths. Three and three eighths is twenty-seven eighths; cube root is three halves, one and a half. Check: one and a half cubed is twenty-seven eighths. Correct.

Now a full mixed calculation at examination standard. Root of four ninths plus cube root of one eighth minus two thirds squared. Each power and root first: root of four ninths is two thirds; cube root of one eighth is one half; two thirds squared is four ninths. Then: two thirds plus one half minus four ninths. Common denominator eighteen: twelve eighteenths plus nine eighteenths minus eight eighteenths is thirteen eighteenths. Three lines: the powers and roots, the common denominator, the answer.

A second, with a product. Three quarters squared times root of sixteen over nine. Three quarters squared is nine sixteenths; root of sixteen over nine is four thirds. Nine sixteenths times four thirds: cancel four into sixteen leaving four, cancel three into nine leaving three, giving three quarters. Interesting — the answer equals the original base, which happens because four thirds is the reciprocal of three quarters, and three quarters squared times four thirds is three quarters times one.

Distinction-level takeaway: powers and roots of fractions act on top and bottom separately; mixed numbers are converted first and never split; squaring a proper fraction shrinks it and rooting enlarges it; simplify before rooting; and in a mixed calculation, do every power and root before combining. Questions on cube roots and mixed calculations follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same powers and roots with a square garden bed, a cube-shaped box and a photograph being shrunk. You will see with your eyes why squaring a fraction makes it smaller, and why a root makes it bigger again.

## Subtopic: A Garden Bed Two Thirds of a Metre Wide

Let us build a square garden bed with sides two thirds of a metre. What is its area? Area of a square is side times side, so two thirds times two thirds. Picture a one-metre square, cut into thirds both ways: nine small squares, each one ninth of a square metre. Your bed covers two by two of them, four small squares. Four ninths of a square metre. Two thirds squared is four ninths, and you can see every one of the nine squares.

Now notice something odd. The side was two thirds of a metre, and the area is four ninths of a square metre, which is less than two thirds. Squaring made the number smaller. Why? Because the bed is less than a metre each way, so it covers less than two thirds of a one-metre strip — it is two thirds of a strip that is itself only two thirds of the big square. Part of a part. Whenever the side is shorter than one metre, the area number comes out smaller than the side number.

Compare a bed with sides one and a half metres. Convert: three halves. Area three halves times three halves is nine quarters, two and a quarter square metres. Bigger than the side, because a side longer than one metre covers more than one strip each way. And watch the common mistake: a learner squares the one to get one and the half to get a quarter and says one and a quarter square metres. Draw it — a one-and-a-half by one-and-a-half square has a full metre square, two half-metre strips, and a quarter in the corner: one plus a half plus a half plus a quarter is two and a quarter. The two strips are the cross terms that the shortcut forgets.

Now the root question. A square bed has an area of nine sixteenths of a square metre. How long is each side? The number that squares to nine sixteenths. Three quarters, because three quarters times three quarters is nine sixteenths. The root of nine sixteenths is three quarters, and the side is longer than the area number, because taking a root undoes the shrink.

So: area of a square is the side squared, top squared over bottom squared, and a side less than a metre gives an area less than the side. The questions now are garden beds; draw the grid if you need to.

## Subtopic: A Half-Metre Box and a Shrinking Photo

Cubes next. A box is a cube with every edge half a metre. What is its volume? Half times half times half. Picture a one-metre cube cut in half every way: eight small cubes. Your box is one of them. One eighth of a cubic metre. A half cubed is an eighth. And look how fast the number dropped: a half, then a quarter, then an eighth. Each extra half makes the box a part of a part of a part.

A box with edges two thirds of a metre: two thirds cubed is eight twenty-sevenths of a cubic metre. Cut the metre cube into thirds every way and you get twenty-seven little cubes; your box fills two by two by two, eight of them. The cube rule — top cubed over bottom cubed — is just counting little cubes.

The cube root is the reverse. A cubic box holds eight twenty-sevenths of a cubic metre; how long is an edge? The number that cubes to eight twenty-sevenths: two thirds. The cube root of eight twenty-sevenths is two thirds. And a box with a volume of three and three eighths cubic metres? Convert: twenty-seven eighths. Cube root: three halves. Edges of one and a half metres. Check: one and a half, three times over, is three and three eighths.

Now a different picture for powers: a shrinking photo. Each time you tap, the photo shrinks to two thirds of its size. After one tap: two thirds. After two: two thirds squared, four ninths. After three: eight twenty-sevenths. The photo gets small fast, because every tap keeps only two thirds of what was left. Powers of a proper fraction are repeated shrinking.

And the root is the question backwards: the photo is now four ninths of its original size after two equal taps — what did each tap do? Root of four ninths: two thirds. Each tap kept two thirds. The questions now are boxes and photos; for each, count the little cubes or the taps.

## Subtopic: Bigger or Smaller? The Takeaway

Here is the one question that organises this whole lesson: will the answer be bigger or smaller than what I started with? For a fraction between zero and one, squaring and cubing make it smaller — part of a part — and square roots and cube roots make it bigger, because they undo the shrinking. For a number bigger than one, it is the other way round: powers grow it, roots shrink it. And one itself never moves: one squared is one, root one is one.

Run the check on the anchors. Two thirds squared is four ninths: two thirds is a proper fraction, so the square must be smaller, and four ninths is. Root of nine sixteenths is three quarters: nine sixteenths is proper, so the root must be bigger, and three quarters is. One and a half squared is two and a quarter: one and a half is bigger than one, so the square must be bigger, and it is. Three checks, three passes, no calculator.

Now the three mistakes the check catches. Squaring only the bottom: two over three squared read as two ninths — but two ninths is a lot smaller than two thirds, suspiciously small, and the brackets would have said the whole fraction is squared. Splitting a mixed number: one and a half squared as one and a quarter — smaller than it should be, and smaller than one and a half itself, which cannot happen for a number bigger than one. Rooting without simplifying: root of eight over eighteen looks impossible until you simplify to four ninths and get two thirds.

Your routine, then: bracket the fraction, convert any mixed number, apply the power or root to top and bottom separately, simplify, and ask bigger or smaller. Five moves, and every question in this topic goes through them.

You started knowing how to square a whole number, and you are leaving able to square, cube and root any fraction or mixed number and to predict the size of the answer before you start — that is a genuine win, and it is exactly the skill the exponents topic will build on next term. The final questions mix powers and roots; ask bigger or smaller before each one.
