### subtopic_1 — Squaring a Fraction — Top Squared Over Bottom Squared
**Assistant:** Why does squaring a proper fraction make it smaller when squaring a whole number makes it bigger?

**Tutor:** Because squaring means multiplying a number by itself, and multiplying by a number less than one takes only a part of what you had. Two thirds squared is two thirds of two thirds, a part of a part, so it must be less than two thirds. With a whole number such as five, squaring means five lots of five, which is more than five. The dividing line is one: one squared is one, numbers above one grow when squared, and numbers between zero and one shrink. The garden-bed picture shows it directly — a square with sides shorter than a metre covers less than a strip of width one, so its area number is smaller than its side number. This size behaviour is worth memorising as a check, because the most common errors on this topic, such as squaring only the denominator or splitting a mixed number, all produce answers that fail it.

### subtopic_1 — Squaring a Fraction — Top Squared Over Bottom Squared
**Assistant:** What goes wrong when a mixed number is squared part by part?

**Tutor:** The cross terms are lost. A mixed number such as one and a half is a sum, one plus a half, and squaring a sum means multiplying every part by every part: one times one, one times a half, a half times one, and a half times a half. Squaring the parts separately keeps only the first and last of those four products, giving one and a quarter, and drops the two middle products, which total one. The correct answer is two and a quarter, found by converting to three halves and squaring to nine quarters. In the garden-bed picture the two lost products are the two half-metre strips along the sides of the one-metre square, and a drawing makes the missing area obvious. The rule is therefore absolute: convert every mixed number to an improper fraction before applying any power or root, and never apply the power to the whole part and fraction part separately.

### subtopic_3 — Square Roots of Fractions
**Assistant:** Why should a fraction be simplified before taking its square root?

**Tutor:** Because the rule for roots of fractions needs the numerator and the denominator to be perfect squares, and an unsimplified fraction can hide that structure. Eight over eighteen has neither a perfect-square top nor a perfect-square bottom, so root of eight over root of eighteen produces nothing neat. Simplified, eight over eighteen is four ninths, and root of four over root of nine is two thirds immediately. Simplifying never changes the value of the fraction, so it never changes the root; it only changes how easy the root is to see. The same advice applies to cube roots. The habit to build is to glance at every fraction under a root sign and divide top and bottom by their highest common factor before doing anything else. If after simplifying the top or the bottom is still not a perfect square, the root is not rational, and at Grade 8 level the question would not be asked.

### subtopic_6 — A Half-Metre Box and a Shrinking Photo
**Assistant:** How can a photo shrinking on each tap explain powers of a fraction?

**Tutor:** Each tap multiplies the current size by the same fraction, so after several taps the original has been multiplied by that fraction several times, which is a power. If every tap keeps two thirds of the picture, then after one tap it is two thirds of the original, after two taps it is two thirds of two thirds, which is two thirds squared or four ninths, and after three taps it is two thirds cubed, eight twenty-sevenths. The picture shrinks quickly because each tap discards a third of whatever is left, so powers of a proper fraction fall towards zero. Running the story backwards gives roots: if a photo is four ninths of its original size after two equal taps, each tap kept the square root of four ninths, which is two thirds. The same model will reappear in Grade 9 when repeated multiplication becomes exponential growth and decay.
