# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies common fractions and mixed numbers to problems in context: grouping, which is division by a fraction; sharing, which is division by a whole number; fractions of whole numbers, which is multiplication; and multi-step problems involving remainders and unknown wholes. The anchor problems are seven and a half metres of fabric cut into three-quarter-metre pieces, giving ten pieces, and a shopper with six hundred rand who spends one third and then one quarter of the remainder, leaving three hundred rand.

## Subtopic: Recognising the Operation — Grouping, Sharing and Of

Word problems with fractions use three operations, and each has a signature. Grouping asks how many pieces of a given size fit into an amount — that is division by the size of the piece. Sharing asks how much each person gets when an amount is split equally — division by the number of people. And of asks for a fraction of an amount — multiplication. Identify the signature first, and write the operation before any calculation.

Grouping. Seven and a half metres of fabric is cut into pieces of three quarters of a metre. How many pieces? How many three-quarters fit into seven and a half: seven and a half divided by three quarters. Convert: fifteen halves divided by three quarters is fifteen halves times four thirds. Cancel three into fifteen leaving five, two into four leaving two: five times two, ten. Ten pieces. Sense check: three quarters is a bit less than one, so a bit more than seven and a half pieces — ten is plausible.

Sharing. Three and three quarter kilograms of sweets are shared equally among five children. How much each? Three and three quarters divided by five: fifteen quarters times one fifth, three quarters. Three quarters of a kilogram each. Sense check: five lots of three quarters is fifteen quarters, three and three quarters. Correct.

Of. Two fifths of a class of forty-five learners play netball. How many? Two fifths times forty-five: forty-five divided by five is nine, times two is eighteen. Eighteen learners. Of means multiply, always.

The tell-tale words. How many pieces, bags, cups, lengths: grouping, divide by the piece size. Shared equally, each, per person: sharing, divide by the number. Of, fraction of, part of: multiply. A question may contain two of these in sequence, and then the order in the story is the order of the operations. Questions on recognising the operation follow.

## Subtopic: Fractions of a Remainder

The problems that separate strong candidates involve a fraction of what is left. The anchor: a shopper has six hundred rand. She spends one third on groceries, then one quarter of the remainder on airtime. How much is left? Work it in order. One third of six hundred is two hundred, spent. Remainder: four hundred. One quarter of the remainder is one hundred, spent. Left: three hundred rand.

The trap is the phrase of the remainder. One quarter of the remainder is not one quarter of the original six hundred, which would be one hundred and fifty. It is one quarter of four hundred. Read the word remainder as a signal to use the current amount, not the starting amount.

Now the same problem answered as a fraction of the original, which the examiner may ask. Groceries: one third of the whole. Airtime: one quarter of two thirds of the whole, which is one quarter times two thirds, two twelfths, one sixth. Total spent: one third plus one sixth is two sixths plus one sixth, three sixths, one half. Left: one half of six hundred, three hundred rand. Same answer by a different route, and the fraction route works even when the amount is unknown.

A second example. A tank is two thirds full. One quarter of the tank's full capacity is used. What fraction is left? Two thirds minus one quarter: eight twelfths minus three twelfths, five twelfths. If the tank holds four hundred and eighty litres, five twelfths of four hundred and eighty is two hundred litres. Note this one subtracted a fraction of the capacity, not a fraction of the contents — the wording decides, so read it twice.

A third, with a fraction of the contents. A tank is two thirds full and one quarter of the water in it is used. Used: one quarter of two thirds, one sixth of the capacity. Left: two thirds minus one sixth is four sixths minus one sixth, three sixths, one half. Compare: five twelfths when a quarter of the capacity is used, one half when a quarter of the contents is used. The difference between those two readings is a whole mark. Questions on remainders follow.

## Subtopic: Finding the Whole from a Part

Sometimes the fraction and the part are given and the whole is unknown. Three fifths of a number is twenty-four. Find the number. If three fifths is twenty-four, then one fifth is twenty-four divided by three, eight, and five fifths is eight times five, forty. The number is forty. Check: three fifths of forty is twenty-four. This unitary method — find one part, then all the parts — is the Grade 8 way and earns full marks.

The same problem as a division: the whole is twenty-four divided by three fifths, which is twenty-four times five thirds, forty. Dividing by the fraction undoes multiplying by it. Both routes are correct; the unitary route is easier to explain, the division route is faster.

A contextual example. A learner spends two sevenths of her pocket money and has one hundred and fifty rand left. How much did she start with? If two sevenths is spent, five sevenths is left, and five sevenths equals one hundred and fifty rand. One seventh is thirty rand. Seven sevenths is two hundred and ten rand. Check: two sevenths of two hundred and ten is sixty, and two hundred and ten minus sixty is one hundred and fifty. Correct. The trap: dividing one hundred and fifty by two sevenths, which treats the amount left as the amount spent. Always ask which fraction the given amount represents.

Another. After a bus had travelled three eighths of its route, forty-five kilometres remained. How long is the route? Remaining fraction five eighths equals forty-five kilometres; one eighth is nine; the route is seventy-two kilometres.

Layout for marks: write the fraction that matches the given amount, find one part, scale up to the whole, and check by working forwards. Four lines. Questions on finding the whole follow.

## Subtopic: Multi-Step Problems and Examination Technique

Examination problems chain two or three of these ideas. Here is one at the top of the Grade 8 range. A baker has twelve and a half kilograms of flour. She uses two fifths of it for bread, then packs the rest into bags of three quarters of a kilogram. How many full bags, and how much flour is left over?

Line one, the bread: two fifths of twelve and a half. Twelve and a half is twenty-five halves; two fifths times twenty-five halves, cancel five into twenty-five leaving five, two into two: five kilograms. Line two, the remainder: twelve and a half minus five is seven and a half kilograms. Line three, grouping: seven and a half divided by three quarters is ten, as in the fabric anchor. Ten full bags, and since ten times three quarters is exactly seven and a half, nothing is left over. Three operations — of, subtract, group — in the order the story gave them.

A second. A recipe for twelve biscuits needs one and one third cups of flour. How many cups for thirty biscuits? Flour per biscuit: four thirds divided by twelve is one ninth of a cup. Thirty biscuits: thirty ninths, three and one third cups. Or scale: thirty is two and a half times twelve, so two and a half times four thirds is five halves times four thirds, ten thirds, three and one third. Both routes earn full marks.

The technique checklist. Read the whole problem before writing. Underline the fraction words and the quantity each fraction is of — the whole, the remainder, the capacity, the contents. Write each step as a fraction operation on its own line with the operation named. Keep units through the working. Give the final answer as a sentence, and check it against a rough estimate: twelve and a half kilograms, two fifths used, so about seven and a half left, in three-quarter bags, about ten. Good.

Distinction-level takeaway: name the operation from the story's signature words, use the current amount for anything described as a remainder, match the fraction to the given quantity when finding a whole, and work the steps in story order. Questions on multi-step problems follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problems told slowly, with the question asked out loud before any numbers move: how many fit, how much each, what fraction of what. Once you can say which question the story is asking, the fraction maths is the easy part.

## Subtopic: Say the Question First

Here is the habit that fixes most fraction word problems: before you touch a number, say the question in one of three ways. How many fit? How much each? How much is this fraction of that? If you can say it, you know the operation.

Fabric. Seven and a half metres, cut into pieces three quarters of a metre long. Say it: how many three-quarter pieces fit into seven and a half metres? That is a how-many-fit question, so divide by the piece size. Seven and a half divided by three quarters. Flip and multiply: fifteen halves times four thirds, ten pieces. Picture it: every metre gives you one piece and a third of the next, so seven and a half metres gives roughly seven pieces plus the leftover thirds adding up — ten feels right.

Sweets. Three and three quarter kilograms shared among five children. Say it: how much does each child get? That is a how-much-each question, so divide by the number of children. Fifteen quarters divided by five is three quarters of a kilogram each. Picture it: fifteen little quarter-kilogram bags, five children, three bags each.

Netball. Two fifths of forty-five learners. Say it: how much is two fifths of forty-five? That is an of question, so multiply. Forty-five divided by five is nine, times two is eighteen learners.

Now the tricky part, where the same numbers can be two different questions. Six litres and two-thirds-litre bottles. How many bottles can I fill? How many fit — six divided by two thirds, nine bottles. But: two thirds of six litres is how much? That is of — two thirds times six, four litres. Same six, same two thirds, different question, different operation, different answer. The words decide.

So before every fraction problem, say the question: how many fit, how much each, or how much of. Then the operation picks itself. The questions now give you a story each; say the question before you choose.

## Subtopic: Of What? Following the Money

The problem that catches almost everyone is the one with a remainder, so let us walk through the money slowly. Six hundred rand in your pocket. You spend one third on groceries. One third of six hundred is two hundred — the of question, multiply. You have four hundred left. Then you spend one quarter of what is left on airtime. Stop. Of what? Of what is left. Not of six hundred. Of four hundred. One quarter of four hundred is one hundred. Now you have three hundred rand.

The wrong turn is taking a quarter of the original six hundred, one hundred and fifty, and ending with two hundred and fifty. The story said of the remainder, and the remainder was four hundred. Every time you see a fraction in a word problem, ask of what, and point at the amount it belongs to. Write the amount down next to the fraction before you multiply.

Here is a second way to see the same problem that will impress a marker. Instead of following the rands, follow the fractions. You spent one third of the whole. Then you spent a quarter of the two thirds that remained: a quarter of two thirds is two twelfths, one sixth of the whole. One third plus one sixth is one half — you spent half of your money altogether. Half of six hundred is three hundred. Same answer, and this route would still work if nobody had told you the six hundred.

Now the tank. Two thirds full. One quarter of the tank is used. Of what? Of the tank, the whole capacity. So subtract a quarter from two thirds: eight twelfths minus three twelfths, five twelfths full. But if the sentence had said one quarter of the water is used, then of what means of the two thirds, and a quarter of two thirds is one sixth, so two thirds minus one sixth is one half. Two words — the tank, the water — and the answer moves from five twelfths to one half.

So: every fraction belongs to something, and your job is to find out what. Say of what, point at it, write it down. The questions now are money and tank questions; for every fraction, say of what before you multiply.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards — The Takeaway

Last skill, and it is the one that feels like detective work: the whole is missing and you have to find it. Three fifths of a number is twenty-four. What is the number? Think of the number as five equal blocks. Three of those blocks make twenty-four, so one block is eight. Five blocks is forty. Done — and it took no formula, just blocks.

A learner spends two sevenths of her pocket money and has one hundred and fifty rand left. Draw seven blocks. Two are spent, five are left, and those five blocks are one hundred and fifty rand. One block is thirty rand. All seven blocks is two hundred and ten rand. The trap here is pairing one hundred and fifty with the two sevenths that were spent; the one hundred and fifty is what is left, so it goes with five sevenths. Read which fraction the money belongs to — of what, again.

A bus has travelled three eighths of its route and forty-five kilometres remain. Eight blocks, three done, five left, five blocks is forty-five, one block is nine, the route is seventy-two kilometres. Same picture every time: draw the blocks, label the ones you know, find one block, count them all.

Here is the whole lesson in one breath. Say the question: how many fit means divide by the piece, how much each means divide by the people, how much of means multiply. Ask of what for every fraction, and use the remainder when the story says remainder. Draw blocks to find a missing whole. Work in story order. Finish with a sentence and a unit.

You started this topic able to add two fractions on a worksheet, and you are leaving able to read a story about flour, fabric, money or a water tank and turn it into the right operations in the right order — that is a real step up, and it is exactly what Grade 9 word problems will expect of you. The final questions mix all of it; say the question, ask of what, and draw blocks if the whole is missing.
