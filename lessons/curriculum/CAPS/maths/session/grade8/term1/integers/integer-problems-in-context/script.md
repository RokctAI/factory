# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies the four operations with integers to problems in context: temperature change, altitude difference, bank balances, scoring systems, time zones and depth. The method is to assign a sign convention, translate the words into a single expression, evaluate with the order of operations, and interpret the result in the language of the problem. The anchor is a bank balance of two hundred and fifty rand plus three times negative one hundred and eighty plus five hundred, which equals two hundred and ten rand.

## Subtopic: The Method — Sign Convention, Expression, Evaluate, Interpret

Every integer problem in context is solved in four moves, and writing all four is what earns full marks. Move one: state the sign convention. Decide which direction is positive and write it down — above sea level positive, below negative; money in positive, money out negative; after noon positive, before noon negative. Move two: translate the story into a single expression with brackets around every negative. Move three: evaluate using the order of operations and the sign rules. Move four: interpret the integer as a sentence in the language of the problem — a temperature, a balance, a depth.

Apply it. The temperature in Sutherland was negative nine degrees at dawn and six degrees at midday. Find the change. Convention: temperatures as given, rise positive. Expression: change is final minus initial, six minus negative nine. Evaluate: six plus nine, fifteen. Interpret: the temperature rose by fifteen degrees. Learners who write six minus nine, negative three, have not bracketed the negative start and have found nothing meaningful.

A second. The Dead Sea shore is four hundred and thirty metres below sea level; the summit of Kilimanjaro is five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five metres above. Find the difference in height. Convention: above sea level positive. Expression: five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five minus negative four hundred and thirty. Evaluate: five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five plus four hundred and thirty, six thousand three hundred and twenty-five. Interpret: the summit is six thousand three hundred and twenty-five metres higher than the shore.

The pattern in both: a difference between a positive and a negative is found by subtracting the negative, which adds the two absolute values. A difference is always reported as a positive number; the sign tells you direction, which the question has already fixed by asking how much higher or how much warmer.

Why insist on move one? Because without a written convention you cannot defend a sign. If a marker sees negative four hundred and thirty with no statement that below sea level is negative, the sign is a guess. One short sentence turns a guess into a method mark. Questions on the method follow.

## Subtopic: Money — Balances, Debit Orders and Deposits

Bank problems combine all four operations, and the anchor is typical. An account has two hundred and fifty rand. Three debit orders of one hundred and eighty rand each go off, and then a deposit of five hundred rand is made. Find the final balance. Convention: money in positive, money out negative. Expression: two hundred and fifty plus three times negative one hundred and eighty plus five hundred. Evaluate, multiplication first: three times negative one hundred and eighty is negative five hundred and forty. Then left to right: two hundred and fifty plus negative five hundred and forty is negative two hundred and ninety; negative two hundred and ninety plus five hundred is two hundred and ten. Interpret: the final balance is two hundred and ten rand.

Notice the intermediate value. After the debit orders and before the deposit, the account stood at negative two hundred and ninety rand — overdrawn. A question may ask for that too, and the expression already contains it. Writing the calculation in order gives you both answers.

A second scenario with division. Four friends share the cost of a two hundred and forty rand data bundle equally, paying from a shared account that held one hundred rand. Then one friend is refunded sixty rand by the network. Find the account balance. Convention as before. The bundle: negative two hundred and forty. The account: one hundred plus negative two hundred and forty plus sixty, which is negative eighty rand. Each friend's share of the bundle: negative two hundred and forty divided by four, negative sixty each. The questions may ask for either the account or the share; label each answer.

A third, with a product of a negative rate and time. A spaza shop loses thirty-five rand a day on an unpopular stock line for twelve days, then sells the remaining stock for a once-off two hundred rand. Net result: twelve times negative thirty-five plus two hundred. Twelve times negative thirty-five is negative four hundred and twenty; plus two hundred is negative two hundred and twenty. Interpret: a net loss of two hundred and twenty rand. Write the word loss; a bare negative number is not an interpretation.

Technique point: in money problems, the negative sign and the word owed or loss say the same thing, and your final sentence should use the word, not the sign. Questions on money follow now.

## Subtopic: Scoring Systems and Time Zones

Scoring systems with penalties are a favourite context because they combine products of positives and negatives in one line. A quiz awards four points for a correct answer, deducts two for a wrong answer, and gives zero for a blank. A learner answers twelve correctly, five wrongly and leaves three blank. Convention: points gained positive, deducted negative. Expression: twelve times four plus five times negative two plus three times zero. Evaluate: forty-eight plus negative ten plus zero, thirty-eight. Interpret: a score of thirty-eight points.

The same system, different learner: seven correct, eleven wrong, two blank. Twenty-eight plus negative twenty-two plus zero, six points. And a learner with four correct and sixteen wrong: sixteen plus negative thirty-two, negative sixteen. A negative score is possible under this system, and the question may ask you to say so. Also note the role of the zero: multiplying by zero contributes nothing, so blanks are safe; the expression shows exactly why guessing is risky.

Golf uses integers relative to par. A golfer's four rounds are negative three, plus two, negative one and plus four. Total: negative three plus two is negative one; negative one plus negative one is negative two; negative two plus four is two. Two over par for the tournament. Or group: positives two plus four is six, negatives negative three plus negative one is negative four, six plus negative four is two.

Time zones are a subtraction problem. South Africa is two hours ahead of Universal Time, written plus two; New York in winter is five hours behind, negative five. The time difference between them is two minus negative five, which is seven hours. If it is three in the afternoon in Johannesburg, fifteen hundred hours, then in New York it is fifteen minus seven, eight in the morning. Convention: ahead of Universal Time positive, and the difference is the larger offset minus the smaller.

One more: Perth is plus eight. The difference between Perth and New York is eight minus negative five, thirteen hours. Difference questions on a time-zone map are always higher offset minus lower offset, exactly like the altitude problem. Questions on scoring and time zones are with you now.

## Subtopic: Depth, Direction and Examination Technique

Depth problems combine several moves in sequence, and left-to-right evaluation with a clear convention keeps them straight. A submarine is at one hundred and twenty metres below the surface. It rises forty-five metres, then dives eighty metres. Find its final depth. Convention: above the surface positive, so below is negative, rising is positive, diving is negative. Expression: negative one hundred and twenty plus forty-five plus negative eighty. Evaluate: negative one hundred and twenty plus forty-five is negative seventy-five; negative seventy-five plus negative eighty is negative one hundred and fifty-five. Interpret: the submarine is one hundred and fifty-five metres below the surface.

A diver descends at a steady six metres per minute for seven minutes from the surface, then ascends at four metres per minute for five minutes. Expression: seven times negative six plus five times four. Negative forty-two plus twenty is negative twenty-two. The diver is twenty-two metres below the surface. The rate-times-time structure is the same as the debit-order structure in the money section: a repeated change is a product.

A mine shaft question with division. A lift descends from the surface to negative six hundred metres in four equal stages. The change per stage is negative six hundred divided by four, negative one hundred and fifty metres: each stage goes down one hundred and fifty metres. After three stages the lift is at three times negative one hundred and fifty, negative four hundred and fifty metres.

Now the examination technique that ties the whole topic together. First, read the question twice and underline every quantity, with its direction. Second, write the convention in words. Third, write one expression with brackets around negatives, and resist the urge to calculate as you read. Fourth, evaluate in order, one line per operation type. Fifth, answer in a sentence with a unit, and check the sign against common sense: a submarine that dives further should end up more negative, and a quiz score with many wrong answers should be low or negative.

Distinction-level takeaway: context problems with integers are translation problems. The arithmetic is a few lines; the marks are in the convention, the expression and the interpreted sentence. Questions on depth and technique follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problem-solving, but slower and with the stories front and centre: a quiz with penalties, a submarine, a golfer, and the day your account went into the red. The arithmetic is the easy part; the translation is what we practise.

## Subtopic: The Quiz Night — Scores That Go Negative

Let us slow down and live inside one problem. It is quiz night at the community hall. Four points for every right answer, two points taken off for every wrong one, nothing for a blank. Your team gets twelve right, five wrong and leaves three blank. Before any arithmetic, say the story as signs: twelve lots of plus four, five lots of negative two, three lots of nothing.

Now the easy part. Twelve times four is forty-eight — that is your earning. Five times negative two is negative ten — that is your penalty. Three times zero is zero — the blanks did nothing. Forty-eight plus negative ten is thirty-eight. Your team has thirty-eight points. Notice that you did the three little products first and only then added them; you cannot add a penalty to a number of right answers, you have to turn each into points first.

Now the rival team. They guess everything: four right, sixteen wrong. Four times four is sixteen points earned. Sixteen times negative two is negative thirty-two in penalties. Sixteen plus negative thirty-two is negative sixteen. They finish on negative sixteen — below zero. Can you really have fewer than no points? In this scoring system, yes, and that is exactly what integers are for: a score that has gone past zero into debt. The sign tells the story.

Here is the strategic question the quiz master likes. Your team is stuck on a question. Should you guess or leave it blank? Blank: plus zero. Guess: either plus four or negative two. If you are fairly sure, guess. If you have no idea, the blank protects you. The integers make the decision visible.

One more twist. Halfway through, the organisers award a bonus of ten points to every team and then deduct five from every team for a noise penalty. Plus ten plus negative five is plus five for everyone, so the gaps between teams do not change — adding the same integer to every score shifts them all together. Your thirty-eight becomes forty-three; the rivals' negative sixteen becomes negative eleven. Still fifty-four points apart, because forty-three minus negative eleven is fifty-four, and thirty-eight minus negative sixteen was fifty-four too. The gap survived, and finding it meant subtracting a negative. The questions now are quiz-night scores; turn each type of answer into points before you add anything.

## Subtopic: The Day the Account Went Red

Here is the anchor as a story, told slowly, because the moment an account goes negative is the moment learners lose the thread. Monday morning, two hundred and fifty rand in the account. During the day, three debit orders of one hundred and eighty rand each go off: gym, phone, insurance. Tuesday, a deposit of five hundred rand arrives. Where does the account end up?

Say the signs first. Starting money: plus two hundred and fifty. Each debit order: negative one hundred and eighty, three of them. Deposit: plus five hundred. Now the three debit orders are a product, not three separate subtractions you have to drag through: three times negative one hundred and eighty is negative five hundred and forty. Think of it as one big payment out.

Now walk the balance. Two hundred and fifty plus negative five hundred and forty. The money going out is heavier than the money you had, by two hundred and ninety, so the balance is negative two hundred and ninety rand. The account is in the red: you owe the bank two hundred and ninety rand. Then Tuesday's five hundred arrives. Negative two hundred and ninety plus five hundred: the deposit is heavier, by two hundred and ten, so the balance is positive two hundred and ten rand. Back in the black.

Write it as one line and see the order of operations doing the work: two hundred and fifty plus three times negative one hundred and eighty plus five hundred. Multiply first, giving negative five hundred and forty; then add from the left, negative two hundred and ninety, then two hundred and ten. Both answers — the overdraft on Monday night and the final balance — sit inside one expression.

Now the question that matters in real life. What if the deposit had come first? Two hundred and fifty plus five hundred is seven hundred and fifty, minus five hundred and forty is two hundred and ten. Same final balance, because addition is commutative. But the account never went negative, so no overdraft fee. The maths says order does not change the total; the bank says order changes the fees. Integers tell you the final balance; understanding tells you to time your deposits. The questions now follow the account through the week; find the sign of each event before you add.

## Subtopic: Reading the Story — The Takeaway

Last stretch, and it is about words, because the words are where these problems are won or lost. Here is a list of phrases and the sign they carry. Below sea level, below zero, overdrawn, in debt, loss, penalty, descends, dives, behind, before, colder, deducted: negative. Above sea level, deposit, profit, bonus, rises, climbs, ahead, after, warmer, awarded: positive. When you read a problem, underline these words first and write the sign above each one. Half the translation is done before you think about numbers.

Next, the three structures that keep appearing. A change from start to finish is finish minus start, and if the start is negative you are subtracting a negative, so the change is bigger than either number. A repeated equal change — three debit orders, seven minutes of diving, twelve wrong answers — is a product of the number of times and the signed change. A difference between two positions, how far apart or how much higher, is the higher minus the lower and comes out positive. Spot which structure a sentence is and the expression almost writes itself.

Then the one check that catches most errors: ask whether the sign of the answer makes sense in the story. If the submarine dived more than it rose, it should end deeper than it started. If a team got more wrong than right, its score should be low or negative. If deposits beat debit orders, the balance should be positive. A wrong sign is almost always a dropped bracket, and the story tells you to go back and look.

Finally, finish with a sentence. Negative one hundred and fifty-five is a number; the submarine is one hundred and fifty-five metres below the surface is an answer. Negative sixteen is a number; the team finished sixteen points below zero is an answer. Markers award the final mark for the sentence.

You started this topic ordering numbers on a line, and you are finishing by reading a bank statement, a quiz sheet and a dive log and turning each into one correct line of maths — that is a genuine step up, and it is exactly what Grade 9 word problems will ask of you. The final questions are all stories; underline the direction words first.
