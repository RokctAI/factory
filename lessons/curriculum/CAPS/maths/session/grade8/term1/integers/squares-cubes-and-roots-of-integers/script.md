# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers squares, cubes, square roots and cube roots of integers, with particular attention to the sign of a power of a negative integer and to the role of brackets. The anchor calculation is bracket negative five bracket squared minus bracket negative two bracket cubed, which equals thirty-three. The perfect squares to at least fifteen squared and the perfect cubes to at least ten cubed are assumed knowledge and are revised.

## Subtopic: Squares of Integers and the Bracket That Changes Everything

To square an integer is to multiply it by itself. Seven squared is seven times seven, forty-nine. The perfect squares from one to fifteen must be instant: one, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five, thirty-six, forty-nine, sixty-four, eighty-one, one hundred, one hundred and twenty-one, one hundred and forty-four, one hundred and sixty-nine, one hundred and ninety-six, two hundred and twenty-five. Learn them as facts, because every root question in this lesson relies on recognising them.

Now square a negative integer. Bracket negative three bracket squared means negative three times negative three. Same signs, positive: nine. The square of any negative integer is positive, because a negative times itself is a product of two negatives. Bracket negative seven bracket squared is forty-nine; bracket negative twelve bracket squared is one hundred and forty-four. The square of an integer is never negative.

Here is the bracket that changes everything. Negative three squared, written with no brackets, does not mean negative three times negative three. The exponent applies only to the three, because exponents are done before the negative sign — the negative sign is really a multiplication by negative one, and that waits for its turn. Negative three squared is the opposite of three squared, which is negative nine. So bracket negative three bracket squared is nine, and negative three squared without brackets is negative nine. Two different expressions, two different answers, and the examiner will put both in the same question.

Make this your reflex: brackets around the negative mean the whole negative number is squared; no brackets mean only the digit is squared and the negative is attached afterwards. Read aloud exactly what is written. If you see bracket negative four bracket squared, say the square of negative four: sixteen. If you see negative four squared, say the opposite of four squared: negative sixteen.

One more pattern. A square of a negative integer equals the square of the matching positive: bracket negative six bracket squared and six squared are both thirty-six. Every positive perfect square therefore has two integers that square to it, one positive and one negative, and that fact is what the square-root section must deal with. Questions on squares are with you now.

## Subtopic: Cubes of Integers — Sign Survives

To cube an integer is to multiply three copies of it. Four cubed is four times four times four: sixteen times four, sixty-four. The perfect cubes to ten cubed should be as instant as the squares: one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and sixteen, three hundred and forty-three, five hundred and twelve, seven hundred and twenty-nine, one thousand.

Cube a negative integer. Bracket negative two bracket cubed is negative two times negative two times negative two. Pair the first two: positive four. Four times negative two: negative eight. Three negative factors — an odd count — so the product is negative. The cube of a negative integer is negative, and the cube of a positive integer is positive. Unlike squaring, cubing preserves the sign. Bracket negative five bracket cubed is negative one hundred and twenty-five; bracket negative ten bracket cubed is negative one thousand.

The bracket question returns, with a twist. Negative two cubed, no brackets, means the opposite of two cubed: the opposite of eight, negative eight. Bracket negative two bracket cubed is also negative eight. For cubes the two readings happen to agree, because an odd power of a negative is negative anyway. Do not let that lull you — for squares, and for every even power, the two readings differ. The habit of reading brackets precisely must be kept regardless of the exponent.

Generalise with the counting rule from the previous lesson. A negative integer raised to an even power is positive; raised to an odd power, negative. Bracket negative one bracket to the power ten is one; bracket negative one bracket to the power eleven is negative one. Bracket negative two bracket to the power four is sixteen; bracket negative two bracket to the power five is negative thirty-two.

Finally, mix squares and cubes in the anchor. Bracket negative five bracket squared minus bracket negative two bracket cubed. Bracket negative five bracket squared is twenty-five. Bracket negative two bracket cubed is negative eight. Twenty-five minus negative eight is twenty-five plus eight, thirty-three. Write the two powers on one line, the subtraction converted to addition on the next, and the answer on the third. Questions on cubes follow.

## Subtopic: Square Roots — Undoing the Square

The square root undoes squaring. The square root of forty-nine is the number that squares to forty-nine. Both seven and negative seven square to forty-nine, so a convention is needed, and here it is: the root sign means the non-negative root only. The square root of forty-nine is seven. If an examiner wants both values, the question says so, usually by writing a plus-or-minus sign in front of the root or by asking for the integers whose square is forty-nine.

Compute roots by recognising perfect squares. Root of one hundred and forty-four is twelve, because twelve squared is one hundred and forty-four. Root of two hundred and twenty-five is fifteen. Root of one is one; root of zero is zero. For a larger number, use prime factors: root of three hundred and twenty-four. Three hundred and twenty-four is four times eighty-one, so its root is two times nine, eighteen. Root of a product is the product of the roots, so split a big number into perfect squares you know.

Now the negative case. The square root of negative sixteen asks for a number whose square is negative sixteen. Four squared is sixteen; negative four squared is sixteen; no integer squared is negative, because a square is a product of two equal signs. So the square root of negative sixteen is not a real number. Do not write negative four; negative four is the square root of sixteen with a sign in front, which is a completely different thing. Write: no real square root.

Distinguish these three carefully. Root of sixteen is four. Negative root of sixteen is the opposite of four: negative four. Root of negative sixteen does not exist among the real numbers. The position of the negative sign — outside or under the root — makes all the difference, exactly as the brackets did for squaring.

Roots inside a calculation follow the order of operations as an exponent would: evaluate what is under the root first, then take the root, then combine. Root of bracket sixteen plus nine bracket is root of twenty-five, five. Root of sixteen plus root of nine is four plus three, seven. The root does not distribute over addition; the two expressions are different and the examiner knows it. Questions on square roots are with you now.

## Subtopic: Cube Roots and Mixed Calculations

The cube root undoes cubing. The cube root of sixty-four is the number that cubes to sixty-four: four. The cube root of one thousand is ten. Because cubing preserves the sign, cube roots of negatives are perfectly fine: the cube root of negative twenty-seven is negative three, since negative three cubed is negative twenty-seven. The cube root of negative one is negative one. There is no convention to worry about — every integer has exactly one cube root, and the sign of the root matches the sign of the number.

Compare with square roots and write the contrast in your notes. Square root of a negative: not real. Cube root of a negative: negative, and allowed. Square root of a positive: by convention the positive root, although two integers square to it. Cube root of a positive: positive, and there is only one. These four sentences cover every root question at Grade 8 level.

Now a mixed calculation at examination standard. Evaluate root of eighty-one minus cube root of negative eight plus bracket negative three bracket squared. Line one: each power or root separately. Root of eighty-one is nine. Cube root of negative eight is negative two. Bracket negative three bracket squared is nine. Line two: nine minus negative two plus nine. Line three: nine plus two plus nine, twenty. Three lines, one idea per line.

A second, with a root that must be simplified before the arithmetic. Two times root of bracket one hundred and sixty-nine minus twenty-five bracket, divided by negative four. Under the root: one hundred and sixty-nine minus twenty-five is one hundred and forty-four. Root: twelve. Two times twelve is twenty-four. Twenty-four divided by negative four is negative six. The root was evaluated as a bracket, then multiplication and division ran left to right.

Distinction-level takeaway: roots and powers are evaluated before multiplication and division; what is under a root sign is a bracket; brackets decide whether a negative is being raised to the power; even powers kill the sign and odd powers keep it. # Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas as tiles on a floor, sugar cubes in a box and a very important pair of brackets. Squares can never be negative for a reason you can see, and cube roots of negatives are allowed for a reason you can feel.

## Subtopic: Tiles on a Floor — Why a Square Is Never Negative

Picture a square bathroom floor covered with tiles, five tiles along each side. How many tiles? Five rows of five, twenty-five. That is why multiplying a number by itself is called squaring: it is literally the number of tiles in a square. Six along each side, thirty-six tiles. Twelve along each side, one hundred and forty-four. The perfect squares are just the floors you can tile as a perfect square.

Now ask the silly question: can a floor have negative twenty-five tiles? No. A floor has a number of tiles or it has none; there is no such thing as a floor with less than nothing on it. Squares are counts of tiles, and counts are never negative. That is the picture behind the fact that the square of any integer is positive or zero. Negative five times negative five is twenty-five, because a five-by-five floor has twenty-five tiles whichever way you walk around it.

Here is how the picture helps with brackets. Bracket negative five bracket squared is a five-by-five floor: twenty-five. Negative five squared, no brackets, is a five-by-five floor that somebody owes — the opposite of twenty-five, negative twenty-five. The brackets tell you whether the negative is part of the side length, which gets wiped out by squaring, or a debt attached afterwards, which stays.

Roots run the picture backwards. You have one hundred and forty-four tiles and you want a square floor: how many along each side? Twelve, because twelve by twelve makes one hundred and forty-four. That is the square root. And if someone asks you to make a square floor out of negative sixteen tiles, you cannot — there is no such pile of tiles. The square root of a negative number has no real answer, and now you know it in your hands, not just in your head.

Hold onto this: squares are tile counts, roots are side lengths, and neither one can be negative. The questions now use floors and tiles; picture each one before you answer.

## Subtopic: Sugar Cubes and Deep Holes — Cubes Keep Their Sign

Now the cube. Stack sugar cubes into a bigger cube, three along each edge: three layers of three-by-three, twenty-seven cubes. Four along each edge, sixty-four. That is cubing, and the perfect cubes — one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five — are the box sizes that come out perfectly cubic.

So why can a cube be negative when a square cannot? Because a cube has three factors, and the third one keeps the sign. Think of a hole dug straight down. Going up is positive, going down is negative. A cube with edge negative two: pair off two of the factors, negative two times negative two is four — the sign is wiped out, like the floor. Then multiply by the third negative two, and the sign comes back: negative eight. Three negatives, one left over, so the cube points downwards. Negative two cubed is negative eight, a cube-shaped hole of eight sugar cubes' worth.

That is also why cube roots of negatives are allowed. The cube root of negative twenty-seven asks: what edge gives a hole of twenty-seven? Negative three, because negative three cubed is negative twenty-seven. Nothing impossible about a hole. Compare the square root of negative sixteen, which asked for a floor with negative tiles — impossible. Floors cannot be negative; holes can. That is the whole difference between even and odd powers.

Let us do the anchor with the pictures. Bracket negative five bracket squared is a five-by-five floor, twenty-five tiles. Bracket negative two bracket cubed is a two-by-two-by-two hole, negative eight. Twenty-five minus negative eight: taking away a hole is like filling it in, so you gain eight — thirty-three. The picture and the sign rule say the same thing.

Cubes keep their sign, squares lose it, and roots run the pictures backwards. The questions now are about boxes and holes; say floor or hole to yourself before you decide the sign.

## Subtopic: Estimating Roots and the Takeaway

One more skill before the finish, because it appears in every Grade 8 paper: estimating a root that is not a perfect square. Between which two integers does the square root of fifty lie? Find the perfect squares on either side. Forty-nine is seven squared and sixty-four is eight squared. Fifty sits just above forty-nine, so its root sits just above seven: between seven and eight, and much closer to seven. You do not need the decimal; you need the two neighbours and the sense of which one is nearer.

Try root of ninety. Eighty-one is nine squared; one hundred is ten squared. Ninety is between them, so the root is between nine and ten. Cube roots work the same way with the cube list. Cube root of one hundred: sixty-four is four cubed, one hundred and twenty-five is five cubed, so the cube root of one hundred is between four and five, nearer five. And a negative: cube root of negative fifty lies between negative four and negative three, because negative sixty-four and negative twenty-seven are the cubes on either side.

This estimating is your calculator check. If you type root of fifty and see seventy point seven, you know a key slipped, because the answer had to be a little over seven. Estimate first, calculate second, compare third.

Now the takeaway, in four sentences you can carry into the test. Brackets decide what gets squared: bracket negative three bracket squared is nine, negative three squared is negative nine. Even powers wipe the sign, odd powers keep it. The square root sign gives the non-negative root, and a negative under a square root has no real answer, but a negative under a cube root is fine. What is under a root is a bracket, and roots are done before multiplying and dividing.

You walked in with a list of perfect squares and you are leaving able to handle signs, brackets and roots in one calculation — a genuine step towards the exponents topic next term, where these same ideas get letters in them. The last questions mix everything; find the neighbours first for any root that is not a perfect square.
