### subtopic_2 — The Multiplicative Inverse — Back to One
**Assistant:** Why is the multiplicative inverse of an integer almost never an integer?

**Tutor:** Because the reciprocal of an integer a is one over a, and that is a whole number only when a divides one exactly, which happens for one and negative one alone. For any other integer the reciprocal is a fraction strictly between negative one and one: one fifth for five, negative one quarter for negative four. The reason is the definition. A multiplicative inverse must multiply the original to give one, and multiplying by a whole number other than one or negative one always makes the size bigger, so to arrive back at one you need a number that makes things smaller — a fraction. This is also why integers are closed under addition and multiplication but not under division: dividing by an integer is multiplying by its reciprocal, and the reciprocal usually takes you outside the integers into the fractions, which is the next topic in this term.

### subtopic_2 — The Multiplicative Inverse — Back to One
**Assistant:** How is zero having no multiplicative inverse the same as division by zero being undefined?

**Tutor:** They are two descriptions of one fact. Dividing by a number means multiplying by that number's reciprocal; dividing by five is multiplying by one fifth. So dividing by zero would mean multiplying by the reciprocal of zero. The reciprocal of zero would have to be a number n with zero times n equal to one, and no such number exists, because every number times zero is zero. Therefore there is nothing to multiply by, and division by zero has no meaning. Looked at the other way round, if division by zero were allowed then twelve divided by zero would give some number c with c times zero equal to twelve, which is impossible for the same reason. One missing inverse, one undefined division — the same gap in the number system, and a reason every algebraic fraction later needs a note saying the denominator is not zero.

### subtopic_4 — Inverses at Work — Undoing Operations
**Assistant:** When undoing two operations, why does the order have to be reversed?

**Tutor:** Because the last operation applied is the outermost layer, and you can only peel from the outside. In two x minus five, the unknown was first multiplied by two and then had five subtracted; the subtraction wraps around the multiplication. To expose x you remove the outer layer first by adding five, which leaves two x, and then remove the inner layer by multiplying by one half. If you multiplied by one half first you would have to apply it to every term, including the five, giving x minus two and a half, which is correct but far messier and a common source of error. The shoes-and-socks picture is exact: socks went on first and shoes second, so shoes come off first. In equation solving, write the operations that were done to x in order, then apply the inverses in the opposite order, one per line.

### subtopic_6 — The Flip — Multiplicative Inverse in Real Life
**Assistant:** If negative one is its own reciprocal, is it also its own additive inverse?

**Tutor:** No, and the question is a good test of whether the two inverses are kept apart. The multiplicative inverse of negative one is negative one, because negative one times negative one is one, so negative one is indeed its own flip. The additive inverse of negative one, however, is positive one, because negative one plus one is zero. The only number that is its own additive inverse is zero, since zero plus zero is zero and no other number added to itself gives zero. The only numbers that are their own multiplicative inverses are one and negative one. So the answers to the two questions are different kinds of statement about different operations, and the adjective in front of the word inverse tells you which operation is meant. Reading that adjective before answering is the single habit that prevents the most common error on this topic.
