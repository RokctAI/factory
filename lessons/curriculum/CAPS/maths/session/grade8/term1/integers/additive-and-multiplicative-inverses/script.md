# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines the additive inverse and the multiplicative inverse of an integer, links them to the identities zero and one, explains why zero has no multiplicative inverse, and shows how inverses are used to undo operations. The anchor facts are that negative five plus five is zero and that negative four times negative one quarter is one.

## Subtopic: The Additive Inverse — Back to Zero

Recall the additive identity from the whole numbers topic: zero is the number that changes nothing under addition, since a plus zero equals a for every integer a. The additive inverse of an integer is the number that takes you back to that identity. Formally: the additive inverse of a is the number which, added to a, gives zero. For seven, that number is negative seven, because seven plus negative seven is zero. For negative twelve, it is twelve, because negative twelve plus twelve is zero. For zero, it is zero itself.

So the additive inverse of an integer is its opposite: same distance from zero, other side. On the number line, seven and negative seven are reflections of each other in zero. Every integer has exactly one additive inverse, and the inverse of the inverse is the original: the additive inverse of negative seven is seven. In symbols, the opposite of the opposite of a is a, which is the formal reason that minus negative seven equals seven.

The anchor fact: negative five plus five is zero. Different signs, equal absolute values, so the subtraction of absolute values gives zero and there is no sign left to argue about. This is the only situation in which adding two non-zero integers gives zero, and it is worth recognising instantly, because in a long chain any pair of opposites may be struck out before you start.

Use the additive inverse to undo addition. If a number has had eight added to it, adding negative eight returns it to where it was: x plus eight plus negative eight is x plus zero, which is x. That is the entire logic behind solving x plus eight equals three: add the additive inverse of eight to both sides, and x equals three plus negative eight, which is negative five. Term 2 will make this an algebraic method; today, know the principle.

In an examination, questions ask for the additive inverse of a given integer, ask what must be added to reach zero, or give a statement such as negative nine plus box equals zero. The box is nine. Write the word inverse, not opposite, when the question uses the formal term, and know that both describe the same number. Questions on additive inverses are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Multiplicative Inverse — Back to One

The multiplicative identity is one, since a times one equals a for every integer. The multiplicative inverse of a number is the number which, multiplied by it, gives one. For five, that number is one fifth, because five times one fifth is one. For negative four, it is negative one quarter, because negative four times negative one quarter is one — two negatives multiply to a positive, and four quarters make one. The multiplicative inverse is also called the reciprocal.

Write an integer as a fraction over one and the reciprocal is simply the fraction flipped. Five is five over one, so its reciprocal is one over five. Negative four is negative four over one, so its reciprocal is one over negative four, which is written negative one quarter. The sign stays; the numerator and denominator swap.

Here is the first surprise of the lesson: the multiplicative inverse of an integer is usually not an integer. One fifth and negative one quarter are fractions. Only two integers have reciprocals that are themselves integers. One times one is one, so the reciprocal of one is one. Negative one times negative one is one, so the reciprocal of negative one is negative one. Every other integer has a reciprocal strictly between negative one and one.

Here is the second surprise, and it connects to a rule you already know. Zero has no multiplicative inverse. The inverse would be a number that, multiplied by zero, gives one. But every number times zero is zero, never one. No such number exists. This is the same fact as the statement that division by zero is undefined — dividing by a number means multiplying by its reciprocal, and zero has none.

Use the multiplicative inverse to undo multiplication. If a number has been multiplied by three, multiplying by one third returns it: three x times one third is one times x, which is x. So three x equals negative twelve is solved by multiplying both sides by one third, which is the same as dividing by three: x equals negative four. That is how every one-step multiplication equation in Grade 8 and Grade 9 is solved. Questions on multiplicative inverses follow.

## Subtopic: Identities and Inverses Together — The Structure

Set the four ideas out as a table, because the structure is what the examiner is really testing. Under addition: the identity is zero, and the inverse of a is negative a, with a plus negative a equal to zero. Under multiplication: the identity is one, and the inverse of a is one over a, with a times one over a equal to one, for every a other than zero. Two operations, two identities, two kinds of inverse, one exception.

Notice the parallel. The identity is the do-nothing number for its operation. The inverse is the number that gets you back to the identity. Addition and multiplication are different operations, so their identities are different numbers and their inverses are different kinds of number — a sign change for addition, a flip for multiplication. Learners who confuse the two write that the inverse of five is negative five when the question said multiplicative, or write one fifth when it said additive. Read the adjective.

Now some consequences you can derive. First, the additive inverse of a sum is the sum of the additive inverses: the opposite of bracket three plus negative eight bracket is the opposite of negative five, which is five, and separately negative three plus eight is five. Second, the reciprocal of a product is the product of the reciprocals: the reciprocal of two times negative three, which is negative six, is negative one sixth, and one half times negative one third is negative one sixth. Third, subtracting a is adding the additive inverse of a, and dividing by a is multiplying by the multiplicative inverse of a. Those two sentences are why only addition and multiplication really exist, with subtraction and division as their undo moves.

A worked example of the kind set in tests. Write down the additive inverse and the multiplicative inverse of negative six, and show that each works. Additive inverse: six, because negative six plus six is zero. Multiplicative inverse: negative one sixth, because negative six times negative one sixth is one. Two lines, two checks, full marks.

And a trap. The multiplicative inverse of negative one quarter is negative four — flipping a fraction back gives the integer. Inverses come in pairs: each is the inverse of the other. Questions on the structure follow now.

## Subtopic: Inverses at Work — Undoing Operations

The purpose of an inverse is to undo. Here is the principle as the examiner will test it next term and for the rest of school: whatever has been done to an unknown, do the inverse to both sides, and the unknown stands alone.

Example one. A number has eight added to it and the result is three. Call the number x: x plus eight equals three. Add the additive inverse of eight, which is negative eight, to both sides. Left: x plus eight plus negative eight is x. Right: three plus negative eight is negative five. So x is negative five. Check: negative five plus eight is three. The additive inverse removed the eight.

Example two. A number is multiplied by negative three and the result is twenty-one. Negative three x equals twenty-one. Multiply both sides by the multiplicative inverse of negative three, which is negative one third. Left: negative three times negative one third is one, so one x, which is x. Right: twenty-one times negative one third is negative seven. So x is negative seven. Check: negative three times negative seven is twenty-one. In practice you will write divide both sides by negative three, and now you know that this is exactly multiplying by the reciprocal.

Example three, two steps, to show the order. Two x minus five equals negative eleven. The five was subtracted last, so undo it first: add five to both sides, giving two x equals negative six. Then undo the multiplication: multiply by one half, giving x equals negative three. Check: two times negative three is negative six, minus five is negative eleven. Inverses are applied in reverse order of the operations, like taking off shoes before socks.

A warning about zero, one more time. You may never undo a multiplication by zero, because zero has no inverse. If zero times x equals zero, x could be anything; if zero times x equals four, nothing works. Both situations are exactly the division-by-zero problem in a new costume.

Distinction-level takeaway: identities do nothing, inverses return you to the identity, and solving an equation is a sequence of inverses applied to both sides in reverse order. Questions on undoing follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two ideas as undo buttons: a debt that cancels a credit, a lift that goes back down, and a flip that returns a stretched picture to its size. By the end they will feel less like definitions and more like common sense.

## Subtopic: The Undo Button — Additive Inverse in Real Life

Think about the undo button on your phone. You type a word, you tap undo, and it is as if the word was never there. The additive inverse is arithmetic's undo button for adding. You add seven; the undo is adding negative seven; you are back where you started, at zero change.

Money shows it perfectly. Your friend sends you one hundred and fifty rand by mistake, plus one hundred and fifty. You send it back, negative one hundred and fifty. Net effect: zero. Nothing happened to your balance. The two transactions are additive inverses of each other. Notice they are the same size and opposite in direction — that is all an additive inverse is.

A lift does it too. You are on the ground floor, zero. You go up six floors, plus six. The additive inverse of plus six is going down six floors, negative six. Six plus negative six is zero: ground floor again. If you were on level negative two, the basement, and went up two, the undo of that trip is going down two — but notice also that going up two from negative two lands you on zero. Negative two plus two is zero, so two is the additive inverse of negative two. The opposite of a negative is a positive, and a lift knows it.

Here is why it is worth a whole section. Whenever you see a pair of opposites in a long sum, they vanish. Negative three plus eight plus three plus negative eight: the threes cancel, the eights cancel, zero. Learners who plod through left to right get there eventually; learners who spot the pairs get there instantly and without sign slips. In a game, a four-point penalty and a four-point bonus are a wash, so skip them both.

So: the additive inverse of a number is the same number with its sign switched, and adding the pair gives you zero every time. The questions now are undo stories — money sent back, lifts going down — and in each one you are looking for the number that brings the total back to zero.

## Subtopic: The Flip — Multiplicative Inverse in Real Life

Now the undo button for multiplying, which is a bit sneakier. Imagine a photo on your phone. You pinch and zoom so it is three times bigger. What undoes that? Shrinking it to one third of its size. Three times one third is one — back to the original. One third is the multiplicative inverse of three, and the picture is a way of seeing why it is a fraction: to undo a stretch you need a squash, and a squash is a number smaller than one.

Recipes give the same idea. You triple a recipe for a party: everything times three. The party is cancelled and you want the original amounts back, so you take one third of everything. Times three, then times one third, is times one. The reciprocal is just the flip of the fraction: three over one becomes one over three.

Now the negative. In the earlier lesson, multiplying by a negative was like flipping a debt to a credit as well as scaling. Multiply a number by negative four: it gets four times bigger and switches sign. To undo that, you need to make it four times smaller and switch the sign back — multiply by negative one quarter. Negative four times negative one quarter: two negatives give a positive, four times a quarter is one. Back to one. The reciprocal keeps the sign and flips the size.

Why zero is left out, in picture form. Zoom a photo by a factor of zero and it becomes a single dot — all the information is gone. No zoom factor can bring the picture back from a dot, because there is nothing left to stretch. That is why zero has no multiplicative inverse, and it is the same reason you cannot divide by zero.

Only two numbers are their own flips: one, which does not zoom at all, and negative one, which only switches sign — do it twice and you are back. Everything else has a reciprocal that is a fraction between negative one and one. The questions are zooms, recipes and a dot you cannot un-shrink; for each, ask what multiplies to get back to one.

## Subtopic: Two Buttons, One Habit — The Takeaway

Let us put the two undo buttons side by side one last time, because telling them apart is the whole examination skill. Additive inverse: switch the sign, add to get zero. Multiplicative inverse: flip the fraction, multiply to get one. Five: additive inverse negative five, multiplicative inverse one fifth. Negative eight: additive inverse eight, multiplicative inverse negative one eighth. One: additive inverse negative one, multiplicative inverse one. Zero: additive inverse zero, multiplicative inverse none.

Here is a quick game to check you have them. I say a number, you say both inverses. Negative ten: ten, and negative one tenth. Three: negative three, and one third. Negative one: one, and negative one. Did the last one catch you? Negative one is its own flip, because negative one times negative one is one, but its additive inverse is positive one. Two different buttons, two different answers.

Now the reason you learnt this today rather than next year. Next term you solve equations, and solving is just pressing undo buttons in the right order. If x has had something added, press the additive inverse. If x has been multiplied, press the multiplicative inverse. Two x plus six equals negative four: undo the plus six by adding negative six, two x equals negative ten; undo the times two by multiplying by one half, x equals negative five. Check: two times negative five is negative ten, plus six is negative four. You just solved an equation using nothing but today's two buttons.

The habits to carry out of this room. Read the word before inverse: additive means sign switch, multiplicative means flip. Remember the odd ones: zero has no flip, one and negative one are their own flips. And in a long sum, hunt for pairs of opposites first, because they vanish.

You walked in knowing that minus seven is the opposite of seven. You are walking out with a second kind of opposite, a reason zero is special, and the method behind every equation you will solve in Grade 8 and Grade 9 — that is a real win. The last questions ask for both inverses and use them to undo; say the adjective out loud before you answer.
