# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the addition and subtraction of like terms, justified by the commutative, associative and distributive laws and by the laws of exponents. Like terms are collected by adding their coefficients, which is the distributive law read backwards; unlike terms, including terms whose variables carry different exponents, cannot be collected. Rearranging and regrouping terms is permitted by the commutative and associative laws provided each sign travels with its term. The anchor simplification is five x squared minus three x plus two x squared plus x minus four, which becomes seven x squared minus two x minus four.

## Subtopic: Collecting Like Terms — The Distributive Law Backwards

Three x plus four x is seven x. Why? Because of the distributive law. Three x plus four x is three times x plus four times x, and the distributive law says a times c plus b times c equals bracket a plus b bracket times c. So three x plus four x is bracket three plus four bracket times x, which is seven x. Collecting like terms is the distributive law run from right to left: pull out the common variable part and add the coefficients.

The same with subtraction. Nine a minus five a is bracket nine minus five bracket a, four a. Two y squared plus y squared: the coefficient of y squared alone is one, so bracket two plus one bracket y squared, three y squared. Three p minus seven p is bracket three minus seven bracket p, negative four p — integer subtraction from Term 1, with the variable part carried along.

What stays the same: the variable part. Collecting changes the coefficient only. Three x plus four x is seven x, not seven x squared. The exponent on x is one in both terms and remains one; nothing in adding coefficients touches the exponent. This is where the laws of exponents come in: exponents add when powers are multiplied, not when they are added. x times x is x squared; x plus x is two x.

Constants collect with constants. Five plus two minus nine is negative two. And a constant never collects with a variable term: five plus two x stays as five plus two x. There is no x in the five to share.

Zero coefficients disappear. Four m minus four m is zero m, which is zero, and you write nothing — or zero if it is the whole answer. Three x squared minus three x squared plus x is just x.

Write the collected expression in descending powers, variable terms first, constant last: seven x squared minus two x minus four, not minus four minus two x plus seven x squared, although both are correct. Questions on collecting follow now.

## Subtopic: Rearranging and Regrouping — Commutative and Associative Laws

Take the anchor: five x squared minus three x plus two x squared plus x minus four. The like terms are scattered. The commutative law of addition says a plus b equals b plus a — order does not matter — so we may move terms around. The associative law says bracket a plus b bracket plus c equals a plus bracket b plus c bracket — grouping does not matter — so we may bracket the like terms together. Rearranged: five x squared plus two x squared, minus three x plus x, minus four. Collected: seven x squared minus two x minus four.

The one rule when moving a term is that its sign goes with it. Negative three x stays negative three x wherever it lands. The expression is really five x squared plus negative three x plus two x squared plus x plus negative four, a sum of signed terms, and a sum can be reordered freely. Think of every minus as a plus-negative and you can never lose a sign by moving a term.

A practical layout: underline or mark each group of like terms with a different mark before collecting — a circle for x squared terms, a line for x terms, a box for constants — including the sign in front of each. Then collect group by group. Marking the signs with the terms is what prevents negative three x from becoming three x.

Subtraction itself is not commutative — five minus three is not three minus five — which is exactly why the sign must be read as part of the term. Once the terms carry their signs, what you are doing is addition, and addition is commutative and associative.

Another example: four a plus three b minus a minus five b plus two. Groups: four a minus a is three a; three b minus five b is negative two b; two. Three a minus two b plus two. Two variables, so there are two groups of variable terms and a constant, and the answer has three terms.

Another: six minus two m plus three m squared minus m plus one. Groups: three m squared; negative two m minus m is negative three m; six plus one is seven. Three m squared minus three m plus seven. Questions on rearranging follow.

## Subtopic: Exponents Decide What Is Like

The laws of exponents are the referee for like terms. Two terms are like when their variable parts are identical, and the variable part includes every exponent. x squared and x are unlike; x squared and three x squared are like; x squared y and x y squared are unlike, because the exponents sit on different letters.

The error to avoid comes from confusing addition with multiplication. x times x is x squared, by the multiplication law for exponents — add the exponents, one plus one. But x plus x is two x: there is no law that adds exponents under addition, because nothing is being multiplied. Learners who write x plus x equals x squared have applied a multiplication law to an addition. Test with x equal to three: three plus three is six, and three squared is nine.

Likewise three x squared plus four x squared is seven x squared, not seven x to the power four. The exponents are not added; the coefficients are. Collecting like terms touches coefficients only, ever.

Multiple variables: two a b plus five a b is seven a b; a b and b a are the same product, so three a b plus two b a is five a b. Two a squared b plus a b squared cannot be collected — look at the exponents letter by letter: a squared b has exponents two and one, a b squared has one and two. Different kinds.

Here is a complete example mixing all of this: three x squared y minus two x y squared plus x squared y plus five x y squared minus x y. Groups: x squared y terms, three and one, four x squared y; x y squared terms, negative two and five, three x y squared; the x y term stands alone, negative x y. Four x squared y plus three x y squared minus x y.

Writing variables in alphabetical order inside each term and arranging terms in descending powers of the first variable makes like terms sit next to each other and errors visible. Questions on exponents and like terms follow.

## Subtopic: Expressions with Brackets and a Subtracted Group

When a bracket is simply added, the bracket can be removed and the terms inside keep their signs. Bracket three x plus two bracket plus bracket five x minus seven bracket is three x plus two plus five x minus seven, which collects to eight x minus five. Adding a group is adding each term in it; the associative law says so.

When a bracket is subtracted, every term inside changes sign. Bracket three x plus two bracket minus bracket five x minus seven bracket: subtracting the group means subtracting five x and subtracting negative seven. Three x plus two minus five x plus seven. Collect: negative two x plus nine. The sign change on every term in a subtracted bracket is the most-tested skill in this lesson, and the term most often missed is the second one — the negative seven becoming plus seven.

Why does the sign flip? Because a minus in front of a bracket is a coefficient of negative one, and the distributive law multiplies every term inside by negative one. Negative one times five x is negative five x; negative one times negative seven is positive seven. Writing the negative one explicitly, as negative one bracket five x minus seven bracket, is a safe habit until the sign change is automatic.

A number in front of a bracket distributes too: two bracket x plus three bracket is two x plus six, and three bracket two a minus b bracket is six a minus three b. Expanding a number times a bracket is strictly next lesson's work, but it follows from the same distributive law, and a subtracted bracket is just the case where the number is negative one.

Example: seven a minus bracket two a minus three bracket plus bracket a minus five bracket. Remove the subtracted bracket with sign changes: seven a minus two a plus three. Remove the added bracket: plus a minus five. Collect: six a minus two.

Example: bracket four x squared minus x bracket minus bracket x squared plus three x minus two bracket. Four x squared minus x minus x squared minus three x plus two. Collect: three x squared minus four x plus two. Questions on brackets follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same simplifying as sorting a drawer of mixed socks, settling a group's lunch bill, and tidying a toolbox where screws and nails must never share a jar — stories in which collecting is sorting, and a minus sign is a sock that has been taken away.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Sock Drawer

Here is collecting like terms as sorting socks. A drawer has five black socks, three white socks, two more black socks, one more white sock and a belt. Write it as an expression with b for black socks and w for white socks and the belt as a constant: five b plus three w plus two b plus w plus one. Sorting means putting blacks with blacks and whites with whites: seven b plus four w plus one. That is the whole of collecting like terms — you sort, and then you count each pile.

Sorting is allowed because the order you find socks in the drawer does not change how many you have. That is the commutative law. Piling them up in any grouping does not change the count either. That is the associative law. The laws are not extra rules to remember; they are the reason sorting is legitimate.

Now the minus signs. Minus three b means three black socks taken out of the drawer. Five b minus three b: five blacks, three removed, two left. Five b minus seven b: five blacks, seven wanted, so you are two short — negative two b, two socks owed. Minus signs are socks removed, and when you move a term to its pile you move the removal with it. A learner who sorts negative three b into the black pile as plus three b has turned a withdrawal into a deposit.

And the exponents. A black sock squared is not a sock. If b is a black sock, b squared is a pair of black socks — a different item that goes in a different pile. You can have three b squared plus two b, three pairs and two loose socks, and they do not combine into five of anything. You can count pairs with pairs, loose socks with loose socks. Exponents mark different piles.

A subtracted bracket is a bag of socks being taken out of the drawer whole. Minus bracket two b plus w bracket means removing two blacks and one white — both are removed, so both get a minus sign: minus two b minus w. And minus bracket two b minus w bracket: the bag contained two blacks and an IOU for one white; removing the bag removes the IOU, which is like adding a white back. Minus two b plus w. Every item in the removed bag flips. Questions on socks follow.

## Subtopic: Settling the Lunch Bill

Four friends share a lunch and keep a running tally in algebra, because they do not yet know the price of a pie, p, or a cold drink, c. The first friend orders two pies and a cold drink: two p plus c. The second orders a pie and two cold drinks: p plus two c. The third orders three pies: three p. The fourth orders a cold drink and pays a ten rand tip in cash: c plus ten. The total bill is the sum: two p plus c plus p plus two c plus three p plus c plus ten. Sort: pies, two plus one plus three, six p. Cold drinks, one plus two plus one, four c. Cash, ten. Six p plus four c plus ten. Nobody needed to know the prices to add up the order.

Now the third friend cancels one pie and the second sends back a cold drink. Subtract a pie and a cold drink: six p plus four c plus ten minus p minus c, which is five p plus three c plus ten. The subtracted items come off their own piles — a cancelled pie does not reduce the number of cold drinks.

Then the waiter brings the wrong order and takes back a bag containing two pies and a voucher worth a cold drink — a bag worth two p minus c, since the voucher reduces the bill. Taking the bag back means subtracting bracket two p minus c bracket: minus two p plus c. The voucher going back raises the bill by a cold drink. Five p plus three c plus ten minus two p plus c is three p plus four c plus ten.

Finally, the prices arrive: a pie is fifteen rand and a cold drink twelve. Three pies, forty-five; four cold drinks, forty-eight; plus ten. One hundred and three rand. Substitution is a later lesson, but it shows why collecting first is worth it: one multiplication per kind of item instead of one per order.

Where would a careless friend go wrong? Adding two pies and a cold drink into three of something — unlike terms. Forgetting the ten rand tip, which has no letter and is easy to lose. And flipping only the first item when the bag comes back, so the voucher is subtracted instead of restored. Each of those is a mark in an examination. Questions on the lunch bill follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for simplifying by collecting. One: remove brackets — an added bracket drops away, a subtracted bracket flips every sign inside, a number in front distributes. Two: mark the like-term groups, sign included, checking exponents letter by letter. Three: add the coefficients in each group, keeping the variable part unchanged. Four: write the result in descending powers with the constant last. Five: check by substituting a small number into the original and the answer.

A typical question: simplify three x squared minus five x plus two minus x squared plus seven x minus six. Groups: three x squared minus x squared, two x squared; negative five x plus seven x, two x; two minus six, negative four. Two x squared plus two x minus four. Check with x equal to one: original is three minus five plus two minus one plus seven minus six, zero; answer is two plus two minus four, zero.

Another: simplify two bracket a plus three b bracket minus bracket a minus b bracket. Distribute the two: two a plus six b. Flip the subtracted bracket: minus a plus b. Collect: a plus seven b.

Another: simplify four m n minus two m squared plus three n m plus m squared. Note that n m is m n. m n terms: four plus three, seven m n. m squared terms: negative two plus one, negative m squared. Negative m squared plus seven m n.

Another: find the perimeter of a rectangle with length three x plus two and breadth x minus one. Perimeter is twice the sum: two bracket three x plus two plus x minus one bracket, which is two bracket four x plus one bracket, eight x plus two. Collecting inside the bracket first keeps the numbers small.

Errors to avoid. Adding unlike terms. Changing an exponent when collecting. Losing the sign of a moved term. Flipping only the first term of a subtracted bracket. Dropping the constant. Writing x plus x as x squared.
