### subtopic_1 — Squaring and Cubing a Monomial
**Assistant:** Why does the exponent get multiplied when I raise a power to a power, when earlier exponents added?

**Tutor:** Both come from the same place — counting factors — and they describe different operations. Exponents add when you multiply two powers of the same base, because you are putting the factors side by side: x squared times x cubed is five x's, x to the power five. Raising a power to a power is repeated multiplication of the same power, so the adding happens several times: bracket x squared bracket squared is x squared times x squared, which is two plus two, four, and bracket x squared bracket cubed is x squared times x squared times x squared, two plus two plus two, six. Adding the exponent to itself n times is the same as multiplying it by n, which is why squaring doubles an exponent and cubing triples it. If you ever forget which to do, write the power out as a product of copies and add; the multiplication rule is only the shortcut for that addition. Grade 9 states it formally as a to the m, all to the n, equals a to the m n.

### subtopic_2 — Square Roots of Monomials
**Assistant:** How can I tell quickly whether a square root of a monomial is possible?

**Tutor:** Check two things: the coefficient must be a perfect square — one, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five, and so on, including fractions like nine sixteenths and decimals like nought comma zero four — and every exponent must be even. If both hold, the root is a monomial: root the coefficient and halve each exponent. If the coefficient is not a perfect square, like twenty, or an exponent is odd, like x to the power five, the root is not a monomial with whole-number exponents; in Grade 8 you leave it under the root sign or state that it cannot be simplified in this way, and Grade 9 will show you how to write such roots as surds. A negative coefficient under a square root is a different matter: the root has no answer at all, because no term squared is negative. For a cube root the checks change to a perfect cube and exponents that are multiples of three, and a negative coefficient is perfectly fine, because cube roots keep the sign.

### subtopic_4 — Like Terms Under a Root — Collect First
**Assistant:** Why can I take the root of a product term by term but not of a sum?

**Tutor:** Because roots are powers in disguise and powers distribute over multiplication but not over addition, which is the same reason bracket a times b bracket squared is a squared b squared while bracket a plus b bracket squared is not a squared plus b squared. The square root of four x times nine x can be found as two root x times three root x, or more simply by multiplying inside first to thirty-six x squared and rooting to six x — both give the same answer because the factors can be regrouped. The square root of sixteen x squared plus nine x squared cannot be found as four x plus three x, because a sum cannot be regrouped into separate roots; it must be collected to twenty-five x squared first, giving five x. The numerical case from earlier in the term is the proof: the root of nine plus sixteen is five, not three plus four. The practical rule is the locked box — one term under the root before anything comes out — and the habit of checking with a substitution exposes any early opening.

### subtopic_5 — The Tile With a Lettered Side
**Assistant:** Why does a cube have a negative edge in algebra when a square cannot have a negative side?

**Tutor:** In geometry neither can be negative, but algebra follows the arithmetic of signs rather than the shapes, and the two operations behave differently. Squaring any term, positive or negative, gives a positive result, because two like signs multiply to positive; so there is no term whose square is negative twenty-five x to the power six, and the square root of a negative has no answer. Cubing keeps the sign, because three negatives leave one unpaired; so negative two a b squared cubed is negative eight a cubed b to the power six, and the cube root of that negative term is the negative term you started with. The geometric pictures — tile and sugar cube — are there to make the coefficient-and-exponent rules visible, and they stop being literal once signs go negative. What survives is the arithmetic: even powers and roots cannot see a sign, odd powers and roots carry it through. That is the even-odd rule from the first exponents lesson, and it governs every root you take.
