# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the determination of squares, cubes, square roots and cube roots of single algebraic terms and of like terms that first collect into a single term. A power of a monomial raises the coefficient to that power and multiplies each exponent by it; a root of a monomial takes the root of the coefficient and divides each exponent by the index of the root. Like terms under a root sign must be collected before the root is taken, because roots do not distribute over sums. The anchor results are bracket three x squared bracket squared, nine x to the power four; the square root of twenty-five x to the power six, five x cubed; and the cube root of negative eight a cubed b to the power six, negative two a b squared.

## Subtopic: Squaring and Cubing a Monomial

To square a monomial, square the coefficient and double every exponent. Bracket three x squared bracket squared: coefficient three squared, nine; exponent two doubled, four. Nine x to the power four. The law behind this is the power of a product from the exponents topic: bracket a times t bracket to the n equals a to the n times t to the n, so the exponent reaches both the number and the letter. And x squared, squared, is x squared times x squared, which by the multiplication law is x to the power four — the exponents add, two plus two, which is the same as doubling.

To cube a monomial, cube the coefficient and triple every exponent. Bracket two a b squared bracket cubed: two cubed, eight; a to the power three; b squared tripled, b to the power six. Eight a cubed b to the power six.

Signs follow the even-odd rule. Bracket negative three x squared bracket squared is positive nine x to the power four; the even exponent removes the sign. Bracket negative two a bracket cubed is negative eight a cubed; the odd exponent keeps it. Bracket negative x bracket squared is x squared; bracket negative x bracket cubed is negative x cubed.

The error to avoid is squaring only the letter: writing bracket three x squared bracket squared as three x to the power four. The three is inside the bracket and must be squared too. The other error is doubling the coefficient instead of squaring it — six x to the power four. Test with x equal to one: bracket three bracket squared is nine, so the coefficient must be nine.

Fractions and decimals as coefficients follow the previous topic: bracket a half x bracket squared is a quarter x squared; bracket nought comma two y bracket cubed is nought comma zero zero eight y cubed.

Without brackets, the exponent applies only to what is immediately before it: three x squared means three times x squared, and is not the square of three x. Bracket three x bracket squared is nine x squared. Read brackets before you do anything. Questions on squaring and cubing follow now.

## Subtopic: Square Roots of Monomials

A square root undoes a square, so the rule reverses: take the square root of the coefficient and halve every exponent. The square root of twenty-five x to the power six: root of twenty-five is five; six halved is three. Five x cubed. Check: bracket five x cubed bracket squared is twenty-five x to the power six.

The square root of thirty-six a squared b to the power four is six a b squared. The square root of x to the power ten is x to the power five. The square root of four y to the power eight is two y to the power four.

Two conditions must hold for the answer to be a monomial with whole-number exponents. The coefficient must be a perfect square, and every exponent must be even. The square root of twenty x to the power six is not a nice term, because twenty is not a perfect square. The square root of twenty-five x to the power five is not a nice term, because five is odd. In Grade 8, when a root is not possible in this sense, you say so or leave it under the root sign; Grade 9 will simplify such roots as surds.

The square root sign gives the non-negative root. The square root of twenty-five x to the power six is five x cubed, taken as the principal root; a question that wants both possible values will ask for the numbers whose square is twenty-five x to the power six, and the answer is then plus or minus five x cubed.

A square root of a negative coefficient is impossible: the square root of negative twenty-five x to the power six has no answer, because no term squared is negative. Negative inside an even root is a stop sign.

With fractions: the square root of nine over sixteen x squared is three quarters x. With decimals: the square root of nought comma zero four y to the power four is nought comma two y squared, since the root of nought comma zero four is nought comma two.

The check is always to square your answer and look for the original. If squaring gives a different coefficient or exponent, the root was taken wrongly — most often by halving the coefficient rather than rooting it, which would give twelve comma five x cubed, or by rooting the exponent rather than halving it. ## Subtopic: Cube Roots of Monomials

A cube root undoes a cube: take the cube root of the coefficient and divide every exponent by three. The cube root of negative eight a cubed b to the power six: cube root of negative eight is negative two; a cubed divided by three gives a; b to the power six divided by three gives b squared. Negative two a b squared. Check: bracket negative two a b squared bracket cubed is negative eight a cubed b to the power six.

Cube roots keep the sign of what is inside, because a negative cubed is negative. The cube root of twenty-seven x to the power nine is three x cubed; the cube root of negative twenty-seven x to the power nine is negative three x cubed. Unlike square roots, a negative under a cube root is fine.

The conditions: the coefficient must be a perfect cube, and every exponent must be a multiple of three. The cube root of sixty-four m to the power twelve is four m to the power four. The cube root of sixteen m to the power twelve is not a nice term, because sixteen is not a perfect cube. The cube root of sixty-four m to the power ten is not a nice term, because ten is not a multiple of three.

Keep the two roots apart. The square root of sixty-four x to the power six is eight x cubed — root sixty-four gives eight, six halved gives three. The cube root of sixty-four x to the power six is four x squared — cube root of sixty-four gives four, six divided by three gives two. Same term under the root, different roots, different answers. Sixty-four is both a square and a cube, which is why examiners like it. Read the small three.

Fractions: the cube root of one over twenty-seven a cubed is one third a. Decimals: the cube root of nought comma zero zero one y to the power six is nought comma one y squared.

Order of operations: a root of a product of monomials can be found by multiplying first, then rooting, or by rooting each factor. The cube root of bracket two x bracket times bracket four x squared bracket is the cube root of eight x cubed, two x. Multiply inside first; it is usually easier. Questions on cube roots follow.

## Subtopic: Like Terms Under a Root — Collect First

A root sign is a bracket: everything under it is worked out before the root is taken. When the terms under a root are like terms, collect them into a single term first, then take the root. The square root of sixteen x squared plus nine x squared: collect, twenty-five x squared; root, five x. Not four x plus three x, which is seven x — roots do not distribute over addition, as the exponents topic made clear with the square root of nine plus sixteen.

The cube root of twenty a cubed plus seven a cubed: collect, twenty-seven a cubed; root, three a. The square root of fifty x to the power four minus fourteen x to the power four: collect, thirty-six x to the power four; root, six x squared. The square root of nine y squared minus y squared: collect, eight y squared; and eight is not a perfect square, so the root is not a nice term — the question has a trick in it, or you have miscollected.

Unlike terms under a root cannot be collected, and the root cannot be taken term by term either. The square root of x squared plus y squared stays as it is; it is not x plus y, as substituting x equal to three and y equal to four shows: the root of twenty-five is five, not seven. In Grade 8 you leave such roots alone.

A root of a product of terms: multiply into a single term first. The square root of four x times nine x: thirty-six x squared, six x. Or root each factor, since roots do distribute over products: two root x times three root x, which is six x — but the first route avoids roots of single letters, which are Grade 9 surds.

A root of a quotient: simplify the fraction into a single term first. The square root of fifty x to the power five over two x: twenty-five x to the power four, five x squared.

The rule in one line: under a root, make one term, then take the root. If one term cannot be made, the root cannot be taken in Grade 8. Questions on collecting under a root follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same squares and roots as stories about a square tile whose side is an algebraic term, a cube of sugar with a lettered edge, and a root sign that behaves like a locked box which must be filled completely before it is opened — stories in which the exponent is a dimension and the root is the question asked backwards.

## Subtopic: The Tile With a Lettered Side

A tile is square and its side is three x squared centimetres — for some x the factory will set later. What is its area? Side squared: bracket three x squared bracket squared. Picture it: the tile is three x squared wide and three x squared tall. Three rows of three gives nine; x squared across and x squared down gives x to the power four. Nine x to the power four square centimetres. The coefficient got squared because there are three times three blocks, and the exponent doubled because the letters multiplied.

A learner who writes three x to the power four has counted one row of three blocks instead of three rows. The three is a side length, and sides multiply.

Now backwards. A square tile has area twenty-five x to the power six square centimetres. What is its side? The side times itself is twenty-five x to the power six. Five x cubed times five x cubed is twenty-five x to the power six, so the side is five x cubed. Root the coefficient: twenty-five is five rows of five. Halve the exponent: six is three plus three. The square root is the side of the square.

Why halve and not root the exponent? Because the exponent counts how many x's are multiplied, and the two sides contribute equal shares. Six x's in the area means three along each side. The exponent is a count, and the count splits in two.

Now the sugar cube with edge two a b squared. Volume: edge cubed, eight a cubed b to the power six. Two times two times two blocks, eight; a along each of three directions, a cubed; b squared along each of three directions, b to the power six. Backwards: a cube of volume negative eight a cubed b to the power six — a strange cube, but let the algebra run — has edge negative two a b squared, because negative two cubed is negative eight and the exponents split into three equal shares. A cube can have a negative edge in algebra, which is why the cube root of a negative exists; a square cannot have a side whose square is negative, which is why the square root of a negative does not.

Area and volume give the operations a body: squaring is a square, cubing is a cube, and the roots are the questions asked backwards — what side, what edge. Questions on the tile and the cube follow.

## Subtopic: The Locked Box

Think of a root sign as a locked box. You may put things into the box, you may tidy what is inside, but you may not take anything out until the box is full and tidy — that is, until what is inside is a single term. Then you open the box, and the root acts on the whole single term at once.

Sixteen x squared plus nine x squared goes into the box. Tidy inside: the two terms are like, so they collect to twenty-five x squared. Now the box is one term. Open it: five x. A learner who opens the box early and takes the root of each term separately gets four x plus three x, seven x, which is wrong — check with x equal to one: the root of twenty-five is five, not seven.

x squared plus y squared goes into the box. Tidy inside: nothing to collect; the terms are unlike. The box stays locked. The square root of x squared plus y squared is left exactly as it is. This is not a failure — it is the honest answer, and Grade 9 and 10 will have much to say about such expressions — but in Grade 8 it simply cannot be opened.

Four x times nine x goes into the box. Tidy: multiply, thirty-six x squared, one term. Open: six x.

Negative eight a cubed b to the power six goes into a cube-root box. One term already. Open: negative two a b squared — cube roots open negative boxes happily, because a negative cubed is negative.

Negative twenty-five x to the power six goes into a square-root box. One term, but the box will not open: no term squared gives a negative. The lock is permanent.

Twenty x to the power six goes into a square-root box. One term, but twenty is not a perfect square. In Grade 8 the box stays shut; you write that it cannot be simplified to a monomial, or you leave the root as it is.

The box picture also protects you when a root sits in a longer calculation: the square root of nine x squared plus the square root of sixteen x squared is two separate boxes, three x plus four x, seven x; the square root of nine x squared plus sixteen x squared is one box, five x. Count the boxes before you open any. Questions on the locked box follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method. For a power of a monomial: read the brackets to see what is being raised; apply the exponent to the sign by the even-odd rule, to the coefficient by arithmetic, and to each letter by multiplying exponents. For a root: make the inside a single term by collecting, multiplying or simplifying; check the coefficient is a perfect square or cube and each exponent divides by two or three; take the root of the coefficient with the right sign and divide the exponents; check by raising your answer back.

A typical question: simplify bracket negative two x cubed y bracket squared. Even exponent, positive; two squared, four; x to the power six; y squared. Four x to the power six y squared.

Another: determine the square root of forty-nine a to the power eight b squared. Seven a to the power four b.

Another: determine the cube root of negative one hundred and twenty-five m to the power six. Negative five m squared.

Another: simplify the square root of three x squared plus six x squared. Collect, nine x squared; root, three x.

Another: simplify bracket two a bracket cubed plus the cube root of twenty-seven a cubed. Eight a cubed plus three a. Two unlike terms; the answer is a binomial.

Another: which is larger for x equal to two, bracket three x bracket squared or three x squared? Thirty-six against twelve. The bracket matters.

Errors to avoid. Squaring the letter but not the coefficient. Doubling the coefficient instead of squaring it. Rooting the exponent instead of halving it. Taking a root term by term under one root sign. Giving an answer to the square root of a negative. Confusing square and cube roots of sixty-four. Reading three x squared as bracket three x bracket squared.
