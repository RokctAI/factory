### subtopic_1 — Monomial Times Monomial
**Assistant:** Why do coefficients multiply but exponents add when I multiply monomials?

**Tutor:** Because they are counting different things. The coefficient is an ordinary number factor, and ordinary numbers multiply: three x times four x squared contains the factors three and four, whose product is twelve. The exponent counts how many times the letter appears as a factor, and when you multiply, you put the factors side by side, so the counts add: x times x squared is one x followed by two more, three in all, x cubed. This is the multiplication law of exponents from earlier in the term, and the stacking-boxes picture applies — two stacks placed one on another add their heights. The two common errors each misapply one rule to the other quantity: seven x squared adds the coefficients as if they were exponents, and twelve x squared leaves the exponent alone as if it were a coefficient that should not change. Checking with x equal to two settles it: three times two times four times four is ninety-six, and twelve times eight is ninety-six, while seven times four and twelve times four are not.

### subtopic_2 — Monomial Times Binomial — The Distributive Law
**Assistant:** What is the quickest way to make sure I multiply every term in the bracket?

**Tutor:** Draw an arc from the monomial to each term inside the bracket before you write anything, and count the arcs against the terms: two for a binomial, three for a trinomial. Then write one product per arc, in order. The habit works because the error is almost never a failure of knowledge — everyone knows the three reaches both terms in three bracket two a plus seven bracket — but a failure of attention, with the eye stopping after the first product. The arcs force the eye to visit every term. A second safeguard is the area picture: every term in the bracket is a strip of the rectangle, and a missing strip means a missing piece of area. A third is the substitution check at the end: for a equal to one, three bracket two plus seven bracket is twenty-seven, and six a plus seven gives thirteen, so the forgotten term shows up immediately. Use all three until the full expansion is automatic.

### subtopic_3 — Negative Monomials and Trinomials
**Assistant:** When the monomial is negative, do I flip the signs first or multiply first?

**Tutor:** Either order gives the same result, but it is safest to treat each product as a complete monomial-times-monomial calculation with three parts — sign, coefficient, exponent — done in that order for each arc. For negative three a times negative two a: signs, negative times negative is positive; coefficients, three times two is six; exponents, a times a is a squared; so plus six a squared. Doing the sign first for each product means you never reach the end of the bracket having flipped some signs and not others, which is the characteristic error. Thinking of the negative monomial as a mirror held up to the whole bracket is a useful overall check: if the bracket had a plus and two minuses, the answer must have a minus and two pluses. If the pattern of signs in your answer is not the exact reverse of the pattern in the bracket, one term missed the mirror.

### subtopic_4 — Multiplying Monomials in Geometry and Checking by Substitution
**Assistant:** Why is x equal to two a better check than x equal to one?

**Tutor:** Because one raised to any power is one, so a substitution of one cannot tell a correct exponent from a wrong one. If you wrote three x times four x squared as twelve x squared instead of twelve x cubed, then at x equal to one both give twelve and the error passes unnoticed. At x equal to two the correct answer gives ninety-six and the wrong one gives forty-eight, and the error is caught. Zero is worse still, because every variable term vanishes and only the constant is tested, so almost any mistake passes. Two is the smallest value that distinguishes powers from each other and is still easy to compute with by hand. For expressions with two letters, use different small values for each, such as two and three, so that terms like a squared b and a b squared, which coincide when a equals b, are told apart. The check costs a few seconds and catches the great majority of expansion errors.
