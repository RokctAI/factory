# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the multiplication of integers and monomials by monomials, binomials and trinomials. A monomial times a monomial multiplies the coefficients and adds the exponents of each variable by the laws of exponents. A monomial times a binomial or trinomial applies the distributive law, multiplying every term inside the bracket by the monomial, with the sign rules for integers governing each product. The anchor products are three x times four x squared, twelve x cubed; two x times bracket three x minus five bracket, six x squared minus ten x; and negative three a times bracket a squared minus two a plus four bracket, negative three a cubed plus six a squared minus twelve a.

## Subtopic: Monomial Times Monomial

A monomial is a single term: three x, four x squared, negative two a b, seven. To multiply two monomials, multiply the coefficients and multiply the variable parts, using the laws of exponents for the variables. Three x times four x squared: coefficients three times four, twelve; variables x times x squared, x to the power one plus two, x cubed. Twelve x cubed.

The law being used is a to the m times a to the n equals a to the m plus n from the exponents topic: when the same letter appears in both factors, add its exponents. Different letters simply sit side by side. Two a times five b is ten a b. Two a times five a is ten a squared.

Signs follow integer rules: same signs give positive, different signs give negative. Negative two x times three x is negative six x squared. Negative two x times negative three x is positive six x squared. Negative four a times five a squared b is negative twenty a cubed b.

The error to avoid: multiplying exponents instead of adding them, or adding coefficients instead of multiplying them. Three x times four x squared is not seven x squared, and it is not twelve x squared. Coefficients multiply; exponents add. A learner who collapses the two rules into one will get one of the wrong answers every time.

Powers of a monomial use the product law for exponents: bracket two x bracket squared is two squared times x squared, four x squared. Bracket negative three a squared bracket cubed is negative twenty-seven a to the power six — the coefficient cubed, sign included, and the exponent multiplied by three. Bracket three x y bracket squared is nine x squared y squared.

Several monomials in a row: two x times three x squared times negative x is negative six x to the power four. Multiply coefficients in one go, two times three times negative one; add exponents in one go, one plus two plus one. A letter with no exponent has exponent one. Questions on monomial products follow now.

## Subtopic: Monomial Times Binomial — The Distributive Law

A binomial is a two-term bracket. To multiply a monomial into a binomial, use the distributive law: a bracket b plus c bracket equals a b plus a c. The monomial multiplies each term inside, and the products are added. Two x times bracket three x minus five bracket: two x times three x is six x squared; two x times negative five is negative ten x. Six x squared minus ten x.

A number times a binomial is the simplest case. Three bracket two a plus seven bracket is six a plus twenty-one. Five bracket x minus four bracket is five x minus twenty. The number reaches both terms. The most common error in Grade 8 algebra is to multiply only the first term: writing three bracket two a plus seven bracket as six a plus seven. Draw an arc from the monomial to each term inside the bracket so that none is missed.

Each product is a monomial times a monomial, so the previous section's rules apply inside each arc: coefficients multiply, exponents add, signs by integer rules. Four a squared bracket two a minus three b bracket: four a squared times two a is eight a cubed; four a squared times negative three b is negative twelve a squared b. Eight a cubed minus twelve a squared b.

The result of a monomial times a binomial is a binomial, unless the two products happen to be like terms, which they will not be if the original bracket had unlike terms. You cannot collect six x squared and negative ten x; the answer stays as two terms.

A check: substitute a small value. For x equal to one, two x bracket three x minus five bracket is two times negative two, negative four; six x squared minus ten x is six minus ten, negative four. Agreement. For a one-term-only error, six x squared minus five at x equal to one gives one, which disagrees, and the error is caught.

Write the arcs, write both products, keep the sign of each term inside the bracket as part of that term when you multiply. Questions on monomial times binomial follow.

## Subtopic: Negative Monomials and Trinomials

A trinomial is a three-term bracket, and the distributive law reaches all three. Two bracket x squared plus three x minus four bracket is two x squared plus six x minus eight. Three arcs, three products.

A negative monomial changes the sign of every term it multiplies. Negative three a times bracket a squared minus two a plus four bracket: negative three a times a squared is negative three a cubed; negative three a times negative two a is positive six a squared; negative three a times four is negative twelve a. Negative three a cubed plus six a squared minus twelve a. Every sign in the bracket has been flipped — plus became minus, minus became plus — because each term was multiplied by a negative.

This is the same sign-flip as a subtracted bracket in the previous lesson, where the coefficient was negative one. Here the coefficient is negative three a, and in addition to flipping signs it multiplies the sizes and adds exponents. Keep the three jobs separate in your head: sign, coefficient, exponent.

Negative x times bracket x minus one bracket: negative x times x is negative x squared; negative x times negative one is positive x. Negative x squared plus x. A learner who writes negative x squared minus x has flipped only the first sign.

The result of a monomial times a trinomial is a trinomial. Two x squared bracket three x squared minus x plus five bracket: six x to the power four minus two x cubed plus ten x squared. Descending powers, three terms.

Multiplying a bracket by a number and then collecting with other terms is a common examination structure. Two bracket x plus three bracket plus three bracket x minus one bracket: expand both, two x plus six plus three x minus three; collect, five x plus three. Five bracket a minus two bracket minus two bracket a plus four bracket: five a minus ten minus two a minus eight; three a minus eighteen. Note the second bracket's plus four became minus eight because negative two reached it. Questions on negatives and trinomials follow.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Monomials in Geometry and Checking by Substitution

Area gives multiplication a picture. A rectangle has length three x plus two and breadth two x. Its area is two x bracket three x plus two bracket, six x squared plus four x. Each term of the answer is the area of a strip: the strip of length three x and breadth two x has area six x squared; the strip of length two and breadth two x has area four x. The distributive law is the statement that the whole rectangle's area is the sum of the strips.

A square of side four a has area bracket four a bracket squared, sixteen a squared — a monomial squared, coefficient squared, exponent doubled. A cube of edge two b has volume bracket two b bracket cubed, eight b cubed. A rectangle of length five m squared and breadth three m has area fifteen m cubed: the exponents add because the lengths are multiplied.

Perimeter is addition and does not multiply the letters: the rectangle with length three x plus two and breadth two x has perimeter two bracket three x plus two bracket plus two bracket two x bracket, six x plus four plus four x, ten x plus four. Questions that ask for both area and perimeter are testing whether you know which operation each needs.

Checking by substitution is the universal safety net. Choose a small value — one, two, or for a trinomial perhaps two to avoid accidental agreements — and evaluate the original and your expansion. Negative three a bracket a squared minus two a plus four bracket at a equal to one: negative three times bracket one minus two plus four bracket, negative three times three, negative nine. Expansion negative three a cubed plus six a squared minus twelve a at a equal to one: negative three plus six minus twelve, negative nine. Agreement.

Avoid a equal to zero as a check, because it makes every variable term vanish and hides most errors; and avoid a equal to one for exponent errors, because one to any power is one and a wrong exponent passes. Two is the safest small check: two squared and two cubed differ. Questions on geometry and checking follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same multiplying as stories about a rectangular garden with an unknown side, a delivery of boxes each holding a mixed order, and a negative number as a mirror that flips everything it touches — stories in which multiplying a bracket is finding an area, and every term inside is a strip that must be counted.

## Subtopic: The Garden With an Unknown Side

A gardener is laying out a rectangular vegetable bed. The breadth is two metres times some number x she has not decided yet — two x metres. The length is three x plus two metres: three times that number, plus two metres for a path at one end. What is the area?

She does not need to decide x first. Draw the rectangle and split the length into its two parts: a part three x long and a part two long. The bed is now two strips. The first strip is two x by three x: six x squared square metres. The second strip is two x by two: four x square metres. Total area: six x squared plus four x. That is two x bracket three x plus two bracket, expanded, and the drawing is why the distributive law is true — a rectangle cut into strips has an area equal to the sum of the strips.

A learner who writes six x squared plus two has forgotten the second strip entirely — the path strip has breadth two x, not one, and its area is four x. Every term in the bracket is a strip, and every strip has the full breadth.

Later she decides x is three. Breadth six metres, length eleven metres, area sixty-six square metres. Check the expression: six times nine plus four times three is fifty-four plus twelve, sixty-six. The expression was right before she knew x, and it stays right after.

Now the fence. Perimeter is two lengths plus two breadths: two bracket three x plus two bracket plus two bracket two x bracket, which is six x plus four plus four x, ten x plus four. With x equal to three, thirty-four metres. The area multiplied the sides; the perimeter added them. Different operations, different shapes of answer — one has x squared in it, the other does not.

A three-part length works the same. If the length were three x plus two plus x squared — three strips — the area would have three terms: two x times each. Two x times x squared is two x cubed, so six x squared plus four x plus two x cubed, written as two x cubed plus six x squared plus four x. Trinomial in, trinomial out. Questions on the garden follow.

## Subtopic: Boxes of Mixed Orders and the Mirror

A shop packs each order into a box containing three pens, two notebooks and one ruler. A school orders five boxes. How many of each? Five times bracket three p plus two n plus r bracket: fifteen p plus ten n plus five r. Fifteen pens, ten notebooks, five rulers. The five reaches every item in the box; it would be absurd to say five boxes contain fifteen pens, two notebooks and one ruler. Yet that is exactly what the error six a plus seven for three bracket two a plus seven bracket amounts to — multiplying the first item in the box and forgetting the rest.

Now x boxes, where x is not yet known. x bracket three p plus two n plus r bracket is three x p plus two x n plus x r. The number of boxes multiplies every item. And if each box also contains x pens — say the pen count depends on the order size — then x bracket x p plus two n bracket is x squared p plus two x n: x times x is x squared, by the exponent law.

Here is the mirror. A negative number in front of a bracket is like holding the bracket up to a mirror: every sign is reversed. Negative two bracket three a minus four bracket: three a becomes negative six a, negative four becomes positive eight. Negative six a plus eight. What was positive is negative, what was negative is positive, and the sizes are doubled. The mirror does not choose which terms to flip. It flips all of them.

A learner's typical slip: negative two bracket three a minus four bracket written as negative six a minus eight. The first term went through the mirror and the second did not. Look at the answer and ask: did every sign change? If the bracket had a plus and a minus, the answer must have a minus and a plus.

Combining the two pictures: negative three a bracket a squared minus two a plus four bracket is three boxes, each containing a squared, minus two a, plus four, with the whole delivery returned — negative. Three of everything, with every sign reversed, and the a multiplying each item: negative three a cubed plus six a squared minus twelve a. Three items in, three items out, all through the mirror. Questions on boxes and mirrors follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for multiplying a monomial into a bracket. One: draw an arc from the monomial to each term inside — two arcs for a binomial, three for a trinomial. Two: for each arc, multiply the signs, multiply the coefficients, add the exponents of each letter. Three: write the products in order. Four: if there are several brackets, expand each and then collect like terms. Five: check with a substitution, preferably two.

A typical question: expand and simplify three x bracket two x minus one bracket minus x bracket x plus four bracket. First bracket: six x squared minus three x. Second, with the negative x: negative x squared minus four x. Collect: five x squared minus seven x.

Another: expand negative two a squared bracket three a squared minus a b plus two b squared bracket. Negative six a to the power four plus two a cubed b minus four a squared b squared. Each sign flipped, each coefficient doubled, exponents of a increased by two.

Another: the length of a rectangle is x plus five and the breadth is three x. Write its area and perimeter. Area: three x squared plus fifteen x. Perimeter: two bracket x plus five bracket plus six x, which is eight x plus ten.

Another: simplify bracket two x bracket squared times three x. Four x squared times three x is twelve x cubed. The power applies to the two as well as the x.

Errors to avoid. Multiplying only the first term in the bracket. Adding exponents of different letters, or multiplying exponents of the same letter. Adding coefficients instead of multiplying them. Flipping only the first sign with a negative monomial. Forgetting that bracket two x bracket squared squares the two. Confusing area, which multiplies, with perimeter, which adds.
