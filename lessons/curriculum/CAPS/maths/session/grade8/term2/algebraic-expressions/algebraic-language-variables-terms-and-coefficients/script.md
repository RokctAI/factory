# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops algebraic language: the interpretation of rules in symbolic form and the identification of variables, constants, like and unlike terms, coefficients and exponents. An expression is a collection of terms separated by plus and minus signs; each term has a coefficient, which may be negative, and variables with exponents; a constant is a term with no variable. Like terms have identical variable parts including exponents. The anchor expression is three x squared minus five x plus seven, which has three terms, coefficients three and negative five, a constant seven, variable x and exponent two on the first term.

## Subtopic: Terms, Variables and Constants

An algebraic expression is a mathematical phrase built from numbers, letters and operations. Three x squared minus five x plus seven is an expression. It is not an equation, because it has no equals sign; an expression is something you can simplify or evaluate, an equation is a statement that two expressions are equal. Keep the words apart.

An expression is made of terms. Terms are the pieces separated by plus and minus signs that are not inside brackets. Three x squared minus five x plus seven has three terms: three x squared, negative five x, and seven. The sign in front of a term belongs to the term. The second term is negative five x, not five x. Count terms by counting the plus and minus signs between pieces and adding one — two signs, three terms.

Multiplication does not separate terms. Three x squared is one term, even though it is three times x times x. Two a b is one term. Four bracket x plus one bracket is one term, because the bracket is multiplied by the four; inside the bracket there are two terms, but the whole product is a single term in the larger expression until the bracket is expanded. A fraction such as x over two is also one term.

A variable is a letter that stands for a number whose value can change or is not yet known. In three x squared minus five x plus seven, the variable is x. Expressions can have several variables: two a plus three b minus c has variables a, b and c.

A constant is a term with no variable — a plain number whose value does not change. Seven is the constant in the anchor expression. If an expression has no plain number, its constant is zero; we say it has no constant term.

The number of terms names the expression. One term is a monomial: five x squared. Two terms is a binomial: three x minus two. Three terms is a trinomial: x squared plus two x plus one. More than three is a polynomial, and in fact all of these are polynomials. Questions on terms, variables and constants follow now.

## Subtopic: Coefficients and Exponents

The coefficient of a term is the number multiplied by the variable part. In three x squared the coefficient is three. In negative five x the coefficient is negative five — the sign is part of the coefficient. In x, written alone, the coefficient is one, because x means one x; the one is understood and not written. In negative x the coefficient is negative one.

The coefficient of a term with several variables is still just the number. In two a b the coefficient is two. In negative four x squared y the coefficient is negative four. A fraction coefficient appears when a term is divided by a number: x over two has coefficient a half, and three x over four has coefficient three quarters.

The exponent of a variable tells how many times it is a factor, exactly as in the exponents topic. In three x squared the exponent of x is two. In negative five x the exponent of x is one, understood and not written. In two a b both a and b have exponent one. In seven, the constant, there is no variable and so no exponent to speak of, though you can think of it as seven x to the power zero if that helps, since x to the zero is one.

The degree of a term is the sum of the exponents of its variables, and the degree of an expression is the highest degree among its terms. Three x squared has degree two; negative five x has degree one; two a b has degree two. Three x squared minus five x plus seven has degree two. Degree is a Grade 9 word in the main, but the idea — looking at the highest exponent — is used now when arranging terms.

Terms are usually written in descending order of exponent: x squared terms, then x terms, then constants. Seven minus five x plus three x squared is the same expression as three x squared minus five x plus seven, rearranged; when you rearrange, carry each sign with its term.

A question may ask for the coefficient of x in an expression that has several x terms before simplifying. In two x plus five minus seven x, the coefficient of x after collecting is two minus seven, negative five. Simplify first, then read off. Questions on coefficients and exponents follow.

## Subtopic: Like and Unlike Terms

Like terms have exactly the same variable part — the same letters with the same exponents. Their coefficients may differ. Three x and negative seven x are like terms: both are a number times x. Two a b and five a b are like terms. Four x squared and x squared are like terms. Seven and negative two are like terms, since both are constants with no variable.

Unlike terms differ in their variable part. Three x and three x squared are unlike: the exponents differ. Two a and two b are unlike: the letters differ. Five x y and five x are unlike: one has a y, the other does not. Six and six x are unlike: one is a constant, the other has a variable.

The test is mechanical. Cover the coefficient and compare what remains. If what remains is identical, the terms are like. x squared and x: cover the coefficients, one and one, and compare x squared with x — not identical, unlike. Three a b and negative a b: compare a b with a b, identical, like.

Order of letters does not matter within a term, because multiplication is commutative. Two a b and three b a are like terms, since b a is a b. Write variables in alphabetical order to make this obvious: three b a becomes three a b.

Why does it matter? Only like terms can be added or subtracted into a single term, which is the whole of the next lesson. Three x plus four x is seven x. Three x plus four x squared stays as it is — two terms, nothing to collect. Learners who write seven x squared, or seven x cubed, have added unlike terms, and this is the most common algebra error in Grade 8.

In an expression such as five x squared minus three x plus two x squared plus x minus four, identify the like groups before doing anything: the x squared terms are five x squared and two x squared; the x terms are negative three x and x; the constant is negative four. Grouping first, with signs attached, prevents mixing. Questions on like and unlike terms follow.

## Subtopic: Translating Word Rules and Symbolic Rules

Algebra is a language for rules, and you must translate both ways. From words to symbols: a number increased by five is x plus five. Twice a number is two x. Three less than a number is x minus three — not three minus x, because less than reverses the order. The product of a number and itself is x squared. Half a number is x over two. The sum of two numbers is a plus b. Five more than twice a number is two x plus five.

From symbols to words: three x minus two reads as two less than three times a number, or three times a number, minus two. x squared plus one reads as one more than the square of a number. Four bracket x plus three bracket reads as four times the sum of a number and three — the bracket means the sum is formed first.

Order of operations in words. Twice the sum of a number and three is two bracket x plus three bracket; the sum is formed first, then doubled. The sum of twice a number and three is two x plus three; the doubling happens first, then the three is added. The two phrases differ by where the word sum sits, and the two expressions differ by a bracket. Read for the structure.

Rules from the patterns topic are expressions. The chairs rule two n plus two is an expression in n with coefficient two and constant two. The matchstick rule three n plus one is an expression with coefficient three and constant one. In context, the coefficient is the rate — chairs per table — and the constant is the once-off amount. The algebraic names from this lesson attach to meanings you already have.

Formulas are rules too. Area of a rectangle: length times breadth, l b, one term with two variables. Perimeter of a rectangle: two l plus two b, two terms. Cost of hiring a hall: five hundred plus forty g, constant five hundred and coefficient forty. Whenever you see a formula, name its parts — it turns a thing to memorise into a thing you understand.

A question may give a rule in words and ask which expression matches, or give an expression and ask for the rule in words. Test with a number if unsure: for x equal to two, three less than a number is negative one, and x minus three gives negative one while three minus x gives one. The test settles it. Questions on translating follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same language through a shopping basket, a bag of mixed coins and a recipe written in code — stories in which a letter stands for something you do not yet know, and the words for the parts of an expression are the words that let two people talk about it without confusion.

## Subtopic: The Shopping Basket

Here is algebra as a shopping basket. You put three apples, five bananas and one loaf of bread in a basket. Written as an expression: three a plus five b plus one l, or three a plus five b plus l. Each kind of item is a term. The letter is the kind of item — the variable, because the price of an apple could be anything until you reach the till. The number in front is how many of that item — the coefficient. The loaf has coefficient one, which you do not bother to write.

Like terms are the same kind of item. If a friend adds two more apples to the basket, you now have three a plus two a, which is five a. You can add apples to apples. You cannot add apples to bananas: three a plus five b is just three a plus five b, two kinds of item, two terms, and no amount of wishing turns it into eight of anything. Three apples and five bananas are not eight apple-bananas. That is the whole like-terms rule in one basket.

A constant is cash. If you also put a ten rand note in the basket, that is plus ten: a term with no letter because it is not an item whose price is unknown. Ten rand is ten rand. The constant is the part of the expression that does not depend on any price.

Exponents in the basket are a stretch, but try this. An apple squared is not an apple — a squared is a different kind of thing from a, the way a square metre is a different kind of thing from a metre. So three a squared and five a are unlike, and a basket with three a squared plus five a has two kinds of thing in it. You cannot collect them. This is why x squared and x are never like terms: the exponent changes what kind of thing the term is.

Negative coefficients are items taken out. Three a minus two a: you had three apples and someone took two, one apple left. Three a minus five a: you had three and someone wants five, so you owe two apples, negative two a. The sign goes with the count.

The basket reading makes two things automatic. Count the kinds of item to count the terms. Only combine the same kind. Questions on the basket follow.

## Subtopic: The Bag of Coins and the Coded Recipe

A bag holds some two-rand coins and some five-rand coins; you do not know how many of each. Let t be the number of two-rand coins and f the number of five-rand coins. The value of the bag is two t plus five f rand. Now the coefficients are not counts but values per coin, and the variables are the counts. Same grammar, different meaning: two t is one term, two is its coefficient, t its variable. If there are also three loose one-rand coins at the bottom, the value is two t plus five f plus three, and the three is a constant because it is known.

Someone tips in four more two-rand coins. The two-rand coins become t plus four, and the value becomes two bracket t plus four bracket plus five f plus three. That bracket is a single term for now, worth two times whatever t plus four is. Next lesson you will expand it to two t plus eight; this lesson you only need to see that two bracket t plus four bracket is one term containing a two-term bracket.

Now a recipe written in code. A chef writes the cost of a dish as four e plus two f plus twelve, where e is the price of an egg and f the price of a slice of fish. Read it: four eggs, two slices of fish and twelve rand of other ingredients whose cost is fixed. Coefficients four and two, constant twelve, variables e and f. If eggs cost three rand and fish costs fifteen, the dish costs twelve plus thirty plus twelve, fifty-four rand — evaluating by substitution, which is a lesson later this topic. If the chef doubles the eggs, the expression becomes eight e plus two f plus twelve: the coefficient of e doubles and nothing else changes.

A trick question the chef might set: how many terms in four e plus two f plus twelve? Three. And in four e f plus twelve? Two, because four e f is one term — four of some item that costs an egg times a fish, which is not a sensible item, but the grammar is still clear. Multiplication inside a term does not make new terms.

Read every expression the way you read these: what kinds of thing, how many of each, what is fixed. Then the names — term, variable, coefficient, constant — are not vocabulary to memorise but a description of what you can see. Questions on coins and recipes follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The checklist for reading any expression. One: count the terms by the plus and minus signs between pieces, not inside brackets. Two: name the variables. Three: for each term, read the coefficient with its sign, the variables and their exponents. Four: find the constant, if any. Five: group like terms by identical variable parts, exponents included.

A typical question: for the expression two x cubed minus x squared plus four x minus nine, state the number of terms, the coefficient of x squared, the constant and the exponent of x in the third term. Four terms. Coefficient of x squared: negative one. Constant: negative nine. Exponent in four x: one.

Another: which of the following are like terms: three a squared b, negative a squared b, two a b squared, five b a squared? Variable parts: a squared b, a squared b, a b squared, a squared b. The first, second and fourth are like; the third has the exponents on the other letters and is unlike.

Another: write an expression for five less than three times a number, and for three times five less than a number. First: three x minus five. Second: three bracket x minus five bracket. The bracket appears when the subtraction is done before the multiplication.

Another: a taxi charges a flag fee of twelve rand and eight rand per kilometre. Write the cost for k kilometres and name the coefficient and constant. Eight k plus twelve; coefficient eight is the rate per kilometre, constant twelve is the flag fee.

Errors to avoid. Dropping the sign of a coefficient — the coefficient of x in three minus x is negative one. Counting a bracketed product as several terms. Treating x squared and x as like terms. Writing three minus x for three less than x. Forgetting the understood one in x and the understood exponent one in x.

Looking ahead: the next lesson adds and subtracts like terms using the laws you have named, and the lessons after multiply and divide terms, take their roots and substitute values. Grade 9 extends to multiplying binomials and to factorising. Final questions follow.
