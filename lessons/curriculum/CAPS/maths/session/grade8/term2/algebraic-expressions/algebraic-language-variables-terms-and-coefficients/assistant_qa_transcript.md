### subtopic_1 — Terms, Variables and Constants
**Assistant:** Is 4(x + 1) one term or two?

**Tutor:** One term, until the bracket is expanded. Terms are separated by plus and minus signs that sit between pieces at the top level of the expression, not by signs inside brackets. Four bracket x plus one bracket is the number four multiplied by the whole bracket, and multiplication does not separate terms, so the product is a single term — in the same way that three x squared is a single term even though it is three times x times x. Inside the bracket there are two terms, x and one, but they belong to the bracket, not to the larger expression. When the bracket is expanded in a later lesson, four bracket x plus one bracket becomes four x plus four, which is two terms, and the count changes because the expression has been rewritten. So the honest answer is: in the form given, one term; after expanding, two. An examiner asking how many terms are in four bracket x plus one bracket plus two expects the answer two, reading the expression as written.

### subtopic_2 — Coefficients and Exponents
**Assistant:** If a term is written as −x, what is its coefficient, and why does it matter?

**Tutor:** The coefficient is negative one. The term negative x means negative one times x, with the one understood and not written, exactly as x alone means one x. It matters because the coefficient is what you add when collecting like terms: three x minus x is three x plus negative one x, which is two x, and a learner who treats the coefficient of negative x as zero or as negative zero will get three x and lose the mark. It also matters when a question asks directly for the coefficient of x in an expression such as five minus x; the answer is negative one, and the two most common wrong answers are one, from dropping the sign, and five, from reading the wrong term. The habit that prevents both is to rewrite any bare letter with its understood coefficient — x as one x, negative x as negative one x — in your head or on paper before you read off or collect.

### subtopic_3 — Like and Unlike Terms
**Assistant:** Why can x² and x never be like terms, when they have the same letter?

**Tutor:** Because like terms must have identical variable parts, and the exponent is part of the variable part. x squared means x times x; it is a different kind of quantity from x, in the way a square metre is a different kind of quantity from a metre — you cannot add three square metres to five metres and get eight of anything. The shopping-basket picture says the same thing: x squared is a different item from x, so a basket with three x squared plus five x holds two kinds of item and the expression has two terms that stay separate. The mechanical test is to cover the coefficients and compare what is left: x squared against x is not identical, so the terms are unlike. This is the single most frequent algebra error in Grade 8 — writing three x plus four x squared as seven x squared or seven x cubed — and it disappears as soon as you check exponents as carefully as you check letters.

### subtopic_4 — Translating Word Rules and Symbolic Rules
**Assistant:** How do I tell whether a word rule needs a bracket?

**Tutor:** Look for a word like sum, difference or total that groups numbers together before another operation acts on them, and check the order of the operations in the words. Twice the sum of a number and three says the sum is formed first — x plus three — and then doubled, so the sum must be in a bracket: two bracket x plus three bracket. The sum of twice a number and three says the doubling comes first and three is added afterwards: two x plus three, no bracket, because multiplication already happens before addition. Three times five less than a number says five less than a number is formed first — x minus five — then multiplied: three bracket x minus five bracket. If you are unsure, test with a number: for x equal to one, twice the sum of one and three is eight, and two times one plus three is five, so the two readings are different and the bracket matters. A bracket is the written form of doing something first.
