### subtopic_1 — Substituting a Positive Whole Number
**Assistant:** Why do I have to write the substitution line instead of just working out the answer in my head?

**Tutor:** Because the substitution line is where the structure of the expression is preserved and where the marks are awarded. Three x squared minus five x plus seven at x equal to three means three times the square of three, minus five times three, plus seven, and writing three bracket three bracket squared minus five bracket three bracket plus seven keeps each of those relationships visible: the square is on the bracket only, the three in front multiplies the result, the five multiplies its own bracket. Done in the head, the most common slips are squaring the product three times three to get eighty-one, or reading three bracket three bracket as thirty-three. The written line also makes the order of operations mechanical — you can see the power to do first, the two multiplications to do next, and the additions last — and if the final answer is wrong, the marker can see where the method was right and give credit for it. One extra line costs ten seconds and typically saves two or three marks per question.

### subtopic_2 — Substituting a Negative Number — Brackets as Seatbelt
**Assistant:** What exactly goes wrong when I substitute a negative without brackets?

**Tutor:** Two separate things, and both are frequent. First, the power attaches to the wrong thing: negative two squared, written without brackets, reads as the negative of two squared, which is negative four, whereas the value you intended to square was negative two, whose square is positive four. In three x squared at x equal to negative two that is the difference between negative twelve and positive twelve. Second, the operation sign and the number's sign collide: minus five x at x equal to negative two becomes minus five minus two, which is read as negative seven, instead of minus five times negative two, which is positive ten. Brackets fix both at once — three bracket negative two bracket squared minus five bracket negative two bracket — because the bracket keeps the negative glued to its number and separate from the operation in front. The habit must be unconditional: every negative, every time, even when you are sure, because the error only needs one lapse.

### subtopic_4 — Substitution as a Check on Algebra
**Assistant:** Can substitution prove that two expressions are equal?

**Tutor:** No, but it can prove they are not equal, and it gives strong evidence of equality when used well. If two expressions are truly equal as algebra, they agree for every value, so a single disagreement is a certain sign of an error somewhere. Agreement at one value is weaker, because two different expressions can coincide by chance — x squared and two x agree at x equal to two, and bracket x plus three bracket squared and x squared plus nine agree at x equal to zero. Agreement at two or three well-chosen values makes coincidence very unlikely for the kinds of expressions you meet in Grade 8, which is why the check is worth doing in an examination. Choose values that expose errors: two rather than one, so that powers differ, and different values for different letters, so that a squared b and a b squared are told apart. The algebraic argument — expanding, collecting, applying the laws — is the proof; substitution is the inspection that catches a slip in the proof.

### subtopic_5 — The Till That Knows Only the Formula
**Assistant:** In the till story, why does 2s² give fifty but (2s)² give a hundred?

**Tutor:** Because the bracket decides what gets squared, and the two expressions describe different things. Two s squared means two times the square of s: the s squared is the area of the tray in square centimetres, and the two is the price per square centimetre, so at s equal to five the area is twenty-five and the cost is fifty rand. Bracket two s bracket squared means the square of two s: double the side first, to ten, and then square, to a hundred — which would be the area of a tray with sides twice as long, not twice the price of the original tray. Order of operations is what makes the unbracketed version square only the s: a power applies to the symbol immediately before it, and the two is multiplied in afterwards. The same distinction appeared in the exponents topic as bracket negative three bracket squared against negative three squared, and in the algebra lessons as bracket three x bracket squared against three x squared. Read the bracket before you press any buttons.
