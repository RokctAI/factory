# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the division of monomials, binomials and trinomials by integers and monomials, and the simplification of the resulting expressions. A monomial divided by a monomial divides the coefficients and subtracts exponents of each common letter by the laws of exponents. A binomial or trinomial divided by a monomial applies the distributive law to division: each term in the numerator is divided by the denominator separately. The anchor quotients are twelve x cubed over three x, which is four x squared; bracket six x squared minus ten x bracket over two x, which is three x minus five; and bracket nine a cubed minus six a squared plus three a bracket over three a, which is three a squared minus two a plus one.

## Subtopic: Monomial Divided by Monomial

Division undoes multiplication, so the rules reverse. To divide one monomial by another, divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents of each common letter. Twelve x cubed divided by three x: coefficients twelve over three, four; exponents three minus one, two. Four x squared. Check by multiplying back: four x squared times three x is twelve x cubed.

The law is a to the m divided by a to the n equals a to the m minus n, from the exponents topic. The exponent on top minus the exponent underneath. Fifteen a to the power five over five a squared is three a cubed. Eight y to the power four over two y to the power four is four y to the zero, which is four, since y to the zero is one — the letters cancel completely when the exponents match.

Signs follow integer rules. Negative twelve x cubed over three x is negative four x squared. Negative twelve x cubed over negative three x is positive four x squared. Twelve x cubed over negative three x is negative four x squared.

Several letters: each letter is handled separately. Twenty a cubed b squared over four a b is five a squared b. A letter that appears only on top stays on top: six x squared y over two x is three x y. A letter that appears only underneath stays underneath: six x squared over two x y is three x over y — a fraction, since the y cannot be cancelled.

When the exponent underneath is larger, the answer is a fraction with the letter underneath. Four x over two x cubed is two over x squared, by cancelling one x from top and bottom and leaving two underneath. Grade 9 writes this as two x to the power negative two; in Grade 8, write the fraction.

Coefficients that do not divide exactly become fractions in lowest terms. Three x squared over six x is x over two, or a half x. Do not write it as a decimal unless the question used decimals.

Write every division as a fraction before you start — twelve x cubed over three x, with a horizontal bar — because the bar makes it clear what divides what, and it will matter when the top has several terms. Questions on monomial division follow now.

## Subtopic: Binomial Divided by a Monomial

A fraction bar with two terms on top means the whole top is divided by the bottom. Bracket six x squared minus ten x bracket over two x. The distributive law applies to division just as to multiplication: bracket a plus b bracket over c equals a over c plus b over c. Each term on top is divided by the bottom separately. Six x squared over two x is three x; negative ten x over two x is negative five. Three x minus five.

Check by multiplying back: two x bracket three x minus five bracket is six x squared minus ten x. Division has exactly undone the multiplication from the previous lesson; the two lessons are mirror images.

The error to avoid: dividing only the first term. Six x squared minus ten x over two x written as three x minus ten x. The ten x was on top as well, under the same bar, and it must be divided too. Draw a bar under each term — split the fraction into two fractions — before dividing. Bracket six x squared minus ten x bracket over two x becomes six x squared over two x minus ten x over two x, and now each piece is a monomial division.

Signs: each term carries its sign into its own fraction. Bracket eight a cubed minus four a squared bracket over negative four a: eight a cubed over negative four a is negative two a squared; negative four a squared over negative four a is positive a. Negative two a squared plus a.

Dividing by a number alone: bracket ten x plus fifteen bracket over five is two x plus three. Bracket four y squared minus six bracket over two is two y squared minus three. The letters are untouched because the bottom has none.

When a term does not divide exactly, leave a fraction in that term. Bracket six x squared plus three bracket over two x is three x plus three over two x. The first term divides, the second does not, and the answer is a binomial with a fractional second term. Do not force it; write what is true.

The result of a binomial divided by a monomial is a binomial, unless a term becomes a constant and another a constant too, which does not happen with unlike terms. Questions on binomial division follow.

## Subtopic: Trinomial Divided by a Monomial and the Term That Becomes One

A trinomial divided by a monomial follows the same law with three pieces. Bracket nine a cubed minus six a squared plus three a bracket over three a. Three fractions: nine a cubed over three a is three a squared; negative six a squared over three a is negative two a; three a over three a is one. Three a squared minus two a plus one.

The last term is the one most often lost. Three a over three a is one, not zero. A learner who writes three a squared minus two a has dropped the third term, apparently thinking that a term divided by itself vanishes. It does not vanish; it becomes one. Any non-zero quantity divided by itself is one, and a term that divides to one is still a term in the answer. Check: three a bracket three a squared minus two a plus one bracket is nine a cubed minus six a squared plus three a, and without the plus one the product would lack the three a.

Another: bracket four x squared y minus eight x y squared plus twelve x y bracket over four x y. Four x squared y over four x y is x; negative eight x y squared over four x y is negative two y; twelve x y over four x y is three. x minus two y plus three.

Another, with a negative divisor: bracket negative six m cubed plus three m squared minus nine m bracket over negative three m. Two m squared minus m plus three. Every sign flipped, as a negative divisor demands, and the middle term's coefficient three over negative three is negative one, giving negative m.

Another, with a numerical divisor: bracket twelve p squared minus eight p plus four bracket over four. Three p squared minus two p plus one. Letters untouched; constant four over four is one.

Collecting after dividing: bracket ten x squared minus five x bracket over five x, plus x. Two x minus one plus x, which is three x minus one. Divide first, because the fraction bar groups its top; then collect with any terms outside the fraction.

Three terms in, three terms out. If your answer to a trinomial division has two terms, a term has been lost — almost always the one that became one. Questions on trinomial division follow.

## Subtopic: Simplifying Fractions with Letters and Checking

Sometimes a division is presented as a fraction to simplify, with common factors to cancel. Twelve x cubed over three x is simplified by cancelling: the three into the twelve leaves four, one x from the bottom cancels one x from the top leaving x squared. Four x squared. Cancelling and subtracting exponents are the same operation seen two ways.

Cancelling is only allowed across a product, never across a sum. In bracket six x squared minus ten x bracket over two x, you may cancel two x from each term because each term is a product containing two x. You may not cancel the x in bracket x plus five bracket over x to get one plus five, because the x on top is only one term of the sum, not a factor of the whole top. Bracket x plus five bracket over x is one plus five over x, by splitting the fraction. The rule: cancel factors, not terms.

Simplifying with the division law when exponents are equal gives a number: six a squared b over three a squared b is two. When the top is a single term that matches the bottom exactly, the answer is one: five x y over five x y is one.

Checking a division has two routes. Multiply back: divisor times answer should give the original top. Three a times bracket three a squared minus two a plus one bracket is nine a cubed minus six a squared plus three a. Or substitute: for a equal to two, the top is seventy-two minus twenty-four plus six, fifty-four; the bottom is six; fifty-four over six is nine. The answer, three a squared minus two a plus one, at a equal to two is twelve minus four plus one, nine. Agreement.

Substitution catches the dropped-term error: three a squared minus two a at a equal to two gives eight, not nine. It also catches a cancelled term: bracket x plus five bracket over x at x equal to one is six, while one plus five is six — accidental agreement at one — so use x equal to two: seven over two, three and a half, against six. Use two, not one.

Avoid dividing by a quantity that could be zero without saying so: bracket six x squared minus ten x bracket over two x is three x minus five provided x is not zero, since division by zero is undefined. The provision is a Grade 9 formality, but knowing why it is there separates understanding from rote. Questions on simplifying and checking follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same dividing as stories about sharing a mixed delivery equally among vans, cutting a garden bed back into its strips, and a fraction bar that works like a bracket — stories in which dividing a sum means dividing every part of the sum, and a stray term left undivided is a van that drove off with the whole load.

## Subtopic: Sharing the Delivery Among Vans

A warehouse has a delivery to share equally among three vans. The delivery is nine a cubed crates of one kind, minus six a squared crates of a second kind that are being returned, plus three a crates of a third kind — never mind what the a stands for; it is some count not yet fixed. Each van gets a third of everything: three a cubed, minus two a squared, plus a. Dividing the whole delivery by three means dividing every part of it by three. That is the distributive law for division, and it is just fairness: you cannot give the first van all the returns and call it an equal share.

Now share the same delivery among three a vans instead. Each van gets nine a cubed over three a, which is three a squared, minus six a squared over three a, which is two a, plus three a over three a, which is one. Three a squared minus two a plus one. The last item is the one drivers forget: three a crates shared among three a vans is one crate each, not zero. A crate does not vanish because the number of vans matches the number of crates.

Here is the error dressed as a story. A driver divides the first kind of crate among the vans and then drives off with all of the second and third kinds in his own van. On paper that is nine a cubed over three a, minus six a squared, plus three a — the first term divided and the rest not. It is wrong on the loading dock, and it is wrong on the page. Everything under the bar gets shared.

A fraction bar is a bracket. Bracket six x squared minus ten x bracket over two x and six x squared minus ten x with a bar beneath both are the same thing; the bar groups the top the way a bracket does. When you write a division on one line, keep the brackets; when you write it with a bar, make the bar long enough to cover every term.

The multiplication lesson filled the vans — three a times bracket three a squared minus two a plus one bracket loaded nine a cubed minus six a squared plus three a. This lesson empties them. If you ever doubt a division, load the vans again by multiplying your answer by the divisor and see whether the original delivery comes back. Questions on the vans follow.

## Subtopic: Cutting the Garden Bed Back Into Strips

Last lesson a gardener found the area of a bed two x wide and three x plus two long: six x squared plus four x. Today she has the opposite problem. She knows the bed has area six x squared plus four x and width two x, and she wants the length. Length is area divided by width: bracket six x squared plus four x bracket over two x.

Picture the bed as its two strips again. The strip with area six x squared and width two x has length three x. The strip with area four x and width two x has length two. Total length three x plus two. Dividing the area by the width means dividing each strip's area by the width, and the lengths add. That is the distributive law for division, drawn.

A learner who divides only the first strip gets a length of three x plus four x — the second strip is still an area, not a length, and the units do not even match. Every strip must be divided by the width.

Now a bed with area nine a cubed minus six a squared plus three a and width three a. Three strips: lengths three a squared, negative two a, and one. Three a squared minus two a plus one. The strip of area three a and width three a has length one — a strip one unit long, not a strip that is not there. Negative strips are a stretch of the picture, but the arithmetic is the same.

The width could be a number. A bed of area ten x plus fifteen and width five has length two x plus three. The letters stay, because the width has none to cancel.

And the bed cannot have width zero; a bed of zero width has no area to divide. That is why the division is only meaningful for a width that is not zero, which is the formal condition x not equal to zero you may see written beside such a quotient.

So: area over width is length, strip by strip, and if the number of strips in your answer is fewer than the number of terms in the area, a strip went missing. Questions on the garden follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for dividing by a monomial. One: write the division as a fraction with the bar covering the whole top. Two: split the fraction into one fraction per term on top, each term keeping its sign. Three: for each fraction, divide the signs, divide the coefficients, subtract the exponents of each common letter. Four: write the quotients in order; a term that divides to one is still a term. Five: check by multiplying back or substituting two.

A typical question: simplify bracket fifteen x to the power four minus ten x cubed plus five x squared bracket over five x squared. Three x squared minus two x plus one.

Another: simplify bracket eight a squared b minus twelve a b squared bracket over negative four a b. Negative two a plus three b.

Another: simplify bracket six m squared minus nine m bracket over three m, minus bracket m plus two bracket. Divide first: two m minus three. Then subtract the bracket: two m minus three minus m minus two, which is m minus five.

Another: a rectangle has area twelve x squared plus eight x and breadth four x. Find the length and the perimeter. Length: three x plus two. Perimeter: two bracket three x plus two bracket plus two bracket four x bracket, six x plus four plus eight x, fourteen x plus four.

Another: simplify bracket x squared plus x bracket over x. x plus one — not x squared plus one, which would be dividing only the second term, and not x plus x, which divides the exponent wrongly.

Errors to avoid. Dividing only the first term. Dropping a term that divides to one. Cancelling a term across a sum instead of a factor across a product. Subtracting coefficients instead of dividing them. Adding exponents instead of subtracting. Forgetting the sign flip with a negative divisor. Writing a fraction bar too short to cover the whole top.
