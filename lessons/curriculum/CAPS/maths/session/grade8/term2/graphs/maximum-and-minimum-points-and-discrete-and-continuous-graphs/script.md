# Part 1 — Expert

This session extends the interpretation of global graphs to maximum and minimum points and to the distinction between discrete and continuous graphs. A maximum is a point where the graph changes from increasing to decreasing and the dependent variable is at its greatest; a minimum is where it changes from decreasing to increasing and is at its least; a graph may have local turning points as well as an overall highest and lowest value. A graph is discrete when the variables can take only separate values, such as counts, and is drawn as unjoined points; it is continuous when the variables can take any value in an interval, such as time, length or temperature, and is drawn as an unbroken line. The anchor graphs are a thrown ball with a maximum height, a day's temperature with an afternoon maximum and a pre-dawn minimum, and the cost of tickets against the number of tickets, which is discrete.

## Subtopic: Maximum and Minimum Points

A maximum point on a graph is a point where the graph stops increasing and starts decreasing: the top of a hill. At the maximum, the dependent variable has its greatest value in that part of the graph. A minimum point is where the graph stops decreasing and starts increasing: the bottom of a dip. At the minimum, the dependent variable has its least value in that part.

The thrown ball. Height against time: the graph rises, reaches the top, and falls. The top is the maximum — the greatest height the ball reaches — and the time at which it happens is read off the horizontal axis. There is no minimum in the middle of the flight; the lowest heights are at the start and end, on the ground.

Temperature through a day. The graph rises from dawn, reaches its highest in the mid-afternoon, and falls through the night to its lowest just before dawn. The afternoon peak is the maximum; the pre-dawn trough is the minimum. A question may ask at what time the maximum occurred — read across to the time axis — or what the maximum temperature was — read up to the temperature axis, if there is a scale.

A graph can have more than one hill and dip. Takings in a shop through the day: a peak mid-morning, a dip at lunchtime, a peak mid-afternoon. Two maxima and one minimum between them. The highest of the maxima is the overall, or global, maximum; the others are local maxima — the top of a smaller hill. The lowest point of the whole graph is the global minimum, which may be at an end rather than at a dip.

The graph is flat for an instant at a maximum or minimum: the quantity has stopped increasing and not yet started decreasing. On a smooth curve the top is rounded; on a graph made of straight pieces the top is a corner. Both are maxima.

To describe a maximum or minimum, give three things: where it is on the horizontal axis, what the value is on the vertical axis if known, and what it means in the situation — the ball is highest at this moment; the temperature peaks mid-afternoon. Questions on maxima and minima follow now.

## Subtopic: Reading Maxima and Minima in Context

Maximum and minimum points answer the questions when was it most and when was it least, which are often the most useful things a graph tells you.

A profit graph for a business against the month: the maximum shows the best month, the minimum the worst, and a minimum below the horizontal axis is a loss. A heart-rate graph during exercise: the maximum is the hardest moment of the workout; the minimum is at rest. A water-level graph for a dam over a year: the maximum after the rainy season, the minimum before it. A learner's test marks over a term: the maximum is the best mark, the minimum the worst, and the graph between is read for improvement or decline.

The horizontal position of a maximum is as informative as its height. The hottest time of day is mid-afternoon, not noon, because the ground keeps absorbing heat after the sun is highest; the graph shows that lag. A shop's busiest time might be eleven in the morning; staffing is planned around the maximum.

A graph with a flat top has a maximum spread over an interval rather than a point: the kettle boiling at one hundred degrees for several minutes has its maximum temperature over the whole boiling interval. Say so: the maximum is one hundred degrees, reached after about two minutes and held while boiling.

End points can be the extremes. A savings graph that increases every month has its maximum at the end of the period and its minimum at the start — there is no hill or dip, but the questions when was it most and least still have answers. State whether an extreme is a turning point or an end point.

A minimum can be the important one. A phone battery percentage through the day decreases, charges, decreases: the minimum shows how close to flat it got, and when. A learner's distance from home has minima at zero every time they are home.

Describe extremes in full sentences with both coordinates and a meaning: the water level was highest, at the top of the dam, in April after the rains; it was lowest in October before they began. Questions on reading extremes in context follow.

## Subtopic: Discrete and Continuous

Some quantities can only take separate, separated values, and others can take every value in between. The number of learners in a class is one, two, three — never two and a half. The number of tickets sold is a whole number. The number of cars in a car park. These are discrete quantities, and a graph of a discrete quantity is a set of separate dots — one dot for each possible value — not joined by a line, because the values between the dots do not exist. Joining the dots would claim that two and a half tickets could be sold.

Other quantities can take any value: time, length, mass, temperature, depth, distance. Between two seconds and three seconds there is every time in between; between twenty and twenty-one degrees there is every temperature. These are continuous quantities, and a graph of a continuous quantity against a continuous quantity is an unbroken line or curve, because every point on it corresponds to a real pair of values.

The anchor: cost against number of tickets at thirty-five rand each. Zero tickets, zero rand; one, thirty-five; two, seventy; three, one hundred and five. Dots, in a straight line pattern, but dots — you cannot buy one and a half tickets. The dots lie on a line, and that pattern is linear, but the graph is discrete. Contrast depth of water in a filling bath against time: a continuous line, because the depth passes through every value as time passes through every instant.

Deciding: ask whether a value between two allowed values makes sense. Between three tickets and four tickets, three and a half tickets does not; discrete. Between three seconds and four seconds, three and a half seconds does; continuous. Money is a subtle case: prices in rand and cents are strictly discrete at the cent, but a cost that depends on a continuous quantity — a fare against distance — is treated as continuous and drawn as a line.

A discrete graph can still be described as linear, increasing, with a maximum: the dots follow a shape. The ticket cost dots are linear and increasing. A graph of goals scored per match over a season is discrete, with a maximum in the best match. The shape words apply to the pattern of the dots.

When drawing, mark the dots clearly for discrete data and do not join them; draw a smooth line or straight segments for continuous data. The mark scheme checks. Questions on discrete and continuous follow.

## Subtopic: Combining the Features and Common Traps

A full description of a global graph now has four parts: linear or non-linear; increasing, decreasing or constant on each part; the maximum and minimum points, if any; and discrete or continuous. A question may ask for all of them, and a complete answer gives each.

The thrown ball: continuous, non-linear, increasing then decreasing, with a maximum at the top of the flight and minima at the ends on the ground. The day's temperature: continuous, non-linear, increasing from dawn to mid-afternoon then decreasing, maximum mid-afternoon, minimum before dawn. The ticket cost: discrete, linear pattern, increasing, with no turning points — the maximum is at the most tickets considered and the minimum at zero. The bath: continuous, linear in pieces, increasing then constant then decreasing, maximum over the constant interval.

Traps. A discrete graph drawn with a line: a learner joins the ticket dots and loses the mark for claiming fractional tickets. A continuous graph drawn as dots: a learner plots the bath depth at each minute and does not join them, claiming the depth jumped between minutes. Confusing the maximum value with its time: the maximum temperature is thirty-two degrees, reached at three in the afternoon — the thirty-two is the value, the three is when. Calling an end point a turning point: a savings graph has its maximum at the end, but the graph did not turn there.

A graph can be discrete in one variable and continuous in the other only in special treatments; in Grade 8, decide by the independent variable on the horizontal axis and whether its values are counts or measurements, and by the dependent variable likewise. If either is a count, the graph is dots.

A linear pattern of dots is still described as linear; the word refers to the shape the dots make. A curve of dots — the number of bacteria at each hour, doubling — is non-linear and discrete, since bacteria come in whole numbers.

One more feature from the previous lesson to carry forward: steepness. A steeper rise to a maximum means it was reached faster. Combine: the shop's morning peak was reached quickly and was higher than the afternoon one, which built slowly. Questions on combining features follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same features as stories about a roller coaster, a shop's till through the day, and a bag of oranges that cannot be bought by the half — stories in which the top of the hill is the maximum, the bottom of the dip is the minimum, and you can only ever buy a whole orange.

## Subtopic: The Roller Coaster

Think of a roller coaster, and draw its height above the ground against time. The car is pulled slowly up the first big hill: height increasing, a gentle straight rise. At the very top it pauses — the maximum. Then it plunges: height decreasing, a steep fall. At the bottom of the dip it is lowest — a minimum — and it climbs again into a smaller hill: increasing to a smaller maximum, a local maximum, lower than the first. Then down, up through a loop, down again, and finally level into the station. Hills are maxima, dips are minima, and the tallest hill is the global maximum.

The roller coaster graph makes the words physical. The maximum is where you stop going up and start going down; your stomach knows the exact moment. The minimum is where you stop going down and start going up. Between them the graph is increasing or decreasing, and the steepness is how fast the car is gaining or losing height. A flat part at the top of a hill is a maximum held for a moment; the long flat part at the end is constant, in the station, and is also the minimum height.

Now the roller coaster through the day, counting riders. The number of people on the ride at each departure: forty, thirty-six, forty, twenty-two at lunchtime, forty, forty, thirty. Plot it against the departure number. Dots — you cannot have half a rider. A discrete graph with a minimum at the lunchtime departure, maxima at the full runs, and a slight fall at the end as the park empties. The pattern of dots can be read exactly like a line — increasing, decreasing, maximum, minimum — but it is not joined, because departures are counted and so are riders.

Compare the two graphs. The height graph is continuous: the car is at some height at every instant. The rider graph is discrete: there are riders only at departures, and only whole riders. Same ride, two kinds of graph, and the difference is in what is being measured.

Which features does a question want? For the height graph: where were the maxima and minima, which hill was tallest, where was the drop steepest. For the rider graph: which departure was fullest, which emptiest, whether the park filled up or emptied through the day. Questions on the roller coaster follow.

## Subtopic: The Till and the Bag of Oranges

A shop's till records money taken each hour. Nine o'clock, a quiet start. Eleven, busy. One o'clock, quiet — lunch. Three, busy again. Five, closing, quiet. Plot takings against the hour and you get two hills and a dip: maxima at eleven and three, a minimum at one. If the question asks when extra staff are needed, the answer is at the maxima; if it asks when the shop is quietest, the minimum. The graph's turning points are the shop's decisions.

Is the till graph discrete or continuous? Takings in rand and cents are technically discrete, but the hours on the horizontal axis are a choice of sampling: money comes in at every moment, and the total takings so far is a continuous quantity that rises in tiny steps too small to see. A graph of total takings so far against time is drawn as a continuous rising line, with a steeper slope in busy hours. A graph of takings per hour, one bar or dot per hour, is discrete in the hour. Both are used; the question tells you which.

Now the bag of oranges. Oranges cost four rand each. Plot cost against number of oranges: one orange, four rand; two, eight; three, twelve. Dots. You cannot buy two and a half oranges, so there is no point between two and three, and drawing a line through the dots would promise a price for two and a half oranges that the shop does not offer. Discrete, linear pattern, increasing, no turning points. Its minimum is at zero oranges and its maximum at however many you consider buying.

Compare orange juice sold by the litre at twenty rand a litre: you can buy any amount, one comma three litres if you like, and the cost is twenty times the litres for every amount in between. Cost against litres is a continuous straight line through the origin. Oranges by the piece: dots. Juice by the litre: line. The thing being counted or measured decides.

The till and the oranges carry the lesson. Turning points are where the action is — busiest, quietest, most, least. Dots or line is decided by asking whether the in-between values exist. A graph that gets both right can be trusted; a graph that joins the oranges or leaves gaps in the juice is telling a small lie. Questions on the till and the oranges follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method. One: read the axes and decide whether each variable is a count or a measurement; if either is a count, the graph is discrete and drawn as dots. Two: read left to right and split into parts: increasing, decreasing or constant; linear or non-linear. Three: mark each turning point: a maximum where increasing turns to decreasing, a minimum where decreasing turns to increasing; identify the global maximum and minimum, which may be at the ends. Four: for each extreme, give its position on the horizontal axis, its value on the vertical axis if there is a scale, and its meaning. Five: write the description as sentences that name the quantities.

A typical question: a graph shows the height of a bouncing ball against time. Describe it. Continuous; non-linear; a series of hills, each lower than the last; maxima at the tops of the bounces, decreasing in height; minima at zero each time the ball hits the ground.

Another: a graph shows the number of learners absent each day of a week: Monday six, Tuesday two, Wednesday one, Thursday three, Friday eight. Describe it. Discrete; decreasing from Monday to Wednesday, increasing from Wednesday to Friday; minimum on Wednesday with one absent; maximum on Friday with eight.

Another: should a graph of a car's fuel in the tank against distance driven be dots or a line? Fuel and distance are both measurements: a continuous, decreasing line, with vertical jumps when the tank is filled.

Another: should a graph of the cost of sending text messages against the number of messages be dots or a line? Messages are counted: dots, in a linear increasing pattern.

Errors to avoid. Joining the dots of a count. Leaving gaps in a measurement. Confusing the value of a maximum with its time. Missing a local maximum because it is lower than the global one. Calling the end of an always-increasing graph a turning point. Forgetting that a flat top is a maximum held over an interval.

Looking ahead: the next lesson draws global graphs from descriptions, putting all four features into a sketch; the lesson after plots exact points on the Cartesian plane. Grade 9 reads maxima and minima from drawn graphs and interprets gradient. Final questions follow.
