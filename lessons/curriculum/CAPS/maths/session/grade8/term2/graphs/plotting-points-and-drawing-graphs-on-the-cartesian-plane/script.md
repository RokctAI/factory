# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the use of tables and ordered pairs to plot points and draw graphs on the Cartesian plane. The plane is defined by a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis meeting at the origin, dividing it into four quadrants; a point is located by its ordered pair, with the x-coordinate measured horizontally and the y-coordinate vertically, negative coordinates lying to the left and below. A table generated from an equation by substitution is plotted point by point, and the points are joined by a line or curve when the variables are continuous and left as dots when they are discrete. ## Subtopic: The Cartesian Plane and Plotting a Point

The Cartesian plane is two number lines at right angles. The horizontal one is the x-axis, with positive numbers to the right and negative to the left. The vertical one is the y-axis, with positive numbers up and negative down. They cross at the origin, the point bracket zero semicolon zero bracket. Both axes are marked with equal steps, and the steps are labelled.

A point is named by an ordered pair bracket x semicolon y bracket. To plot bracket two semicolon five bracket, start at the origin, move two to the right along the x-axis, then five up parallel to the y-axis, and mark the point. Across first, then up: the x-coordinate is always the first number and the horizontal move, the y-coordinate the second and the vertical move. Bracket five semicolon two bracket is a different point — five across, two up — and confusing the two is the most common plotting error.

Negative coordinates go the other way. Bracket negative three semicolon two bracket is three to the left and two up. Bracket one semicolon negative four bracket is one to the right and four down. Bracket negative two semicolon negative one bracket is two left and one down.

Points on the axes have a zero coordinate. Bracket four semicolon zero bracket is on the x-axis, four to the right. Bracket zero semicolon negative three bracket is on the y-axis, three down. The origin has both zero.

Mark a plotted point with a small clear cross or dot, and label it with its coordinates if asked. Use a sharp pencil and a ruler for reading across to the axes.

Reading a point's coordinates is the reverse: from the point, go straight down or up to the x-axis and read the number, then straight across to the y-axis and read that number; write them in order with a semicolon between. A point three left of the origin and two down reads bracket negative three semicolon negative two bracket.

The scale need not be one unit per square. If the axes are marked in twos or fives, read the labels and plot accordingly; a point at bracket ten semicolon fifteen bracket on axes marked in fives is two squares across and three up. Questions on plotting follow now.

## Subtopic: The Four Quadrants

The axes divide the plane into four regions called quadrants, numbered anticlockwise from the top right with Roman numerals. The first quadrant is top right: both coordinates positive, bracket two semicolon five bracket. The second is top left: x negative, y positive, bracket negative three semicolon two bracket. The third is bottom left: both negative, bracket negative two semicolon negative one bracket. The fourth is bottom right: x positive, y negative, bracket one semicolon negative four bracket.

The signs of the coordinates tell you the quadrant without plotting. Plus plus is first; minus plus is second; minus minus is third; plus minus is fourth. Points on an axis are in no quadrant.

Knowing the quadrant is a quick check on a plotted point: bracket negative three semicolon two bracket must land top left, and if your cross is bottom left you have mixed up the sign of y. It is also the vocabulary for describing where a graph lies: the graph of y equals x squared lies in the first and second quadrants only, because y is never negative; the line y equals two x plus one passes through the first, second and third quadrants.

Reflections across the axes change one sign. The point bracket two semicolon five bracket reflected in the x-axis is bracket two semicolon negative five bracket; reflected in the y-axis it is bracket negative two semicolon five bracket. Transformation geometry in Term 4 builds on this, and the four-quadrant picture is where it starts.

Distances between points on the same horizontal or vertical line are found by subtracting coordinates. From bracket negative three semicolon two bracket to bracket four semicolon two bracket is seven units horizontally — four minus negative three. Draw the axes to cover all four quadrants when the table has negative values. A table for y equals two x plus one with x from negative three to three has y from negative five to seven, so the y-axis must run at least from negative five to seven, and the x-axis from negative three to three. ## Subtopic: Plotting a Table and Drawing a Straight-Line Graph

A relationship is drawn by plotting its table. For y equals two x plus one, make the table for x from negative two to three: y is negative three, negative one, one, three, five, seven. Write the pairs: bracket negative two semicolon negative three bracket, bracket negative one semicolon negative one bracket, bracket zero semicolon one bracket, bracket one semicolon three bracket, bracket two semicolon five bracket, bracket three semicolon seven bracket. Draw axes that fit: x from negative two to three, y from negative three to seven. Plot each point with a cross.

The six crosses lie in a straight line. That is the picture of a linear relationship — the constant step of two in y for each step of one in x is a constant slope — and a ruler through the points is the graph. Draw the line with a ruler through all the points, extending it a little beyond the first and last and adding arrows if the relationship continues, and label it with its equation.

If one cross is off the line, the table has an error at that point: recompute it. A straight-line graph is a check on the table as much as a picture of it.

Where the line crosses the y-axis is where x is zero: the point bracket zero semicolon one bracket, and one is the constant in the equation. Where the line crosses the x-axis is where y is zero: two x plus one equals zero gives x equals negative a half, the point bracket negative a half semicolon zero bracket. Reading these crossing points is the Grade 9 work on intercepts, previewed.

Join the points when the variables are continuous. x and y here are any numbers, so the line is continuous and is drawn through. For the ticket cost — thirty-five times the number of tickets — the points bracket zero semicolon zero bracket, bracket one semicolon thirty-five bracket, bracket two semicolon seventy bracket lie on a straight line but are not joined, because tickets are counted.

A second line: y equals ten minus x, for x from zero to ten. Lines rise when the coefficient is positive and fall when it is negative; a horizontal line is y equals a constant.

## Subtopic: Curves, Reading Off the Graph and Comparing Graphs

Not every table gives a straight line. For y equals x squared with x from negative three to three: y is nine, four, one, zero, one, four, nine. Plot the seven points and they lie on a curve, symmetric about the y-axis, with its lowest point at the origin. Join them with a smooth freehand curve, not with straight segments, because the relationship is continuous and the changing steps in the table — one, three, five — mean the slope is changing everywhere. This curve is called a parabola; Grade 10 studies it. Here it is enough to plot it well and to recognise that a non-constant step in the table means a curve.

The curve has a minimum at the origin, lies entirely in the first and second quadrants, and is non-linear and decreasing for negative x, increasing for positive x — the global-graph language from the earlier lessons applied to an exact graph.

Reading values off a graph. Given x equal to two and a half on y equals two x plus one: go up from two and a half on the x-axis to the line, then across to the y-axis, and read six. Check by substitution: five plus one. Given y equal to four: across from four on the y-axis to the line, then down to the x-axis, and read one and a half. Check: two x plus one equals four gives x equal to one and a half. Reading from a graph is approximate; substitution is exact; use the graph for a quick answer and the equation to confirm.

Comparing graphs on the same axes. Plot y equals two x plus one and y equals x plus three on one set of axes. They cross where their y values are equal: two x plus one equals x plus three, x equals two, y equals five. The point bracket two semicolon five bracket lies on both lines — the pair that satisfied both equations in the tables lesson. The steeper line is the one with the larger coefficient: two x plus one rises two per step, x plus three rises one. Grade 9 uses the crossing point to solve simultaneous equations; Grade 8 reads it off.

A horizontal line y equals three and a vertical line x equals negative two are also graphs: every point on the first has y equal to three; every point on the second has x equal to negative two. Questions on curves and reading follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same plotting as stories about a treasure map with a grid, a city laid out in streets and avenues, and a dot-to-dot picture that only works if you join the dots in the right way — stories in which across comes before up, and the grid is what lets two people agree on exactly where a point is.

## Subtopic: The Treasure Map

A treasure map has a grid printed over it, with letters along the bottom and numbers up the side, and the instructions say the treasure is at D seven. Everyone knows what to do: find column D, go up to row seven, dig. Nobody digs at G four, because the letter comes first and means across, and the number comes second and means up. The grid is what lets the map-maker and the digger agree on one spot without pointing.

The Cartesian plane is the same grid with numbers on both axes and with the grid extended left and down past zero. Bracket two semicolon five bracket is column two, row five: across two, up five. Bracket five semicolon two bracket is column five, row two — a different hole. The first number is always across, the second always up, and the semicolon is the comma in D seven.

The extension past zero is what the treasure map does not have. On the plane, the columns to the left of the origin are negative, and the rows below are negative. Bracket negative three semicolon two bracket is three columns left of the centre and two rows up. If the treasure map had a centre marked and said three west, two north, that would be the second quadrant. Three west, one south would be the third. The four quadrants are the four compass combinations: north-east, north-west, south-west, south-east, going anticlockwise from the top right.

Now a line of treasure. The map says: start at the old oak, and for every step east take two steps north plus one. Step zero east: one north. One east: three north. Two east: five north. Three east: seven north. The treasure chests lie in a straight line — y equals two x plus one with the oak as the origin — and a ruler through the first two chests points at every chest after. That is why a straight-line graph can be drawn from two points and checked with a third: the rule is steady, so the line is straight.

And a warning from the map. If the instructions were for every whole step east, you may not dig between the steps — the chests are at whole-number positions only, like tickets. If the instructions were for every distance east, you may dig anywhere on the line. Dots or line, decided by what the map allows. Questions on the treasure map follow.

## Subtopic: Streets, Avenues and Dot-to-Dot

Some cities are laid out as a grid: streets running one way, numbered, and avenues running the other, numbered. A meeting point is given as the corner of fifth street and third avenue, and you walk along to fifth street and up to third avenue. The city grid is a Cartesian plane with the origin at the city centre; the corner of fifth and third is bracket five semicolon three bracket if streets are across and avenues are up. Cities with a central square that streets run out from in both directions have negative streets too — west fifth street is x equal to negative five.

Walking a route on the grid is drawing a graph. Suppose a friend says: I will meet you at the corner that is on a straight line from the centre through fifth and third, but twice as far out. Twice bracket five semicolon three bracket is bracket ten semicolon six bracket, and it is on the same straight line through the origin, because both coordinates were doubled. Points on a line through the origin have coordinates in a fixed ratio — y is always three fifths of x — which is the straight-line graph of y equals three fifths x.

Now dot-to-dot. A child's puzzle gives numbered dots, and joining them in order makes a picture. Joining them out of order makes a mess. Plotting a table and drawing a graph is dot-to-dot with the order given by x: plot the points, then join them in order of increasing x. For y equals x squared, joining bracket negative three semicolon nine bracket to bracket three semicolon nine bracket directly would draw a straight line across the top and miss the curve; joining in order of x — negative three, negative two, negative one, zero, one, two, three — traces the parabola.

The dot-to-dot also teaches when not to join. Some puzzles have several separate pictures on one page, and a line between them spoils both. Ticket costs are separate dots; joining them draws a line through positions where no ticket exists.

Finally, dot-to-dot with a ruler and dot-to-dot freehand. For a straight-line table, the ruler is right and shows every intermediate value. For a curve, the ruler is wrong between dots — it would make the parabola a chain of straight pieces — and a smooth freehand curve is the honest picture of a quantity that changes smoothly. Count the steps in the table: constant means ruler, changing means freehand. Questions on streets and dot-to-dot follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for drawing a graph from a table or equation. One: make or complete the table by substitution, including negative and zero values if asked. Two: write the ordered pairs, x first. Three: choose axes that fit the smallest and largest x and y, with a sensible equal scale, and label them. Four: plot each pair with a cross — across then up, minding signs. Five: look at the pattern: constant steps mean a straight line, changing steps mean a curve, a count means dots. Six: join with a ruler, a smooth freehand curve, or not at all; extend a line slightly and label it with its equation. Seven: read any required values off the graph and confirm by substitution.

A typical question: draw the graph of y equals three x minus two for x from negative one to three. Table: negative five, negative two, one, four, seven. Axes: x from negative one to three, y from negative five to seven. Plot, ruler, label. It crosses the y-axis at bracket zero semicolon negative two bracket.

Another: on the same axes draw y equals four minus x and find where the two lines cross. Table: five, four, three, two, one. The lines cross at bracket one comma five semicolon two comma five bracket — check: three times one comma five minus two is two comma five, and four minus one comma five is two comma five.

Another: plot the cost of tickets at thirty-five rand each for zero to four tickets. Dots at zero, thirty-five, seventy, one hundred and five, one hundred and forty, not joined, on a y-axis scaled in thirty-fives or fives.

Another: the point bracket a semicolon six bracket lies on y equals two x plus one. Find a. Six equals two a plus one; a equals two and a half.

Errors to avoid. Swapping x and y. Plotting negative coordinates on the wrong side. Unequal scales that distort the line. Joining dots for a count. Using a ruler on a curve. Axes too short for the table. Forgetting to label axes and the line.
