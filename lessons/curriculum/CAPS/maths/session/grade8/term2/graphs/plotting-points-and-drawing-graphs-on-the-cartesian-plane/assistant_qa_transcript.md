### subtopic_1 — The Cartesian Plane and Plotting a Point
**Assistant:** Why is across before up the rule, rather than the other way round?

**Tutor:** It is a convention, agreed so that everyone reads the same pair as the same point, and it follows the alphabetical order of the letters: x is the horizontal axis and comes first, y is vertical and comes second. The convention has been fixed since Descartes, and every map, graph, spreadsheet and computer screen uses it, so learning it once pays off everywhere. The treasure-map habit — letter first for the column, number second for the row — is the same rule in another costume. The reason it matters is that bracket two semicolon five bracket and bracket five semicolon two bracket are different points, and a graph plotted with the coordinates swapped is reflected in the diagonal line y equals x and no longer represents the table. In South African notation the two coordinates are separated by a semicolon because the comma is the decimal separator, so bracket one comma five semicolon four bracket reads unambiguously as x equal to one and a half and y equal to four. Say across, then up, under your breath for every point until it is automatic.

### subtopic_3 — Plotting a Table and Drawing a Straight-Line Graph
**Assistant:** How many points do I need to plot to draw a straight-line graph?

**Tutor:** Two points fix a straight line, so two are enough in principle, but you should plot at least three and preferably the whole table the question gives. The third point is a check: if the rule is linear, all three lie on one line, and if one does not, there is an arithmetic error in the table that you would not otherwise catch. Plotting the full table also shows the examiner the method and gives a more accurate line, because small plotting errors average out when a ruler is laid through several crosses. Choose points that are far apart rather than adjacent, since a ruler through two close points is more easily tilted. Include the point where x is zero, which gives the crossing with the y-axis and is the constant of the equation, and include any negative x values the question asks for so that the line extends into the correct quadrants. Draw the line through all the points with a ruler, extend it slightly past the end ones, and label it with its equation.

### subtopic_4 — Curves, Reading Off the Graph and Comparing Graphs
**Assistant:** When I read a value off a graph, how accurate is it, and is it accepted as an answer?

**Tutor:** A reading from a well-drawn graph is accurate to about half a small square on the grid, which is usually good enough for one decimal place at the scale used, and it is accepted when the question says use your graph or read from the graph. If the question says calculate or determine, it wants the exact value from the equation, and a graph reading alone will lose marks. The best practice is to do both: read the value off the graph to see roughly what to expect, then substitute into the equation to get it exactly, and if the two disagree by more than a small amount, look for a plotting error. Reading y from x means going up from the x-axis to the line and across to the y-axis; reading x from y goes the other way. Mark the lines you draw to read off, lightly, so the examiner can see the method. For a crossing point of two lines, read the coordinates from the graph and then verify by substituting them into both equations.

### subtopic_6 — Streets, Avenues and Dot-to-Dot
**Assistant:** If my table steps are not constant, how do I know whether to join the points with a curve or leave dots?

**Tutor:** The two questions are separate. Whether to join at all depends on whether the variables are continuous — measurements that take every value — or discrete — counts that take only whole values. Whether to join with a ruler or a smooth curve depends on whether the steps are constant or changing. For y equals x squared with x any number, the variable is continuous, so the points are joined, and the steps one, three, five are changing, so the join is a smooth freehand curve in order of increasing x. For the cost of tickets, the variable is a count, so the points are not joined at all, even though the steps are constant and the dots happen to line up. For the number of bacteria at each hour, the count is discrete and the steps change, so the dots are left unjoined and they follow a curve. Decide join-or-not from the meaning of the variables, then decide ruler-or-freehand from the steps, and never let a ruler turn a curve into a chain of straight pieces.
