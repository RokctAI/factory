# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the analysis and interpretation of global graphs, which show the general shape of a relationship between two quantities without precise scales. A graph is linear when it is a straight line and non-linear when it curves; it is increasing where the dependent variable rises as the independent variable increases, decreasing where it falls, and constant where it stays level. Graphs are read from left to right, with the independent variable on the horizontal axis and the dependent variable on the vertical axis. The anchor graphs are a car at steady speed, whose distance–time graph is a rising straight line; a bath that fills, is used and drains, whose depth–time graph rises, is constant and then falls; and a ball thrown upwards, whose height–time graph is a curve that rises and then falls.

## Subtopic: Reading a Global Graph — Axes, Direction and Shape

A global graph shows the shape of a relationship without exact numbers. It has two axes: the horizontal axis carries the independent variable — usually time — and the vertical axis carries the dependent variable — distance, temperature, depth, cost. The axes are labelled with the quantities, often with no scale at all. What matters is the shape of the line.

Read a global graph from left to right, the way you read a sentence, because time moves left to right. At each point ask: as I move right, is the line going up, going down or staying level? Going up means the dependent variable is increasing — more distance, higher temperature. Going down means decreasing. Level means constant — not changing. A graph can do different things on different parts: rise, then stay level, then fall.

The second question is about the shape of the line itself: is it straight or curved? A straight line is called linear. A curve is non-linear. A straight rising line means the quantity increases at a steady rate — the same amount every unit of time. A curve means the rate is changing: getting steeper means speeding up, flattening means slowing down.

The anchor: a car on a highway at steady speed. Distance from the start against time. The car covers the same distance every minute, so the graph is a straight line rising from the origin — linear and increasing. If the car speeds up, the line curves upwards, steeper and steeper — non-linear and increasing. If the car stops, the distance stays the same, and the graph is a horizontal line — constant.

Note what a horizontal line does not mean: it does not mean the car went back; it means the car did not move. Going back would be the line coming down, because the distance from the start is decreasing.

Describing a global graph is giving these answers for each part: linear or non-linear; increasing, decreasing or constant. Three straight pieces — rising, level, falling — is a complete description of a bath that fills, is used, and drains, if the taps run steadily. Questions on reading global graphs follow now.

## Subtopic: Linear and Non-Linear

A linear graph is a straight line: the dependent variable changes by the same amount for each unit of the independent variable. The hall cost against guests from the previous topic — forty rand more for every guest — is linear, and so is every relationship of the form rate times input plus once-off. The taxi fare against kilometres is linear. A tank filling from a tap at a steady flow gives a linear depth–time graph. The table of a linear relationship has a constant step in the outputs, which is the same fact seen in numbers.

A non-linear graph curves: the rate of change is itself changing. The area of a square against its side is non-linear — the area grows faster and faster as the side grows, because area is side squared, and the graph curves upwards. A ball thrown into the air has a height–time graph that curves up, flattens at the top and curves down: it slows as it rises and speeds up as it falls. A cup of hot tea cooling gives a temperature–time graph that falls steeply at first and then flattens as the tea approaches room temperature — non-linear and decreasing. The number of bacteria doubling every hour gives a graph that curves upwards ever more steeply.

The distinction is visible in the table too. Inputs one to four with outputs three, five, seven, nine have constant steps of two: linear. Outputs one, four, nine, sixteen have steps three, five, seven: non-linear.

A straight line can rise, fall or be horizontal; all three are linear. A horizontal line is linear and constant — a steady rate of zero. A falling straight line is linear and decreasing — a tank draining at a steady rate, or a prepaid balance falling by the same amount every day.

Do not confuse steep with non-linear. A very steep straight line is still linear — a fast car at a steady speed. Non-linear is about bending, not slope. And do not confuse non-linear with decreasing: a curve can increase, decrease or do both, and a straight line can too.

When describing a graph, give both words: the car graph is linear and increasing; the cooling tea is non-linear and decreasing; the filling bath is linear and increasing, then constant, then linear and decreasing. Questions on linear and non-linear follow.

## Subtopic: Increasing, Decreasing and Constant

Increasing means the dependent variable goes up as the independent variable goes up: the graph rises from left to right. Decreasing means it goes down: the graph falls. Constant means it stays the same: the graph is horizontal. A graph can be increasing on one part and decreasing on another, and the description must say where each happens.

The bath. Depth against time. The tap runs: depth increases — a rising straight line if the tap is steady. Someone gets in and lies there: depth stays the same — horizontal. The plug is pulled: depth decreases — a falling line. Three parts: increasing, constant, decreasing. If the person gets in quickly, there is a sudden short rise at that moment, a near-vertical jump; the overall shape is still rise, level, fall.

The thrown ball. Height against time. Increasing while the ball rises, then decreasing as it falls, with the turning point at the top. The graph is a curve, so each part is non-linear — the increase slows to zero at the top and the decrease speeds up — but increasing then decreasing is the right global description.

A prepaid electricity balance. Units against days. Decreasing steadily while power is used — a falling straight line. A sudden vertical jump when units are bought. Then decreasing again. The jumps are not increasing over time; they are instantaneous. The decreasing parts are linear if usage is steady.

Temperature through a day. Increasing from dawn to mid-afternoon, decreasing from mid-afternoon to the next dawn, with a maximum in the afternoon and a minimum before dawn. Non-linear — the curve is smooth.

A common error is to read a decreasing graph as the quantity being negative. A falling distance–time graph means the distance is getting smaller — the car is returning — not that the distance is below zero. Another is to say a horizontal line means nothing is happening; something may be happening, but the quantity on the vertical axis is not changing. A person lying in the bath is doing something; the depth is constant.

Always say what is increasing: the depth is increasing, the distance from home is decreasing. The vertical-axis quantity is the subject of the sentence. Questions on increasing and decreasing follow.

## Subtopic: Matching Situations to Graphs and Telling the Story

Two tasks appear in examinations: given a situation, choose or sketch the graph; given a graph, tell the story. Both depend on deciding, part by part, linear or non-linear and increasing, decreasing or constant.

Situation to graph. A learner walks to school at a steady pace, stops at a shop for a few minutes, then runs the rest of the way. Distance from home against time: a rising straight line, a horizontal line, then a steeper rising straight line. Steeper because running covers more distance per minute. Three linear parts. A kettle switched on: temperature rises, non-linearly — fast at first, then slower as it nears boiling — then stays constant at boiling while it bubbles, then falls when switched off, non-linearly. A savings account with monthly deposits and no interest: increasing in steps, each step the same height — linear overall.

Graph to story. A distance–time graph rises steeply, then is horizontal for a long time, then falls gently to zero. Story: someone travelled quickly away from home, stayed somewhere for a long time, then came home slowly. The gentle fall means a slower return — the slope tells speed, and a shallower line is slower. A temperature–time graph falls, is constant at a low value, then rises: something was put in a fridge, kept there, then taken out.

Use the shape words precisely. Steep means fast change; shallow means slow change; horizontal means no change; curving upwards means speeding up; curving towards horizontal means slowing down. A graph that rises and curves to level off describes something approaching a limit — a car reaching top speed, tea reaching room temperature.

Avoid reading the graph as a picture of the path. A distance–time graph that goes up and then down does not show a hill; it shows going away and coming back. A height–time graph of a ball shows height, not the arc the ball traced through the air — though for a ball thrown straight up the two look similar, which is a trap.

State the axes in every story: as time passes, the distance from home increases steadily, then stays the same, then decreases. Questions on matching and stories follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same graphs as stories about a school day told as a graph of how far you are from home, a kettle on the boil, and a cyclist's trip with a puncture — stories in which the line on the page is a diary, and the shape of the line is what happened.

## Subtopic: A School Day as a Graph

Draw your own school day as a graph of distance from home against time, without any numbers. You wake up at home: distance zero, a point on the horizontal axis. You walk to the taxi rank at a steady pace: the line rises, straight, because every minute you are the same bit further away. You wait at the rank: the line is horizontal — time passes, you are not moving. The taxi takes you to school, fast: the line rises again, steeper than the walking part, because a taxi covers more ground per minute than your legs. At school all day: a long horizontal line, far from home. After school you walk to a friend's house in the direction of home but not all the way: the line falls, because you are getting closer to home, and stops at a level above zero. You stay there: horizontal. Then your parent fetches you by car: the line falls steeply to zero. Home.

Read it back. Rising means moving away. Horizontal means not moving — whatever else you are doing. Falling means coming closer. Steeper means faster. Every part is a straight line because each movement was at a steady pace, so the whole graph is linear in pieces. Nothing in the graph is a picture of the roads you took; it is a record of one number, your distance from home, as time passed.

Now change one thing. Suppose the taxi was stuck in traffic and crawled, then sped up when the road cleared. The taxi part of the graph is no longer straight: it rises slowly and then more steeply — a curve, or two straight pieces of different slope. Non-linear, or piecewise. The shape records the speed.

And a trap. Your friend's house is on a hill. Does the graph go up for the hill? No. The vertical axis is distance from home, not height above the ground. The graph goes down as you approach the friend's house, because the distance from home is decreasing, hill or no hill.

Everyone's school-day graph has the same grammar: up for away, level for stop, down for home, steep for fast. The details differ, the reading does not. Questions on the school day follow.

## Subtopic: The Kettle and the Puncture

Put a kettle on and watch the temperature. At first it rises quickly — the element is hot and the water is cold, so heat pours in. As the water warms, it rises more slowly, and the graph bends: still going up, but flattening. At one hundred degrees it stops rising altogether and stays there while it boils — a horizontal line, constant, as long as the kettle is on. Switch it off: the temperature falls, quickly at first because the water is much hotter than the room, then more and more slowly, the curve flattening towards room temperature and never quite reaching it. Rising curve, flat top, falling curve. Non-linear increasing, constant, non-linear decreasing.

The kettle's graph is curved because the rate of heating and cooling depends on the temperature difference, and that difference changes. Compare a tank filling from a tap: the tap does not care how full the tank is, so the depth rises by the same amount every minute, and the graph is a straight line. Steady cause, straight line. Cause that changes with the quantity, curve.

Now a cyclist. Distance against time. She rides at a steady pace: straight rising line. A puncture: she stops to fix it — horizontal line for ten minutes, distance unchanged. She sets off again but slower, nursing the wheel: rising straight line, shallower than before. Then she realises she has forgotten something and rides back: falling line. Rise, level, shallower rise, fall. All linear, because each stretch was at a steady pace.

Someone reading the graph without the story could still say: she moved away steadily, stopped for a while, moved away more slowly, then came back. They could not say puncture — the graph records distance, not reasons — but they could say everything about the motion.

Two graphs, two grammars. The kettle is non-linear because the physics changes as it goes; the cyclist is linear in pieces because each piece was steady. Both are read the same way: left to right, up or down or level, straight or bent. Questions on the kettle and the cyclist follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for a global graph. One: read the axis labels and say what depends on what. Two: read left to right and split the graph into parts where its behaviour changes. Three: for each part, say increasing, decreasing or constant. Four: for each part, say linear or non-linear, and for linear parts compare steepness. Five: tell the story in a sentence per part, naming the vertical-axis quantity. Six: for a sketch from a situation, do the same in reverse — decide the parts first, then draw them in order with sensible relative steepness.

A typical question: describe the graph of a tank being filled by a steady tap, then left, then drained by a pump that slows as the tank empties. Depth against time: linear increasing, constant, non-linear decreasing — falling steeply at first and flattening.

Another: a graph of a shop's daily takings rises during the morning, falls at lunchtime, rises in the afternoon and falls to zero at closing. Describe. Increasing, decreasing, increasing, decreasing; non-linear if smooth. The maximum points are mid-morning and mid-afternoon.

Another: sketch distance from home against time for a jog at a steady pace out, a rest, and a steady jog back. Rising straight line, horizontal, falling straight line back to zero, with the two sloped lines equally steep if the pace was the same.

Another: which of two graphs shows a car speeding up — a straight rising line or a line curving upwards? The curve.

Errors to avoid. Reading the graph as a picture of the path. Taking a horizontal line to mean the quantity is zero. Taking a falling line to mean a negative quantity. Calling a steep straight line non-linear. Forgetting to say which quantity is increasing. Describing the whole graph with one word when it has several parts.
