### subtopic_1 — Maximum and Minimum Points
**Assistant:** Is every highest point a maximum, and is every maximum the highest point?

**Tutor:** Not quite, and the two words that keep them apart are local and global. A maximum, in the turning-point sense, is any point where the graph stops increasing and starts decreasing — the top of any hill, however small. Each such hill-top is a local maximum. The global maximum is the single highest value the graph reaches anywhere, and it is either the tallest of the local maxima or an end point of the graph where the line finishes high without turning. A savings graph that rises every month has a global maximum at its end but no turning-point maximum at all; a shop's takings graph with two peaks has two local maxima, one of which is also the global maximum. The same applies to minima. In an answer, say which you mean: the graph has local maxima at eleven and three, with the global maximum at eleven. Questions that ask when was it highest want the global maximum; questions that ask to identify the turning points want every local one.

### subtopic_3 — Discrete and Continuous
**Assistant:** Money is counted in cents, so why are cost graphs sometimes drawn as lines?

**Tutor:** Because the decision is made by the independent variable as much as the dependent one, and by what the graph is for. When cost depends on a count — tickets, oranges, text messages — the horizontal axis takes only whole numbers, there are no points between them, and the graph is dots. When cost depends on a measurement — litres of juice, kilometres travelled, minutes of calls — the horizontal axis takes every value, and the cost for any amount is a point on the graph, so it is drawn as a line even though the cents are, strictly, discrete steps far too small to see. The practical test is still whether the in-between values exist: one and a half litres has a cost, one and a half tickets does not. Grade 8 asks you to make that judgement sensibly. If a question insists that payments are made only in whole rand or that a fare is charged per whole kilometre, then the cost jumps in steps and a step graph or dots would be right; otherwise treat a cost against a measurement as continuous.

### subtopic_4 — Combining the Features and Common Traps
**Assistant:** When a question asks for the maximum, do I give the time or the value?

**Tutor:** Give both, and say which is which, unless the question specifies one. The maximum is a point on the graph, and a point has two coordinates: its position on the horizontal axis, which for a time graph is when it happened, and its height on the vertical axis, which is the value reached. The maximum temperature was thirty-two degrees, and it occurred at three in the afternoon: the thirty-two is the maximum value, the three o'clock is the time of the maximum. Learners lose marks by giving only one of them, or by giving the time when the value was asked. If the graph has no scale, give the position in words — mid-afternoon — and describe the value relatively — the highest of the day. Add the meaning in the situation, because that is usually the point of the question: the shop should put extra staff on at eleven, when takings peak. Position, value, meaning is the full answer for any extreme.

### subtopic_6 — The Till and the Bag of Oranges
**Assistant:** Is a discrete graph with dots in a straight-line pattern linear?

**Tutor:** Yes, in the sense the word is used for global graphs: linear describes the shape the points make, and the ticket or orange dots lie exactly on a straight line because each extra item adds the same cost. You describe such a graph as discrete, with a linear increasing pattern. What you must not do is draw the line through the dots, because that would assert values — the cost of two and a half oranges — that do not exist. So the two features are separate: discrete or continuous says whether the in-between values exist, linear or non-linear says what shape the existing values follow. A graph can be discrete and non-linear too, such as the number of bacteria at each hour doubling, where the dots lie on a curve. In Grade 9, the equation of the line through the ticket dots will be written as cost equals thirty-five times tickets, and the same care applies: the equation describes the pattern, and the domain — whole numbers only — tells you to plot it as points.
