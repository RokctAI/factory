### subtopic_1 — Choosing and Labelling the Axes
**Assistant:** Why does the graph not always start at the origin?

**Tutor:** Because the origin means both quantities are zero at the start, and many descriptions begin with the dependent quantity already above zero. A kettle starts at room temperature, not at zero degrees, so its temperature–time graph begins partway up the vertical axis. A savings account with an opening balance begins above the axis; a bath that already has some water in it begins above the axis; a car that sets off from a town fifty kilometres from home begins at a distance of fifty. The horizontal axis almost always starts at zero because time or the count starts at the beginning of the story, but the vertical starting value is the first fact in the description and must be read from it. A sketch that drags every graph down to the origin tells a different story — a kettle starting from freezing, an account starting empty — and loses the mark for the start point. Read the opening sentence of the description for the starting value, and place the first point there before drawing anything else.

### subtopic_2 — Breaking the Description into Stages
**Assistant:** How exact do the relative lengths and steepnesses need to be in a sketch?

**Tutor:** Roughly right, in proportion, and consistent with the description. A global graph has no scale, so nobody will measure that a ten-minute stage is exactly twice as long as a five-minute one; but it must be clearly longer, and about double, because an examiner reads relative duration from relative length. The same goes for steepness: a taxi stage must be visibly steeper than a walking stage, and two stages at the same pace must look the same. Where the description gives numbers — five minutes, ten minutes, three minutes — use them to set proportions; where it gives only words — quickly, slowly, for a long time — use your judgement, making sure the ordering is right. The checks that matter are comparative: is the faster stage steeper, is the longer stage longer, does the drain that takes less time than the fill fall more steeply than the fill rose. Precision beyond that belongs to the next lesson, where points are plotted on scaled axes.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing Straight and Curved Segments and Turning Points
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a sharp corner and a rounded top at a maximum?

**Tutor:** A sharp corner says the quantity changed direction instantly: a cyclist who reaches a point and turns round at once has a distance–time graph that rises in a straight line and immediately falls, with a corner at the top. A rounded top says the change was gradual: a ball thrown upwards slows as it rises, is momentarily still at the top, and then gains speed as it falls, so its height–time graph curves smoothly over its maximum. The description tells you which. Words like steadily, at once, immediately, turned round suggest straight segments and corners; words like slowed, gradually, faster and faster, approached suggest curves and rounded turning points. Drawing a rounded top for the cyclist would claim she slowed to a halt before turning, and drawing a corner for the ball would claim it reversed direction instantly at full speed, which is impossible. The shape of the turning point is part of the story, and it is marked.

### subtopic_5 — The Storyboard
**Assistant:** If I run out of room on the page, can I just squash the last stage in?

**Tutor:** You can rescue the sketch that way, but it misrepresents the story, because the width of a stage shows how long it lasted, and a squashed final stage claims that the school day was shorter than the walk to the taxi rank. The better approach is to avoid the problem by storyboarding first: count the stages and estimate their relative lengths before drawing the axes, then draw the horizontal axis long enough for all of them. If a stage is very long compared with the others — a whole day at school after a twenty-minute journey — it is acceptable to indicate a break in the axis or to note that the horizontal line continues, as long as the stage is clearly the longest. If you have already drawn and run out of room, redraw rather than squash; a second attempt that fits earns more than a cramped first one. Examiners read relative length as relative duration, so a sketch whose proportions contradict the description will lose marks even if every direction and shape is right.
