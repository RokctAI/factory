# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the drawing of global graphs from descriptions of problem situations. A sketch is built by identifying the independent and dependent variables and labelling the axes, dividing the description into stages, and representing each stage by a segment whose direction shows increasing, decreasing or constant, whose shape shows linear or non-linear change, and whose steepness shows the relative rate. Turning points are placed where the behaviour changes, end points reflect the start and finish of the situation, and the choice between dots and a line follows from whether the variables are discrete or continuous. The anchor sketches are the bath that fills, is used and drains; the journey to school with a wait and a faster taxi; and the ball thrown upwards.

## Subtopic: Choosing and Labelling the Axes

A sketch begins with the axes, and the axes begin with a question: what depends on what? In the bath, the depth depends on the time, so time goes on the horizontal axis and depth on the vertical. In the journey, distance from home depends on time. In the ticket cost, cost depends on the number of tickets. The independent variable — the one that runs on its own, usually time or a count — goes horizontally; the dependent variable goes vertically.

Label both axes with the quantity and, where it matters, the unit: time in minutes, depth in centimetres. A global graph needs no numbers on the axes, but it needs the labels, because without them the graph means nothing. A rising line labelled depth against time is a bath filling; the same line labelled distance against time is a car driving away.

Mark the origin and decide where the graph starts. A bath that begins empty starts at the origin. A journey from home starts at the origin. A kettle starts at room temperature, which is above zero, so the graph starts partway up the vertical axis. A savings account with an opening balance starts above the origin. The starting point is the first piece of information in the description.

Decide where the graph ends. The bath drains to empty: the graph returns to the horizontal axis. The journey ends at home: back to zero. The ball lands: height zero. A journey that ends at a friend's house ends above the axis.

Decide dots or line now, from the variables. Depth and time are measurements: a line. Cost and tickets: a count, so dots. If the description involves a count, every point you draw will be a dot and nothing will be joined.

Draw the axes with a ruler, arrows at the ends, labels along them, and leave room for the whole story: if there are five stages, do not run out of horizontal room after two. A sketch that is cramped at the right-hand side misrepresents the relative lengths of the stages. Questions on axes follow now.

## Subtopic: Breaking the Description into Stages

A description is a sequence of stages, and each stage becomes one segment of the graph. Read the description and list the stages before drawing. The bath: the tap runs for five minutes; someone sits in the bath for ten minutes; the plug is pulled and it drains in three minutes. Three stages. For each stage, decide three things: direction — increasing, decreasing or constant; shape — straight or curved; and relative length along the horizontal axis.

Stage one, filling: depth increasing; straight, because the tap runs steadily; five minutes long. Stage two, sitting: depth constant; horizontal; ten minutes, twice as long as the filling. Stage three, draining: depth decreasing; straight if the drain is steady; three minutes, shorter than the filling, so steeper, since the same depth is lost in less time.

The journey to school: walk to the rank for ten minutes; wait five minutes; taxi for eight minutes; at school all day. Four stages. Walk: distance increasing, straight, moderate slope. Wait: constant, horizontal, half the walk's length. Taxi: increasing, straight, steeper than the walk because it covers more distance per minute. School: constant, a long horizontal — longer than everything before it put together.

The ball: thrown up, rises, slows, stops at the top, falls faster and faster, lands. One continuous stage with a turning point: height increasing then decreasing, curved throughout, maximum at the top, symmetric if air resistance is ignored.

Write the stages as a list with the three decisions beside each before you draw. The list is your plan, and in an examination it is evidence of method if the sketch is imperfect.

Relative lengths matter. A ten-minute wait should be drawn about twice as long horizontally as a five-minute fill. Relative heights matter too: if the bath drains completely, the falling line returns to the axis; if the person gets out and the bath drops a little, the graph steps down slightly and stays level. Match the sketch to the words. Questions on stages follow.

## Subtopic: Drawing Straight and Curved Segments and Turning Points

Straight segments are drawn with a ruler. A steady tap, a steady walk, a steady drain: ruler. The slope shows the rate, and you choose it relative to the other segments: steeper for faster. Two stages at the same rate — walking out and walking back at the same pace — get the same steepness, one up and one down.

Curved segments are drawn freehand, and the direction of the curve tells the story. A curve that gets steeper as it goes — bending upwards — shows a rate that is increasing: a car accelerating, a population growing by doubling. A curve that gets flatter — bending towards horizontal — shows a rate that is decreasing: a kettle approaching the boil, a cup of tea approaching room temperature, a car reaching its top speed. Decide which way the curve bends from the description: fast at first then slower means flattening; slow at first then faster means steepening.

Turning points are where a rising segment meets a falling one, or the reverse. Draw a smooth rounded top for a maximum on a curve — the ball at the top of its flight — and a sharp corner for a maximum on straight segments — the cyclist who turns round at once. The turning point should be placed at the horizontal position where the description says the change happened: the ball reaches its top halfway through its flight; the cyclist turns at the end of the outward ride.

Constant stages are horizontal, drawn with a ruler, at the height the quantity has reached. If the bath is full, the horizontal is at the top; if the person has got out and the level dropped, the horizontal is lower.

Sudden changes are near-vertical lines: a person jumping into the bath raises the level at once; buying electricity units raises the balance at once. Draw them as short steep jumps, not as gradual rises, because the description says at once.

Join the segments end to end so that the graph is one continuous path for a continuous quantity. The end of one stage is the start of the next; depth does not jump between stages unless the description says so. Questions on drawing segments follow.

## Subtopic: Discrete Sketches and Checking the Sketch

When the independent variable is a count, the sketch is a set of dots. The cost of tickets at thirty-five rand each: a dot at zero tickets and zero rand, a dot at one ticket, a dot at two, and so on, each the same height above the last, in a straight-line pattern, unjoined. The number of absent learners each day: a dot for Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, at heights that follow the description — high on Monday, falling to Wednesday, rising to Friday. Dots can show increasing, decreasing, maxima and minima just as a line can; they simply are not joined.

Draw discrete dots clearly and equally spaced along the horizontal axis, since the counts are equally spaced. Do not draw a line through them, and do not draw a dot at a position between counts.

Checking the sketch. Read it back as a story and compare with the description, stage by stage. Does the graph start where the description starts — empty, at home, at room temperature? Does each stage go the right way — up, level, down? Is each stage the right shape — straight or curved — and bending the right way? Are the stages in the right order and about the right relative lengths? Are faster stages steeper? Are turning points where the behaviour changes, and are maxima and minima where the description puts the most and least? Does the graph end where the description ends? Are the axes labelled, and are dots used for counts?

Common faults to look for. A horizontal drawn at the wrong height after a drop. A curve drawn bending the wrong way. All stages drawn the same length regardless of time. A stage missing. A line through discrete dots. A graph that looks like a picture of the path — hills for hills — rather than the quantity against time.

An examiner reads a sketch for these features, not for artistic quality. A rough but correct sketch with labelled axes earns full marks; a beautiful curve that bends the wrong way does not. Questions on discrete sketches and checking follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same sketching as stories about a storyboard for a short film, a weather forecaster drawing tomorrow's temperature by hand, and a goalkeeper's heart rate through a penalty shoot-out — stories in which the graph is drawn before the numbers exist, from the shape of what will happen.

## Subtopic: The Storyboard

A film director plans a short film with a storyboard: a row of boxes, one per scene, each with a quick sketch and a note — wide shot, two seconds; close-up, five seconds; chase, twenty seconds. The storyboard is not the film, but anyone can read the film's shape from it: what happens, in what order, for how long.

A global graph is a storyboard for a quantity. Each stage of the description is a scene, and the line in that stage is the sketch in the box. Take the bath. Scene one: the tap runs — the line rises. Scene two: someone sits — the line is level. Scene three: the plug is pulled — the line falls. The notes on the storyboard become the shapes: steadily means straight; slowly then quickly means curving upwards; for ten minutes means twice as long as for five.

The director's first job is to list the scenes before drawing any of them. Yours is the same: read the description, list the stages, and only then pick up the ruler. A learner who starts drawing at the first sentence runs out of page by the third and squashes the ending, or forgets the wait at the rank altogether. The storyboard is written in full first.

The director's second job is pacing: a twenty-second chase gets a wider box than a two-second shot. Yours is relative length: a ten-minute stage is drawn twice as long as a five-minute stage. And a chase scene is drawn with motion lines — your steeper slope for a faster stage.

The director's third job is continuity: a character wearing a hat in one scene wears it in the next unless the script says they took it off. Yours is joining the segments: the depth at the end of filling is the depth at the start of sitting; the line does not jump unless the description says someone jumped in.

Finally, the director checks the storyboard against the script. You read your graph back as a story and compare it with the description. If the graph says the bath drained before anyone sat in it, the scenes are in the wrong order. If the graph is still rising when the description says the tap was off, a scene has the wrong direction.

Storyboard first, draw second, check third. Questions on the storyboard follow.

## Subtopic: The Forecaster and the Goalkeeper

A weather forecaster is asked to sketch tomorrow's temperature by hand, before the computer has run. She knows the shape: cold before dawn, warming through the morning, hottest in mid-afternoon, cooling through the evening and night. Her sketch is a smooth curve: starting low at midnight, dipping slightly to a minimum around five in the morning, rising through the day — gently at first, then faster, then flattening — to a maximum around three in the afternoon, then falling through the evening. No numbers, but the shape is right: non-linear, a minimum before dawn, a maximum mid-afternoon, and the maximum later than noon because the ground keeps warming after the sun is highest.

If the forecast is for a cold front at four in the afternoon, she changes one scene: the curve drops sharply at four and stays low. The sketch records the story she expects. When the real temperatures arrive, she will plot them as exact points on a scaled graph — the next lesson — but today's job is the shape.

Now a goalkeeper in a penalty shoot-out, with a monitor on his heart rate. Sketch heart rate against time. Before the shoot-out: moderately high, roughly level. As the first kicker steps up: rising sharply. The kick: a peak. Saved — a brief further spike, then falling as he walks back. The next kicker: rising again to another peak. Five kicks: five hills, each a maximum, with dips between as he recovers, though the dips get shallower as the tension builds and he never fully recovers. After the last kick: falling slowly towards normal, curving flatter as it goes.

The goalkeeper's graph is continuous — a heart rate exists at every instant — and non-linear everywhere, with several local maxima of which the highest, probably at the decisive kick, is the global maximum. The dips are local minima. Drawing it is listing the kicks as scenes and giving each a hill, with the hills getting taller and the valleys shallower as the description says the tension builds.

Both sketches were made from descriptions and expectations, not from data. Both would be marked on features: the forecaster's for the pre-dawn minimum and afternoon maximum; the goalkeeper's for five peaks with shallowing dips and a slow curved recovery. Questions on the forecaster and the goalkeeper follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for sketching from a description. One: identify the dependent and independent variables and draw labelled axes with room for the whole story. Two: decide dots or a line from whether the variables are counts or measurements. Three: list the stages with direction, shape and relative length for each. Four: mark the start point and the end point. Five: draw each stage in order — ruler for straight, freehand for curved, bending the right way, steeper for faster, horizontal for constant, near-vertical for sudden — joining the segments. Six: mark and, if asked, label maxima and minima. Seven: read the sketch back against the description and correct anything that disagrees.

A typical question: sketch a graph of the water level in a tank that is filled quickly by a hose for two minutes, then slowly by a trickle for ten minutes until full, then left full for an hour, then emptied steadily in five minutes. Level against time: a steep straight rise, a shallow straight rise to the top, a long horizontal at the top, a straight fall to the axis steeper than the trickle but shallower than the hose.

Another: sketch the number of people in a cinema from opening to closing for one screening. People against time: dots or a step graph rising as people arrive before the film, level during the film, falling quickly after. People are counted, so the graph is discrete — in practice a step graph — but the global shape is rise, level, fall.

Another: sketch the height of a bungee jumper against time. Starts high, falls fast — curving steeper — reaches a minimum when the cord is fully stretched, bounces up to a lower maximum, down to a higher minimum, bounces shrinking, settles level above the ground.

Errors to avoid. Unlabelled axes. Starting at the origin when the description starts elsewhere. Equal lengths for unequal times. A curve bending the wrong way. Joining dots for a count. Forgetting a stage. Drawing the path instead of the quantity.
