### subtopic_1 — Reading a Global Graph — Axes, Direction and Shape
**Assistant:** If a global graph has no numbers on its axes, what can I actually read from it?

**Tutor:** Everything about the shape of the relationship, which is usually what the question wants. You can read whether the dependent quantity is increasing, decreasing or constant on each part by whether the line rises, falls or runs level from left to right. You can read whether the rate of change is steady or changing by whether the line is straight or curved, and you can compare rates by comparing steepness — a steeper straight line means a faster change, even without numbers. You can read the order of events along the time axis and the relative lengths of the parts. What you cannot read is exact values — how far, how hot, how long — and a good answer never invents them. A global graph is like a story told without dates: you know what happened, in what order, and roughly how fast, but not the precise figures. The next lessons add scales and exact points on the Cartesian plane; this lesson is about reading the shape, which is a skill in its own right and the first thing a scientist or an economist does with any graph.

### subtopic_2 — Linear and Non-Linear
**Assistant:** How can I tell linear from non-linear if the curve is very gentle?

**Tutor:** Lay a ruler along the line, or imagine one: if the line stays on the ruler, it is straight and linear; if it drifts off, however slightly, it is curved and non-linear. In a table, check the steps between consecutive outputs for equally spaced inputs: constant steps mean linear, changing steps mean non-linear, and even a small drift in the steps counts. In a situation, ask whether the cause of the change is steady or depends on the current state. A tap running at a fixed rate adds the same depth every minute whatever the depth, so the graph is straight; a kettle heats faster when the water is cold and slower when it is hot, so the graph bends. A car at a fixed speed covers the same distance every minute, straight; a car accelerating covers more each minute, curved. The physical reasoning is often the most reliable guide when the drawing is small. And remember that steepness is irrelevant to the question: a very steep straight line is linear, and a nearly flat curve is non-linear.

### subtopic_3 — Increasing, Decreasing and Constant
**Assistant:** Does a decreasing graph mean the quantity has become negative?

**Tutor:** No. Decreasing describes the direction of change — the quantity is getting smaller as time passes — and says nothing about whether it is above or below zero. A falling distance-from-home graph means the distance is shrinking, which is a person coming home; the distance stays positive until it reaches zero at the front door. A falling temperature graph means the temperature is dropping, and it may drop from thirty degrees to twenty, both positive. The quantity is negative only when the graph goes below the horizontal axis, which for distance and depth cannot happen, and for temperature can. Similarly a constant graph does not mean the quantity is zero; a horizontal line high up means a large unchanging value — a bath that is full and staying full, a person far from home and staying there. Separate the two ideas in your sentences: say the depth is decreasing, or the depth is constant at a high level, and reserve the word negative for values below zero.

### subtopic_5 — A School Day as a Graph
**Assistant:** Why does the hill not show on a distance-from-home graph?

**Tutor:** Because the vertical axis measures distance from home, not height above the ground, and a graph can only show the quantity its axis names. Walking up a hill towards a friend's house that is nearer home than school makes the distance from home decrease, so the line falls, even though your body is rising. The graph is not a drawing of the route; it is a record of one number as time passes. This is the single most common misreading of distance–time graphs — treating the line as a picture of the path — and it produces wrong stories, such as a hill where the graph actually shows going away and coming back. The cure is to name the axis in every sentence you write: as time passes, the distance from home decreases. If a question wanted height, the vertical axis would say height, and then the hill would show as a rise. Always read the label before the line, and let the label decide what the ups and downs mean.
