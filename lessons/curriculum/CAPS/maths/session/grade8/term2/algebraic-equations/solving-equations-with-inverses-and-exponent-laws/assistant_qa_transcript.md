### subtopic_1 — The Balance Principle and Additive Inverses
**Assistant:** Why is it called the additive inverse rather than just subtracting?

**Tutor:** Because the idea is more general than subtraction and it explains why the step works. The additive inverse of a number is the number you add to it to get zero — the inverse of negative four is four, the inverse of seven is negative seven — and adding it to a term wipes that term out, leaving zero, which disappears from the side. Subtracting seven is the same as adding negative seven, so for a positive constant the two descriptions coincide; for a negative constant, as in three x minus four, the inverse is positive four and the step is an addition, which is where learners who think only in terms of subtracting go wrong and write three x equals twenty minus four. Thinking in inverses also lines up with the multiplicative step, where the inverse of a coefficient is the number that multiplies it to one, and with the Term 1 work on inverses for integers and fractions. The principle in both cases is the same: apply the inverse to both sides, and the unwanted piece becomes the identity — zero for addition, one for multiplication — and vanishes.

### subtopic_2 — Multiplicative Inverses and Two-Step Equations
**Assistant:** Why remove the constant before the coefficient — would the other order be wrong?

**Tutor:** The other order is not wrong, but it is usually harder, and the reason is the order in which the expression was built. In three x minus four, the x was first multiplied by three and then four was subtracted, so the subtraction is the outer layer and comes off first; undoing in reverse order keeps every intermediate line in whole numbers. If you divide first, you must divide the whole of both sides by three, giving x minus four thirds equals twenty thirds, which is correct but means adding fractions to finish. The exception is an equation like three bracket x minus two bracket equals twelve, where the bracket shows that the subtraction was done first and the multiplication last; there the multiplication is the outer layer, and dividing both sides by three first, to get x minus two equals four, is the clean route. Read the structure: whatever was done last to the variable is undone first. When in doubt, removing the constant term first is the safe default for unbracketed two-step equations.

### subtopic_4 — Equations with Powers and Exponents
**Assistant:** When I take the square root of both sides, why do I get two answers, and must I always give both?

**Tutor:** You get two because squaring destroys the sign: seven squared and negative seven squared are both forty-nine, so the equation x squared equals forty-nine is satisfied by both and a complete solution lists both, written as x equals seven or x equals negative seven, or x equals plus or minus seven. Whether you keep both depends on the context. If the equation is pure algebra, both are solutions and leaving one out loses a mark. If the equation came from a situation in which the variable is a length, an age, a count or a price, the negative is impossible and you discard it with a sentence saying why. Cube roots behave differently because cubing keeps the sign — only negative two cubed is negative eight — so x cubed equals negative eight has the single solution negative two. And a square equal to a negative number, such as x squared equals negative nine, has no solution at all in Grade 8, because no real number squared is negative. Checking both candidates in the original equation is the final step either way.

### subtopic_6 — Unwrapping the Parcel and the Safe Combination
**Assistant:** How do I solve an equation when the unknown is in the exponent?

**Tutor:** Write both sides as powers of the same base and then equate the exponents, which the laws of exponents justify: two powers of the same base are equal only when their exponents are equal. Two to the power x equals thirty-two becomes two to the power x equals two to the power five, so x is five — the formal version of counting how many times two was multiplied to reach thirty-two. If the equation has several powers of the base, use the laws to combine them first: two to the power x times two cubed is two to the power x plus three by the multiplication law, so against two to the power seven it gives x plus three equals seven and x equals four; three to the power x over three squared is three to the power x minus two by the division law. The skill is recognising the right side as a power of the base, which is why the squares, cubes and powers of two, three, five and ten from the exponents topic are worth knowing by sight. Grade 9 extends this to negative exponents; in Grade 8 the exponents are whole numbers and listing powers finds them.
