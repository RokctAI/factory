# Part 1 — Expert

This session extends the solving of equations to the formal use of additive and multiplicative inverses and the laws of exponents. An equation remains true when the same operation is applied to both sides; adding the additive inverse of a term removes it from one side, and multiplying by the multiplicative inverse of a coefficient isolates the variable. Equations in which the variable is raised to a power are solved by taking the matching root, with attention to two solutions for even powers, and equations in which the variable is an exponent are solved by writing both sides as powers of the same base. The anchor equations are three x minus four equals twenty, with solution eight; five x minus nine equals two x, with solution three; x squared equals forty-nine, with solutions seven and negative seven; and two to the power x equals thirty-two, with solution five.

## Subtopic: The Balance Principle and Additive Inverses

An equation is a balance: the two sides have equal value. If you do the same thing to both sides, they stay equal. Add the same number to both sides, subtract the same number from both, multiply or divide both by the same non-zero number — the balance holds. This is the principle behind every formal step in solving.

The additive inverse of a number is what you add to get zero: the inverse of negative four is four, of seven is negative seven. To remove a term from one side of an equation, add its additive inverse to both sides. x plus seven equals fifteen: add negative seven to both sides, which is subtracting seven. Left: x plus seven minus seven, x. Right: fifteen minus seven, eight. x equals eight. Check: eight plus seven is fifteen.

x minus five equals twelve: add five to both sides. x equals seventeen. Check. x plus nine equals four: subtract nine from both sides. x equals negative five. Check: negative five plus nine is four.

Write the operation beside the line, or on its own line: three x minus four equals twenty; add four to both sides; three x equals twenty-four. The intermediate equation three x equals twenty-four is the same middle line that inspection produced last week, now arrived at by a rule rather than a question. The rule is what makes the method work when the numbers are too awkward to see through.

The side you remove a term from does not matter in principle, but the aim is to gather the variable terms on one side and the numbers on the other. In most two-step equations the number is removed first, leaving the coefficient times the variable for the next section.

A caution: do the same thing to both sides, and to the whole of each side. In three x minus four equals twenty, adding four to the left adds it to the whole left side, giving three x; it does not add four to the three or to the x separately. Questions on the balance principle follow now.

## Subtopic: Multiplicative Inverses and Two-Step Equations

The multiplicative inverse of a number is what you multiply it by to get one: the inverse of three is a third, of two fifths is five halves, of negative four is negative a quarter. To remove a coefficient, multiply both sides by its multiplicative inverse — which, for a whole-number coefficient, is dividing both sides by it. Three x equals twenty-four: divide both sides by three, or multiply by a third. x equals eight.

Now the full two-step anchor. Three x minus four equals twenty. Add four to both sides: three x equals twenty-four. Divide both sides by three: x equals eight. Check: three times eight minus four is twenty. Two lines of working, each with its operation named, then the check.

Two x plus five equals seventeen: subtract five, two x equals twelve; divide by two, x equals six. Five minus two x equals eleven: subtract five, negative two x equals six; divide by negative two, x equals negative three. Dividing by a negative is allowed and gives a negative solution; check: five minus two times negative three is five plus six, eleven.

Order: undo addition and subtraction before multiplication and division — remove the constant term first, then the coefficient — because the coefficient is attached to the variable and the constant is attached to the whole side. Doing it the other way round works but produces fractions: dividing three x minus four equals twenty by three gives x minus four thirds equals twenty thirds, which is correct but awkward.

Fractional coefficients: x over three plus one equals five. Subtract one: x over three equals four. Multiply both sides by three: x equals twelve. Two fifths x equals eight: multiply both sides by five halves, x equals twenty. Or multiply both sides by five first, two x equals forty, then divide by two.

Decimals: nought comma five x plus two equals six. Subtract two, nought comma five x equals four; divide by nought comma five, x equals eight. Or multiply both sides by two.

The taxi: eight k plus twelve equals sixty. Subtract twelve: eight k equals forty-eight. Divide by eight: k equals six. Six kilometres. Questions on multiplicative inverses follow.

## Subtopic: The Letter on Both Sides and Brackets

When the variable appears on both sides, gather the variable terms on one side by adding the additive inverse of one of them to both sides. Five x minus nine equals two x. Subtract two x from both sides: three x minus nine equals zero. Add nine: three x equals nine. Divide by three: x equals three. Check: fifteen minus nine is six, and two times three is six.

Four x plus one equals x plus ten. Subtract x from both sides: three x plus one equals ten. Subtract one: three x equals nine. x equals three. Check: thirteen and thirteen.

Choose to move the smaller variable term so that the remaining coefficient is positive. In two x plus seven equals five x minus two, subtract two x: seven equals three x minus two. Add two: nine equals three x. Divide: three equals x. An equation can be read in either direction; x equals three.

Brackets are expanded first, with the distributive law from the algebra topic. Three bracket x minus two bracket equals twelve: expand, three x minus six equals twelve; add six, three x equals eighteen; x equals six. Alternatively divide both sides by three first: x minus two equals four; x equals six — quicker here because twelve divides by three. Two bracket x plus three bracket equals x plus eleven: two x plus six equals x plus eleven; subtract x, x plus six equals eleven; x equals five.

Like terms on one side are collected before anything crosses over. Four x minus x plus seven equals sixteen: three x plus seven equals sixteen; three x equals nine; x equals three.

A quick identity check: two bracket x plus three bracket equals two x plus six collects to zero equals zero — true for all x, every number is a solution. x plus three equals x plus five collects to three equals five — false for all x, no solution. The formal method discovers these cases by itself.

Write each operation. Markers award method marks line by line, and a line that says subtract two x from both sides earns credit even if a later arithmetic slip spoils the answer. Questions on letters on both sides follow.

## Subtopic: Equations with Powers and Exponents

When the variable is squared, undo the square with a square root — and remember that a positive number has two square roots. x squared equals forty-nine: x equals seven or x equals negative seven, because both square to forty-nine. Write both. If the context forbids a negative — a length, a count — say so and discard it; otherwise both are solutions.

x squared minus five equals twenty: add five, x squared equals twenty-five; x equals five or negative five. Two x squared equals fifty: divide by two, x squared equals twenty-five; x is plus or minus five. x squared equals negative nine: no solution, since no number squares to a negative.

When the variable is cubed, take the cube root, and there is one solution with the sign of the right side. x cubed equals twenty-seven: x equals three. x cubed equals negative eight: x equals negative two. x cubed plus one equals sixty-five: x cubed equals sixty-four; x equals four.

Roots of the variable are undone by powers. The square root of x equals six: square both sides, x equals thirty-six. The cube root of x equals negative two: cube both sides, x equals negative eight. Check: the cube root of negative eight is negative two.

When the variable is the exponent, write both sides as powers of the same base and equate the exponents — this is the laws of exponents at work. Two to the power x equals thirty-two: thirty-two is two to the power five, so x equals five. Three to the power x equals eighty-one: eighty-one is three to the power four; x equals four. Five to the power x equals one: one is five to the power zero; x equals zero. Ten to the power x equals one million: six.

Combined with the laws: two to the power x times two cubed equals two to the power seven. The left is two to the power x plus three, so x plus three equals seven, x equals four. Three to the power x over three squared equals three to the power five: x minus two equals five, x equals seven. The law turns the equation about exponents into an ordinary equation in x.

Always check by substituting the solution into the original equation, and for a squared variable check both solutions. Questions on powers and exponents follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same solving as stories about a balance scale with bags of marbles, a wrapped parcel being unwrapped layer by layer, and a safe whose combination is a power — stories in which whatever you do to one pan you do to the other, and the letter is the bag you are not allowed to open.

## Subtopic: The Balance Scale

Picture an old-fashioned balance scale with two pans. On the left pan are three identical sealed bags, each holding the same unknown number of marbles, and a note that says take four marbles away. On the right pan are twenty loose marbles. The scale balances. That is three x minus four equals twenty, with x the marbles in a bag.

You want to know what is in a bag without opening one. Rule of the scale: whatever you do to one pan you must do to the other, or the balance tips and you learn nothing. First, deal with the note. Taking four away from the left is the same as owing four; cancel the debt by adding four marbles to the left pan — and, to keep the balance, four to the right. Now the left has three bags, the right has twenty-four marbles. Three x equals twenty-four.

Next, three bags balance twenty-four marbles, so one bag balances a third of twenty-four. Divide both pans into three equal shares and keep one share each: one bag against eight marbles. x equals eight. You never opened a bag, and you know what is in it.

Check by opening one: eight marbles. Three bags of eight, twenty-four, take four away, twenty — balances against twenty. Correct.

Now a harder scale: five bags with nine marbles owed on the left, two bags on the right. Five x minus nine equals two x. Remove two bags from each pan — bags from both sides, the same thing to both: three bags with nine owed against nothing. Pay the nine on both sides: three bags against nine marbles. One bag against three. x equals three.

Every operation in formal solving is a move on this scale. Adding the additive inverse is paying a debt on both pans. Multiplying by the multiplicative inverse is dividing both pans into equal shares. Moving a letter term is lifting bags off both pans. The scale is why it works: equal stays equal when both sides change together.

The one thing the scale will not let you do is change one pan alone. A learner who writes three x minus four equals twenty and then three x equals twenty minus four has tipped the scale: the four was subtracted on the left, and to keep balance it must be added, not subtracted, on the right. Watch the sign as the marbles cross. Questions on the scale follow.

## Subtopic: Unwrapping the Parcel and the Safe Combination

Think of an expression as a parcel wrapped around the letter in layers. Three x minus four: the first layer put on x was multiply by three, and the second layer was subtract four. To get at x, unwrap in reverse — the last layer first. Undo the subtract four by adding four. Undo the multiply by three by dividing by three. That is why the constant comes off before the coefficient: it was the outer layer of wrapping. And because the parcel sits on a balance, every layer you remove on the left you remove on the right too.

Bracket x minus two bracket times three is wrapped differently: first subtract two, then multiply by three. Unwrap: divide by three first, then add two. Three bracket x minus two bracket equals twelve; divide both sides by three, x minus two equals four; add two, x equals six. Reading the wrapping order tells you the unwrapping order, and brackets show that the inside was wrapped first.

x squared is a parcel wrapped once, with squaring. Unwrap with a square root — but squaring hides the sign of what went in, so unwrapping gives back two possible contents: x squared equals forty-nine means x was seven or negative seven. Cubing does not hide the sign, so x cubed equals negative eight unwraps to one answer, negative two.

Now the safe. A safe's combination is a single number, and the hint on the door reads: two to the power of the combination is thirty-two. The letter is in the exponent — it is the number of times two was multiplied. Count: two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two — five multiplications. The combination is five. Writing thirty-two as two to the power five and matching exponents is the formal version of counting.

A second safe: three to the power of the combination, times three cubed, is three to the power seven. The laws of exponents say the left is three to the power of the combination plus three. So the combination plus three is seven; the combination is four. The exponent law converted a question about powers into an ordinary one-step equation.

Parcel, scale, safe: unwrap in reverse order, keep both pans equal, and when the letter is upstairs in the exponent, write both sides as powers of one base and bring the letter down. Questions on parcels and safes follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for solving formally. One: expand brackets and collect like terms on each side. Two: gather variable terms on one side by adding the additive inverse of a variable term to both sides. Three: remove the constant term from the variable side by adding its additive inverse to both sides. Four: remove the coefficient by multiplying both sides by its multiplicative inverse. Five: if the variable is raised to a power, take the matching root, giving two answers for even powers; if the variable is an exponent, write both sides as powers of the same base and equate exponents. Six: check in the original equation, and return the answer to the situation with units.

A typical question: solve seven x plus nine equals one hundred and twenty-one. Subtract nine: seven x equals one hundred and twelve. Divide by seven: x equals sixteen. The weight from the fete, now in two lines.

Another: solve four bracket x plus one bracket equals two x plus fourteen. Expand: four x plus four equals two x plus fourteen. Subtract two x: two x plus four equals fourteen. Subtract four: two x equals ten. x equals five.

Another: solve x over four minus three equals two. Add three: x over four equals five. Multiply by four: x equals twenty.

Another: solve two x squared minus eight equals ten. Add eight: two x squared equals eighteen. Divide by two: x squared equals nine. x equals three or negative three.

Another: solve five to the power x equals one hundred and twenty-five. One hundred and twenty-five is five cubed; x equals three.

Another: the hall — forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred. Subtract five hundred: forty g equals one thousand two hundred. Divide: g equals thirty. Thirty guests.

Errors to avoid. Changing one side only. Subtracting where the inverse requires adding. Removing the coefficient before the constant and drowning in fractions. Forgetting the negative square root. Dividing by a negative and losing the sign. Not writing the operations. Not checking.
