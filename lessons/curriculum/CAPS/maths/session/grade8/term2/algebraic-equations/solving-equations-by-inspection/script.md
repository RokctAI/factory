# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the solving of equations by inspection. An equation is read as a question about an unknown number — what number, added to seven, gives fifteen — and the answer is found from number facts, working backwards one operation at a time, or by systematic trial and improvement. Every solution is verified by substituting it into the original equation. The anchor equations are x plus seven equals fifteen, with solution eight; three x minus four equals twenty, with solution eight; and twenty-four over x equals six, with solution four.

## Subtopic: Reading an Equation as a Question

Solving by inspection means reading the equation as a question and answering it from what you know about numbers. x plus seven equals fifteen asks: what number plus seven gives fifteen? Eight. x minus five equals twelve asks: what number minus five gives twelve? Seventeen. Four x equals thirty-two asks: four times what gives thirty-two? Eight. x over two equals nine asks: what number halved gives nine? Eighteen.

Each of these is a one-step equation, and the question it asks is a basic number fact run backwards. Addition facts, subtraction facts, times tables and division facts are the whole toolkit. Twelve x equals ninety-six: twelve times what is ninety-six? Eight, from the twelve times table. x over seven equals six: six times seven is forty-two.

The solution is the value that makes the equation true, and a solution is only a solution once it has been checked. Substitute: eight plus seven is fifteen, true. Seventeen minus five is twelve, true. The check is part of the method, not an optional extra — write it down.

Watch the direction of subtraction and division. x minus five equals twelve has solution seventeen, found by adding five. Five minus x equals twelve asks: five minus what gives twelve? The answer is negative seven, because five minus negative seven is five plus seven, twelve. Integers from Term 1 are needed, and the question must be read exactly as written. Twenty-four over x equals six asks: twenty-four divided by what gives six? Four. x over twenty-four equals six asks: what number divided by twenty-four gives six? One hundred and forty-four. Different questions, different answers.

Negative solutions appear naturally. x plus nine equals four: what number plus nine gives four? Negative five. Three x equals negative twenty-one: three times what is negative twenty-one? Negative seven. An equation does not promise a positive answer, and a learner who refuses to consider negatives will declare some equations impossible that are not. Questions on reading equations as questions follow now.

## Subtopic: Two-Step Equations — Working Backwards

Three x minus four equals twenty. Two operations sit on the x: multiply by three, then subtract four. Inspect in two stages. First ask: what number, minus four, gives twenty? Twenty-four. So three x is twenty-four. Then ask: three times what is twenty-four? Eight. x is eight. Check: three times eight is twenty-four, minus four is twenty. True.

The order of the inspection is the reverse of the order of operations. In three x minus four, the multiplication was done first and the subtraction second; to undo, deal with the subtraction first and the multiplication second. Peel the outside layer, then the inside. This is the idea behind inverse operations in a later lesson, but at this stage you simply ask two questions in the right order.

Two x plus five equals seventeen: what plus five is seventeen? Twelve. Two times what is twelve? Six. Check: twelve plus five, seventeen.

x over three plus one equals five: what plus one is five? Four. What divided by three is four? Twelve. Check: four plus one, five.

Five minus two x equals eleven: five minus what is eleven? Negative six. So two x is negative six, and x is negative three. Check: two times negative three is negative six; five minus negative six is eleven. True.

Eight k plus twelve equals sixty, the taxi from the previous lesson: what plus twelve is sixty? Forty-eight. Eight times what is forty-eight? Six. The trip was six kilometres. Check: forty-eight plus twelve, sixty. And answer the question that was asked: six kilometres, with the unit.

The intermediate value — twenty-four, twelve, four, negative six, forty-eight — is worth writing down as a short line: three x equals twenty-four. It records the first inspection and makes the second one easy, and it is what the marker looks for as method. Questions on two-step inspection follow.

## Subtopic: Trial and Improvement

When the answer is not obvious, try a value, see whether the result is too big or too small, and adjust. Seven x plus nine equals one hundred and twenty-one. Try x equals ten: seventy plus nine, seventy-nine, too small. Try twenty: one hundred and forty plus nine, one hundred and forty-nine, too big. The answer is between ten and twenty. Try fifteen: one hundred and five plus nine, one hundred and fourteen, too small but close. Try sixteen: one hundred and twelve plus nine, one hundred and twenty-one. Exactly. x is sixteen.

Trial and improvement is inspection with a notebook. Keep a small table: the value tried, the result, and whether it is too big or too small. Each trial narrows the range. Choose the next trial inside the range, and move towards the side that was closer.

It works on equations that inspection alone struggles with. x squared plus x equals fifty-six. Try seven: forty-nine plus seven, fifty-six. First try, by luck. x squared minus three x equals forty. Try five: twenty-five minus fifteen, ten, too small. Try ten: one hundred minus thirty, seventy, too big. Try eight: sixty-four minus twenty-four, forty. x is eight. Quadratic equations are Grade 10 work, but trial and improvement solves simple ones now, and that is the point of the method — it needs no theory.

It also works when the answer is not a whole number, if the question allows it. Three x equals ten: try three, nine, too small; try four, twelve, too big; try three and a third, ten. x is ten thirds. Fraction answers are legitimate, and an equation such as three x equals ten should not be declared unsolvable because no whole number works.

Two cautions. First, trial and improvement finds a solution but does not by itself show it is the only one; x squared equals sixteen has solutions four and negative four, and trying positive values only finds one. Second, it is slow for equations that the next lessons will solve in two lines. Use it when inspection stalls, and move to inverse operations once you have learned them. Always finish with a check in the original equation. Questions on trial and improvement follow.

## Subtopic: Checking, Equations with Letters on Both Sides, and No Solution

A check is a substitution into the original equation, not into a rearranged version, because the rearrangement might be where the error is. Three x minus four equals twenty with x equals eight: left side three times eight minus four, twenty; right side twenty. Left equals right, so eight is the solution. Write LHS and RHS, or left and right, and show both values.

Equations with the letter on both sides can also be inspected. Five x minus nine equals two x: for what x is five x minus nine the same as two x? Try three: fifteen minus nine is six, and two times three is six. x is three. Or reason: the left is three x more than the right apart from the nine, so three x must be nine. Four x plus one equals x plus ten: try three: thirteen and thirteen. x is three.

Some equations have every number as a solution. Two bracket x plus three bracket equals two x plus six is true for any x — it is the magician's trick from the previous lesson. Inspection will find that every trial works. Some equations have no solution. x plus three equals x plus five asks what number is both three more and five more than x — there is none; the two sides differ by two for every x. x squared equals negative four has no solution in Grade 8, because no number squares to a negative. Recognising no solution is itself an answer.

An equation can have two solutions. x squared equals twenty-five is solved by five and by negative five. Inspection should always ask: is there another? For equations with only x to the first power there is exactly one; for x squared, think about the negative.

Finally, answer the question that was asked. If the equation was set up from a situation, the solution must be returned to the situation with its unit and checked for sense. A trip of six kilometres makes sense; a trip of negative six kilometres does not, and a negative answer in such a problem means the set-up was wrong. Questions on checking and special cases follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same solving as stories about a missing number in a card trick, a guess-the-weight stall at a fete, and a locked gate whose code is found by trying and adjusting — stories in which inspection means looking carefully, and a check means looking again.

## Subtopic: The Missing Card

A card trick. A friend lays out some cards face down, adds seven more, and says there are now fifteen on the table. How many did she start with? You do not need to turn over a card. Something plus seven is fifteen; the something is eight. That is x plus seven equals fifteen solved by inspection — looking at the situation and seeing the missing number.

She tries again. She takes her cards, deals them into three equal piles, takes four cards away from the table altogether, and says twenty remain. How many did she start with? Think backwards. Twenty remain after four were removed, so there were twenty-four before. Twenty-four were in three equal piles, so each pile had eight — and she started with twenty-four. But wait: what is the unknown? If the question is how many did she start with, it is twenty-four. If the question is how many in each pile, it is eight. Read the question. The equation three x minus four equals twenty, with x as the pile, has solution eight; the number she started with is three x, twenty-four.

Now the trick she cannot do. She deals cards into some equal piles with seven cards each, keeps three aside, and says there are thirty-one in total. How many piles? Seven piles of what, plus three, is thirty-one? Seven times something is twenty-eight; four piles. Seven f plus three equals thirty-one, f equals four. Check: twenty-eight plus three, thirty-one.

What does inspection actually mean here? It means you look at the structure — something plus seven, three somethings minus four, seven somethings plus three — and ask the number question the structure poses. The equation is just the structure written compactly. If you can see the structure in the cards, you can see it in the symbols, and the other way round.

And the check is turning the cards over. After you say eight, she turns them face up and counts: eight. If she counted nine, your inspection was wrong, however confident it felt. Substitution is the count. Questions on the cards follow.

## Subtopic: Guess the Weight and the Gate Code

At a school fete there is a guess-the-weight stall. The rule written on the card is: seven times the weight in kilograms, plus nine, equals one hundred and twenty-one. Nobody will tell you the weight; you have to find it. You guess ten kilograms: seven times ten plus nine is seventy-nine. The stallholder says: too light. You guess twenty: one hundred and forty-nine. Too heavy. So it is between ten and twenty. You guess fifteen: one hundred and fourteen, too light, but close. Sixteen: one hundred and twenty-one. You have it. Sixteen kilograms.

That is trial and improvement, and the stallholder's too light and too heavy are the whole method. Each answer cuts the range. You do not need to be clever; you need to be systematic — write down each guess and its result so you never guess outside the range you have already found.

Now the gate. A gate has a four-digit code and a hint: the code is a number which, when you square it and subtract three times it, gives forty. You try five: twenty-five minus fifteen, ten. Too small. You try ten: a hundred minus thirty, seventy. Too big. You try eight: sixty-four minus twenty-four, forty. The gate opens. Notice that this equation, x squared minus three x equals forty, is not one you could solve by reading it as a simple number question — the x appears twice, once squared — but trial and improvement did not care. It works on anything you can evaluate.

A thought before you walk through the gate: could another number also work? Try negative five: twenty-five plus fifteen, forty. It does. The gate has two codes, eight and negative five, and if the hint had said the code is positive, you would be right to pick eight; if not, you should mention both. Inspection should always ask whether there is a second answer, especially when the unknown is squared.

The lesson of the fete: a guess is not a failure, it is information. Too light and too heavy are the two most useful words in solving by inspection, as long as you write them down. Questions on the stall and the gate follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for solving by inspection. One: read the equation as a question — what number makes this true? Two: for one step, answer from number facts. Three: for two steps, undo the last operation first and write the middle line, then undo the next. Four: if the answer is not clear, try a value, record too big or too small, and narrow the range. Five: check by substituting into the original equation, showing left and right. Six: ask whether there could be another solution, and return the answer to the situation with its unit.

A typical question: solve x minus eleven equals negative three by inspection. What number minus eleven is negative three? Eight. Check: eight minus eleven is negative three.

Another: solve four x plus three equals twenty-seven. Four x is twenty-four; x is six. Check: twenty-four plus three is twenty-seven.

Another: solve thirty-six over x equals nine. Thirty-six divided by what is nine? Four. Check.

Another: solve two x minus one equals x plus four. Try five: nine and nine. x is five.

Another: a number squared is forty-nine. Seven and negative seven; both must be given.

Another: the hall from last lesson — forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred. Forty g is one thousand two hundred; g is thirty. Thirty guests. Check: one thousand two hundred plus five hundred.

Errors to avoid. Reading five minus x as x minus five. Answering the intermediate value instead of the solution — saying twenty-four for three x minus four equals twenty. Not checking. Giving only the positive root of a square. Declaring an equation unsolvable because the answer is negative or a fraction. Forgetting the unit when the equation came from a situation.

Looking ahead: the next lesson uses substitution to build tables of ordered pairs from equations, and the lesson after solves equations formally with additive and multiplicative inverses — the written form of what inspection does in the head. Grade 9 solves equations with brackets, fractions and the variable on both sides routinely. Final questions follow.
