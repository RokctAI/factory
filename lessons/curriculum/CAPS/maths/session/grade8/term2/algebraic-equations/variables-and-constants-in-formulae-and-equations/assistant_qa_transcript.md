### subtopic_1 — Variables and Constants in a Formula
**Assistant:** What is the simplest test for whether something in a formula is a variable or a constant?

**Tutor:** Ask whether it could be different in another instance of the same kind of situation. In P equals two l plus two b, pick two different rectangles: their lengths differ, their breadths differ, their perimeters differ — so l, b and P are variables — but both have exactly two lengths and two breadths, so the twos are the same for every rectangle and are constants. In C equals eight k plus twelve, two trips with the same taxi company have different distances and costs, so k and C vary, but the same rate and flag fee, so eight and twelve are constants. The test works for letters as well as numbers: pi is the same for every circle, so it is a constant even though it is written as a letter, and g is the same everywhere on Earth. It also explains why the same quantity can switch roles — for one specific trip the cost is fixed at sixty, so in the equation for that trip the cost is a constant and only the distance is open.

### subtopic_2 — Constants Written as Letters
**Assistant:** If a letter can be a constant, how do I avoid treating it as an unknown to solve for?

**Tutor:** Read the statement that accompanies the formula. A letter that is a constant is always introduced with its fixed value or a description of one: where pi is approximately three comma one four two, where g is nine comma eight, where a and b are the rate and flag fee for the company. A letter that is a variable is described as a quantity that changes — the radius, the distance, the number of guests. If no description is given, context helps: pi, g and c have conventional meanings, and letters near the start of the alphabet, a, b, c, k, are often used for given constants while x, y, n and t are often variables, though this is habit rather than rule. When you substitute, you replace variables with the values the question gives and use the known values of the constants, which come from the formula and not from the question. If you find yourself trying to solve for pi, you have misread the roles.

### subtopic_3 — Inputs, Outputs and Dependence
**Assistant:** Can the same letter be an input in one question and an output in another?

**Tutor:** Yes, and that is normal. In d equals s t the formula is written to calculate d from s and t, so d is the subject and output. If a question gives distance and time and asks for speed, speed is the quantity you want: substitute the known values, one hundred and fifty equals s times three, and solve the equation by inspection to get s equal to fifty. In Grade 9 you will learn to rearrange the formula to make s the subject, s equals d over t, which turns speed into the output directly. The roles of input and output are set by what you know and what you want, not by the letters themselves. What does not change is the set of variables and the set of constants: d, s and t are always the variables of this formula and the understood coefficient one is its only constant, whichever one you are solving for. Identifying the variables first, before deciding which is wanted, keeps the two questions separate.

### subtopic_6 — The Cellphone Contract and the Thermometer
**Assistant:** In F equals nine fifths C plus thirty-two, why is thirty-two a constant when the temperature can obviously be thirty-two degrees?

**Tutor:** Because the thirty-two in the formula is not a temperature reading; it is the fixed difference between the two scales at the freezing point of water. Whatever the temperature is today, the Fahrenheit reading is always found by multiplying the Celsius reading by nine fifths and adding the same thirty-two, so the thirty-two does not change when the temperature changes, which is exactly what makes it a constant. The temperature itself is the variable, C or F, and it may happen to take the value thirty-two on some day — thirty-two degrees Fahrenheit is freezing, thirty-two Celsius is a hot day — but that is a value of a variable, not the constant in the formula. The two uses of the number are unrelated. The same confusion arises with the twelve in the taxi formula, which is the flag fee and not a distance of twelve kilometres. A constant in a formula is a number that stays put while the variables move; a variable may land on any number, including one that also appears as a constant.
