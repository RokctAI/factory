# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the determination of numerical values by substitution and the use of substitution to generate tables of ordered pairs from equations in two variables. For an equation of the form y equals an expression in x, each chosen x value is substituted to produce a y value, and the pair x then y is recorded in order. Tables are completed in both directions, patterns in the y values are related to the coefficient of x, and the pairs are written in the bracket notation that the Cartesian plane will use. The anchor equation is y equals two x plus one, with pairs zero and one, one and three, two and five, three and seven.

## Subtopic: Equations in Two Variables and Ordered Pairs

So far equations have had one letter and one or two solutions. An equation like y equals two x plus one has two letters, and it is not a question with one answer — it is a rule connecting x and y. Choose any x, and the rule gives a y. Choose x equal to zero: y equals two times zero plus one, one. Choose x equal to one: three. Choose two: five. Three: seven. Every choice of x produces a pair of numbers, and the pair is written in brackets with x first and y second: bracket zero semicolon one bracket, bracket one semicolon three bracket, bracket two semicolon five bracket, bracket three semicolon seven bracket.

The order matters, which is why they are called ordered pairs. Bracket one semicolon three bracket means x is one and y is three; bracket three semicolon one bracket means x is three and y is one, and that second pair does not satisfy y equals two x plus one, since two times three plus one is seven, not one. x first, y second, always — the alphabetical order is the convention, and the Cartesian plane later this term depends on it.

In South African notation the two numbers are separated by a semicolon, because a comma is the decimal separator: bracket one comma five semicolon four bracket is the pair x equal to one and a half, y equal to four. Some textbooks use a comma between the coordinates; be ready to read either, but write the semicolon.

The letter x is called the input or the independent variable: you choose it freely. The letter y is the output or the dependent variable: it depends on x through the rule. Other letters may play the roles — n and T in patterns, k and C in the taxi — but the structure is the same: choose the first, calculate the second.

An ordered pair is a solution of the two-variable equation in the sense that it makes the equation true: substituting x equal to two and y equal to five into y equals two x plus one gives five equals five. Checking whether a given pair satisfies an equation is substitution of both values. Bracket four semicolon nine bracket: nine equals two times four plus one, true. Bracket four semicolon eight bracket: eight equals nine, false. Questions on ordered pairs follow now.

## Subtopic: Completing a Table from an Equation

A table of values lists x in the top row and y in the bottom row, and completing it is repeated substitution. For y equals two x plus one with x from negative two to three: x negative two gives two times negative two plus one, negative three; negative one gives negative one; zero gives one; one gives three; two gives five; three gives seven. The bottom row reads negative three, negative one, one, three, five, seven.

Negative x values need the brackets habit from substitution: two bracket negative two bracket plus one. Fractions and decimals are allowed too: x equal to a half gives two; x equal to one comma five gives four.

A second rule: y equals three x minus two, x from zero to four. Zero gives negative two; one gives one; two gives four; three gives seven; four gives ten. A third: y equals x squared, x from negative three to three. Nine, four, one, zero, one, four, nine — the squares are symmetric about zero because squaring loses the sign, and this table will give a curve, not a straight line, when plotted later.

A fourth: y equals ten minus x, x from zero to five. Ten, nine, eight, seven, six, five — decreasing as x increases, because x is subtracted.

The rule may be in other letters. The taxi cost C equals eight k plus twelve for k from zero to five: twelve, twenty, twenty-eight, thirty-six, forty-four, fifty-two. The pattern term T equals four n minus one for n from one to five: three, seven, eleven, fifteen, nineteen — the patterns topic from Term 1, which was tables of ordered pairs all along.

Lay the table out neatly, with x values in order and equally spaced where possible, because the next section reads patterns from the spacing. Write each pair under the table in bracket form if asked: bracket negative two semicolon negative three bracket and so on. Questions on completing tables follow.

## Subtopic: Finding a Missing x — Substitution Backwards

A table may give a y value and ask for the x that produced it. That is solving an equation by inspection, from the previous lesson. For y equals two x plus one, which x gives y equal to eleven? Two x plus one equals eleven; two x is ten; x is five. Which x gives y equal to negative five? Two x is negative six; x is negative three.

For y equals three x minus two, which x gives y equal to thirteen? Three x is fifteen; x is five. For y equals ten minus x, which x gives y equal to two? Ten minus x is two; x is eight. For C equals eight k plus twelve, which k gives C equal to sixty? Eight k is forty-eight; k is six — the taxi again.

For y equals x squared, which x gives y equal to sixteen? Four, and also negative four. A table with squares has two inputs for most outputs, and both must be given unless the table restricts x to positive values.

Some y values have no x in whole numbers. For y equals two x plus one, which x gives y equal to eight? Two x is seven; x is three and a half. The table may include it as a fraction or decimal, or the question may say x is a whole number, in which case eight is not in the table. For y equals x squared, y equal to negative four has no x at all.

A table with gaps in both rows is completed by substituting forwards for the missing y values and solving backwards for the missing x values, one cell at a time. Write the equation for each backward step: two x plus one equals eleven, two x equals ten, x equals five. That line is the method.

Check backwards results forwards: x equal to five into two x plus one gives eleven, as required. The two directions check each other, and an examiner awards the check. Questions on missing x values follow.

## Subtopic: Patterns in the Table and Checking Pairs

When the x values go up by one each step, the y values of y equals two x plus one go up by two each step: negative three, negative one, one, three, five, seven. The constant difference in y is the coefficient of x. For y equals three x minus two the differences are three; for y equals ten minus x they are negative one, a decrease of one each step. This is the difference method from Term 1 patterns, and it is the same fact: the coefficient of x is the rate of change of y.

The constant term is the y value when x is zero. For y equals two x plus one, x equal to zero gives one; for three x minus two, negative two; for ten minus x, ten. Reading a table, the y under x equal to zero is the constant, and the step between neighbouring y values is the coefficient, so a table with equally spaced x values lets you recover the equation. A table with y values one, three, five, seven for x zero, one, two, three has constant one and difference two: y equals two x plus one.

For y equals x squared the differences are not constant — one, three, five, seven as x goes from zero to four — which is the signature of a non-linear rule. Constant differences mean a straight-line graph; changing differences mean a curve. The graphs topic will draw both.

Checking a pair against an equation is substitution of both values. Does bracket three semicolon seven bracket lie on y equals two x plus one? Two times three plus one is seven; yes. Does bracket three semicolon seven bracket lie on y equals three x minus two? Nine minus two is seven; yes — the same pair can satisfy two different equations, and that pair is where the two lines will cross when they are drawn. Does bracket negative one semicolon negative one bracket lie on y equals two x plus one? Negative two plus one is negative one; yes. Does it lie on y equals x squared? One is not negative one; no.

A table is a set of ordered pairs; an equation is a rule that generates infinitely many; a graph, soon, is the picture of all of them. Questions on patterns and checking follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same tables as stories about a vending machine that turns coins into snacks by a fixed rule, a bus fare chart on a timetable, and a thermometer with two scales side by side — stories in which every input has exactly one output, and the table is the machine's memory.

## Subtopic: The Vending Machine

A vending machine has one rule: whatever number of coins you put in, it gives you twice that many sweets plus one free. Put in zero coins: one sweet, the free one. One coin: three. Two coins: five. Three coins: seven. The machine is y equals two x plus one with x the coins and y the sweets, and the table of what it does is the table of ordered pairs.

Three things about the machine. First, every input gives exactly one output: two coins always give five sweets, never six. That is what makes it a rule — the same x always gives the same y. Second, the output depends on the input and not the other way round: you choose the coins; the machine chooses the sweets. Input first, output second, which is why the pair is written bracket two semicolon five bracket and never bracket five semicolon two bracket. Third, the machine's memory is the table: a list of what it has done, one pair per line.

Now a friend comes back with eleven sweets and asks how many coins she put in. Twice something plus one is eleven; twice something is ten; five coins. That is reading the machine backwards, and it is solving an equation. If she came back with eight sweets, you would say the machine is broken, because twice a whole number plus one is always odd — or that she put in three and a half coins, which this machine does not accept. Not every output has an input, depending on what inputs are allowed.

A second machine gives you the square of the coins: y equals x squared. One coin, one sweet; two coins, four; three coins, nine. A friend returns with sixteen sweets: four coins. But a mischievous friend who puts in negative four coins — takes four out — also gets sixteen by the rule, so the backward question has two answers, and the machine's designer should have said that only positive coins are accepted.

Write the machine's rule, make its table, read it forwards by substituting and backwards by solving, and check each pair by putting it back through the rule. That is the whole lesson, with sweets. Questions on the machine follow.

## Subtopic: The Fare Chart and the Two-Scale Thermometer

A minibus taxi route posts a fare chart: a flag fee of twelve rand plus eight rand per kilometre. The chart is a table — zero kilometres, twelve rand; one, twenty; two, twenty-eight; three, thirty-six; four, forty-four; five, fifty-two — and it was made by substituting into C equals eight k plus twelve. A passenger does not need the equation; the table is enough. But the table only goes to five kilometres, and a passenger going nine kilometres needs the rule: eight times nine plus twelve, eighty-four rand. The equation extends the table to any input.

Look down the fare column: twelve, twenty, twenty-eight, thirty-six. Each step is eight — the rate per kilometre. The first entry, twelve, is the fare for zero kilometres — the flag fee. From the chart alone you could recover the rule: constant twelve, step eight, C equals eight k plus twelve. A table and its equation carry the same information.

A passenger paid sixty rand. How far did she travel? Read the chart backwards: sixty is not in the chart, which stops at fifty-two, so use the rule. Eight k plus twelve equals sixty; eight k is forty-eight; six kilometres. Backward reading is solving.

Now a thermometer with Celsius on one side and Fahrenheit on the other. The two scales are a table of ordered pairs made by F equals nine fifths C plus thirty-two: zero and thirty-two; ten and fifty; twenty and sixty-eight; thirty and eighty-six; forty and one hundred and four. Each step of ten in Celsius is a step of eighteen in Fahrenheit — nine fifths of ten — and the Fahrenheit reading at zero Celsius is thirty-two, the constant. The thermometer is the table drawn as a picture, with the two scales side by side, and reading across from one scale to the other is substitution.

Reading backwards: body temperature is about ninety-eight comma six Fahrenheit; what is that in Celsius? Nine fifths C plus thirty-two equals ninety-eight comma six; nine fifths C is sixty-six comma six; C is thirty-seven. Solving, again.

Every table in this lesson is a chart somebody could post on a wall, and every equation is the rule that made the chart and extends it. Questions on the fare chart and thermometer follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for tables of ordered pairs. One: identify the input letter and the output letter; the equation is usually written as output equals expression in input. Two: for each given input, substitute with brackets and calculate the output. Three: for each given output, write the equation with that value and solve by inspection for the input; ask whether a second input exists if the input is squared. Four: write the pairs in brackets, input first, semicolon, output second. Five: check a pair or two by substituting both values. Six: read the pattern — the step in the outputs and the output at input zero — and relate it to the coefficient and constant.

A typical question: complete the table for y equals four x minus three with x equal to negative one, zero, one, two, and y equal to nine. Substituting: negative seven, negative three, one, five. Solving four x minus three equals nine: four x is twelve; x is three. Pairs: bracket negative one semicolon negative seven bracket, bracket zero semicolon negative three bracket, bracket one semicolon one bracket, bracket two semicolon five bracket, bracket three semicolon nine bracket.

Another: a table shows y values five, eight, eleven, fourteen for x zero, one, two, three. Find the equation. Constant five, step three: y equals three x plus five. Check: three times two plus five is eleven.

Another: does bracket negative two semicolon five bracket lie on y equals x squared plus one? Four plus one is five; yes.

Another: for y equals six minus two x, find x when y is zero. Six minus two x equals zero; two x is six; x is three. The input that makes the output zero is a value the graphs topic will care about.

Errors to avoid. Swapping the order in a pair. Dropping brackets around a negative input. Giving only one input for a squared rule. Using a comma between coordinates where a decimal comma could be confused. Reading the step as the constant or the constant as the step. Forgetting to check.

Grade 9 draws straight-line graphs from equations directly. Final questions follow.
