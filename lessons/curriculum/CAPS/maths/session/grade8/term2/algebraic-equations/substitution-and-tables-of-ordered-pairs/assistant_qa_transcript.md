### subtopic_1 — Equations in Two Variables and Ordered Pairs
**Assistant:** Why is the order in an ordered pair so important?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers play different roles — one is the input you chose and the other is the output the rule produced — and swapping them describes a different situation. For y equals two x plus one, bracket two semicolon five bracket says that an input of two gives an output of five, which is true; bracket five semicolon two bracket says an input of five gives an output of two, which is false, since five gives eleven. Later this term the Cartesian plane makes the order physical: the first number is the distance across and the second the distance up, so a swapped pair is plotted at a different point and the graph comes out wrong. The convention is input first, output second, which for x and y is alphabetical order, and for other letters follows the equation — k then C for the taxi, n then T for a pattern. The semicolon between the numbers is the South African convention, because the comma is reserved for decimals, so bracket one comma five semicolon four bracket is unambiguous.

### subtopic_3 — Finding a Missing x — Substitution Backwards
**Assistant:** When I am given a y value and asked for x, is that still substitution?

**Tutor:** It is substitution of the known value into the equation, followed by solving for the unknown — which is why this lesson sits inside the equations topic. Putting y equal to eleven into y equals two x plus one gives the one-variable equation two x plus one equals eleven, and solving it by inspection gives x equal to five. The forward direction, choosing x and calculating y, is pure substitution; the backward direction, knowing y and finding x, is substitution to set up an equation and then solving. The two directions check each other: having found x equal to five, substitute forwards and confirm that two times five plus one is eleven. Some backward questions have two answers, when x is squared, because four and negative four both give sixteen; some have none in whole numbers, because twice a whole number plus one is never eight; and some have none at all, because no number squares to a negative. Always ask which case you are in.

### subtopic_4 — Patterns in the Table and Checking Pairs
**Assistant:** How do the step and the constant in a table connect to the pattern work from Term 1?

**Tutor:** They are the same ideas with new names. In Term 1 a number pattern with a constant difference had a general term of the form difference times n plus a constant, and the constant was what you would get at position zero. In a table for y equals two x plus one with x increasing by one, the y values increase by two — the difference — and the y value at x equal to zero is one — the constant. So the equation of a table is read exactly as the rule of a pattern was: step gives the coefficient, value at zero gives the constant. The matchstick pattern three n plus one is the table of y equals three x plus one with x starting at one. The new element is that x may now be zero, negative or fractional, so the table is a window onto a rule that extends in both directions, and the graphs topic will draw that rule as a straight line whose steepness is the step and whose starting height is the constant. Non-constant steps, as in y equals x squared, signal a curve.

### subtopic_6 — The Fare Chart and the Two-Scale Thermometer
**Assistant:** If a table already gives me the fares, why do I need the equation at all?

**Tutor:** Because the table only lists the inputs somebody chose to include, while the equation produces the output for any input and lets you go backwards from an output to its input. The fare chart stops at five kilometres; a passenger travelling nine kilometres needs the rule, eight times nine plus twelve, to know the fare of eighty-four rand. A passenger who paid sixty rand and wants to know the distance cannot find sixty in the chart, so she solves eight k plus twelve equals sixty to get six kilometres. The equation also explains the chart — the step of eight is the rate and the entry of twelve at zero kilometres is the flag fee — so that a change in the tariff can be understood as a change in one number rather than a whole new chart. Tables are convenient summaries; equations are the complete rule. The two carry the same information when the inputs are equally spaced, and the graphs topic adds a third form, the picture, that shows the whole rule at a glance.
