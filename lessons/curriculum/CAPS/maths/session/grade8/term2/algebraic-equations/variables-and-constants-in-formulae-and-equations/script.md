# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the identification of variables and constants in formulae and equations. A variable is a quantity that can take different values and is represented by a letter; a constant is a quantity with a fixed value, usually a number but sometimes a letter standing for a fixed number. In a formula the variables are divided into the subject, which is the output, and the other variables, which are inputs; the constants are the coefficients and standing terms. The anchor formulae are P equals two l plus two b for the perimeter of a rectangle, C equals eight k plus twelve for a taxi fare, and A equals pi r squared for the area of a circle, in which pi is a constant written as a letter.

## Subtopic: Variables and Constants in a Formula

A formula is an equation that expresses one quantity in terms of others and holds for every case of its kind. The perimeter of a rectangle: P equals two l plus two b. The letters P, l and b are variables — the perimeter, length and breadth change from one rectangle to the next. The twos are constants — every rectangle has two lengths and two breadths, whatever its size. The formula says: whatever the rectangle, double the length, double the breadth, add.

The taxi: C equals eight k plus twelve. C and k are variables — different trips have different distances and different costs. Eight and twelve are constants — the tariff fixes the rate and the flag fee for every trip. A different taxi company would have different constants but the same variables.

Simple interest: I equals P r t over one hundred. Four variables — interest, principal, rate, time — and one constant, the hundred that converts the percentage. Area of a triangle: A equals a half b h. Three variables and the constant a half. Speed: d equals s t. Three variables and no written constant, though the understood coefficient one is there.

The test for a variable is: could this take a different value in a different instance of the same situation? The test for a constant is: is this fixed by the definition of the situation? In P equals two l plus two b, the number of sides of a rectangle is fixed by what a rectangle is — the twos are constants. The length is not fixed — a variable.

A formula has a subject: the letter alone on the left, which the formula calculates. P is the subject of P equals two l plus two b. The subject is the output variable; the other variables are inputs. Changing the subject — rewriting to make l the subject — is Grade 9 work; identifying it is this lesson's.

List the parts in a sentence. In P equals two l plus two b: variables P, l, b; subject P; inputs l and b; constants two and two. That sentence is the full answer to an identify question. Questions on variables and constants follow now.

## Subtopic: Constants Written as Letters

Not every letter is a variable. Some letters stand for fixed numbers, and the most famous is pi. The area of a circle: A equals pi r squared. A and r are variables — circles come in all sizes. Pi is a constant — about three comma one four two, the same for every circle, written as a letter because its decimal never ends. The circumference: C equals two pi r. Variables C and r; constants two and pi.

Other letters-as-constants appear in science. The acceleration due to gravity, g, is about nine comma eight metres per second squared everywhere on the Earth's surface; in the formula for the weight of an object, W equals m g, m and W are variables and g is a constant. The speed of light, c, is a constant. In a formula for a particular taxi company, a textbook may write C equals a k plus b, where a and b are constants — the rate and flag fee — whose values are given elsewhere, and k and C are the variables. The letters a and b are constants because the problem says they are fixed for that company.

How do you tell? Context. A formula comes with a statement of what its letters mean, and a constant letter is described as a fixed value: where pi is approximately three comma one four two, where g is nine comma eight. If a letter is said to represent a quantity that changes from case to case — the radius, the mass, the distance — it is a variable. If the letter has a fixed numerical value you could look up, it is a constant.

In patterns, T equals four n minus one has variables T and n and constants four and negative one. If a textbook writes T equals a n plus c for a general linear pattern, a and c are constants for any particular pattern — its difference and its position-zero value — and n and T are the variables.

The distinction matters when you substitute: you substitute values for variables, and the constants are already there. For A equals pi r squared with r equal to five, you substitute five for r and use three comma one four two for pi, giving about seventy-eight comma five five. Pi was never a blank to fill in from the question; it is part of the formula. Questions on letter constants follow.

## Subtopic: Inputs, Outputs and Dependence

A formula describes how one quantity depends on others. In P equals two l plus two b, the perimeter depends on the length and the breadth: choose l and b, and P is determined. P is the dependent variable, or output; l and b are independent variables, or inputs. In C equals eight k plus twelve, the cost depends on the distance: k is the input and C the output.

The subject of the formula is normally the output. A formula is written with the output alone on the left so that substituting the inputs gives the output directly. P equals two l plus two b is set up to calculate P.

Which letter is input and which output can depend on the question. In d equals s t, if you know speed and time, d is the output. If a question gives distance and time and asks for speed, s is the quantity you want, and in Grade 9 you would rearrange the formula to make s the subject; in Grade 8 you substitute what you know and solve the resulting equation by inspection: three s equals one hundred and fifty, so s is fifty.

Reading dependence from a formula: in C equals eight k plus twelve, as k increases by one, C increases by eight — the coefficient of the input is the rate at which the output changes. In P equals two l plus two b, increasing l by one increases P by two. In A equals pi r squared, doubling r multiplies A by four, not two, because r is squared; the dependence is not a straight line.

A formula with one input and one output is a two-variable equation of the kind that gave tables of ordered pairs last lesson, and a formula with two inputs — P equals two l plus two b — needs both inputs chosen before the output is fixed, so its table would have three columns.

A question may ask: on what does the interest depend? From I equals P r t over one hundred: on the principal, the rate and the time. Each is an input; interest is the output; the hundred is a constant. Answer by naming the variables and their roles, in words. Questions on inputs and outputs follow.

## Subtopic: Variables and Constants in Equations and Their Solutions

An equation from a situation also has variables and constants, and the roles are slightly different. In eight k plus twelve equals sixty, k is the variable — the one unknown — and eight, twelve and sixty are constants. Compare the formula C equals eight k plus twelve: the sixty has replaced C. A formula becomes an equation when one of its variables is given a value, and the remaining variable is then the unknown.

So the same letters can be variables in one setting and known values in another. In the formula, both C and k vary. In the equation for one trip, C is sixty — a constant for that trip — and k is to be found. Once solved, k is six, and for that trip nothing varies any more.

In an equation with letters on both sides, like five x minus nine equals two x, x is the variable and five, negative nine and two are constants. In an equation with two letters, like y equals two x plus one, both are variables and the equation has infinitely many solution pairs, as last lesson's tables showed.

A coefficient is a constant that multiplies a variable. In eight k plus twelve, eight is a coefficient and twelve is a constant term — both are constants, but the names distinguish the one attached to the variable from the one standing alone. In a formula written with letter constants, C equals a k plus b, a is the coefficient and b the constant term, and both are constants.

The unknown in an equation is sometimes called the variable even though, once solved, it has one value. The word variable refers to its role in the equation — the thing whose value is open — not to the fact that it varies. Do not be troubled by the word; identify the letter whose value the equation determines.

Finally, the equals sign. In a formula it means is always equal to. In an equation it means is equal to in this case, and solving finds the case. Identifying variables and constants is the first step of both using a formula and solving an equation, because you must know what you are substituting for and what is already fixed. Questions on equations follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same formulae as stories about a recipe card, a cellphone contract and a thermometer — stories in which some numbers are printed on the card and never change, while others are blanks filled in differently every day.

## Subtopic: The Recipe Card

A recipe card for pancakes reads: flour in cups equals a half times the number of pancakes; eggs equal the number of pancakes divided by four; milk in millilitres equals thirty-seven comma five times the number of pancakes. Three little formulae. Which parts are printed on the card and never change, and which are blanks you fill in each time?

The number of pancakes is a blank — some days you make eight, some days twenty. That is the input variable. Flour, eggs and milk are blanks too — they come out different each time, depending on the pancakes. Those are the output variables. The half, the four and the thirty-seven comma five are printed on the card: they are the recipe itself, the ratios that make these pancakes and not some other pancakes. Those are the constants.

Now imagine a different card for a richer pancake: flour equals a half times pancakes, eggs equal pancakes divided by two, milk equals forty times pancakes. Same variables — pancakes, flour, eggs, milk — different constants. A constant is what makes this recipe this recipe. A variable is what makes this batch this batch.

The card might print a letter for a constant. Suppose it says: milk equals m times the number of pancakes, where m is the millilitres per pancake for your brand of flour, given on the flour packet. Then m is a constant — fixed once you have your flour — even though it is a letter. You would look it up, write it in, and it would stay the same for every batch.

If a friend asks how many pancakes you can make with three eggs, you have turned the formula into an equation: pancakes divided by four equals three; pancakes equals twelve. The eggs, which were an output, have become a known value, and the pancakes, which were an input, have become the unknown. Same letters, new roles, because the question changed.

Reading a formula is reading a recipe card: find the blanks, find the printed numbers, and say which blank the card is designed to fill in. Questions on the recipe card follow.

## Subtopic: The Cellphone Contract and the Thermometer

A cellphone contract costs ninety-nine rand a month plus one comma five rand per minute of calls. The bill is B equals one comma five m plus ninety-nine, where m is the minutes. Which parts can change from month to month? The minutes — you talk more some months than others — and therefore the bill. m and B are the variables. Which parts are fixed by the contract you signed? The ninety-nine rand subscription and the one comma five rand per minute. Those are the constants, and they stay constant until the contract changes.

Interpret each constant. The ninety-nine is the constant term — the bill when m is zero, a month with no calls. The one comma five is the coefficient — the rate at which the bill grows per minute. If the network raises the per-minute rate to one comma eight, the formula becomes B equals one comma eight m plus ninety-nine: one constant changes, the variables are unchanged.

Now the thermometer formula F equals nine fifths C plus thirty-two. Variables: C and F, because temperatures change. Constants: nine fifths and thirty-two, because the relationship between the two scales never changes — water always freezes at zero Celsius and thirty-two Fahrenheit, and a Celsius degree is always nine fifths of a Fahrenheit degree. The thirty-two is the Fahrenheit reading when C is zero; the nine fifths is how many Fahrenheit degrees each Celsius degree is worth.

Here is a question that tests the whole lesson. In F equals nine fifths C plus thirty-two, is thirty-two a variable because the temperature can be thirty-two degrees? No. The temperature is a variable; the thirty-two in the formula is a fixed offset between the scales. The same number can appear as a value of a variable in one place and as a constant in another, and the formula's thirty-two is a constant because it does not change when the temperature does.

And one more: the formula has no constant written as a letter, but if a textbook wrote F equals a C plus b for a general conversion between two linear scales, a and b would be constants to be given, and C and F would still be the variables. Letters that are constants are always introduced with a phrase like where a is. Questions on the contract and the thermometer follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for identifying variables and constants. One: read the statement of what each letter means. Two: for each letter, ask whether it changes between instances of the situation — if yes, variable; if it has a fixed value you could look up or that the situation sets, constant. Three: for each number, it is a constant; name it coefficient if it multiplies a variable and constant term if it stands alone. Four: identify the subject as the output and the other variables as inputs. Five: answer in a sentence that names each letter and its role.

A typical question: identify the variables and constants in V equals l b h. Variables V, l, b, h; subject V; inputs l, b, h; no written constant other than the understood coefficient one.

Another: in the formula for the circumference of a circle, C equals two pi r, which letters are variables and which are constants? Variables C and r; constants two and pi.

Another: in the equation three n minus five equals sixteen, identify the variable and the constants. Variable n; constants three, negative five and sixteen; three is the coefficient of n.

Another: a formula for the cost of printing is C equals nought comma five p plus twenty, where p is the number of pages. What does each constant represent? Nought comma five rand per page, the rate; twenty rand, a fixed set-up fee.

Another: in s equals u t plus a half a t squared, from science, with a the acceleration, a is a variable in general but a constant in a problem where the acceleration is fixed, such as falling under gravity, where it is g. Context decides.

Errors to avoid. Calling pi a variable because it is a letter. Calling a number a variable because a variable could take that value. Confusing coefficient with constant term. Naming the wrong letter as the subject. Forgetting that the understood coefficient one is a constant.
