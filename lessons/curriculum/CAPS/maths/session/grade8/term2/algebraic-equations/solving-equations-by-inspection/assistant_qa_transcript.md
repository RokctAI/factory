### subtopic_1 — Reading an Equation as a Question
**Assistant:** Is solving by inspection just guessing?

**Tutor:** No. Inspection means reading the equation as a precise number question and answering it from number facts you know — what number plus seven gives fifteen is a question with one answer, eight, and recognising it is knowledge, not luck. Trial and improvement, which comes in when the facts do not give the answer at once, is also not guessing in the casual sense: every trial is recorded, every result is labelled too big or too small, and every next trial is chosen inside the range the previous ones established, so the method is guaranteed to close in on the answer. What makes both methods legitimate is the check — substituting the proposed solution into the original equation and showing that left equals right. A guessed number that passes the check is a solution; the check is what turns a candidate into an answer. The next lessons give a more mechanical method with inverse operations, but inspection remains the fastest way for simple equations and the best way to build the sense of what an equation is asking.

### subtopic_2 — Two-Step Equations — Working Backwards
**Assistant:** Why do I undo the last operation first when working backwards?

**Tutor:** Because undoing is reversing a sequence, and a sequence is reversed from its end. In three x minus four, the operations applied to x were multiply by three and then subtract four — the subtraction was done last, so it is the outermost layer, and it must come off first. Asking what number minus four gives twenty recovers three x as twenty-four; only then can you ask three times what gives twenty-four. If you tried to undo the multiplication first, you would be dividing twenty by three and getting a fraction that has nothing to do with the answer, because the four has not yet been dealt with. The picture is wrapping a parcel: the last layer of paper put on is the first taken off. The order of operations tells you which layer is outside — multiplication and division bind more tightly to the letter than addition and subtraction — so the plus or minus is usually the outer layer and comes off first. Writing the middle line, three x equals twenty-four, records that the first layer is off.

### subtopic_4 — Checking, Equations with Letters on Both Sides, and No Solution
**Assistant:** How do I know whether an equation has one solution, two, or none?

**Tutor:** Look at how the unknown appears. If the letter appears only to the first power — no squares — and the equation is not an identity, there is exactly one solution, because the left and right sides change at different steady rates and can meet only once. If the letter is squared, there may be two solutions, one positive and one negative, because a number and its negative have the same square: x squared equals twenty-five is solved by five and negative five. If the two sides are the same expression in disguise, as in two bracket x plus three bracket equals two x plus six, every number is a solution and the equation is really an identity. If the two sides differ by a fixed amount, as in x plus three equals x plus five, no number is a solution. And a square equal to a negative, like x squared equals negative four, has no solution in Grade 8 because no number squares to a negative. The habit is to ask after finding a solution: could there be another, and should I say so?

### subtopic_6 — Guess the Weight and the Gate Code
**Assistant:** When should I use trial and improvement rather than inspection or inverse operations?

**Tutor:** Use inspection first for anything you can read as a direct number question, since it is fastest. Use inverse operations, from the lesson after next, for any one- or two-step equation where the number facts are not immediate, such as seven x plus nine equals one hundred and twenty-one, because two written steps give the answer with no searching. Use trial and improvement when neither applies — when the unknown appears squared or in two places in a way you cannot yet untangle, as in x squared minus three x equals forty, or when a problem gives you a rule to evaluate rather than an equation to rearrange. Its strength is that it works on anything you can calculate; its weaknesses are that it is slow, that it may miss a second solution if you only try one sign, and that it can suggest no solution exists when the answer is a fraction you have not tried. Keep a table of trials, move towards the closer side, finish with a check, and remember that Grade 10 will give you an exact method for the squared cases.
