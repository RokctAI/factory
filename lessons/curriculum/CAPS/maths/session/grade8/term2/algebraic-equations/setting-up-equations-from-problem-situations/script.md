# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the setting up of equations to describe problem situations and the analysis and interpretation of equations that describe a situation. An equation is a statement that two expressions are equal; setting one up requires defining a variable, translating each quantity into an expression in that variable, and identifying the two quantities the situation says are equal. Interpreting an equation reverses the process: each term is matched to a feature of the situation. The anchor situation is a taxi with a flag fee of twelve rand and a rate of eight rand per kilometre whose trip costs sixty rand, giving the equation eight k plus twelve equals sixty.

## Subtopic: Expression, Equation and the Equals Sign

An expression is a phrase: eight k plus twelve. An equation is a sentence: eight k plus twelve equals sixty. The equals sign is a verb — it claims that what is on the left has the same value as what is on the right. An equation can be true or false depending on the value of its variable: eight k plus twelve equals sixty is true when k is six and false when k is five. Solving an equation means finding the values that make it true, and that is the business of the next lessons. This lesson is about writing the sentence correctly in the first place.

Every equation describing a situation has three ingredients. A variable, which is a letter standing for the unknown quantity, with a definition: let k be the number of kilometres. An expression for each side, built from the situation: eight k plus twelve for the cost of the trip, and sixty for what was paid. And the claim of equality between them, which comes from the situation: the trip cost sixty rand.

The definition of the variable is not optional. Let k be the number of kilometres is a sentence you write before the equation, and it earns a mark. It also prevents the most common confusion, which is solving for the right number and answering the wrong question — finding k equals six and writing six rand instead of six kilometres.

Choose a variable for the quantity you are asked to find, not for something else. If the question asks how many kilometres, let k be the kilometres. If it asks for a person's age, let a be the age. If two unknowns are related, express the second in terms of the first: if one number is three more than another, let the smaller be n and the larger n plus three. One letter, two expressions.

An equation is not a formula. A formula like A equals l b holds for all rectangles; an equation like eight k plus twelve equals sixty holds for one particular trip. Both have equals signs, but a formula is a rule and an equation is a question. Questions on expressions and equations follow now.

## Subtopic: Setting Up One-Step and Two-Step Equations

Start with the simplest situations. A number plus seven is fifteen: let the number be n, then n plus seven equals fifteen. A number multiplied by four is thirty-two: four n equals thirty-two. Half a number is nine: n over two equals nine. A number decreased by five is twelve: n minus five equals twelve. Each is a one-step equation — one operation separates the variable from the answer.

The taxi is two-step. Let k be the number of kilometres. The cost is the flag fee plus the rate times the distance: twelve plus eight k, or eight k plus twelve. The trip cost sixty rand. Eight k plus twelve equals sixty. Two operations sit between k and sixty: multiply by eight, add twelve.

A hall costs five hundred rand to hire plus forty rand per guest, and the total was one thousand seven hundred rand. Let g be the number of guests. Forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred.

A phone contract charges ninety-nine rand a month plus one comma five rand per minute, and the bill was one hundred and fifty-nine rand. Let m be the minutes. One comma five m plus ninety-nine equals one hundred and fifty-nine.

A learner has saved two hundred and forty rand and saves thirty rand a week; when will she have four hundred and eighty? Let w be the weeks. Thirty w plus two hundred and forty equals four hundred and eighty.

Three times a number, less four, is twenty. Three n minus four equals twenty. Four less than three times a number — the same thing, three n minus four. Four less three times a number is different: four minus three n. Read the order carefully, as in the algebraic language lesson.

The pattern in every two-step situation: a rate times the variable, plus or minus a once-off amount, equals a total. Rate, once-off, total — find those three numbers in the words and the equation writes itself. Questions on setting up follow.

## Subtopic: Interpreting an Equation — Reading It Back

Interpreting an equation is translating it back into the situation. Take eight k plus twelve equals sixty for the taxi. Eight is the rate, eight rand per kilometre. k is the number of kilometres. Eight k is the distance charge, the part of the fare that depends on how far you go. Twelve is the flag fee, charged once regardless of distance. Eight k plus twelve is the total fare for k kilometres. Sixty is what this particular trip cost. The equals sign says: this trip's fare was sixty rand.

Now an unfamiliar equation: twenty-five t plus one hundred and fifty equals four hundred, describing a plumber's bill. What could it mean? One hundred and fifty is a once-off amount — a call-out fee. Twenty-five is a rate — twenty-five rand per something, and t suggests time, perhaps per minute or per hour, though twenty-five rand an hour is cheap for a plumber. Four hundred is the total bill. Interpretation: the plumber charges a call-out fee of one hundred and fifty rand plus twenty-five rand per unit of time, and the bill came to four hundred rand. Finding t will tell us how long the job took.

The coefficient is the rate; the constant is the once-off; the right-hand side is the total. If a term is subtracted, it is a reduction: two hundred minus fifteen d equals eighty might be a prepaid balance of two hundred rand decreasing by fifteen rand a day until eighty rand is left, and d is the number of days.

Interpretation also means checking units. In eight k plus twelve equals sixty, every term is in rand: eight rand per kilometre times kilometres gives rand; twelve rand; sixty rand. If the units do not match across a plus sign, the equation is wrong. You cannot add kilometres to rand.

A question may give an equation and ask what a specific part represents. In forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred, what does forty g represent? The total cost of the guests — forty rand each for g guests. What does the five hundred represent? The fixed hire charge. What does the equation as a whole say? That the hall cost one thousand seven hundred rand in total. Answer in a sentence that names the situation, not just the algebra. Questions on interpreting follow.

## Subtopic: Harder Set-Ups — Two Related Unknowns and Geometry

Some situations have two unknown quantities related to each other. One letter handles both. Two numbers add to forty and one is six more than the other. Let the smaller be n; the larger is n plus six; their sum is forty: n plus n plus six equals forty, which collects to two n plus six equals forty. One letter, one equation.

Ages. A mother is three times as old as her daughter and the sum of their ages is forty-eight. Let the daughter be d years old; the mother is three d; d plus three d equals forty-eight, four d equals forty-eight. Choose the letter for the smaller or simpler quantity so that the other is a multiple or a sum of it rather than a fraction.

Consecutive numbers. Three consecutive whole numbers add to seventy-two. Let the first be n; the others are n plus one and n plus two; n plus n plus one plus n plus two equals seventy-two, three n plus three equals seventy-two.

Geometry. A rectangle has length five centimetres more than its breadth and perimeter forty-two centimetres. Let the breadth be b; the length is b plus five; perimeter is two lengths plus two breadths: two bracket b plus five bracket plus two b equals forty-two, which expands to four b plus ten equals forty-two. The angles of a triangle add to one hundred and eighty degrees: if they are x, two x and three x, then six x equals one hundred and eighty. A straight line is one hundred and eighty degrees: angles of three x minus ten and x plus thirty on a straight line give four x plus twenty equals one hundred and eighty.

Money with two kinds. A purse has ten coins, some two-rand and some five-rand, worth thirty-two rand. Let t be the number of two-rand coins; then the five-rand coins number ten minus t; the value is two t plus five bracket ten minus t bracket equals thirty-two. Expanding and collecting is next topic's business, but setting it up is this lesson's.

In every case, define one letter, write the other quantity in terms of it, find the sentence in the problem that says two things are equal — sum is, perimeter is, total is — and write that sentence in symbols. Questions on harder set-ups follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same equations as stories about a birthday party budget, a bag of marbles shared out, and a mystery number in a magician's trick — stories in which the equals sign is a balanced scale, and the letter is the one thing nobody at the table knows yet.

## Subtopic: The Party Budget

You are planning a birthday party with a budget of nine hundred rand. The venue costs three hundred rand flat. Food is forty rand a head. How many guests can you invite? You do not know the number of guests — that is the unknown, so give it a letter: let g be the number of guests.

Now describe the cost in words first. Venue, three hundred. Food, forty rand for each guest, so forty times g. Total cost: forty g plus three hundred. And the situation says you want to spend exactly the budget: the total equals nine hundred. Forty g plus three hundred equals nine hundred. You have written an equation without solving anything, and the equation is the plan.

Read it back. Forty is the cost per guest. g is the guests. Forty g is the food bill. Three hundred is the venue. Nine hundred is the budget. The equals sign says the plan spends exactly the budget. If it said less than or equal, that would be a budget you may not exceed — an inequality, which is Grade 9 — but exactly is an equation.

Now change the story and watch the equation change. The venue raises its price to three hundred and fifty: forty g plus three hundred and fifty equals nine hundred. You find cheaper food at thirty-five a head: thirty-five g plus three hundred equals nine hundred. Your aunt gives you two hundred rand extra: forty g plus three hundred equals one thousand one hundred. Each change in the story moves exactly one number in the equation, and knowing which number moves is what interpreting means.

A wrong set-up to recognise: three hundred g plus forty equals nine hundred. That says the venue charges three hundred per guest and the food is a once-off forty rand, which is not the party you are planning. The coefficient belongs to the per-guest cost; the constant belongs to the once-off cost. Swapping them is the most common set-up error, and reading the equation back aloud catches it: three hundred rand per guest? No.

Write the equation, then say it in words. If the words describe the party, the equation is right. Questions on the party follow.

## Subtopic: Marbles and the Magician

A bag of marbles is shared among four friends; each gets seven and three are left over. How many were in the bag? Let m be the marbles. Four friends times seven each is twenty-eight, plus three left over, equals the bag: four times seven plus three equals m, so m equals thirty-one. Here the unknown sits alone on one side and the equation is almost already solved — but notice that it is still an equation, and writing it down is still the first step.

Turn it round. A bag of thirty-one marbles is shared among some friends; each gets seven and three are left. How many friends? Let f be the friends. Seven f plus three equals thirty-one. Now the unknown is inside the expression, and the equation needs solving — next lesson. The same situation produced two different equations depending on which quantity was unknown, and choosing the letter for the quantity asked about is what makes the equation useful.

Now a magician. Think of a number. Double it. Add six. Halve the result. Subtract the number you first thought of. The magician announces: your answer is three. How? Let the number be n. Double: two n. Add six: two n plus six. Halve: n plus three. Subtract n: three. The answer is three whatever n was — the equation two n plus six over two minus n equals three is true for every n, which is why the trick works. Setting it up in algebra exposes the trick.

A different magician says: I am thinking of a number; if I triple it and subtract five I get sixteen. What is my number? Let it be n: three n minus five equals sixteen. This equation is true for exactly one n, and finding it is solving. One magician's equation is always true; the other's is true once. Both are equations; the difference is what they tell you.

Last, a trick to avoid. A learner writes the marble equation as four m plus three equals seven, having let m be the marbles and then multiplied the marbles by the friends. Read it back: four bags of marbles plus three equals seven? Nonsense. Letters are quantities, and multiplying two quantities must mean something — four friends times seven marbles each means twenty-eight marbles; four times the whole bag means nothing here. Every term should be sayable in words. Questions on marbles and magicians follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for setting up an equation. One: find the quantity the question asks for and define a letter for it in a sentence. Two: express every other quantity in the problem in terms of that letter. Three: find the sentence in the problem that says two things are equal — costs, totals, sums, perimeters — and write those two things as expressions. Four: join them with an equals sign. Five: read the equation back in words and check that each term makes sense and that the units agree.

A typical question: a school bus costs one thousand two hundred rand to hire and tickets are thirty-five rand each; the trip must exactly cover its cost. Set up an equation for the number of tickets. Let t be the tickets sold. Thirty-five t equals one thousand two hundred. Here the once-off is on the other side: ticket income equals bus cost.

Another: a rectangle's length is twice its breadth and the perimeter is sixty centimetres. Let the breadth be b; length two b; two bracket two b bracket plus two b equals sixty, six b equals sixty.

Another: interpret two hundred and fifty plus eighteen h equals five hundred and eighty-six. A once-off two hundred and fifty, a rate of eighteen per h, a total of five hundred and eighty-six. Perhaps a repair with a call-out fee and an hourly rate; h is hours.

Another: a number is multiplied by five and nine is subtracted; the result is twice the number. Let the number be n: five n minus nine equals two n. Both sides have the variable; that is allowed and common.

Errors to avoid. Not defining the letter. Swapping rate and once-off. Writing three minus n for three less than n. Multiplying two quantities that should not be multiplied. Mismatched units across a plus. Writing an expression and calling it an equation.

Looking ahead: the next lessons solve these equations — first by inspection, then with inverse operations — and substitution checks every solution. Grade 9 sets up and solves equations with brackets and fractions and simultaneous pairs. Final questions follow.
