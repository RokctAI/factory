# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises and extends the equivalence of descriptions of the same relationship presented verbally, in flow diagrams, in tables, as formulae, as number sentences and as equations. Each form encodes the same rule; translation between forms preserves the rule, and two descriptions are equivalent when they produce the same output for every input. A number sentence is one instance of the formula with specific numbers; an equation is the formula with the output known and the input unknown. The anchor relationship is the hall-hire cost of five hundred rand plus forty rand per guest, C equals forty g plus five hundred.

## Subtopic: Six Descriptions of One Relationship

A hall costs five hundred rand to hire plus forty rand per guest. That is a relationship between the number of guests and the cost, and it can be described in six ways.

Verbally: the cost is five hundred rand plus forty rand for every guest. As a flow diagram: input the number of guests, multiply by forty, add five hundred, output the cost. As a table: ten guests, nine hundred rand; twenty guests, one thousand three hundred; thirty guests, one thousand seven hundred; forty guests, two thousand one hundred. As a formula: C equals forty g plus five hundred, where g is the number of guests and C the cost in rand. As a number sentence: forty times twenty plus five hundred equals one thousand three hundred — the formula with one specific input and its output filled in. As an equation: forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred — the formula with the output known and the input to be found.

All six describe the same relationship. They differ in what they make easy. The words explain the situation. The flow diagram shows the order of the operations. The table gives several answers at a glance. The formula gives every answer and can be rearranged. The number sentence checks one case. The equation asks a question — how many guests for one thousand seven hundred rand? — and its solution, thirty, is in the table.

The number sentence and the equation are both the formula with numbers in it. The difference is which numbers are known. In the number sentence everything is known and it is a true statement to be checked. In the equation one number is missing and it is a question to be solved. Both come from the formula by substitution.

Equivalence means producing the same output for every input. Two descriptions that agree on a few inputs are probably equivalent; two that disagree on even one are not. The hall formula and the hall table agree everywhere the table goes; the hall formula and a formula C equals forty g plus four hundred disagree everywhere, by one hundred rand. Questions on the six descriptions follow now.

## Subtopic: Translating Between Forms

Any form can be turned into any other. From words to formula: find the rate and the once-off amount; the rate is the coefficient, the once-off is the constant. Forty rand per guest and five hundred rand flat: forty g plus five hundred. From words to flow diagram: the rate is the multiplication and the once-off is the addition, in that order: multiply by forty, then add five hundred.

From formula to table: substitute a set of inputs. From table to formula: step in outputs over step in inputs for the coefficient, output at input zero or one pair for the constant, as in the previous lesson. The hall table steps by four hundred for every ten guests: forty per guest; forty times ten is four hundred against nine hundred: plus five hundred.

From formula to words: read the coefficient as a rate and the constant as a fixed amount, and name the quantities: the cost in rand is forty times the number of guests plus five hundred. From flow diagram to formula: apply the operations in order to the letter, with brackets where an addition precedes a multiplication.

From formula to number sentence: choose an input, substitute, evaluate, and write the whole thing with an equals sign: forty times twenty plus five hundred equals one thousand three hundred. From formula to equation: replace the output with a known value and leave the input as the letter.

From equation back to formula: replace the known output with the output letter. Forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred came from C equals forty g plus five hundred. From a number sentence back to formula: identify which number was the input and replace it and the output with letters — forty times twenty plus five hundred equals one thousand three hundred becomes forty g plus five hundred equals C, provided you can tell that twenty was the input.

Letters other than x and y are normal, and the letters should be defined: g for guests, C for cost. A relationship in words must become a formula with defined letters before it can become an equation, and a question that gives words and asks for an equation is really asking for two translations in a row. Questions on translating follow.

## Subtopic: Deciding Whether Two Descriptions Are Equivalent

Two descriptions are equivalent when they give the same output for every input. To test, translate both into the same form — usually a formula — and compare, or evaluate both at several inputs and compare the outputs.

Two learners describe a pattern. One says: multiply the position by two and add two. The other says: add one to the position and double it. Formulas: two n plus two and two bracket n plus one bracket. Expand the second: two n plus two. Equivalent — the same rule, as the patterns topic showed. Test with numbers: n equal to three gives eight from both.

Another pair: three x minus two and three bracket x minus two bracket. Expand the second: three x minus six. Not equivalent; they differ by four for every x. Test: x equal to four gives ten and six.

Another: a table with inputs one to four and outputs five, eight, eleven, fourteen, against the formula y equals three x plus two. Substitute: five, eight, eleven, fourteen. Equivalent on the table's inputs, and since the table steps by three with a constant of two, the formula is the table's rule.

Another: the words half of a number, plus four, against the flow diagram add four then halve. Formulas: x over two plus four and bracket x plus four bracket over two, which is x over two plus two. Not equivalent. At x equal to ten: nine against seven.

A table can only show equivalence on the inputs it lists, so a formula that matches a table is equivalent to it as far as the table goes, and the table is taken as a sample of the rule. Two formulas can be shown equivalent for every input by algebra — expanding and collecting until they are identical — which a table cannot do.

The equation and number sentence forms are tested by substitution: a number sentence is true or false, and an equation's solution either satisfies the formula or does not. Forty times twenty plus five hundred equals one thousand three hundred is true; forty times twenty plus five hundred equals one thousand two hundred is false, and belongs to no relationship with these numbers. Questions on equivalence follow.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Right Form for the Question

Each form answers some questions better than others, and part of the skill is choosing. If the question asks what the cost will be for a given number of guests, substitute into the formula, or read the table if the number is there. If it asks how many guests for a given cost, write the equation and solve it. If it asks to describe the relationship, give the words or the formula with defined letters. If it asks how the cost changes as guests increase, read the coefficient — forty rand more per guest — or the step in the table. If it asks for several costs at once, make a table. If it asks to check a claim — forty guests cost two thousand one hundred rand — write the number sentence and evaluate.

Here is a mixed question. A phone contract is ninety-nine rand a month plus one comma five rand per minute. Describe it as a formula; find the bill for one hundred minutes; find the minutes for a bill of two hundred and nineteen rand; and say by how much the bill rises for each extra ten minutes. Formula: B equals one comma five m plus ninety-nine. Bill for one hundred minutes: one hundred and fifty plus ninety-nine, two hundred and forty-nine. Minutes for two hundred and nineteen: one comma five m equals one hundred and twenty, m equals eighty. Ten extra minutes: fifteen rand. Four questions, four forms — formula, number sentence, equation, rate.

A flow diagram is the right form when the order of operations is the point — when a learner must see that add five then double is not double then add five. A table is the right form when the relationship is to be plotted, which the Graphs topic does next.

When a question provides one form and asks for another, do the translation explicitly and label it: here is the formula, here is the table from it. Marks are given for each form, and an unlabelled jumble of numbers earns fewer than a clean table.

Remember that the equation form has a solution that must be returned to the situation. Forty g plus five hundred equals one thousand seven hundred gives g equal to thirty, and the answer is thirty guests, not thirty. Questions on choosing forms follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same relationship as stories about the same journey described by a map, a timetable and a text message, and about two learners who argue over whether their rules are different and discover they are the same — stories in which the thing described never changes, only the language.

## Subtopic: One Journey, Three Descriptions

Think about a bus trip from Durban to Pietermaritzburg. You could describe it with a map — a line between two dots with the road drawn in. You could describe it with a timetable — departs seven, arrives eight fifteen, stops at Pinetown and Hillcrest. You could describe it with a text message — leaving now, be there by quarter past eight. Three descriptions, one journey. The map is good for seeing where you go; the timetable for knowing when; the message for telling someone quickly. Nobody thinks these are three different journeys.

A relationship in mathematics is the same. The hall-hire cost is one thing. Five hundred plus forty a head is the text message — quick, in words. The flow diagram is the map — it shows the route the number takes, multiply then add. The table is the timetable — specific stops: ten guests, nine hundred; twenty, one thousand three hundred. The formula is the complete route description that lets you work out any stop. The number sentence is one line of the timetable read aloud. The equation is a passenger asking: I have one thousand seven hundred rand — how many guests can I bring?

If someone shows you a timetable and a map and asks whether they describe the same trip, you check: does the map go through Pinetown, which the timetable lists? Does the timetable's arrival time fit the map's distance? If they agree on every check, they are the same journey. If the map shows a stop the timetable does not, or the timetable says arrive at eight but the map shows a route that takes two hours, they are different. That is exactly how you test whether a table and a formula describe the same relationship: check that every row of the table satisfies the formula.

And a text message can be ambiguous in a way a timetable is not. Double it and add two could mean double, then add two — or it could be misread. The flow diagram or the formula removes the ambiguity, which is why examiners want the formula with its letters defined, not just the words.

So when a question hands you one form and asks for another, you are not inventing a new relationship. You are describing the same journey to a different person. Questions on the journey follow.

## Subtopic: The Argument About the Chairs

Two learners are given a pattern: tables in a row, chairs around them. One table seats four, two tables seat six, three seat eight. They are asked for a rule. The first writes: multiply the number of tables by two and add two. The second writes: add one to the number of tables and multiply by two. They look at each other's work and each says the other is wrong.

Who is right? Test with numbers. Three tables: two times three plus two is eight; three plus one is four, times two is eight. Five tables: twelve and twelve. Ten tables: twenty-two and twenty-two. They agree every time. Now algebra: two n plus two, and two bracket n plus one bracket, which expands to two n plus two. Identical. Both are right, because the two descriptions are equivalent — the same relationship in two sets of words, and in two formulas that look different but are the same after expanding.

Their flow diagrams look different too: multiply by two then add two, and add one then multiply by two. Different arrows, same outputs for every input. A flow diagram is a particular route to the output, and two routes can arrive at the same place every time.

Now a third learner writes: multiply by two and add one. Two n plus one. Three tables: seven. Wrong — the real pattern gives eight. This description is not equivalent; it disagrees at the very first check. One disagreement is enough.

The argument teaches three things. First, two descriptions that look different can be the same relationship, and the test is outputs, not appearance. Second, algebra settles it for every input at once: expand and compare. Third, a description that fails even one check is not equivalent, no matter how plausible it sounds.

Here is the same lesson in money. One shop says: the price is one comma one five times the marked price. Another says: the marked price plus fifteen percent. Equivalent — one comma one five m is m plus nought comma one five m. A third says: the marked price plus fifteen rand. Not equivalent, except for a marked price of exactly one hundred rand, where both give one hundred and fifteen. Agreement at one input proves nothing; agreement at every input is equivalence. Questions on the argument follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method. One: identify the relationship — what depends on what — and define letters. Two: to translate, go through the formula: words to formula by rate and once-off, formula to table by substitution, formula to diagram by order of operations, formula to number sentence by one substitution, formula to equation by fixing the output. Three: to test equivalence, bring both descriptions to formulas and compare by algebra, or evaluate both at several inputs; one disagreement means not equivalent. Four: to answer a question, choose the form that fits: substitution for an output, equation for an input, coefficient for a rate, table for many values. Five: label every form and return equation solutions to the situation.

A typical question: a gym charges a joining fee of two hundred rand and one hundred and fifty rand a month. Give the relationship as a formula, as a flow diagram, as a table for one to four months, as a number sentence for six months, and as an equation for a total of one thousand four hundred rand; solve the equation. Formula: T equals one hundred and fifty m plus two hundred. Diagram: multiply by one hundred and fifty, then add two hundred. Table: three hundred and fifty, five hundred, six hundred and fifty, eight hundred. Number sentence: one hundred and fifty times six plus two hundred equals one thousand one hundred. Equation: one hundred and fifty m plus two hundred equals one thousand four hundred; m equals eight months.

Another: are the descriptions subtract three then multiply by four and multiply by four then subtract twelve equivalent? Four bracket x minus three bracket is four x minus twelve. Yes.

Another: a table shows inputs one, two, three with outputs four, seven, ten. Which of y equals three x plus one and y equals four x matches? Three x plus one gives four, seven, ten; four x gives four, eight, twelve. The first.

Errors to avoid. Confusing a number sentence with an equation. Treating agreement at one input as equivalence. Translating words into a formula with the rate and once-off swapped. Dropping a bracket when a flow diagram adds before multiplying. Giving an equation's solution without its unit.

Looking ahead: the Graphs topic adds a seventh description — the picture — and reads increasing, decreasing, linear and non-linear from it. Grade 9 treats these relationships as functions and compares their graphs, tables and equations systematically. Final questions follow.
