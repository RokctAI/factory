### subtopic_1 — Input, Rule, Output — The Flow Diagram
**Assistant:** What makes a flow diagram a rule, rather than just a picture of some arithmetic?

**Tutor:** The fact that it assigns exactly one output to every input, and does so the same way every time. Multiply by three then subtract two sends four to ten today, tomorrow and for anyone who uses it, and never sends four anywhere else. That reliability is what allows every other question in the lesson: you can predict outputs for inputs you have never tried, you can run the diagram backwards from an output to the one input that produced it, and you can reconstruct the operations from a table of pairs because the same operations lie behind every pair. A list of unrelated calculations would not have this property. The flow diagram also fixes the order of the operations, which matters because multiply then subtract is a different rule from subtract then multiply, and the arrows make the order impossible to misread. Grade 9 will call such a rule a function and will insist on the one-output-per-input condition as its defining feature; this lesson is where that condition is first made explicit.

### subtopic_2 — Rule to Formula and Formula to Table
**Assistant:** How do I decide where the bracket goes when I turn a flow diagram into a formula?

**Tutor:** Apply the operations to the letter x one at a time in the order of the arrows, and put a bracket round the result of a step whenever the next step would otherwise apply to only part of it. Add five then multiply by two: after the first step you have x plus five; the second step multiplies the whole of that, so you write two bracket x plus five bracket — without the bracket, two x plus five would multiply only the x, which is a different rule. Multiply by three then subtract two: after the first step you have three x, and subtracting two from it is three x minus two with no bracket needed, because the subtraction already applies to the whole. Square then subtract one is x squared minus one; subtract one then square is bracket x minus one bracket squared. The rule of thumb is that a bracket is needed whenever an addition or subtraction is followed by a multiplication, division or power. Translating back, the order of operations tells you which came first: anything inside a bracket, then powers, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction.

### subtopic_3 — Finding the Input — Working Backwards and Equations
**Assistant:** Why does running the diagram backwards give the same steps as solving the equation?

**Tutor:** Because both are the same idea — undoing the operations that were applied to the input, in reverse order — written in two notations. The forward diagram applies multiply by three and then subtract two to x to produce y. To recover x from y you undo the last operation first by adding two, then undo the multiplication by dividing by three. The formal solution of three x minus two equals ten adds two to both sides and then divides both sides by three: the same two inverse operations in the same order, with the balance principle guaranteeing that the equation stays true. The backward diagram is quicker to draw and the formal method is what the mark scheme wants, so it is worth doing one and checking with the other. The exceptions to watch are squares and even powers, where the reverse has two branches — a square root gives a positive and a negative input — and operations that cannot be reversed for a particular output, as when the output of a square-then-subtract-one rule is negative five and no input exists.

### subtopic_4 — Finding the Rule from a Table
**Assistant:** What if the inputs in a table are not consecutive numbers?

**Tutor:** Divide the step in the outputs by the step in the inputs to get the multiplier, because the multiplier is the change in output for each unit change in input, not for each row of the table. Inputs two, four, six, eight with outputs seven, thirteen, nineteen, twenty-five have an input step of two and an output step of six, so the multiplier is six over two, three; then three times two is six against an output of seven, so the constant is plus one, and the rule is y equals three x plus one. If the inputs are irregular — say one, three, four, seven — pick two rows, divide the output difference by the input difference, and verify on the rest. Only when the inputs go up by one each time can you read the multiplier straight off the output steps. This is the same reasoning as the rate in the patterns and equations work, and it is why checking the rule on every pair in the table is essential: a multiplier found from the wrong pair of rows will fail on the others and reveal the slip.
