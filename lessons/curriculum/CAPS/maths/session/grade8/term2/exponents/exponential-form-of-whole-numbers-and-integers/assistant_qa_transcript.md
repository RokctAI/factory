### subtopic_1 — Base, Exponent and Expanded Form
**Assistant:** Why is anything to the power zero equal to one and not zero?

**Tutor:** Because the exponent counts factors, and a power with zero factors of the base is the starting point of multiplication, which is one, not zero. Think of the pattern of powers of two: two to the power three is eight, two squared is four, two to the power one is two, and each step down divides by two. Dividing two by two gives one, so two to the power zero must be one for the pattern to hold. The same reasoning works for any non-zero base: three cubed is twenty-seven, three squared is nine, three to the power one is three, and dividing by three once more gives one. If two to the power zero were zero, the pattern would break and every rule in the next lessons would fail. The laws of exponents lesson proves it formally: a power divided by itself is one, and subtracting equal exponents gives exponent zero, so the two must agree.

### subtopic_3 — Brackets Change the Base
**Assistant:** Does the bracket rule matter when the exponent is odd, since the answers come out the same?

**Tutor:** The values agree for an odd exponent, but the meanings differ, and you should still write the brackets. Bracket negative two bracket cubed means three factors of negative two, which is negative eight. Negative two cubed without brackets means the negative of two cubed, which is also negative eight. They coincide only because one unpaired negative in the first version produces the same sign as the single minus sign out front in the second. The moment the exponent is even the two forms split apart — positive sixteen against negative sixteen — and an examiner who wants to test your understanding will choose an even exponent for exactly that reason. Writing brackets around every negative base is a habit that costs nothing and protects you from the case where it matters; leaving them out is a habit that will eventually be punished by an even exponent.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing and Ordering Powers
**Assistant:** When comparing two powers, is the one with the bigger base always bigger?

**Tutor:** No, and this is worth checking rather than assuming. Two to the power five is thirty-two and five squared is twenty-five, so the power with the smaller base is the larger number. The exponent usually matters more than the base once both are above two, because each extra factor multiplies the whole value, while a bigger base only enlarges each factor. Three to the power four, eighty-one, beats four cubed, sixty-four, for the same reason. The only reliable method is to evaluate both powers and compare the values, especially when negative bases are involved, since then the sign decides first and size second: negative sixty-four is smaller than negative twenty-seven even though sixty-four is the larger size. Write the values beside the powers before you choose the inequality symbol; that written step is where the mark is.

### subtopic_6 — The Freezer Thermometer — Negatives That Flip
**Assistant:** If each multiplication by a negative flips the sign, why does the size not flip too?

**Tutor:** Because sign and size are separate pieces of information in a number and multiplication treats them separately. The size, or absolute value, of a product is the product of the sizes, so multiplying by negative two always doubles the size whatever the sign is: one becomes two, two becomes four, four becomes eight, and so on. The sign of a product follows its own rule — same signs give positive, different signs give negative — which is what makes a multiplication by a negative behave like a switch that flips each time. So after five multiplications by negative two the size is two to the power five, thirty-two, because the size has been doubled five times, and the sign is negative because the switch has been flipped an odd number of times. Keeping the two questions apart — what is the sign, what is the size — is the whole method, and it is faster and safer than multiplying a long string of negatives left to right.
