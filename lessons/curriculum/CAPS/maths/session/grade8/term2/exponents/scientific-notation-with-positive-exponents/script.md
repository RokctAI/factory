# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the representation and comparison of large numbers in scientific notation, limited to positive exponents of ten. A number in scientific notation has the form a times ten to the power n, where a is at least one and less than ten and n is a positive whole number. The anchor values are one hundred and fifty million, the approximate Earth–Sun distance in kilometres, written as one comma five times ten to the power eight; the population of South Africa, about sixty-two million, written as six comma two times ten to the power seven; and the speed of light, about three hundred thousand kilometres per second, written as three times ten to the power five.

## Subtopic: Powers of Ten and the Shape of Scientific Notation

Scientific notation is built on powers of ten, so start there. Ten to the power one is ten. Ten squared is one hundred. Ten cubed is one thousand. Ten to the power six is one million, a one followed by six zeros. The exponent on ten is the number of zeros after the one. Ten to the power nine is a billion, a one followed by nine zeros.

Multiplying a number by a power of ten moves its decimal comma to the right by the exponent. Three comma four times ten squared moves the comma two places: three hundred and forty. Three comma four times ten to the power five moves it five places: three hundred and forty thousand. If the comma runs out of digits, fill the gaps with zeros.

Scientific notation writes a large number as a product of two factors: a number between one and ten — at least one, less than ten — and a power of ten. Three comma four times ten to the power five is in scientific notation, because three comma four is between one and ten and ten to the power five is a power of ten. Thirty-four times ten to the power four has the same value but is not in scientific notation, because thirty-four is not less than ten. Nought comma three four times ten to the power six is also the same value and also not scientific notation, because nought comma three four is less than one.

The first factor is sometimes called the coefficient or the mantissa, and the exponent on the ten is the order of magnitude. A number has exactly one scientific-notation form, which is what makes the notation useful for comparison: two numbers in correct scientific notation can be compared by their exponents first.

The reason scientists use it is practical. The mass of the Earth is about six followed by twenty-four zeros kilograms. Nobody wants to write or count those zeros, and a calculator cannot display them. Six times ten to the power twenty-four is short, unambiguous and checkable. ## Subtopic: Converting a Large Number into Scientific Notation

To convert a large number into scientific notation, place the decimal comma after the first non-zero digit and count how many places it moved. Take one hundred and fifty million: one five zero zero zero zero zero zero zero. The comma is currently at the end, after the last zero. Move it to sit after the first digit, the one, giving one comma five. Count the places moved: eight. So one hundred and fifty million is one comma five times ten to the power eight.

The population of South Africa is about sixty-two million: six two zero zero zero zero zero zero. Comma after the six: six comma two. Places moved: seven. Six comma two times ten to the power seven.

The speed of light is about three hundred thousand kilometres per second: three zero zero zero zero zero. Comma after the three: three. Places moved: five. Three times ten to the power five. When the first factor is a whole number, as here, you write it without a comma; three comma zero is acceptable but unnecessary.

Trailing zeros disappear into the power of ten; non-zero digits stay in the first factor. Four hundred and twenty-five thousand becomes four comma two five times ten to the power five — all three non-zero digits are kept. Seven billion, seven followed by nine zeros, becomes seven times ten to the power nine.

A quick check: the exponent is always one less than the number of digits in the whole number. One hundred and fifty million has nine digits, so the exponent is eight. Sixty-two million has eight digits, exponent seven. This check catches the off-by-one error that comes from miscounting the places moved.

The other common error is a first factor outside the range. Fifteen times ten to the power seven has the right value for one hundred and fifty million but fifteen is not less than ten. Move the comma one more place, and raise the exponent by one to compensate: one comma five times ten to the power eight. Questions on converting follow.

## Subtopic: Converting Back to an Ordinary Number

Converting from scientific notation back to an ordinary number reverses the process: move the decimal comma to the right by the exponent, adding zeros as needed. Two comma seven times ten to the power six: start with two comma seven and move the comma six places right. One place uses the seven; the remaining five places need zeros. Two million seven hundred thousand. Write it with spaces as thousand separators: two space seven hundred space zero zero zero.

Nine comma zero five times ten to the power four: move four places. Two places use the zero and the five; two more need zeros. Ninety thousand five hundred. Notice that the zero in the middle of the first factor is a real digit and must be kept — nine comma zero five is not nine comma five, and the ordinary numbers differ by ten percent.

Eight times ten to the power three: the first factor has no decimal digits, so all three places are zeros. Eight thousand.

A check: the ordinary number should have one more digit than the exponent. Two comma seven times ten to the power six gives a seven-digit number; two million seven hundred thousand has seven digits. If you get six or eight digits, recount.

Calculators display scientific notation in their own way, and you must read it correctly. A screen showing two comma seven followed by a small zero six, or two comma seven E zero six, means two comma seven times ten to the power six. The E does not mean anything is wrong; it means the number was too long for the display. Write it out in full or in proper scientific notation — never copy the E form into an examination answer.

Numbers in scientific notation can be rounded in the first factor without touching the exponent. One comma four nine six times ten to the power eight, the Earth–Sun distance in kilometres to four significant figures, rounds to one comma five times ten to the power eight. ## Subtopic: Comparing and Ordering Numbers in Scientific Notation

The power of scientific notation is comparison. Two numbers written correctly in scientific notation can be compared by their exponents first. Three comma nine times ten to the power six against one comma two times ten to the power seven: the exponents are six and seven, so the second number is larger, regardless of the first factors. Three comma nine million is less than twelve million.

Only when the exponents are equal do the first factors decide. Four comma eight times ten to the power five against four comma two five times ten to the power five: same exponent, so compare four comma eight with four comma two five. Four comma eight is larger. Compare the first factors as decimals, digit by digit from the left, as you learned in Term 1.

The method fails if either number is not in correct scientific notation. Twenty-five times ten to the power five looks as if it has exponent five, but it is really two comma five times ten to the power six, and comparing it with three times ten to the power five by exponent alone would give the wrong answer. Always normalise first: make every first factor at least one and less than ten, adjusting the exponent, and only then compare.

Ordering a list: suppose you have six comma two times ten to the power seven, the South African population; one comma four times ten to the power nine, the population of India; three comma three times ten to the power eight, the population of the United States; and two comma one times ten to the power seven, the population of Mozambique. Group by exponent: the two sevens, then the eight, then the nine. Within the sevens, two comma one is less than six comma two. Ascending order: Mozambique, South Africa, United States, India.

To find how many times larger one number is than another, divide. India's population over South Africa's: one comma four times ten to the power nine divided by six comma two times ten to the power seven. Ten to the power nine over ten to the power seven is one hundred; one comma four over six comma two is about nought comma two three. Roughly twenty-three times. The laws of exponents in the next lesson make that division of powers precise. Questions on comparing follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same notation through a calculator screen that runs out of digits, a planet's distance that will not fit on a cheque, and a comparison of grains of sand with stars in the sky — stories about numbers too big to say comfortably.

## Subtopic: The Calculator Runs Out of Room

Here is where scientific notation comes from, in a story you can test on your own calculator. Type a big number and keep multiplying by ten. Twelve. One hundred and twenty. One thousand two hundred. Keep going. At some point the screen stops showing all the digits and shows something like one comma two with a small ten and a small ten, or one comma two E ten. The calculator has run out of room and has switched to scientific notation on its own. It is telling you: here is the start of the number, and here is how many places the comma should move.

That is all the notation is. The first part, between one and ten, carries the digits that matter. The power of ten carries the size. One comma two times ten to the power ten is twelve billion — the digits one and two, with the comma moved ten places.

Think of the exponent as a zoom setting. Ten to the power three is the thousands zoom, ten to the power six is the millions zoom, ten to the power nine is the billions zoom. Two numbers on different zoom settings cannot be compared by their digits; the one on the bigger zoom is bigger, full stop. Only at the same zoom do the digits matter. That is the comparison rule from the previous section, told differently.

Now, the first factor has a job: it must be at least one and less than ten. Why that range? Because that is the range where exactly one digit sits before the comma. One digit before the comma means the position of the comma is fixed, which means every number has exactly one way of being written — and that is what lets you compare by exponent. If you allowed thirty-four times ten to the power four, or nought comma three four times ten to the power six, the same number would have many disguises and the exponent would tell you nothing.

So when you convert, check the first factor by asking: is there exactly one non-zero digit before the comma? If there are two, move the comma one more place left and add one to the exponent. If there is a zero before the comma, move it one place right and subtract one from the exponent. Questions on the calculator's trick follow.

## Subtopic: Planets, People and Grains of Sand

Let me put some real numbers into the notation. The Earth is about one hundred and fifty million kilometres from the Sun: one comma five times ten to the power eight kilometres. Neptune is about four comma five billion kilometres away: four comma five times ten to the power nine. How many times further is Neptune? The exponents differ by one, so Neptune is ten times further for every unit of the first factor, and four comma five over one comma five is three — about thirty times further. Reading the exponents makes the comparison a one-line job.

The population of South Africa is about six comma two times ten to the power seven, sixty-two million people. The world population is about eight times ten to the power nine, eight billion. South Africans are what fraction of the world? Exponents seven and nine, a gap of two, so a factor of one hundred; six comma two over eight is a bit under one. Roughly one in a hundred and thirty people on Earth is South African.

Grains of sand on all the beaches of the world are estimated at around seven comma five times ten to the power eighteen. Stars in the observable universe are estimated at around two times ten to the power twenty-two. Which is more? Twenty-two beats eighteen, so there are more stars than grains of sand — about ten thousand times more, since the exponents differ by four and the first factors are of similar size. Without scientific notation that comparison would mean counting zeros across two numbers twenty digits long.

Money works the same way. The South African government's annual budget is about two comma four times ten to the power twelve rand — two comma four trillion rand. A cheque book has no room for that many digits, which is why financial reports write it as R2,4 trillion. A learner earning two hundred rand a week would need ten to the power ten weeks — ten billion weeks — to earn it.

In each of these, the exponent does the heavy lifting: it tells you the size class, and the first factor fine-tunes within the class. Questions on real numbers follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

Here is the whole lesson as a checklist. To write a large number in scientific notation: put the comma after the first non-zero digit, count the places moved, write first factor times ten to the power of that count, and check the first factor is at least one and less than ten. To convert back: move the comma right by the exponent, filling with zeros. To compare: normalise both, then compare exponents, then first factors.

A typical question: write three hundred and seven million in scientific notation. Three zero seven zero zero zero zero zero zero, nine digits. Comma after the three: three comma zero seven. Exponent nine minus one, eight. Three comma zero seven times ten to the power eight. Keep the zero in the first factor; three comma seven would be a different number.

Another: write five comma zero two times ten to the power six as an ordinary number. Move six places: five zero two, then four zeros. Five million and twenty thousand, written five space zero two zero space zero zero zero. Seven digits, one more than the exponent — check.

Another: arrange in descending order seven times ten to the power five, six comma eight times ten to the power six, and seventy times ten to the power four. Normalise the last: seventy times ten to the power four is seven times ten to the power five, the same as the first. So the order is six comma eight times ten to the power six, then the two equal values. An examiner who includes an un-normalised number is checking that you normalise before comparing.

Errors to avoid. A first factor of ten or more, or less than one. An exponent that counts digits instead of places moved — remember it is one less than the digit count. Dropping a zero inside the first factor. Writing the calculator's E form in an answer. Comparing first factors when the exponents differ.
