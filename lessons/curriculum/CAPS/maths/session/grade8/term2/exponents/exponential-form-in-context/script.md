# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies numbers in exponential form to problems in context. The contexts are repeated doubling or tripling, areas of squares and volumes of cubes and their reverse problems, large quantities written in scientific notation, and growth by a fixed factor. In each case the problem is translated into a power or a root, the laws of exponents are used where they shorten the work, and the answer is given in the units of the situation. The anchor problems are a bacterial colony doubling from one cell to two to the power six, sixty-four cells in six hours; a cube of volume three hundred and forty-three cubic centimetres with edge seven centimetres; and a square field of area six thousand four hundred square metres with side eighty metres.

## Subtopic: Repeated Doubling and Tripling

The most common exponent context is repeated multiplication by the same factor. A bacterial colony starts with one cell and doubles every hour. After one hour, two cells. After two hours, four. After three, eight. After h hours, two to the power h cells. After six hours, two to the power six, sixty-four cells. After ten hours, two to the power ten, one thousand and twenty-four. The exponent is the number of doublings, which here equals the number of hours.

If the colony starts with more than one cell, multiply. Five cells doubling every hour: after six hours, five times two to the power six, five times sixty-four, three hundred and twenty. Write the starting amount times the growth factor to the power of the number of periods: start times factor to the power n.

Tripling works identically with base three. A chain message is sent to three people, each of whom sends it to three more the next day, and so on. On day one, three people receive it; on day two, nine; on day d, three to the power d. On day five, three to the power five, two hundred and forty-three people receive it that day. The total who have ever received it is a sum, three plus nine plus twenty-seven plus eighty-one plus two hundred and forty-three, which is three hundred and sixty-three — a sum of powers is not itself a power, as the laws lesson warned.

Watch the units of the period. If bacteria double every twenty minutes, then in two hours there are six periods, not two, and the count is two to the power six, sixty-four times the start. Convert the time to the number of doublings before you write the exponent.

The reverse question — how many hours until the colony reaches a given size — is answered by finding the exponent. When does one cell become five hundred and twelve? Two to the power nine is five hundred and twelve, so nine hours. In Grade 8 you find the exponent by listing powers, which is fast for bases two, three, five and ten. Questions on repeated doubling follow now.

## Subtopic: Areas of Squares and Volumes of Cubes

A square of side s has area s squared; a cube of edge e has volume e cubed. These are the two geometric meanings of the exponents two and three, and they generate a family of problems in both directions.

Forward: a square tile has side fifteen centimetres; its area is fifteen squared, two hundred and twenty-five square centimetres. A cubic box has edge twelve centimetres; its volume is twelve cubed, one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight cubic centimetres. Units: squaring a length gives square units, cubing gives cubic units. Write them.

Backward: a square field has area six thousand four hundred square metres; its side is the square root of six thousand four hundred. Sixty-four times one hundred, so eight times ten, eighty metres. A cube has volume three hundred and forty-three cubic centimetres; its edge is the cube root of three hundred and forty-three, seven centimetres. The root problem is the forward problem asked in reverse, and the anchor numbers of this lesson are both of this type.

Fences and paint. The field with side eighty metres needs a fence around it: the perimeter is four times eighty, three hundred and twenty metres. The cube with edge seven centimetres has six faces each of area forty-nine square centimetres, so its total surface area is two hundred and ninety-four square centimetres. Problems often chain a root with a perimeter or surface-area calculation, and the root must come first.

Fractions and decimals appear here naturally. A square with side nought comma five metres has area nought comma two five square metres. A cube with edge two thirds of a metre has volume eight twenty-sevenths of a cubic metre. A square with area one and nine sixteenths square metres has side five quarters, one and a quarter metres.

Scaling. If the side of a square is doubled, the area is multiplied by two squared, four. If the edge of a cube is tripled, the volume is multiplied by three cubed, twenty-seven. This is the product law in action: bracket two s bracket squared is four s squared. A question that doubles a side and asks about the area is testing whether you square the two. Questions on squares and cubes follow.

## Subtopic: Scientific Notation in Context

Large quantities in real problems arrive in scientific notation, and the laws of exponents let you work with them. The population of South Africa is about six comma two times ten to the power seven. If each person uses on average two hundred litres of water a day, the daily total is six comma two times ten to the power seven times two times ten squared. Multiply the first factors: twelve comma four. Add the exponents: nine. Twelve comma four times ten to the power nine — but twelve comma four is not between one and ten, so normalise: one comma two four times ten to the power ten litres. Twelve point four billion litres a day.

Division. Light travels at about three times ten to the power five kilometres per second and the Sun is one comma five times ten to the power eight kilometres away. Time is distance over speed: one comma five over three is nought comma five; ten to the power eight over ten to the power five is ten cubed. Nought comma five times ten cubed, which is five times ten squared, five hundred seconds — about eight minutes. Sunlight is eight minutes old when it reaches you.

Comparison. The Moon is about three comma eight times ten to the power five kilometres away. How many times further is the Sun? One comma five times ten to the power eight over three comma eight times ten to the power five: exponents give ten cubed, first factors give about nought comma four. About four hundred times further.

Addition in scientific notation needs matching exponents. Three times ten to the power six plus five times ten to the power five: write the second as nought comma five times ten to the power six, then add the first factors: three comma five times ten to the power six. Or convert both to ordinary numbers, three million plus five hundred thousand, three million five hundred thousand, and convert back. Never add exponents when adding numbers.

Rounding. Real data is approximate, so answers in scientific notation are usually given to two or three significant figures in the first factor. State the rounding you have used. Questions on scientific notation in context follow.

## Subtopic: Growth by a Fixed Factor and Mixed Problems

Money and populations sometimes grow by a fixed factor each period, which is the same mathematics as doubling bacteria. A savings amount that is multiplied by one comma one each year — ten percent growth — becomes, after three years, the start times one comma one cubed. One comma one cubed is one comma three three one. One thousand rand becomes one thousand three hundred and thirty-one rand. That is compound growth, which Grade 9 treats formally; here it is simply a power of a decimal.

A town's population of eight thousand triples every twenty years. After sixty years, that is three periods: eight thousand times three cubed, eight thousand times twenty-seven, two hundred and sixteen thousand. The period conversion — sixty years is three twenty-year periods — is the step most often skipped.

Halving is the reverse of doubling but still an exponent problem. A medicine dose of three hundred and twenty milligrams halves in the body every four hours. After twelve hours, three halvings: three hundred and twenty divided by two cubed, three hundred and twenty over eight, forty milligrams. Dividing by a power is the same as multiplying by the matching fraction: three hundred and twenty times one eighth.

Mixed problems combine a power with ordinary arithmetic. A square courtyard of side twelve metres is tiled with square tiles of side nought comma five metres. How many tiles? Area of courtyard: one hundred and forty-four square metres. Area of a tile: nought comma two five square metres. Tiles: one hundred and forty-four over nought comma two five, five hundred and seventy-six. Alternatively, tiles along a side: twelve over nought comma five, twenty-four; squared, five hundred and seventy-six. Both routes work; the second uses the product law — bracket twelve over nought comma five bracket squared.

Cubic packing. A crate is a cube of edge nought comma six metres; small cubic boxes have edge nought comma two metres. How many fit? Three along each edge, three cubed, twenty-seven. Or volumes: nought comma two one six over nought comma zero zero eight, twenty-seven.

In every problem the exponent is a count: of periods, of dimensions, of places the comma moved. Find the count first and the base second. Questions on growth and mixed problems follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problems as stories about a chain message, a chessboard of rice grains, and a water tank that is a perfect cube — stories where the exponent is the number of times something happened, and the root is the question asked backwards.

## Subtopic: The Chessboard of Rice

An old story. A king offers a reward to the inventor of chess, who asks only for rice: one grain on the first square of the board, two on the second, four on the third, doubling each square to the sixty-fourth. The king laughs at such a modest request. He should not have.

Square one has one grain, which is two to the power zero. Square two has two, two to the power one. Square three has four, two squared. Square n has two to the power of n minus one. Square eleven has two to the power ten, one thousand and twenty-four grains — about a cupful. Square twenty-one has two to the power twenty, which is just over a million grains, a few sacks. Square thirty-one has two to the power thirty, about a billion grains — a warehouse. Square forty-one has two to the power forty, about a trillion. By the sixty-fourth square the number is two to the power sixty-three, about nine times ten to the power eighteen grains, more rice than has been grown in the whole of human history.

The story teaches three things. First, doubling grows faster than any amount of adding: two times sixty-three is one hundred and twenty-six grains, two to the power sixty-three is a number with nineteen digits. Second, the exponent is one less than the square number, because the first square has one grain and no doublings have happened yet — the classic off-by-one, which you met in patterns last term. Third, the power of ten tells you the scale at a glance: ten to the power three is a cup, ten to the power six is sacks, ten to the power nine is a warehouse, ten to the power eighteen is impossible.

Here is the same story in a form you can use. A chain message that doubles each day, starting with one person, reaches two to the power of the day number minus one new people on each day; by day twenty-one it would reach a million people in a single day. A rumour tripling daily reaches three to the power of the day number minus one on each day. The count of periods is always the number of times the multiplying has happened, and that is one fewer than the number of squares, days or generations if the first one is counted as the start.

So when a problem says starts with and every, write down the start, the factor and the number of periods — and ask yourself whether the first period has already happened. Questions on the chessboard follow.

## Subtopic: The Cube-Shaped Water Tank

A farmer buys a water tank that is a perfect cube and holds one thousand litres. One litre is one thousand cubic centimetres, so the tank holds one million cubic centimetres, ten to the power six. How long is each edge? The cube root of ten to the power six: third the exponent, ten squared, one hundred centimetres. The tank is one metre along each edge. A thousand litres is a cubic metre — a fact worth knowing on its own.

The farmer wants a bigger tank: eight thousand litres. Eight times the volume. Does the edge become eight times longer? No. Eight times one million is eight times ten to the power six cubic centimetres; the cube root is the cube root of eight times the cube root of ten to the power six, two times one hundred, two hundred centimetres. The edge doubles when the volume goes up by eight, because two cubed is eight. To get an edge three times as long you would need twenty-seven times the volume.

Now the concrete base. The tank with edge two hundred centimetres stands on a square base of side two metres, so the base is two squared, four square metres. The farmer wants a border of paving half a metre wide all the way round: the paved square has side three metres, area nine square metres, and the paving alone is nine minus four, five square metres. Squares inside squares are a difference of two powers, and the border is never just the width times the side.

Here is the reverse problem from the shop. A square water trough is sold by its base area: two comma two five square metres. The farmer needs to know whether it fits a gap one comma six metres wide. The side is the square root of two comma two five: two hundred and twenty-five has root fifteen, two decimal places become one, one comma five metres. It fits.

Every one of these is a power or a root with units attached. The volume question is a cube, the edge question is a cube root, the base is a square, the side is a square root, and the scaling question — eight times the volume — is the product law for roots. Read the problem, decide whether you are going from side to size or size to side, and the exponent or the root follows. Questions on the tank follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for any exponent problem in context. One: identify the base — the factor by which something is multiplied, or the length being squared or cubed. Two: identify the exponent — the number of periods, counted carefully, or two for area, three for volume. Three: decide direction — forward is a power, backward is a root. Four: calculate, using laws of exponents to shorten where possible, and normalise any scientific notation. Five: write the answer with units and check its size against common sense.

A typical question: a colony of forty bacteria doubles every half hour; how many after three hours? Periods: six. Forty times two to the power six, forty times sixty-four, two thousand five hundred and sixty.

Another: a cubic box has volume one thousand seven hundred and twenty-eight cubic centimetres; find the edge and the area of one face. Edge: cube root, twelve centimetres — check twelve cubed. Face: twelve squared, one hundred and forty-four square centimetres.

Another: the Earth's mass is about six times ten to the power twenty-four kilograms and the Moon's about seven times ten to the power twenty-two; how many times heavier is the Earth? Exponents differ by two, a factor of a hundred; six over seven is about nought comma nine. About eighty-six times, or roughly ninety.

Another: a square photograph of side ten centimetres is enlarged so that each side is one comma five times longer; by what factor does the area grow? One comma five squared, two comma two five.

Errors to avoid. Counting hours instead of periods. Adding the start to the power instead of multiplying. Thinking eight times the volume means eight times the edge. Forgetting units, or giving square units for a length. Leaving scientific notation un-normalised. Adding exponents when adding numbers.
