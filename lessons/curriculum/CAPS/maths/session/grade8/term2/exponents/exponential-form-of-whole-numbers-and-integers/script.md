# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the representation of whole numbers in exponential form and extends it to integers. A power consists of a base and an exponent; the exponent counts how many times the base appears as a factor. The sign of a power with a negative base depends on whether the exponent is even or odd, and the placement of brackets determines whether the negative sign is part of the base. The anchor values are two to the power five, which is thirty-two; negative two to the power five, which is negative thirty-two; and bracket negative three bracket squared, which is nine, against negative three squared, which is negative nine.

## Subtopic: Base, Exponent and Expanded Form

A power is a compact way of writing repeated multiplication. Two to the power five means two multiplied by itself so that five factors of two appear: two times two times two times two times two. The number being multiplied, two, is the base. The small raised number, five, is the exponent or index, and it counts the factors. The value of two to the power five is thirty-two. The three forms — the power, the expanded product and the value — are three ways of writing one number, and the examiner can give you any one and ask for another.

Writing a product in exponential form means counting identical factors. Seven times seven times seven has three factors of seven, so it is seven cubed, seven to the power three. Five times five times five times five times five times five has six factors, so it is five to the power six. When the factors are not all the same, group them: three times three times five times five times five is three squared times five cubed. Do not add exponents across different bases; three squared times five cubed is not fifteen to the power five.

Expanding a power is the reverse. Four to the power three expands to four times four times four, which is sixty-four. Ten to the power four is ten times ten times ten times ten, which is ten thousand — and powers of ten are worth knowing by sight, because the exponent is simply the number of zeros. Ten to the power six is one million.

Two special exponents need care. Any number to the power one is itself: nine to the power one is nine. Any non-zero number to the power zero is one: nine to the power zero is one, and so is two hundred to the power zero. The reason for the zero rule will be proved in the laws of exponents lesson; for now, learn it as a fact, and never write that nine to the power zero is zero.

The vocabulary matters because the mark scheme uses it. Base, exponent, power, expanded form, value. A question that says write in exponential form wants the compact power; one that says write in expanded form wants the product; one that says evaluate or determine the value wants the number. Questions on the three forms follow now.

## Subtopic: Negative Bases — Even and Odd Exponents

Now the base may be a negative integer. Bracket negative two bracket to the power five means five factors of negative two multiplied together: negative two times negative two times negative two times negative two times negative two. Multiply in pairs. The first two negatives give positive four. The next two give positive four again. Four times four is sixteen, and one factor of negative two remains, so sixteen times negative two is negative thirty-two. Bracket negative two bracket to the power five is negative thirty-two.

Compare bracket negative two bracket to the power four: four factors, which pair off completely, two pairs each giving positive four, and four times four is sixteen. Positive sixteen. The pattern is general and it is the key fact of this lesson: a negative base raised to an even exponent gives a positive result, because the negatives pair off; a negative base raised to an odd exponent gives a negative result, because one unpaired negative remains.

The size of the answer is the same as for the positive base; only the sign is in question. So bracket negative three bracket cubed has size twenty-seven and, with an odd exponent, is negative twenty-seven. Bracket negative five bracket squared has size twenty-five and, with an even exponent, is positive twenty-five. Bracket negative one bracket to the power one hundred is positive one; bracket negative one bracket to the power one hundred and one is negative one.

This means the sign can be decided before any multiplying is done. Look at the exponent: even means positive, odd means negative. Then compute the size with the positive base. That two-step method avoids sign slips in long products.

A related fact: the square of any integer, positive or negative, is never negative. There is no integer whose square is negative nine, which is why the square root of a negative number has no answer in this grade. Cubes, by contrast, can be negative: negative twenty-seven is the cube of negative three. Questions on negative bases follow.

## Subtopic: Brackets Change the Base

The single most-tested idea in this topic is the difference between bracket negative three bracket squared and negative three squared, written without brackets. They look nearly identical and have different values.

Bracket negative three bracket squared: the brackets make negative three the base. Two factors of negative three: negative three times negative three is positive nine. The answer is nine.

Negative three squared, without brackets: the base is three, not negative three. The exponent applies to the three only, and the negative sign sits in front, waiting. Three squared is nine, and then the negative sign is applied: negative nine. Read it as the negative of three squared.

The rule: an exponent applies only to the symbol immediately to its left. If that symbol is a closing bracket, the exponent applies to everything inside the bracket. If it is a number, the exponent applies to that number only, and any sign in front is applied afterwards. Negative two to the power four, without brackets, is the negative of sixteen, negative sixteen; bracket negative two bracket to the power four is positive sixteen.

With odd exponents the two forms happen to agree in value: bracket negative two bracket cubed is negative eight, and negative two cubed is the negative of eight, also negative eight. They agree by accident of the odd exponent, not because they mean the same thing, and the examiner will choose an even exponent to catch the confusion.

When you write your own working, put brackets around every negative base. A negative base without brackets is either an error or an invitation to one. When you read a question, look for the brackets before you do anything else and say out loud what the base is. Questions on brackets follow.

## Subtopic: Comparing and Ordering Powers

To compare two powers, evaluate them — do not compare bases and exponents separately. Two to the power five is thirty-two and five squared is twenty-five, so two to the power five is greater than five squared even though its base is smaller. Three to the power four is eighty-one and four cubed is sixty-four, so three to the power four is the larger. Swapping base and exponent changes the value, and the version with the larger exponent usually wins once the numbers are above two.

Ordering a set of powers that includes negative bases requires the sign first. Suppose you must order bracket negative two bracket cubed, bracket negative three bracket squared, negative two to the power four and two to the power three from smallest to largest. Evaluate each: negative eight, nine, negative sixteen, eight. Negative numbers are smaller than positive ones, and among negatives the one with the larger size is smaller, so the order is negative sixteen, negative eight, eight, nine — that is, negative two to the power four, then bracket negative two bracket cubed, then two cubed, then bracket negative three bracket squared.

Equal values can hide in different forms. Two to the power six is sixty-four, four cubed is sixty-four and eight squared is sixty-four, so all three are equal. Recognising that sixty-four can be written as a power of two, four or eight is a skill that returns in the laws of exponents and in square and cube roots.

The inequality symbols must point correctly. Thirty-two is greater than twenty-five, written with the open end towards thirty-two. When a question asks you to insert the correct symbol between two powers, write both values beside them first and then choose the symbol; comparing the written values is where marks are earned and lost.

Finally, powers of ten give a sense of scale that will be needed for scientific notation in the next lesson. Ten squared is a hundred, ten cubed is a thousand, ten to the power six is a million, ten to the power nine is a billion. Each step up in the exponent multiplies by ten, which is why the exponent is sometimes called the order of magnitude. Questions on comparing powers follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same powers through folded paper, a spreading rumour and a thermometer in a freezer — stories in which repeated multiplication either explodes or flips sign, and where one pair of brackets changes the answer completely.

## Subtopic: Folding Paper — Why Powers Grow So Fast

Take a sheet of paper and fold it in half. One fold, two layers. Fold again: four layers. Again: eight. The number of layers after each fold is a power of two — two to the power of the number of folds. After five folds you have two to the power five, thirty-two layers, and the stack is already hard to fold. After ten folds you would have two to the power ten, one thousand and twenty-four layers, thicker than a book. Nobody manages more than seven or eight folds with ordinary paper, and this is why: the layers do not grow by adding two each time, they double, and doubling runs away from you.

This is the feel of exponents. Multiplying by two seven times is not the same as adding two seven times. Adding gives fourteen. Multiplying gives one hundred and twenty-eight. The difference between two times seven and two to the power seven is the difference between a short walk and a rocket.

Here is a way to check that you have the roles of base and exponent right: the base is what you do each time, and the exponent is how many times you do it. Folding paper, you double — base two — and you fold five times — exponent five. If you tripled a pile of stones five times, that would be three to the power five, which is two hundred and forty-three stones from one. The base is the action, the exponent the count.

A rumour works the same way. Suppose each person who hears a rumour tells three new people the next day. Day one, one person knows. Day two, three new people. Day three, nine new. Day four, twenty-seven. The new tellers on any day are three to the power of one less than the day number. By day seven that is three to the power six, seven hundred and twenty-nine people in a single day. A school of a thousand learners is told within a week, from one person, by threes.

And in the other direction: starting with a number and squaring, cubing and so on, you are counting how many factors of that number you have multiplied. Four cubed is three fours multiplied, sixty-four. Write the expanded form whenever you are unsure; it is the one form that can never mislead you. Questions on growth follow.

## Subtopic: The Freezer Thermometer — Negatives That Flip

Here is a picture for negative bases. Think of multiplying by a negative number as flipping a switch: the sign changes every time. Start with positive one and multiply by negative two. Flip: negative two. Multiply by negative two again. Flip back: positive four. Again: negative eight. Again: positive sixteen. Again: negative thirty-two. Every multiplication flips the sign, so after an even number of flips you are back where you started, positive, and after an odd number you are flipped, negative.

That is all the even-odd rule is. Bracket negative two bracket to the power four is four flips, back to positive, sixteen. Bracket negative two bracket to the power five is five flips, negative, thirty-two with a minus sign. You never have to multiply out a long string of negatives to find the sign; you count the flips.

Now the brackets. Imagine the thermometer outside a freezer reading negative three degrees. If someone writes negative three squared without brackets, they mean: take three, square it to get nine, and then put the minus sign back on — negative nine. If they write bracket negative three bracket squared, they mean: take the whole reading, negative three, and multiply it by itself — two flips, positive nine. The brackets say whether the minus sign is inside the squaring or outside it. Inside, it gets squared away. Outside, it survives.

A simple way to remember: brackets protect the sign. A negative sign inside brackets is part of the base and goes through the exponent; a negative sign outside brackets is not touched by the exponent and is applied last.

So when you meet a negative base in a question, do two things in order. One, find the base: is the minus inside the brackets or not? Two, if it is inside, count the flips with the exponent: even gives positive, odd gives negative. The size is just the positive power. Negative three to the power four without brackets is the negative of eighty-one, negative eighty-one; bracket negative three bracket to the power four is four flips, positive eighty-one. Questions on flipping follow.

## Subtopic: Putting It Together — Examination Technique

Let me gather the whole lesson into a method you can run on any exponent question in this section. Step one: read the question word. Exponential form means write a power. Expanded form means write the product. Evaluate means find the number. Step two: identify the base, checking the brackets. Step three: decide the sign from the exponent if the base is negative. Step four: compute the size using the positive base. Step five: if the question compares or orders, write all the values down before choosing symbols.

A typical question: write negative five times negative five times negative five in exponential form and evaluate. Three identical factors of negative five, so bracket negative five bracket cubed — with brackets, because the base is negative. Odd exponent, so negative. Size: five cubed is one hundred and twenty-five. Answer: negative one hundred and twenty-five.

Another: evaluate negative two to the power four plus bracket negative two bracket to the power four. First term, no brackets, the negative of sixteen: negative sixteen. Second term, brackets, even exponent, positive sixteen. Sum: zero. That question exists to test exactly the bracket distinction, and the answer zero is the signature that you got both halves right.

Another: which is greater, bracket negative four bracket cubed or bracket negative three bracket to the power four? Evaluate: negative sixty-four and positive eighty-one. The positive one is greater. Notice that a learner who ignored the signs would compare sixty-four with eighty-one and still get the right answer here, but would get the wrong answer if the question were bracket negative four bracket cubed against bracket negative three bracket cubed — negative sixty-four against negative twenty-seven, where the smaller size is the greater number.

Common errors to avoid. Multiplying base by exponent: two to the power five is not ten. Forgetting that the square of a negative is positive. Dropping the brackets when you copy a negative base. Writing anything to the power zero as zero. Adding exponents across different bases.

Looking ahead: the next lesson uses powers of ten to write very large numbers in scientific notation, and the lesson after that proves the laws of exponents that let you multiply and divide powers without expanding them. Grade 9 extends all of this to negative exponents and to scientific notation for very small numbers. Final questions follow.
