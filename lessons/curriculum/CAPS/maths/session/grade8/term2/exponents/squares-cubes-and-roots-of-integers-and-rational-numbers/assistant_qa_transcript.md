### subtopic_2 — Squares and Cubes of Fractions
**Assistant:** Why does squaring make a fraction smaller when it makes a whole number bigger?

**Tutor:** Because squaring means multiplying a number by itself, and multiplying by a number less than one shrinks, while multiplying by a number greater than one grows. Two thirds squared is two thirds of two thirds; taking two thirds of anything leaves less than you had, so the answer four ninths is less than two thirds. Three squared is three lots of three, which is more than three. The dividing line is one: one squared is one, numbers above one grow when squared, numbers between zero and one shrink. The same holds for cubes, only more so — two thirds cubed is eight twenty-sevenths, smaller again. The pizza picture makes it concrete: a two-by-two block of small pieces out of a three-by-three grid is clearly less than two whole strips out of three. Expect a question that tests whether you know this, usually by asking you to order a fraction, its square and its cube.

### subtopic_3 — Square Roots and Cube Roots of Fractions
**Assistant:** What if the numerator and denominator are not perfect squares — is the root impossible?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily. First simplify the fraction, because cancelling a common factor can turn both parts into perfect squares. Eighteen over fifty looks hopeless, but dividing top and bottom by two gives nine over twenty-five, whose root is three fifths. Twelve over twenty-seven simplifies to four over nine, root two thirds. Only after simplifying can you say whether the root is a nice fraction. If the simplified fraction still has a part that is not a perfect square — two over nine, say — then the root is not a fraction of whole numbers; it is what Grade 9 will call a surd, and in Grade 8 you either leave it under the root sign or estimate it, since the root of two over nine is the root of two over three, about nought comma four seven. The same advice holds for cube roots with perfect cubes, and for decimals, where converting to a fraction over ten, a hundred or a thousand shows at once whether a terminating root exists.

### subtopic_4 — Squares, Cubes and Roots of Decimals
**Assistant:** Is there a quick way to know whether a decimal has a nice square root?

**Tutor:** Count its decimal places and look at its digits. A terminating decimal has a terminating square root only if the number of decimal places is even and the digits, read as a whole number, form a perfect square. ### subtopic_6 — The Dropped Comma
**Assistant:** Why does the fraction method never drop a comma?

**Tutor:** Because it replaces the comma with a denominator that is a power of ten, and powers of ten have obvious roots. Nought comma zero nine becomes nine over one hundred; the root of nine is three and the root of a hundred is ten, so the answer is three over ten, which converts back to nought comma three with no counting of places at all. Nought comma zero zero eight becomes eight over one thousand; the cube root of eight is two and of a thousand is ten, so two tenths, nought comma two. The method also exposes impossible cases immediately: nought comma nine is nine over ten, and ten is not a perfect square, so there is no terminating root. The counting-places method is faster once you trust it, and the fraction method is the one to fall back on whenever the counting feels uncertain or the number has many zeros; doing both and comparing is the most reliable check of all.
