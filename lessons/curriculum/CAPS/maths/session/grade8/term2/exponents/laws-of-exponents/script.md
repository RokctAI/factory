# Part 1 — Expert

This session establishes the general laws of exponents from the meaning of a power as a repeated product. The laws are: a to the m times a to the n equals a to the m plus n; a to the m divided by a to the n equals a to the m minus n; bracket a times t bracket to the n equals a to the n times t to the n; and a to the zero equals one for non-zero a. Each law is derived by writing the powers in expanded form and counting factors, and each is then applied to numerical and simple algebraic examples. The anchor examples are two cubed times two to the power four, which is two to the power seven; five to the power six over five squared, which is five to the power four; and bracket two times three bracket to the power four, which is two to the power four times three to the power four.

## Subtopic: Multiplying Powers of the Same Base

Start with two cubed times two to the power four. Expand both. Two cubed is two times two times two: three factors. Two to the power four is two times two times two times two: four factors. Multiplying them gives a string of three plus four, seven factors of two, which is two to the power seven. In numbers: eight times sixteen is one hundred and twenty-eight, and two to the power seven is one hundred and twenty-eight. The expansion and the arithmetic agree.

The general law: a to the m times a to the n equals a to the m plus n. The reason is that m factors of a followed by n factors of a is m plus n factors of a. The law is a statement about counting factors, which is why it needs the same base on both sides — you cannot count apples and pears together.

Apply it. Five squared times five to the power five is five to the power seven. Ten cubed times ten to the power four is ten to the power seven, which is ten million — a fact you used when multiplying by powers of ten in scientific notation. x to the power two times x to the power three is x to the power five, the same law with a letter base. Three to the power four times three, where the lone three is three to the power one, is three to the power five.

The trap is to multiply the bases or the exponents. Two cubed times two to the power four is not four to the power seven, and it is not two to the power twelve. Adding exponents is the whole law; the base stays as it is.

Different bases cannot be combined by this law. Two cubed times three squared stays as it is or is evaluated: eight times nine is seventy-two. There is no exponent shortcut because there is no common base. Equally, two cubed plus two to the power four cannot be simplified by adding exponents — the law is about multiplication, not addition. Eight plus sixteen is twenty-four, which is not a power of two at all. Questions on the first law follow now.

## Subtopic: Dividing Powers of the Same Base

Now five to the power six divided by five squared. Expand: six factors of five on top, two factors of five underneath. Each five underneath cancels with a five on top — five over five is one — and two cancellations remove two factors from the top. Six minus two, four factors remain: five to the power four. Check with values: fifteen thousand six hundred and twenty-five divided by twenty-five is six hundred and twenty-five, and five to the power four is six hundred and twenty-five.

The general law: a to the m divided by a to the n equals a to the m minus n. Dividing removes factors, so the exponents subtract. The order matters: the exponent on top minus the exponent underneath. Three to the power seven over three to the power three is three to the power four, eighty-one. Ten to the power nine over ten to the power seven is ten squared, one hundred — which is the division you did roughly in scientific notation last lesson, now made exact.

With letters: y to the power eight divided by y to the power five is y cubed. Seven to the power five over seven, with the lone seven as seven to the power one, is seven to the power four.

The same cautions apply as for multiplication. The bases must be the same; six to the power four over two to the power four cannot be simplified by subtracting exponents to six to the power zero or two to the power zero. Evaluate instead, or use the product law from the next section. And the law is for division only: five to the power six minus five squared is fifteen thousand six hundred and twenty-five minus twenty-five, fifteen thousand six hundred, which is not a power of five.

A question of form: what happens when the exponent underneath is larger? Two cubed over two to the power five would give two to the power negative two by the law. Negative exponents are Grade 9 work; in Grade 8 you write the answer as one over two squared, one quarter, by cancelling three factors and leaving two underneath. Questions on dividing powers follow.

## Subtopic: A Power of a Product

The third law concerns a product raised to a power. Bracket two times three bracket to the power four means four factors of the product two times three: two times three, times two times three, times two times three, times two times three. Multiplication can be done in any order, so collect the twos together and the threes together: four twos and four threes. Two to the power four times three to the power four. Check with values: two times three is six, six to the power four is one thousand two hundred and ninety-six; two to the power four is sixteen, three to the power four is eighty-one, sixteen times eighty-one is one thousand two hundred and ninety-six.

The general law: bracket a times t bracket to the n equals a to the n times t to the n. The exponent distributes over every factor inside the bracket. With letters: bracket two x bracket cubed is two cubed times x cubed, which is eight x cubed. Bracket three a b bracket squared is nine a squared b squared.

The most common error is to apply the exponent to the letter and forget the number: writing bracket two x bracket cubed as two x cubed. The two is a factor inside the bracket and must be cubed too. Eight x cubed, not two x cubed. Test with x equal to one: bracket two bracket cubed is eight, and two x cubed with x one is two. Different.

The law does not apply to a sum inside the bracket. Bracket two plus three bracket squared is five squared, twenty-five; two squared plus three squared is four plus nine, thirteen. Not equal. The exponent distributes over multiplication, never over addition. This is a Grade 9 topic in its own right — expanding bracket a plus b bracket squared — but the warning is needed now.

Used backwards, the law lets you combine different bases with the same exponent. Two to the power four times five to the power four is bracket two times five bracket to the power four, ten to the power four, ten thousand. That is the escape route for the different-bases case that the first two laws could not handle, provided the exponents match. Questions on the product law follow.

## Subtopic: The Zero Exponent and Combining the Laws

The fourth law: any non-zero number to the power zero is one. Here is the proof using the division law. Five cubed divided by five cubed is one hundred and twenty-five over one hundred and twenty-five, which is one — anything divided by itself. But by the division law, five cubed over five cubed is five to the power three minus three, five to the power zero. Both are the same quantity, so five to the power zero is one. Nothing special about five: a to the m over a to the m is one and is also a to the zero, so a to the zero is one for any non-zero a.

The pattern argument from the first lesson says the same thing. Two cubed is eight, two squared is four, two to the power one is two, each step dividing by two, so two to the power zero is one. Why non-zero? Zero to the power zero would involve dividing zero by zero, which is undefined; the law excludes it.

Watch the scope of the exponent. Three x to the power zero, with the exponent on x only, is three times one, three. Bracket three x bracket to the power zero is one, because the whole product is raised to zero. Negative five to the power zero, brackets around the negative five, is one. Negative five to the power zero without brackets is the negative of one, negative one — the exponent applies to the five only.

Now combine the laws. Simplify two to the power five times two cubed over two to the power six. Top: five plus three, two to the power eight. Then divide: eight minus six, two squared, four. Simplify bracket three squared bracket times three to the power four over three to the power six: two plus four is six on top, six minus six is zero, three to the power zero, one. Simplify bracket two a bracket cubed times a squared: eight a cubed times a squared is eight a to the power five.

Order of operations still applies. Powers are evaluated before multiplication and division, and brackets first of all. When a question mixes the laws, write each step on its own line and name the law in your head: multiply, add; divide, subtract; product, distribute; zero, one. Questions on the zero law and combinations follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four laws as stories about stacking boxes, sharing out a warehouse and photocopying a photocopy — where counting the factors instead of multiplying them out is the shortcut that makes big numbers manageable.

## Subtopic: Stacking Boxes — Why Exponents Add

Think of a power as a stack of boxes, each box a factor. Two cubed is a stack of three boxes, each labelled two. Two to the power four is a stack of four. Multiply the two powers and you are putting one stack on top of the other: a stack of seven boxes. That is the first law. You add the number of boxes. You do not change what is written on them — the base stays two — and you certainly do not multiply the heights.

Dividing is taking boxes away. Five to the power six is a stack of six; dividing by five squared removes two boxes from the stack, because each five underneath cancels a five on top. Four boxes remain. Six minus two. That is the second law, and the picture tells you the order: boxes on top minus boxes taken away.

The reason the bases must match is now obvious. A stack of twos and a stack of threes cannot be combined into a single stack with one label. Two cubed times three squared is just two stacks standing side by side — eight times nine, seventy-two if you want the number, but no single power.

Here is why adding powers does not add exponents. Two cubed plus two to the power four is a stack of three next to a stack of four, and you are asked how much the two stacks are worth together. Eight plus sixteen is twenty-four. There is no way to push the two stacks into one neat stack of twos, because twenty-four is not a power of two. Addition and the exponent laws simply do not talk to each other.

A shortcut that comes free with the picture: a power raised to a power. Bracket two cubed bracket squared is two stacks of three, which is a stack of six — three times two. That is not one of the four laws on your Grade 8 list, but the picture gives it to you, and Grade 9 names it. For now, if you meet it, expand: bracket two cubed bracket squared is two cubed times two cubed, and the first law gives two to the power six. Questions on stacking follow.

## Subtopic: Photocopying a Photocopy — The Product Law and Zero

A photocopier is set to enlarge by two in width and by three in height. Put a stamp through it and the area grows by two times three, six. Put the copy through again: another six. Four times through the machine, the area has grown by six to the power four. But you could also think of it as: the width was doubled four times, two to the power four, and the height was tripled four times, three to the power four. Six to the power four is two to the power four times three to the power four. The exponent is shared out over each factor in the product. That is the product law.

Now a stamp with a number on it, say two x — a two and an x multiplied. Cube it: both the two and the x get cubed. Eight x cubed. A learner who writes two x cubed has cubed the x and forgotten the two went through the copier too.

What the copier will not do is share an exponent over a plus sign. A stamp showing two plus three — that is five — enlarged squared becomes twenty-five. If you enlarge the two squared and the three squared separately you get four and nine, thirteen, which is not twenty-five. The exponent only distributes over multiplication.

And zero. Put the stamp through the copier zero times. It is unchanged — the area is multiplied by one. That is what six to the power zero means: no enlargement at all, a factor of one. Not a factor of zero, which would shrink the stamp to nothing. Anything to the power zero is one because doing something zero times leaves things as they are, and the thing that leaves a product as it is, is one.

Put the pieces together on an exam-style line: simplify bracket three b bracket squared times b to the power zero over three. Product law: nine b squared. b to the zero: one. So nine b squared over three, which is three b squared. Three laws, three lines, one answer. Questions on the copier follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

Here are the four laws as a card. Same base, multiply: add the exponents. Same base, divide: subtract, top minus bottom. Product in a bracket, raised to a power: give the exponent to every factor. Any non-zero base to the power zero: one. Each law comes from counting factors in expanded form, and if you ever doubt a law, expand a small case and count.

A typical question: simplify three to the power five times three squared over three to the power four and give the value. Add: five plus two, seven. Subtract: seven minus four, three. Three cubed, twenty-seven. Write the exponent form and the value; questions often ask for both.

Another: simplify bracket four x bracket squared times x cubed. Product law: sixteen x squared. Multiply same base: two plus three, five. Sixteen x to the power five.

Another: simplify twelve a to the power six over four a squared. Divide the numbers: twelve over four, three. Subtract the exponents: six minus two, four. Three a to the power four. Numbers and letters are handled separately, the numbers by ordinary division, the letters by the law.

Another: evaluate bracket two to the power six times two to the power four bracket to the power zero. Whatever is inside, the whole bracket to the zero is one. Do not waste time simplifying inside.

Errors to avoid. Multiplying bases when adding exponents: two cubed times two to the power four is not four to the power seven. Adding exponents under addition: two cubed plus two to the power four is twenty-four. Forgetting the number inside a bracket: bracket two x bracket cubed is eight x cubed. Distributing an exponent over a plus. Saying anything to the power zero is zero. Combining different bases by adding exponents.
