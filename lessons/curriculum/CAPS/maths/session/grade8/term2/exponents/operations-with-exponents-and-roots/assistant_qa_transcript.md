### subtopic_1 — Roots Undo Powers
**Assistant:** Why does the square root of a negative number have no answer, when the cube root of a negative does?

**Tutor:** Because squaring any number, positive or negative, gives a non-negative result, so there is nothing to undo when you ask for the square root of a negative. Four squared is sixteen and negative four squared is also sixteen; no number squared gives negative sixteen, and so the square root of negative sixteen has no answer among the numbers you know. Cubing is different: a negative number cubed stays negative, because three negative factors leave one unpaired negative. Negative two cubed is negative eight, so the cube root of negative eight is negative two, and every negative number has a cube root. This is the even-odd rule from the first lesson appearing in reverse — even powers lose the sign, odd powers keep it — and it means that square roots of negatives are off the table in Grade 8 and Grade 9, while cube roots of negatives are ordinary.

### subtopic_2 — The Root of a Product and the Root of a Sum
**Assistant:** If roots split over products, why can I not split them over sums?

**Tutor:** Because a root is really a power, and powers distribute over multiplication but not over addition. The square root of a times b is the square root of a times the square root of b for the same reason that bracket a times b bracket squared is a squared times b squared: factors can be regrouped, so the root can be taken factor by factor. A sum cannot be regrouped that way — bracket a plus b bracket squared has cross terms, and the square root of a plus b likewise has no simple pieces. The numbers show it: the square root of nine plus sixteen is five, while three plus four is seven, and on a tiled floor you can see why, because a nine-tile square and a sixteen-tile square rearrange into a twenty-five-tile square whose side is five, not seven. The safe habit is to read the root sign as a bracket: whatever is under it is worked out completely before the root is taken, and only if what is under it is a pure product may you split.

### subtopic_3 — Roots of Powers
**Assistant:** How do I know whether to halve or third an exponent when taking a root?

**Tutor:** Look at the root. A square root undoes squaring, and squaring a power doubles its exponent, so a square root halves the exponent: the square root of two to the power six is two cubed because two cubed times two cubed is two to the power six. A cube root undoes cubing, and cubing a power triples its exponent, so a cube root divides the exponent by three: the cube root of two to the power six is two squared because two squared times two squared times two squared is two to the power six. The check is to raise your answer back to the power and see whether you recover the original exponent. If an exponent does not divide evenly — the square root of two to the power five, say — the result is not a whole power, and in Grade 8 you leave it or estimate it. With a coefficient, treat the number and the letter separately: the square root of nine x to the power four is three x squared, because the root of nine is three and half of four is two.

### subtopic_6 — The Cashier Who Added First
**Assistant:** What is the single fastest way to avoid order-of-operations mistakes on a long line?

**Tutor:** Rewrite the line once with every power and every root replaced by its value, before you add, subtract, multiply or divide anything. Two cubed plus the square root of one hundred times three becomes eight plus ten times three, and from there it is hard to go wrong: ten times three is thirty, plus eight is thirty-eight. The rewrite forces you to look at what each root sign actually covers, so the square root of nine plus sixteen becomes the square root of twenty-five, five, rather than three plus four. It also exposes a fraction bar as a bracket for the top and the bottom, so the cube root of one hundred and twenty-five minus the square root of sixteen, all over two squared, becomes one over four. Markers award method marks for exactly that line, which means the habit earns credit even when a later slip spoils the final number.
