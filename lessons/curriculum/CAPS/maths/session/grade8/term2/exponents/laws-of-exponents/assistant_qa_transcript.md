### subtopic_1 — Multiplying Powers of the Same Base
**Assistant:** Why do the bases have to be the same before I can add exponents?

**Tutor:** Because an exponent counts factors of one particular base, and adding exponents only makes sense when the factors you are counting are all the same thing. Two cubed times two to the power four is seven factors of two, so the count is seven and the base is still two. Two cubed times three squared is three factors of two and two factors of three; there is no single base those five factors share, so there is no single power to write. You can still evaluate it — eight times nine is seventy-two — but you cannot simplify it with the exponent law. The one exception that looks like combining different bases is the product law used backwards: two to the power four times five to the power four has matching exponents, so it can be written as bracket two times five bracket to the power four, ten to the power four. There you are not adding exponents; you are collecting matching factors into a product.

### subtopic_2 — Dividing Powers of the Same Base
**Assistant:** What happens if the exponent underneath is bigger than the one on top?

**Tutor:** The law still works and gives a negative exponent, which is Grade 9 territory, so in Grade 8 you handle it by cancelling and leaving the remaining factors underneath. Two cubed over two to the power five: write out three twos on top and five underneath, cancel three pairs, and two factors of two remain in the denominator, giving one over two squared, which is one quarter. The division law would give two to the power three minus five, two to the power negative two, and in Grade 9 you will learn that a negative exponent means exactly this — one over the positive power — so the two answers are the same thing in different notation. For now, write the fraction form. The important habit is the order of subtraction: top minus bottom. If the result would be negative, that is your signal that the answer is a fraction less than one.

### subtopic_3 — A Power of a Product
**Assistant:** Why does the exponent distribute over multiplication but not over addition?

**Tutor:** Because a power of a product can be rearranged into separate powers using the fact that multiplication can be done in any order, and there is no matching fact for a mixture of addition and multiplication. Bracket two times three bracket to the power four is four copies of two times three; since factors can be regrouped freely, you can gather the four twos and the four threes separately and get two to the power four times three to the power four. Bracket two plus three bracket squared is bracket two plus three bracket times bracket two plus three bracket, and multiplying that out gives four terms — two times two, two times three, three times two, three times three — not just two squared and three squared. The two middle terms, twelve in total, are the gap between twenty-five and thirteen. Grade 9 studies that expansion in detail; in Grade 8 it is enough to know that an exponent on a sum must be worked out by evaluating the sum first.

### subtopic_5 — Stacking Boxes — Why Exponents Add
**Assistant:** Is raising a power to a power one of the Grade 8 laws, and how do I deal with it?

**Tutor:** It is not on the Grade 8 list of four laws, but it falls out of the first law, so you can handle it if it appears. Bracket two cubed bracket squared means two cubed times two cubed, which by the multiplication law is two to the power three plus three, two to the power six. In the stacking picture, it is two stacks of three boxes, which is a stack of six — three times two. Grade 9 names this the power-of-a-power law and states it as a to the m, all to the n, equals a to the m times n. In Grade 8, write the expansion step explicitly: bracket two cubed bracket squared is two cubed times two cubed is two to the power six. That earns the marks and shows the examiner you understand where the law comes from rather than quoting something not yet in the syllabus.
