# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the recognition and use of the laws of operations with exponents and with square and cube roots. Roots undo powers: the square root of a squared number returns the original non-negative number and the cube root of a cubed number returns the original number. The root of a product is the product of the roots, roots of powers can be found by halving or thirding the exponent, and the order of operations places powers and roots at the same level, above multiplication and division. The anchor calculations are the square root of sixty-four plus the cube root of twenty-seven, which is eleven; the square root of four times nine, which is six by either method; and the square root of two to the power six, which is two cubed, eight.

## Subtopic: Roots Undo Powers

A square root undoes a square. Eight squared is sixty-four, so the square root of sixty-four is eight. A cube root undoes a cube. Three cubed is twenty-seven, so the cube root of twenty-seven is three. The root and the power are inverse operations in the same way that subtraction undoes addition and division undoes multiplication: apply one and then the other, and you are back where you started.

The square root of a squared number returns the original number, provided the original was not negative. The square root of five squared is five. The cube root of a cubed number returns the original number with no condition: the cube root of bracket negative four bracket cubed is negative four, because negative sixty-four has cube root negative four.

The square root of a negative number has no answer in this grade. There is no integer, and no fraction or decimal either, whose square is negative sixteen, because squaring never produces a negative. The cube root of a negative number does exist: the cube root of negative eight is negative two. This asymmetry comes straight from the even-odd rule of the first lesson.

The perfect squares and cubes to know on sight: squares one, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-five, thirty-six, forty-nine, sixty-four, eighty-one, one hundred, one hundred and twenty-one, one hundred and forty-four, one hundred and sixty-nine, one hundred and ninety-six, two hundred and twenty-five; cubes one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and sixteen, three hundred and forty-three, five hundred and twelve, seven hundred and twenty-nine, one thousand. Sixty-four is both a square and a cube, which makes it a favourite in questions.

In symbols, the radical sign with no small number means square root, and a small three in the hook means cube root. Write the small three clearly; a cube root written as a square root loses the mark. Questions on roots as inverses follow now.

## Subtopic: The Root of a Product and the Root of a Sum

The square root of a product is the product of the square roots. The square root of four times nine: the product is thirty-six, whose square root is six. Or take the roots separately: the square root of four is two, of nine is three, two times three is six. Same answer, and the law holds in general: the square root of a times b equals the square root of a times the square root of b. It is the product law of exponents in disguise — a root is a kind of power, and powers distribute over products.

The same holds for cube roots: the cube root of eight times twenty-seven is the cube root of two hundred and sixteen, six, and also two times three, six. And for quotients: the square root of thirty-six over four is the square root of nine, three, and also six over two, three.

Now the warning that is worth a mark in every examination. The square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots. The square root of nine plus sixteen: add first, twenty-five, square root five. The sum of the roots: three plus four, seven. Five is not seven. The root does not distribute over a plus sign, for exactly the same reason an exponent does not. Nor over a minus sign: the square root of twenty-five minus sixteen is the square root of nine, three; five minus four is one.

The practical rule: under a root sign, treat everything as if it were in brackets. Work out the whole quantity under the root first, then take the root. The root sign is a grouping symbol as well as an operation.

The product law is useful in reverse for roots of numbers you do not know by heart. The square root of one thousand six hundred: that is sixteen times one hundred, so four times ten, forty. The square root of two hundred and twenty-five: nine times twenty-five, so three times five, fifteen. The cube root of eight thousand: eight times one thousand, two times ten, twenty. Breaking a number into a product of known squares or cubes turns an unfamiliar root into a familiar one. Questions on roots of products and sums follow.

## Subtopic: Roots of Powers

A power with an even exponent is a perfect square, and its square root is found by halving the exponent. The square root of two to the power six: two to the power six is sixty-four, whose root is eight, and eight is two cubed. Halve the exponent: six over two, three. The square root of two to the power six is two cubed. In general, the square root of a to the power two n is a to the power n, because a to the n times a to the n is a to the power two n by the multiplication law.

Likewise a power with an exponent divisible by three is a perfect cube, and its cube root is found by dividing the exponent by three. The cube root of two to the power six: six over three, two; two squared is four. Check: four cubed is sixty-four. The cube root of five to the power nine is five cubed.

With letters, the same. The square root of x to the power eight is x to the power four. The cube root of y to the power twelve is y to the power four. The square root of x to the power ten is x to the power five. If the exponent does not divide, the root is not a whole power — the square root of two to the power five is not a whole number, and in Grade 8 you leave such roots alone or estimate them.

Combine with the product law for roots. The square root of nine x to the power four: the square root of nine is three, the square root of x to the power four is x squared, so three x squared. The cube root of twenty-seven a to the power six: three a squared. The square root of sixteen times two to the power four: four times two squared, four times four, sixteen.

A check for every root of a power: raise the answer back to the power and confirm you recover the original. Three x squared, squared, is nine x to the power four. Three a squared, cubed, is twenty-seven a to the power six. The check is quick and catches halved exponents that should have been thirded. Questions on roots of powers follow.

## Subtopic: Order of Operations with Powers and Roots

Powers and roots sit at the same level in the order of operations: after brackets, before multiplication and division, before addition and subtraction. Remember it as brackets, then exponents and roots, then multiply and divide left to right, then add and subtract left to right. A root sign also acts as a bracket around whatever is beneath it.

Evaluate the square root of sixty-four plus the cube root of twenty-seven. Roots first: eight and three. Then add: eleven. The anchor. There are no brackets grouping the sixty-four with the twenty-seven, so the roots are taken separately and then added — correctly, this time, because the roots are not of a sum.

Evaluate two cubed plus the square root of one hundred times three. Power and root first: eight and ten. Then multiply: ten times three, thirty. Then add: eight plus thirty, thirty-eight. A learner who adds eight and ten first gets eighteen times three, fifty-four, which is wrong because addition waits for multiplication.

Evaluate the square root of nine plus sixteen, minus two squared. Under the root is a bracket: nine plus sixteen, twenty-five, root five. Two squared is four. Five minus four is one.

Evaluate bracket two plus three bracket squared minus two squared minus three squared. Bracket first: five, squared is twenty-five. Then four and nine. Twenty-five minus four minus nine, left to right: twenty-one, then twelve. Twelve is the gap between twenty-five and thirteen that the previous lesson warned about.

Evaluate the cube root of one hundred and twenty-five minus the square root of sixteen, all divided by two squared. The top: five minus four, one. The bottom: four. One over four, a quarter. A fraction bar groups its top and its bottom like brackets.

When a question is long, rewrite it with the powers and roots replaced by their values before doing any adding or multiplying. That one rewrite line removes most order-of-operations errors, and it is the line the marker reads to award method marks. Questions on order of operations follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same operations as stories about tiling a square floor, cutting a cube of cheese, and a cashier who adds before taking a root — stories in which doing the operations in the wrong order gives an answer that is wrong by exactly the amount an examiner is looking for.

## Subtopic: Tiling the Floor — Roots as Side Lengths

Here is a square root you can stand on. A square room is tiled with sixty-four identical square tiles. How many tiles along one wall? The room is a square, so the tiles form a square grid, and a square grid of sixty-four has eight along each side, because eight times eight is sixty-four. The square root of the area in tiles is the side in tiles. That is what the square root means in the world: the side of a square with a given area.

Now a bigger room, with one thousand six hundred tiles. You may not know its root by heart, but you can factor: one thousand six hundred is sixteen times one hundred. A sixteen-tile square has side four and a hundred-tile square has side ten, so the side of the big room is the square root of sixteen times the square root of one hundred, four times ten, forty tiles. Forty times forty is one thousand six hundred, so the room is forty tiles along each wall. The product law for roots is just breaking a big square into a grid of smaller known squares.

What does not work: adding rooms. One room of nine tiles has side three; another of sixteen has side four. Push the two rooms together and you have twenty-five tiles, which happens to be a perfect square with side five — but five is not three plus four. The side of the combined room is not the sum of the sides, because the tiles rearrange into a new square. The square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots, and here it is on the floor.

Cubes do the same in three dimensions. A cube of cheese is cut into twenty-seven identical small cubes. How many along an edge? Three, because three cubed is twenty-seven. The cube root of the volume in small cubes is the edge in small cubes. A block of one thousand small cubes has edge ten; a block of eight thousand has edge twenty, since eight thousand is eight times one thousand, and two times ten is twenty.

So when a root appears in a problem, ask: what square or cube is this the side of? The question usually answers itself. Questions on floors and cheese follow.

## Subtopic: The Cashier Who Added First

A cashier is working out a strange bill. The formula on the till says: square root of sixty-four plus the cube root of twenty-seven. She reads it left to right as a sentence, adds sixty-four and twenty-seven to get ninety-one, and starts looking for the square root of ninety-one. There is no whole number whose square is ninety-one, and she is stuck. The problem is that she added before taking roots. Each root belongs to its own number: the square root of sixty-four is eight, the cube root of twenty-seven is three, and the bill is eleven.

Now flip the situation. The till says: square root of, bracket, nine plus sixteen, bracket. Here the bracket — or the root sign stretching over both numbers — says add first. Twenty-five, root five. If she takes roots first, three and four, and adds, she gets seven. Wrong by two. The root sign is a bracket; what is under it is done first.

The two mistakes are mirror images. Adding before separate roots, and taking separate roots before a shared one. The cure is the same: look at exactly what sits under each root sign. If a root sign covers one number, take that root alone. If it covers a sum, do the sum.

Next the till says: two cubed plus square root of one hundred, times three. She works out eight and ten, adds them to get eighteen, multiplies by three, fifty-four. Wrong. Multiplication comes before addition, so ten times three is thirty first, and then eight plus thirty is thirty-eight. Powers and roots first, then multiplying, then adding — every time, no matter how the line reads as a sentence.

Finally the till says: the cube root of one hundred and twenty-five minus the square root of sixteen, all over two squared. The fraction bar is a bracket for the top and a bracket for the bottom. Top: five minus four, one. Bottom: four. A quarter.

If you ever feel the order slipping, do what a careful cashier would do: rewrite the line with every power and root replaced by its value, then work the plain arithmetic. Eight plus three. Five minus four. Eight plus ten times three. Those lines are hard to get wrong. Questions on the cashier's bills follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for any expression mixing exponents and roots. One: identify what each root sign covers; a root over a sum means add first. Two: evaluate every power and every root, writing the values in place. Three: multiply and divide left to right. Four: add and subtract left to right. Five: check by estimation that the answer is sensible.

A typical question: evaluate the square root of eighty-one minus the cube root of sixty-four, plus two to the power four. Nine minus four plus sixteen: five plus sixteen, twenty-one.

Another: simplify the square root of twenty-five x to the power six. Root of twenty-five is five; root of x to the power six is x cubed. Five x cubed. Check: five x cubed squared is twenty-five x to the power six.

Another: evaluate the square root of bracket three squared plus four squared bracket. Inside: nine plus sixteen, twenty-five. Root: five. This is the Pythagoras calculation you will meet in Term 4, and the three-four-five triangle is its most famous instance.

Another: the square root of sixteen times the square root of nine, versus the square root of sixteen plus nine. The first is four times three, twelve, which is also the square root of one hundred and forty-four. The second is the square root of twenty-five, five. Products split; sums do not.

Another: evaluate the cube root of two to the power nine. Nine over three is three, so two cubed, eight. Or evaluate: two to the power nine is five hundred and twelve, whose cube root is eight. Either route earns the marks; the exponent route is faster.

Errors to avoid. Splitting a root over a plus or a minus. Adding before multiplying. Treating the square root of a negative as a negative number. Halving an exponent for a cube root. Writing a cube root sign without its small three. Forgetting that the fraction bar groups.
