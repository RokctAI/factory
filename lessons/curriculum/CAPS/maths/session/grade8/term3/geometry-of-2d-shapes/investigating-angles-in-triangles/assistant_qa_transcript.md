### subtopic_1 — Constructing Triangles and Measuring the Angle Sum
**Assistant:** If my constructed triangle's angles add to 178 or 182, have I disproved the fact?

**Tutor:** No; you have measured the limits of a school protractor. Each angle reading can be out by a degree or so — the centre slightly off the vertex, the baseline not quite along the side, the scale read a little high or low — and three readings can accumulate an error of two or three degrees either way. A total of 178 or 182 is exactly what careful measurement of a real triangle produces. Thirty learners' totals will cluster around 180, some above, some below, which is itself evidence that 180 is the true value and the scatter is error. That is also why measurement can only suggest the fact and reasoning is needed to establish it: the parallel line through a vertex shows the three angles fill a straight angle exactly, for every triangle, with no protractor involved. When you report an investigation, give your measured total honestly, state the likely measurement error, and then state the exact fact with its explanation. The investigation and the proof do different jobs, and both belong in the write-up.

### subtopic_3 — Sides and Base Angles of an Isosceles Triangle
**Assistant:** An isosceles triangle has an angle of 50 degrees. Why are there two possible answers?

**Tutor:** Because the 50 could be the apex angle or one of the base angles, and the two roles give different triangles. If the 50 is the apex — the angle between the two equal sides — then the base angles share the remaining 130 equally, 65 each, giving angles 50, 65, 65. If the 50 is a base angle, then the other base angle is also 50 and the apex is 180 minus 100, which is 80, giving angles 50, 50, 80. Both are genuine isosceles triangles. The diagram decides: the dashes mark the equal sides, and the apex is the vertex where they meet; if the given 50 sits there it is the apex, otherwise it is a base angle. When a question gives only the words — an isosceles triangle has an angle of 50 — and no diagram, the complete answer presents both cases. The only time a single case is forced is when the given angle is 90 or more, because two base angles of that size would already use up the whole 180, so an obtuse or right angle in an isosceles triangle must be the apex.

### subtopic_4 — Calculating Angles in Triangles with Reasons
**Assistant:** In an algebra problem, how do I know whether to include the isosceles equality or just the sum?

**Tutor:** Look at the dashes and arcs. If the triangle is marked isosceles — two sides dashed — then two of its angles are equal, and that equality is a second fact you may need, either to set up the equation or to check the answer. Often the problem builds the equality in already, by labelling both base angles with the same expression, such as 2x plus 5 each, so the sum equation alone is enough: add the three expressions and set them equal to 180. Sometimes the base angles carry different expressions, such as 3x minus 10 and x plus 30, and then the equality itself is the equation to solve — set them equal, find x — and the sum gives the apex afterwards. If the triangle is not marked isosceles, do not assume any equality; use only the sum. After solving, substitute back and look at the three angles: if two came out equal, the triangle is isosceles whether or not the question said so, and you may be asked to classify it. The marks tell you the facts available; the expressions tell you which fact makes the useful equation.

### subtopic_5 — The Torn Corners
**Assistant:** The torn-corner demonstration is convincing. Why is it not accepted as a proof?

**Tutor:** Because it shows the fact for the thirty triangles in that room, not for the infinitely many triangles nobody has drawn, and because fitting paper pieces together by eye cannot distinguish 180 from 179,5. A proof must show that the conclusion follows for every triangle from facts already established, with no measurement at all. The parallel-line argument does that: for any triangle, a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side exists; the alternate angles are equal because the lines are parallel, a fact already known; and angles along a straight line add to 180, also already known. Nothing in the argument depends on the particular triangle, so it holds for all of them at once. The torn corners are still valuable: they make the fact believable before it is proved, they suggest what the proof should do — move the base angles up to the apex — and they give the proof a picture to hang on. Mathematics usually works this way: investigate until a pattern is clear, then prove the pattern holds in general.
