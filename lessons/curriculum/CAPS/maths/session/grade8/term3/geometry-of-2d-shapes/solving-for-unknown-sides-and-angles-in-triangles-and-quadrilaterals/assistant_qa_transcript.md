### subtopic_1 — Unknown Angles in Triangles from Several Facts
**Assistant:** When a triangle's angle comes out as a decimal like 46,67, have I made a mistake?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily. Angle problems with algebraic unknowns are set up so that the equation is linear, and a linear equation can have any rational solution; 3x equals 140 gives x equal to 140 over 3, which is 46,67 to two decimal places, and the triangle with angles 40, 46,67 and 93,33 is a perfectly good triangle. Textbook problems usually choose numbers that give whole-number answers, so a decimal is worth a second look at the equation — check that you used 180 and not 360, that every angle was included, and that the expressions were copied correctly — but if the setup is right, the decimal stands. Report it to a sensible precision, usually one or two decimal places, or leave it as the exact fraction 140 over 3 if the question allows. Then check by adding: 40 plus 46,67 plus 93,33 is 180 to the rounding. What would signal an error is an angle that is negative, zero or over 180, or a side length that is negative; those mean the wrong fact or the wrong equation, not an unusual answer.

### subtopic_2 — Unknown Sides from Definitions and Perimeters
**Assistant:** Why do I write the perimeter of a rectangle as 2 times the sum rather than adding four sides?

**Tutor:** Both are correct, and the bracketed form is just the four-side sum with the equal opposite sides collected: length plus width plus length plus width is 2 times the quantity length plus width. Writing the factor of 2 outside a bracket keeps the algebra short — one bracket to simplify instead of four terms — and makes the next step natural, dividing both sides by 2 before dealing with x. It also reminds you to use the rectangle's defining property, equal opposite sides, which is the fact that justifies the shortcut; for a general quadrilateral you have no such shortcut and must add all four sides. The same idea gives a square's perimeter as 4 times the side, a rhombus's as 4 times the side, and a kite's as 2 times the sum of one short and one long side. Use whichever form you can write without error, but always make sure every side is counted exactly once; the most common slip is to add only the two different sides and forget that each appears twice.

### subtopic_3 — Unknown Angles in Quadrilaterals and Composite Figures
**Assistant:** In a composite figure, how do I know whether to add or subtract the angles at a shared vertex?

**Tutor:** Look at where the two shapes lie relative to the shared side. If they lie on opposite sides of it — the equilateral triangle sitting outside the square on its top edge — then at a shared vertex the two shapes' angles sit next to each other and the outline angle is their sum: 90 plus 60 gives 150. If one shape lies inside the other — the triangle drawn inside the square on the same edge — then the smaller angle sits inside the larger and the remaining angle is the difference: 90 minus 60 gives 30. A quick check is to add up all the angles meeting at that vertex: on the outside, 90 plus 60 plus the exterior reflex angle must make 360; on the inside, the 60 and the 30 must together make the square's 90. Draw the figure large, shade each shape, and ask whether the two angles are side by side or one within the other; the picture answers before the arithmetic does. Never decide from the numbers alone.

### subtopic_5 — The House-Shaped Window
**Assistant:** The learner left two lengths for Term 4. Is it acceptable in an examination to say a length cannot be found yet?

**Tutor:** In an examination, the question will not ask for something the syllabus has not yet covered, so you will not be put in that position; every length asked for in a Term 3 problem can be found from the Term 3 facts — equal sides, perimeters and scale factors. The learner's situation was a real workshop, where the glazier needed a height that genuinely requires the Theorem of Pythagoras, and the honest answer was to name what was needed rather than estimate. That honesty is a mathematical virtue worth keeping: when a problem seems to require a fact you do not have, say so and state what fact would finish it, rather than inventing a value. In an examination, if you find yourself apparently needing Pythagoras in a Term 3 question, it is a signal that you have missed a simpler route — an equal side from a definition, a perimeter, or a similar triangle — so go back to the marks and look again. The diagonal of the square and the height of the equilateral triangle are exactly the first two lengths Term 4 will find.
