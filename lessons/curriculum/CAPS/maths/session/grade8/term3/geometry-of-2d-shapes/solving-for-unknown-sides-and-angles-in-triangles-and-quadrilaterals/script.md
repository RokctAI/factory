# Part 1 — Expert

This session solves geometric problems involving unknown sides and angles in triangles and quadrilaterals. Unknown angles are found from the angle sum of a triangle, the angle sum of a quadrilateral, the base angles of an isosceles triangle, the angles of an equilateral triangle, the opposite and adjacent angles of a parallelogram, and the straight-line and parallel-line relationships. Unknown sides are found from the definitions — equal legs of an isosceles triangle, equal opposite sides of a parallelogram, equal sides of a rhombus or square — together with perimeters and, where figures are similar, scale factors. Unknowns given algebraically produce linear equations. The anchor problems are an isosceles triangle on a straight line, a rectangle with algebraic sides and a known perimeter, a parallelogram with a diagonal, and a composite figure of a square and an equilateral triangle.

## Subtopic: Unknown Angles in Triangles from Several Facts

The first kind of problem gives a triangle with extra lines and asks for angles. The facts available are the angle sum of a triangle, the base angles of an isosceles triangle, the angles of an equilateral triangle, angles on a straight line, and vertically opposite angles. Choose from the marks.

An isosceles triangle ABC has AB equal to AC, marked with dashes, and the side BC is extended past C to a point D. The exterior angle ACD is 110 degrees. Find the angles of the triangle. The angle ACB is on a straight line with 110, so it is 180 minus 110, which is 70. Angles on a straight line. The angle ABC equals the angle ACB because the sides opposite them are equal: 70, base angles of an isosceles triangle. The apex angle BAC is 180 minus 70 minus 70, which is 40, angle sum of a triangle. Three steps, three reasons.

A triangle has one angle of 40 and another marked x; the third is marked 2x. Angle sum: 40 plus x plus 2x equals 180, so 3x equals 140, x equals 46,67 — a decimal, which is acceptable; the angles are 40, about 46,7 and about 93,3, and the triangle is obtuse-angled. Not every answer is a whole number.

Two triangles share a vertex at the crossing of two straight lines, forming a bow-tie. One triangle has angles 50 and 60 at its far end. Its angle at the crossing is 180 minus 110, which is 70, angle sum. The other triangle's angle at the crossing is vertically opposite, so 70. If the other triangle is isosceles with the crossing angle as apex, its base angles are 180 minus 70, which is 110, halved, 55 each.

An equilateral triangle ABC has a point D on BC such that AD is drawn. The angle BAD is 25. Find the angle ADC. The angle B is 60, equilateral triangle. In triangle ABD, the angle ADB is 180 minus 60 minus 25, which is 95, angle sum. The angle ADC is on a straight line with it: 180 minus 95, which is 85.

The pattern in every case: read the marks, take the fact nearest a given, write statement, value and reason, mark the result on the diagram, repeat. Questions on triangle angles follow now.

## Subtopic: Unknown Sides from Definitions and Perimeters

Unknown sides come from the definitions of the shapes and from perimeters. The definitions supply equal sides: the two legs of an isosceles triangle; all three sides of an equilateral triangle; opposite sides of a parallelogram and rectangle; all four sides of a rhombus and square; the two pairs of adjacent sides of a kite. The perimeter is the sum of all the sides.

An isosceles triangle has legs of x and a base of x plus 4, and its perimeter is 34. Perimeter: x plus x plus x plus 4 equals 34, so 3x plus 4 equals 34, 3x equals 30, x equals 10. Sides 10, 10 and 14. Check: 10 plus 10 plus 14 is 34, and 10 plus 10 exceeds 14, so the triangle exists.

An equilateral triangle has perimeter 27; each side is 27 divided by 3, which is 9.

A rectangle has sides 2x plus 1 and x plus 5 and perimeter 42. Opposite sides are equal, so the perimeter is twice the sum of two adjacent sides: 2 times the quantity 2x plus 1 plus x plus 5 equals 42. Inside the bracket, 3x plus 6; so 3x plus 6 equals 21, 3x equals 15, x equals 5. Sides 2 times 5 plus 1, which is 11, and 5 plus 5, which is 10. A rectangle 11 by 10. Check: 2 times 21 is 42.

A square has perimeter 36; side 9. A rhombus has perimeter 52; side 13. A parallelogram has one side 8 and perimeter 30; the opposite side is 8, the other two total 14, each 7.

A kite has adjacent sides a, a, b, b with a equal to 7 and perimeter 40. Then 14 plus 2b equals 40, 2b equals 26, b equals 13. Sides 7, 7, 13, 13.

A parallelogram has sides marked 3x minus 1 and 2x plus 4 opposite each other, and another side 9. Opposite sides equal: 3x minus 1 equals 2x plus 4, so x equals 5 and those sides are 14 each. The perimeter is 14 plus 9 plus 14 plus 9, which is 46.

When a perimeter is given, write the sum of all sides in terms of x, set it equal to the perimeter, solve, substitute, and check by adding the sides. When equal sides are given as different expressions, set them equal first. Always finish with the actual lengths and the units. Questions on sides follow.

## Subtopic: Unknown Angles in Quadrilaterals and Composite Figures

Quadrilateral problems use the angle sum of 360, the parallelogram properties, the trapezium's co-interior leg angles, the kite's equal angles, and, when a diagonal is drawn, the triangle facts inside.

A quadrilateral has angles x, 2x, 3x and 90. Angle sum: 6x plus 90 equals 360, so 6x equals 270, x equals 45. Angles 45, 90, 135 and 90. Check: 45 plus 90 plus 135 plus 90 is 360.

A parallelogram ABCD has angle A equal to 65. Then angle C is 65, opposite angles of a parallelogram; angles B and D are each 180 minus 65, which is 115, co-interior angles. Now draw the diagonal AC and suppose AB equals BC, marked. Triangle ABC is isosceles with apex B of 115, so its base angles BAC and BCA are 180 minus 115, which is 65, halved: 32,5 each. The angle CAD is then 65 minus 32,5, which is 32,5, so the diagonal bisects angle A — and that means the parallelogram with two equal adjacent sides is a rhombus, whose diagonals bisect its angles.

A trapezium ABCD with AB parallel to DC has angle A equal to 2x and angle D equal to x plus 30. The leg AD joins the parallels, so A plus D is 180, co-interior: 3x plus 30 equals 180, 3x equals 150, x equals 50. Angles A and D are 100 and 80.

Composite figures. A square ABCD has an equilateral triangle ABE built on its side AB, pointing outwards, making a house shape. Find the angle EBC. Angle ABE is 60, equilateral triangle; angle ABC is 90, square; so angle EBC is 60 plus 90, which is 150. If instead the triangle points inwards, into the square, angle EBC is 90 minus 60, which is 30, and triangle EBC, with BE equal to BC since both equal AB, is isosceles with apex 30 and base angles 75 each.

A rectangle has a diagonal drawn, and the diagonal makes 35 degrees with one long side. The other angle in that triangle, at the far corner, is 90 minus 35, which is 55, angle sum with the right angle. By alternate angles between the parallel long sides, the diagonal makes 35 with the other long side too.

Every composite figure is solved by naming the shapes it is made of, using each shape's facts at the shared vertices, and adding or subtracting the angles that meet there. Questions on quadrilaterals and composites follow.

## Subtopic: Sides and Angles Together, and Similar Figures

Some problems mix sides and angles, and some use similarity to find sides.

A triangle has two sides marked equal with dashes and angles marked 2x minus 10 and x plus 20 at the ends of the unmarked side. The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, so 2x minus 10 equals x plus 20, x equals 30, and each base angle is 50. The apex is 180 minus 100, which is 80. If the legs are 3y and y plus 8, then 3y equals y plus 8, y equals 4, legs 12 each.

A kite has angles 100 and 60 at the ends of its symmetry diagonal and perimeter 50 with short sides 9. The other two angles share 360 minus 160, which is 200, so each is 100, kite's equal angles. The long sides are 50 minus 18, which is 32, halved: 16 each.

Similar triangles. Triangle ABC has sides 6, 8 and 10; triangle PQR is similar with its shortest side 9. Scale factor 9 over 6, which is 1,5. The other sides are 8 times 1,5, which is 12, and 10 times 1,5, which is 15. Its angles equal those of ABC — and since 36 plus 64 is 100, ABC is right-angled, so PQR is too.

A small triangle sits inside a large one, sharing the top vertex, with its base parallel to the large base — a common diagram. The two triangles are similar, by corresponding angles with the parallel bases. If the small triangle's sides along the arms are 4 and 5 and the large triangle's corresponding sides are 10 and the unknown, the scale factor is 10 over 4, which is 2,5, and the unknown is 5 times 2,5, which is 12,5.

Perimeter and similarity. A triangle with perimeter 24 is similar to one with perimeter 36; scale factor 36 over 24, which is 1,5; a side of 8 in the first corresponds to 12 in the second.

The method for mixed problems is to separate the angle work from the side work. Angles come from the angle facts and are independent of the lengths; sides come from the definitions, perimeters and scale factors and are independent of the angles, except that similarity needs the angles to match. Do each in turn, write each with its reason, and check that the two halves describe one consistent figure. Questions on mixed problems follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problem solving as stories about a house-shaped window made of a square and a triangle, a kite builder working out the lengths of the spars from the perimeter, and a surveyor's sketch with one angle given and six unknown — stories about reading a figure until it gives everything up.

## Subtopic: The House-Shaped Window

A carpenter is making a window in the shape of a house: a square of glass with an equilateral triangle of glass on top, sharing the square's upper side. The square has side 80 centimetres. The glazier needs every length and every angle of the frame.

A learner helping in the workshop starts with what the shapes give for free. The square: four sides of 80, four angles of 90. The equilateral triangle on the top side: three sides of 80, since its base is the square's side and all its sides are equal, and three angles of 60.

The frame's outline is a pentagon: the two sloping roof edges, the two vertical walls, the floor. Its sides are 80, 80, 80, 80, 80 — all equal, the learner notes, though the pentagon is not regular because its angles are not all equal. Perimeter 5 times 80, which is 400 centimetres. The two interior angles where the roof meets the walls are each 90 plus 60, which is 150. The angle at the roof's peak is 60. The two floor corners are 90. Check: 150 plus 150 plus 60 plus 90 plus 90 is 540, which is the angle sum of a pentagon — three triangles' worth, 3 times 180.

The glazier also wants the height of the window, from floor to peak. The square gives 80. The triangle's height is from the top side to the peak; the learner cannot find it yet with the Term 3 facts — it needs the Theorem of Pythagoras from Term 4 — so he leaves it as the height of an equilateral triangle of side 80, to be found later. Honest incompleteness is better than a guess.

The carpenter adds a horizontal glazing bar across the triangle, parallel to its base, halfway up. The small triangle above the bar is similar to the whole triangle — parallel base, shared peak — with scale factor one half, so the bar is 40 centimetres long and the little triangle is equilateral with side 40. Its angles are still 60, since similar figures keep angles.

Finally, a diagonal bar across the square from corner to corner. It splits the square into two right-angled isosceles triangles, each with angles 90, 45 and 45, so the diagonal makes 45 with every side it meets. Its length, again, waits for Term 4.

The learner's sheet for the glazier: five sides of 80, angles 150, 150, 60, 90, 90, a 40-centimetre bar, 45-degree diagonal corners, two lengths marked to follow. Questions on the window follow.

## Subtopic: The Kite Builder and the Surveyor's Sketch

A kite builder has 3 metres of edging tape for the outline of a diamond-shaped kite — a true kite in the geometric sense, with two short spars-ends and two long. He wants the two short edges to be 50 centimetres each. How long are the long edges? The perimeter is 300 centimetres; the short edges take 100; the long edges share 200, so each is 100 centimetres. Sides 50, 50, 100, 100. He checks the shape can close: at each side vertex a 50 meets a 100, and the symmetry diagonal must be shorter than 150 for the two triangles to exist — it is.

He wants the angle at the top of the kite, where the short edges meet, to be 110, and the angle at the bottom, where the long edges meet, to be 40. The two side angles then share 360 minus 150, which is 210, so each is 105. He marks the four angles on his plan: 110, 105, 40, 105. The symmetry diagonal from top to bottom bisects the 110 and the 40, making 55 and 20 with the edges — the angles he needs to cut the spar notches.

Across town, a surveyor's assistant has a sketch of a plot shaped like a trapezium with one angle given, 72 degrees, at the end of one leg, and the plot's parallel sides marked. She needs the other three angles and the lengths of two sides for which only the perimeter, 180 metres, and the parallel sides, 60 and 40, are known, the legs being equal — an isosceles trapezium.

Angles. The leg's other end is co-interior with the 72: 180 minus 72, which is 108. The isosceles trapezium's base angles are equal, so the other end of the 60-metre side also has 72 and the other end of the 40-metre side also has 108. Check: 72 plus 72 plus 108 plus 108 is 360.

Sides. The legs share 180 minus 60 minus 40, which is 80, so each leg is 40 metres. The sketch is complete: 60, 40, 40, 40 and angles 72, 72, 108, 108.

The assistant adds the diagonal from one 72-corner to the opposite 108-corner for the fence contractor, and notes that the triangle it makes with the 40-metre leg and the 40-metre parallel side is isosceles — two sides of 40 — so its base angles are equal; with the apex 108 between them, they are 36 each. One given angle, and the whole sketch has surrendered.

Both builders worked the same way: definitions for the equal sides, perimeter for the rest, angle sums and pairs for the angles, a check at the end. Questions on the kite and the sketch follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for unknown sides and angles. One: read the marks and name every shape in the figure. Two: list the facts each shape supplies — equal sides from definitions; angle sums of 180 and 360; isosceles base angles; equilateral 60s; parallelogram opposite sides and angles equal, adjacent angles 180; trapezium leg-end angles 180; kite equal angles where unequal sides meet; straight-line and parallel-line pairs; similarity ratios. Three: find angles and sides in order of ease, nearest a given first, writing statement, value and reason, and marking each result on the diagram. Four: for algebraic unknowns, choose the equation that matches the fact, solve, substitute and check. Five: for perimeters, sum the sides using equal-side facts. Six: check — angles of each triangle sum to 180, of each quadrilateral to 360; sides satisfy the perimeter; the triangle inequality holds; the answers are positive and sensible.

A typical examination item: an isosceles triangle on a straight line with an exterior angle. Straight line, base angles, angle sum.

Another: a rectangle or parallelogram with algebraic sides and a perimeter. Twice the sum of adjacent sides; solve; substitute.

Another: a quadrilateral with algebraic angles. Sum to 360.

Another: a composite of two named shapes with an angle at a shared vertex. Add or subtract the shapes' angles.

Another: two similar triangles with a missing side. Scale factor; multiply or divide.

Another: a figure with six unknowns labelled a to f. Work through them in order, reusing each.

Errors to avoid. Assuming a shape from appearance. Using a parallelogram fact on a trapezium. Setting adjacent angles equal. Stopping at x. Forgetting that a perimeter counts every side. Mis-pairing similar sides. Not checking the sums.

Looking ahead: Term 4 opens with the Theorem of Pythagoras, which finds the lengths this topic had to leave — the heights and diagonals — and continues with transformations, area, perimeter and volume. Grade 9 proves the facts used here and extends them to polygons and congruence. Final questions follow.
