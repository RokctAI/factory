# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines quadrilaterals by their sides and angles. A quadrilateral is a closed figure with four straight sides, four vertices, four interior angles and two diagonals. A trapezium has at least one pair of opposite sides parallel; a parallelogram has both pairs of opposite sides parallel; a rectangle is a parallelogram with a right angle, and so four right angles; a rhombus is a parallelogram with four equal sides; a square is a rectangle with four equal sides, equivalently a rhombus with a right angle; a kite has two pairs of adjacent sides equal. The definitions are nested into a hierarchy in which every property of a more general figure is inherited by the more special one. The anchor figures are a rectangle of 6 by 4, a rhombus of side 5, a square of side 5, a trapezium with parallel sides 8 and 4, and a kite with sides 3, 3, 5, 5.

## Subtopic: What a Quadrilateral Is

A quadrilateral is a closed figure bounded by four straight line segments. It has four sides, four vertices and four interior angles. It is named by its vertices taken in order around the figure: quadrilateral ABCD has sides AB, BC, CD and DA, and the order matters — ABCD and ACBD are different figures, the second crossing itself and not a quadrilateral at all in Grade 8.

Opposite sides are the pairs that do not meet: AB and CD; BC and DA. Adjacent sides share a vertex: AB and BC meet at B. Opposite angles are at vertices not joined by a side: A and C; B and D. Adjacent angles share a side: A and B both touch AB.

A diagonal joins two opposite vertices: AC and BD are the two diagonals. Every quadrilateral has exactly two. A diagonal splits a quadrilateral into two triangles, which is how the next lesson finds that its angles add to 360.

Quadrilaterals can be drawn in any orientation and need not be symmetric or neat. An irregular quadrilateral with four different sides and four different angles is as much a quadrilateral as a square. The special quadrilaterals of this lesson are the ones with extra properties — parallel sides, equal sides, right angles — and the properties, not the appearance, define them.

Marks on a diagram declare the properties. Matching arrowheads on two sides mean they are parallel; a second pair of sides parallel to each other get double arrowheads. Matching dashes mean equal lengths. A small square means a right angle. Matching arcs mean equal angles. A quadrilateral with no marks and no given values can be classified only as a quadrilateral.

The special quadrilaterals are grouped by what they have in common. Those with at least one pair of parallel sides: trapezium, parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square. Those with equal adjacent sides: kite, rhombus, square. The sections that follow define each, and the fourth section fits them into one tree. Questions on quadrilaterals follow now.

## Subtopic: Trapezium and Parallelogram

A trapezium is a quadrilateral with at least one pair of opposite sides parallel. The parallel sides are often called the bases, and they may be different lengths — typically they are, with parallel sides 8 and 4 in the anchor figure and two non-parallel sides joining them. A trapezium whose non-parallel sides are equal is an isosceles trapezium, and its base angles are then equal, as with the isosceles triangle. In a trapezium, the two angles at the ends of each non-parallel side are co-interior between the parallel bases and so add to 180 degrees — a fact used in calculations.

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel. The diagram shows single arrowheads on one pair and double arrowheads on the other. The definition says nothing about lengths or angles, but consequences follow and are investigated in the next lesson: in a parallelogram the opposite sides are equal in length, the opposite angles are equal, and adjacent angles add to 180. A parallelogram pushed over — leaning — is still a parallelogram, because leaning does not change which sides are parallel.

Every parallelogram is a trapezium under the at least one pair definition, since it has a pair of parallel sides and then some. South African textbooks follow this inclusive definition; be aware that some other books define a trapezium as having exactly one pair, which would exclude parallelograms. Follow the inclusive definition in CAPS: a trapezium has at least one pair of parallel sides.

Classifying from marks. One pair of arrowheads only: trapezium. Two pairs: parallelogram. Two pairs plus a right angle, or plus four equal sides — the next section.

Sketching. A trapezium: draw two horizontal parallel lines of different lengths, join their ends; mark the arrowheads. A parallelogram: draw two equal horizontal lines offset from each other, join their ends; mark two sets of arrowheads. Neither needs right angles; a parallelogram drawn with right angles would be claiming to be a rectangle.

The parallel sides are the whole definition. A quadrilateral with two equal sides that are not parallel is not a trapezium; a quadrilateral with equal opposite sides is a parallelogram only once the parallel property is established, which the next lesson shows it always is. Questions on trapezium and parallelogram follow.

## Subtopic: Rectangle, Rhombus and Square

A rectangle is a parallelogram with a right angle. One right angle is enough to force all four: adjacent angles of a parallelogram add to 180, so the angle next to a 90 is 90, and so on round. So a rectangle has four right angles, and because it is a parallelogram its opposite sides are equal — the 6 by 4 anchor has two sides of 6 and two of 4. The diagram shows a square mark in at least one corner and may show the parallel arrowheads; the right angles make the parallel sides automatic, since co-interior angles of 90 and 90 add to 180.

A rhombus is a parallelogram with four equal sides. Side 5, side 5, side 5, side 5; the diagram shows a single dash on every side. Because it is a parallelogram, its opposite angles are equal and adjacent angles add to 180 — but its angles need not be right angles, and in a typical rhombus they are not: two acute and two obtuse. A rhombus looks like a pushed-over square, which is exactly what it is.

A square is a rectangle with four equal sides — equivalently, a rhombus with a right angle. It has everything: both pairs of sides parallel, four equal sides, four right angles. Side 5 and every angle 90. The diagram shows a square mark and a dash on every side.

The three are related by inheritance. Start with a parallelogram. Add a right angle: rectangle. Add four equal sides instead: rhombus. Add both: square. So a square is both a rectangle and a rhombus; a rectangle with unequal sides is not a rhombus; a rhombus without a right angle is not a rectangle.

Classifying from marks. Two pairs of arrowheads and a square: rectangle. Two pairs of arrowheads and four dashes: rhombus. A square mark and four dashes: square. A figure drawn to look square but marked with only two pairs of arrowheads is a parallelogram and nothing more, because the drawing is not to scale.

The test questions that trip people up are about the inheritance. Is every square a rectangle: yes, it is a parallelogram with a right angle. Is every rectangle a square: no, its sides need not be equal. Is every square a rhombus: yes. Is every rhombus a square: no, its angles need not be right. Is every rectangle a rhombus: only if it is a square. Questions on rectangle, rhombus and square follow.

## Subtopic: The Kite and the Family Tree

A kite is a quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent sides equal. The anchor kite has sides 3, 3, 5, 5 in order round the figure: the two 3s meet at one vertex, the two 5s meet at the opposite vertex. The diagram shows single dashes on one pair of adjacent sides and double dashes on the other. A kite has one line of symmetry — the diagonal through the two vertices where the equal sides meet — and the angles at the other two vertices, where a 3 meets a 5, are equal. Its diagonals cross at right angles, a fact met in Grade 9.

A kite need not have parallel sides and usually has none. If both pairs of adjacent sides are equal and also all four sides are equal — sides 5, 5, 5, 5 — the kite is a rhombus. So a rhombus is a special kite as well as a special parallelogram, and a square is a special kite too.

The family tree. At the top, quadrilateral. Below it, two branches: trapezium, at least one pair of parallel sides; and kite, two pairs of equal adjacent sides. Under trapezium, parallelogram, both pairs parallel. Under parallelogram, rectangle, a right angle, and rhombus, four equal sides. Rhombus also hangs under kite. Under both rectangle and rhombus, square.

Reading the tree downwards adds a property; reading it upwards keeps every property. A square is a rhombus, so it has four equal sides and the rhombus's equal opposite angles; a square is a rectangle, so it has four right angles; a square is a parallelogram, a trapezium, a kite and a quadrilateral. A rectangle is a parallelogram and a trapezium but not a rhombus or a kite, unless it is a square.

The most specific name is the one to give in a classification: a figure with four equal sides and four right angles is a square, even though parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, kite and trapezium are all true of it.

Statements to test with the tree. Every rhombus is a kite: true. Every kite is a rhombus: false; sides 3, 3, 5, 5. Every rectangle is a trapezium: true, inclusively. Every trapezium is a parallelogram: false; one pair of parallel sides only. Some kites are parallelograms: true; the rhombuses. No kite is a rectangle: false; the squares are both. Questions on the kite and the tree follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same definitions as stories about a farm gate that sagged from a rectangle into a parallelogram without changing a single side, a learner who settled a playground argument about whether a square is a rhombus, and a kite-maker who knows exactly which sides must match — stories about what a shape keeps and what it loses when it is pushed.

## Subtopic: The Gate That Sagged

A farm gate is made as a rectangle: two long rails of 3 metres, two short uprights of 1,2 metres, bolted at the corners. When it is new and hung, every corner is a right angle, the rails are parallel, the uprights are parallel. A rectangle, by definition: a parallelogram with a right angle.

After a season the far end drops. The bolts hold, so the four sides are the same lengths as before — 3, 1,2, 3, 1,2 — but the corners have turned: two are now about 80 degrees and two about 100. A learner asks what shape the gate is now.

The rails are still parallel to each other, because the uprights are equal and the rails were bolted to their ends; the uprights are still parallel to each other for the same reason. Both pairs of opposite sides parallel: a parallelogram. It has lost its right angles, so it is no longer a rectangle. It kept its parallel sides and its equal opposite sides, so it is still a parallelogram and still a trapezium. The sag changed the angles and nothing else, and the definitions track exactly that: rectangle needs the right angle; parallelogram does not.

The farmer fixes it with a diagonal brace, a strip from one corner to the opposite one. The learner sees why that works: the diagonal and two sides make a triangle, and a triangle with three fixed side lengths has fixed angles — the angle sum lesson's construction with compasses showed that three sides determine a triangle completely. A quadrilateral with four fixed sides does not have fixed angles, which is why it sagged; a triangle does, which is why the brace holds the corner at 90.

The learner tries the same thinking on a square frame from a picture. Pushed, it becomes a rhombus: four equal sides kept, right angles lost. It stays a parallelogram and a kite and a trapezium; it stops being a rectangle and a square. Push the rectangular gate and it stays a parallelogram; push the square frame and it stays a rhombus. Each shape falls to the name immediately above it in the tree when it loses the property that put it lower.

The farmer, who did not need the tree, has braced gates for forty years. But the learner now knows which properties a push removes — angles — and which it leaves — parallel sides, equal sides — and that is the whole content of the definitions. Questions on the gate follow.

## Subtopic: Is a Square a Rhombus? The Playground Argument

Two learners argue at break. One says a square is a rhombus. The other says a rhombus is the diamond shape, and a square is a square, and they are obviously different. A third learner is dragged in to decide, and she does it with the definitions rather than with pictures.

What is a rhombus, she asks. A parallelogram with four equal sides, they agree. Does a square have both pairs of opposite sides parallel: yes. Does a square have four equal sides: yes. Then a square satisfies the definition of a rhombus, and so a square is a rhombus. The second learner protests that the rhombus is tilted. She points out that tilting is not in the definition; a rhombus can stand on a side as easily as on a corner, and a square turned 45 degrees looks like a diamond but is still a square.

Then she asks the reverse. Is every rhombus a square. A square needs a right angle. A rhombus with angles 60, 120, 60, 120 has no right angle, so it is not a square. So some rhombuses are squares and some are not; the square is the special rhombus with a right angle.

The first learner, pleased, pushes further: is a square a rectangle. Same method. A rectangle is a parallelogram with a right angle. A square is a parallelogram and has a right angle, so yes. And is a rectangle a square: only if its four sides are equal, so not usually.

The second learner, now interested, asks about kites. A kite has two pairs of adjacent sides equal. A square's adjacent sides are all equal, so it has two pairs of equal adjacent sides and more — so a square is a kite. A rhombus is a kite. A rectangle that is not a square is not a kite, because its adjacent sides are unequal.

The third learner draws the tree in the sand and places the square at the bottom, under both rectangle and rhombus, with kite and trapezium above those. The rule she gives them: never settle a classification by how a shape looks; go to the definition, check each clause, and say yes or no. Then find the most specific name that fits, because every name above it in the tree is also true but less informative.

The bell goes. The second learner concedes, on the condition that nobody calls a square a diamond. Questions on the argument follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for classifying quadrilaterals. One: read the marks — arrowheads for parallel, dashes for equal, squares for right angles — and any given lengths or angles; ignore how the figure looks. Two: count parallel pairs: none, one, or two. Three: check equal sides: adjacent pairs, opposite pairs, or all four. Four: check for a right angle. Five: apply the definitions in order from most specific — square, then rectangle or rhombus, then parallelogram, then trapezium or kite, then quadrilateral — and give the most specific name that fits. Six: for true-or-false statements about the families, test the definition clause by clause and give a counter-example when the statement is false.

A typical examination item gives a marked figure and asks for its name. Two parallel pairs and four dashes with no square: rhombus. One parallel pair: trapezium. Two pairs of adjacent dashes: kite.

Another asks for a definition. State it exactly: a parallelogram is a quadrilateral with both pairs of opposite sides parallel; a kite is a quadrilateral with two pairs of adjacent sides equal.

Another asks true or false with a reason. Every square is a rectangle: true, a square is a parallelogram with a right angle. Every rectangle is a square: false, a 6 by 4 rectangle has unequal sides.

Another asks for a sketch of a named figure with marks. Draw it, mark the parallel pairs, equal sides and right angles that the definition requires, and label the vertices in order.

Another asks what a figure becomes when a property is removed. A rectangle without its right angles: a parallelogram. A square without its right angles: a rhombus.

Errors to avoid. Classifying by appearance. Calling a square a rhombus or rectangle when the most specific name is asked. Using the exactly one pair definition of trapezium. Thinking a kite must look like a toy kite — a rhombus is a kite. Marking a parallelogram sketch with right angles. Reversing a true statement and assuming the reverse is true.

Looking ahead: the next lesson investigates the angle sum of quadrilaterals and the side and angle properties of parallelograms, and uses them in calculations. Grade 9 adds the diagonal properties and proves them. Final questions follow.
