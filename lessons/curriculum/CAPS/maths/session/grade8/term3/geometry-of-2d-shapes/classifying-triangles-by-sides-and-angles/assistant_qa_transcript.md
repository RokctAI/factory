### subtopic_2 — Classifying by Sides: Scalene, Isosceles, Equilateral
**Assistant:** If an equilateral triangle is also isosceles, why not just call it isosceles?

**Tutor:** Because classification asks for the most informative true name, and equilateral says more. The definitions are nested: isosceles means at least two equal sides, and an equilateral triangle meets that condition with room to spare, so logically it is isosceles. But saying isosceles of a triangle with three equal sides withholds information — a reader would picture two equal legs and a different base — whereas equilateral pins the shape down completely. Mathematics uses this most-specific convention throughout: a square is a rectangle, but you call it a square; a multiple of 10 is even, but you say multiple of 10 when you know it. In an examination, classify by sides: equilateral, is the expected answer for three equal sides, and isosceles would usually be marked as incomplete. The nesting matters in the other direction too: any fact proved about isosceles triangles, such as equal base angles, automatically holds for equilateral triangles, which is one reason the at least two wording is chosen.

### subtopic_3 — Classifying by Angles: Acute-Angled, Right-Angled, Obtuse-Angled
**Assistant:** Can I tell whether a triangle is right-angled from the sides alone, without any angle marked?

**Tutor:** Yes, by the Theorem of Pythagoras, which is a Term 4 topic: a triangle is right-angled exactly when the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two. Sides 3, 4 and 5 satisfy it, since 9 plus 16 is 25, so that triangle is right-angled with the right angle opposite the 5. Sides 5, 6 and 7 do not — 25 plus 36 is 61, not 49 — so that triangle is not right-angled; in fact the test extends to tell you it is acute-angled, since 61 is more than 49. For now, in this lesson, the expected method is to use the marks and the given angles: a small square or a stated 90 degrees. If a diagram gives only sides and asks for the angle type, either the question expects the Pythagoras test — in which case you will have met it — or it is asking only for the side classification. Read the question's wording carefully, and never conclude right-angled from the drawing alone.

### subtopic_4 — Combining the Two Classifications
**Assistant:** In an isosceles triangle, how do I know which side is the base if it is not at the bottom?

**Tutor:** The base is the side that is not one of the two equal sides, wherever it happens to be drawn. Find the two sides carrying matching dashes, or the two given equal lengths; those are the legs, and the remaining side is the base. The apex is the vertex where the two legs meet, and the base angles are the two angles at the ends of the base — equivalently, the angles opposite the equal sides. Orientation is irrelevant: the triangle may be drawn with the base vertical, or with the apex pointing down, as in a yield sign turned on its side. A helpful habit is to turn the page so the base is horizontal and the apex is at the top; the names then match the picture and the equal base angles are easy to see. In an equilateral triangle there is no distinguished base, since any side could play the role, and the question will say which side to treat as the base if it matters.

### subtopic_6 — Sorting the Box of Cardboard Triangles
**Assistant:** The triangle measured 10, 10 and 10,2. Is it isosceles or equilateral?

**Tutor:** By the measurements, isosceles: two sides are equal and the third is different, and classification goes by what is true, not by what was intended. The right thing to write in a measurement exercise is isosceles, and to note that the difference is small and may be a cutting error. In a textbook or examination question, however, lengths are given exactly, and 10, 10 and 10,2 would be a deliberate isosceles example. The case shows two things worth remembering. First, real measurement always carries error, so real objects are never perfectly equilateral, and a practical classification should say how much difference counts — perhaps anything within the ruler's precision is treated as equal. Second, geometry's definitions are exact: an equilateral triangle has sides exactly equal, and the 60-degree angles follow from that exactness. When you reason with the definitions, you work with the exact figure the question describes; when you measure a cardboard triangle, you report the measurements and classify from them, with a note about precision.
