### subtopic_1 — Congruent Shapes: Same Shape and Same Size
**Assistant:** Why does the order of the letters matter in a congruence statement if the triangles are the same anyway?

**Tutor:** Because the statement is not only saying that the two triangles match; it is saying how they match — which vertex lands on which. Triangle ABC congruent to triangle DEF declares that A corresponds to D, B to E and C to F, and therefore that AB equals DE, BC equals EF, CA equals FD, and the angle at A equals the angle at D, and so on. If the triangles are congruent but with A actually matching E, the statement triangle ABC congruent to triangle DEF is false even though the triangles are identical, because it pairs the wrong sides and angles — it would claim AB equals DE when in fact AB equals a different side of DEF. The ordering is what lets you read off equal parts without the diagram, which is exactly how later proofs use congruence: establish the congruence with the vertices in the right order, then quote any pair of corresponding sides or angles as equal. The same convention applies to similarity statements, where the order fixes which sides are divided by which to get the scale factor.

### subtopic_2 — Similar Shapes: Same Shape, Any Size
**Assistant:** Why are equal angles enough to make triangles similar but not enough for quadrilaterals?

**Tutor:** Because a triangle's three angles leave no freedom in its shape, while a quadrilateral's four angles do. Fix three angles and try to draw a triangle: whatever length you choose for the first side, the other two sides are forced by the angles at its ends, and every triangle you can draw is an enlargement of every other — the shape is locked, only the size is free. Fix four angles of 90 and try to draw a quadrilateral: you can choose both the width and the height independently, giving a 2 by 3 rectangle or a 3 by 5 rectangle, which have the same angles and different proportions. The extra side introduces an extra freedom that angles alone do not control. The same is true of any polygon with more than three sides — two rhombuses with equal angles are similar only because their definition forces all four sides equal, while two parallelograms with equal angles can have different side ratios. So the similarity test for triangles can stop at angles, and the test for anything else must also compare the side ratios.

### subtopic_3 — Scale Factor and Missing Lengths
**Assistant:** How do I know which way round to divide to get the scale factor?

**Tutor:** Decide first which figure is the original and which is the image, and then always divide image by original. The scale factor is the number that multiplies every length of the original to give the corresponding length of the image: if the image is bigger it is more than 1, if smaller it is between 0 and 1. For the 3, 4, 5 and 6, 8, 10 triangles, taking the small one as original gives 6 over 3, factor 2; taking the large one as original gives 3 over 6, factor one half. Both are correct descriptions of the same pair — they are reciprocals — but you must use the one that matches the direction you then multiply in. The common error is to compute 2 and then divide a large side by 2 to find a small one, which is right, but then multiply a small side by 2 to find a large one, which is also right, while mixing up which side is which; writing original and image above the two figures and labelling every known length under its heading prevents it. A final check: every pair of corresponding sides, divided the same way, must give the same factor.

### subtopic_6 — Paving Tiles and the Flagpole's Shadow
**Assistant:** The flagpole's shadow method assumes the sun's rays are parallel. Why is that justified, and what else must be true?

**Tutor:** The sun is so far away that the rays reaching the stick and the rays reaching the flagpole, a few metres apart, are parallel to within an angle far too small to measure — the difference is less than a millionth of a degree. Parallel rays mean the angle each ray makes with the level ground is the same at the stick and at the pole, so the two shadow triangles share that angle. Both triangles also have a right angle, provided the stick and the pole are vertical and the ground is level; those are the other conditions. If the stick leans, its triangle is no longer right-angled at the base and the similarity fails. If the pole stands on a slope, the shadow length is distorted. And the two shadows must be measured at the same time, because the sun's angle changes by the minute; the learner's second measurement an hour later gave different shadow lengths but the same scale factor only because she measured both shadows together again. With those conditions met, two right-angled triangles with one shared acute angle have all three angles equal and are similar, and the scale factor from the stick to the pole does the rest.
