# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates angles in triangles using accurately constructed figures. Measurement of constructed triangles suggests that the interior angles sum to 180 degrees, that each angle of an equilateral triangle is 60 degrees, and that the angles opposite the equal sides of an isosceles triangle are equal; the angle sum is then explained by a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side, and the isosceles fact by the symmetry of the figure. The facts are applied as reasons — angle sum of a triangle, equilateral triangle, base angles of an isosceles triangle — to calculate unknown angles, including those given algebraically. The anchor figures are a constructed scalene triangle, an equilateral triangle of side 6 centimetres and an isosceles triangle with apex angle 40 degrees.

## Subtopic: Constructing Triangles and Measuring the Angle Sum

An investigation starts with accurate figures. Construct a triangle with sides 7, 8 and 10 centimetres: draw the 10-centimetre base with a ruler; set the compass to 7, centre one end, draw an arc; set it to 8, centre the other end, draw a second arc; join the crossing point to both ends. Measure the three angles with a protractor, reading the correct scale and placing the centre exactly on the vertex. A careful construction gives angles near 44, 53 and 83 degrees. Add them: 44 plus 53 is 97; plus 83 is 180.

Construct a second triangle, this time from angles: a base of 8 centimetres, an angle of 30 degrees at one end and 70 at the other, the sides extended until they meet. Measure the third angle: 80. Sum: 30 plus 70 plus 80 is 180.

A third: any triangle at all, drawn freehand and large. Measure and add. Within the accuracy of a school protractor — a degree or two each way — the total is 180 again. Every learner in a class constructs a different triangle, and every total comes to 180 give or take measurement error. That is the investigation's result: the interior angles of a triangle add to 180 degrees.

Measurement suggests; it does not prove. A protractor cannot tell 180 from 179,8, and thirty triangles are not all triangles. So the fact is explained by reasoning. Take triangle ABC and draw a line through A parallel to BC. The line makes two angles with the sides AB and AC, one on each side of the angle at A. The angle on the left is alternate to the angle at B — a Z through the transversal AB — so it equals B. The angle on the right is alternate to the angle at C, so it equals C. The three angles at A — the left one, A itself and the right one — lie along the straight line through A, so they add to 180. Therefore B plus A plus C is 180. This holds for every triangle, because every triangle has a vertex and an opposite side to draw the parallel to.

The written reason, when the fact is used, is: angle sum of a triangle, or interior angles of a triangle. Given two angles, the third is 180 minus their sum: with 50 and 60, the third is 70. Questions on constructing and the angle sum follow now.

## Subtopic: Angles of an Equilateral Triangle

Construct an equilateral triangle of side 6 centimetres: a 6-centimetre base, a compass set to 6 from each end, arcs crossing above, join. Measure each angle: 60, 60, 60, within protractor error. Construct another with side 9: 60, 60, 60 again. The side length changes the size of the triangle but not its angles.

The reasoning. An equilateral triangle has three equal sides. The angles opposite equal sides are equal — the isosceles fact, applied to each pair of sides in turn — so all three angles are equal. They add to 180, so each is 180 divided by 3, which is 60. The written reason when the fact is used: angles of an equilateral triangle, or each angle of an equilateral triangle is 60 degrees.

The converse holds too: a triangle with three angles of 60 has three equal sides and is equilateral. More sharply, a triangle with two angles of 60 has a third of 60 as well — 180 minus 120 — and so is equilateral. One 60-degree angle is not enough: a triangle with angles 60, 50 and 70 is scalene.

Equilateral triangles appear in calculations as sources of known angles. A figure built from an equilateral triangle and a square sharing a side has, at the shared vertex, 60 from the triangle and 90 from the square, so the angle between the triangle's free side and the square's free side, if they fill the rest of a straight line, is 180 minus 60 minus 90, which is 30. Two equilateral triangles placed side by side on a line make angles of 60, 60 and a remaining 60 on the straight line between them — three equilateral triangles exactly fill a half-turn, and six fill a full turn around a point, which is why equilateral triangles tile a floor.

Halving an equilateral triangle along a line from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side gives two right-angled triangles with angles 30, 60 and 90 — the 60-30 set square. The 30 is half the 60 at the apex, the 90 is at the foot, and the 60 is untouched at the base. This set-square triangle is the one whose sides have the special ratio met in Grade 10 trigonometry.

Diagrams mark an equilateral triangle with three matching dashes; the 60s are then known without being written. Questions on equilateral triangles follow.

## Subtopic: Sides and Base Angles of an Isosceles Triangle

Construct an isosceles triangle with legs of 7 centimetres and a base of 5: a 5-centimetre base, compass at 7 from each end, arcs crossing, join. Measure the two base angles: about 69 each. Measure the apex: about 42. Sum: 69 plus 69 is 138; plus 42 is 180. Construct another with legs 7 and base 9: base angles about 50 each, apex about 80. In every isosceles triangle, the two angles at the base — opposite the equal sides — measure the same.

The reasoning uses symmetry. Fold the constructed triangle along the line from the apex to the midpoint of the base. The two legs are equal, so they land on each other; the two halves of the base land on each other; the two base angles land on each other and so are equal. The fold line is perpendicular to the base and bisects the apex angle — it is a line of symmetry. Grade 9 turns this fold into a congruence proof; Grade 8 records the fact: in an isosceles triangle, the angles opposite the equal sides are equal. The written reason: base angles of an isosceles triangle, or angles opposite equal sides.

The converse also holds: if two angles of a triangle are equal, the sides opposite them are equal, and the triangle is isosceles. Written reason: sides opposite equal angles. So an isosceles triangle can be recognised either from two equal sides or from two equal angles.

Calculations. Apex angle 40: the base angles are equal and add with 40 to 180, so together they are 140, and each is 70. Base angle 65: the other base angle is 65, and the apex is 180 minus 130, which is 50. Apex angle 100: base angles 40 each, and the triangle is isosceles obtuse-angled.

Identify which angle is the apex before calculating: it is the angle between the two equal sides, marked by the dashes. Given an isosceles triangle with one angle of 50, two cases are possible: if the 50 is the apex, the base angles are 65 each; if the 50 is a base angle, the other base angle is 50 and the apex is 80. The question or diagram must say which, and an answer that considers both cases when the diagram does not decide earns full credit.

One exception to two cases: if the given angle is obtuse, it must be the apex, since two obtuse base angles are impossible. Questions on isosceles triangles follow.

## Subtopic: Calculating Angles in Triangles with Reasons

The three facts become reasons in calculations, written in the statement, value, reason style. A triangle has angles 47 and 61; the third is 180 minus 47 minus 61. 180 minus 47 is 133; 133 minus 61 is 72. Third angle 72 degrees, angle sum of a triangle.

A right-angled triangle has one other angle of 38. The third is 180 minus 90 minus 38, which is 52. Angle sum of a triangle. The two non-right angles of a right-angled triangle always add to 90, so 90 minus 38 is a shortcut to the same 52.

An isosceles triangle with dashes on two sides and an apex of 36: base angles 180 minus 36, which is 144, halved, 72 each. Reasons: angle sum of a triangle, and base angles of an isosceles triangle. An isosceles triangle with a base angle of 72: the other base angle is 72, base angles of an isosceles triangle; the apex is 180 minus 144, which is 36.

Algebraic angles. A triangle has angles x, 2x and 3x. Angle sum: 6x equals 180, so x equals 30, and the angles are 30, 60 and 90 — a right-angled scalene triangle. A triangle has angles x plus 10, 2x minus 20 and 3x plus 10. Sum: 6x equals 180, x equals 30, angles 40, 40 and 100 — an isosceles obtuse-angled triangle, which the equal 40s reveal. An isosceles triangle has base angles of 2x plus 5 each and apex of x; then 2x plus 5 plus 2x plus 5 plus x equals 180, so 5x plus 10 equals 180, 5x equals 170, x equals 34; base angles 73 each, apex 34; check 73 plus 73 plus 34 is 180.

Triangles combined with straight lines. A triangle sits on a straight line, and the angle outside the triangle on the line next to a vertex is 115. The interior angle at that vertex is 180 minus 115, which is 65, angles on a straight line. If another interior angle is 50, the third is 180 minus 65 minus 50, which is 65, angle sum of a triangle — and the two 65s make the triangle isosceles, sides opposite equal angles.

Every step carries its reason. Mark each found angle on the diagram. Check at the end that the three interior angles add to 180. Questions on calculating follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same facts as stories about a torn paper triangle whose corners make a straight line, a class that constructed thirty different triangles and got the same total thirty times, and a bridge truss where one measured angle gave away every other — stories about facts you can hold in your hands before you prove them.

## Subtopic: The Torn Corners

A teacher hands every learner a sheet of coloured paper and a ruler and says: draw any triangle you like, as big as the page allows, and cut it out. Thirty triangles appear — long thin ones, fat ones, one almost a right angle, one with an obtuse corner so wide it looks like a slice of pie.

Then: tear off the three corners. Not cut — tear, so the pieces are ragged and nobody can say they were measured. Now put the three corners together on your desk with their points touching at one spot and their edges meeting.

Thirty desks, thirty results, and on every one the three torn corners fit together to make a straight edge. A half-turn. One learner's pieces leave a small gap, and she finds a sliver of paper still attached to a corner; once it is torn away the gap closes. One learner's pieces overlap, and he discovers one of his corners is actually two pieces because the tear went wrong; reassembled, it fits.

The teacher asks why it works, and the room is quiet, because it is obvious and not obvious at the same time. The three corners are the three interior angles. Put together at one point, they fill a straight angle, which is 180 degrees. So the angles of any triangle add to 180. The learner with the pie slice checks hers: a huge corner and two tiny ones, and the two tiny ones fit exactly into what the huge one leaves of the half-turn.

The teacher then draws the explanation on the board: a triangle, a line through the top vertex parallel to the base, alternate angles bringing the two base angles up to sit beside the apex angle along the parallel line. The torn-corner demonstration, she says, is the parallel-line proof done with your hands: moving the corners to one point is what the alternate angles do on paper.

Someone asks whether it would work for a quadrilateral. Try it, says the teacher. Four torn corners — and they make a full turn, 360, with no gap and no overlap. That is the next lesson. For today the fact goes into the exercise books in its exam form: the interior angles of a triangle add to 180 degrees, reason angle sum of a triangle, and a note that thirty triangles agreed and the parallel line explains why all of them must. Questions on the torn corners follow.

## Subtopic: The Bridge Truss and One Measured Angle

A footbridge over a stream is built from steel triangles — a truss — and a learner on a school outing is given a sheet with its drawing and asked to find every angle in it from a single measurement. The engineer accompanying the group reads one angle at the end of the bridge: 50 degrees, between the sloping end strut and the horizontal deck.

The truss is a row of identical isosceles triangles, apex up, standing on the deck, each sharing a sloping side with the next. The first triangle's base angle at the end of the bridge is the 50 that was measured. Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal, so its other base angle is 50 too. Its apex is 180 minus 50 minus 50, which is 80. Angle sum of a triangle.

The second triangle is identical, so its angles are 50, 50 and 80 as well. But the learner is asked to reason it, not assume it. She redraws the joint where the first triangle meets the second: at each deck joint two struts rise, one from each neighbouring triangle, and the angles along the deck at that joint are the first triangle's base angle, the gap between the two struts, and the second triangle's base angle. The two base angles are 50 each, base angles of identical isosceles triangles, and the three angles lie along the straight deck, so the gap is 180 minus 50 minus 50, which is 80. Angles on a straight line. So the gap between struts at each deck joint is 80, the same as the apex angle of each upright triangle.

The upside-down triangles between the upright ones — formed by two struts and the top rail — then have base angles of 50 and 50 at the rail, by alternate angles if the top rail is parallel to the deck, and apex 80 at the deck joint, matching the gap just found. Every angle in the truss is 50 or 80, and the learner has reasons for each: isosceles base angles, angle sum, angles on a straight line, alternate angles with the parallel rails.

The engineer confirms it from the drawing: the struts were cut at 50 and 80, nothing else, which is why the bridge could be built from one jig. One measurement, a sheet of reasons, a whole bridge. Questions on the truss follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for angles in triangles. One: read the marks — dashes for equal sides, a square for a right angle, arcs for equal angles — and the given values. Two: the three facts, with their written reasons: the interior angles add to 180, angle sum of a triangle; each angle of an equilateral triangle is 60, equilateral triangle; the angles opposite the equal sides of an isosceles triangle are equal, base angles of an isosceles triangle, with the converse, sides opposite equal angles. Three: identify the apex of an isosceles triangle from the dashes before using the base-angle fact, and consider both cases if the given angle could be apex or base. Four: for algebraic angles, write the sum equal to 180, include the isosceles equality where it applies, solve, substitute, and check. Five: combine with straight-line and parallel-line facts where the triangle sits in a larger figure. Six: mark each found angle on the diagram and check the three interior angles add to 180.

A typical examination item gives two angles and asks for the third with a reason. Subtract from 180; angle sum of a triangle.

Another shows an isosceles triangle with the apex given and asks for the base angles. Subtract the apex from 180 and halve; two reasons.

Another shows an isosceles triangle with one base angle given and asks for the apex. Double the base angle, subtract from 180.

Another gives angles in terms of x. Sum to 180, solve, find the angles, classify if asked.

Another sets a triangle on a straight line or between parallels. Use straight-line or alternate angles to enter the triangle, then the angle sum.

Another asks you to explain why the angle sum is 180. Describe the parallel line through a vertex and the two alternate angles.

Errors to avoid. Using 360 for a triangle. Halving before subtracting the apex. Treating the given angle as the apex when the dashes make it a base angle. Omitting reasons. Forgetting the isosceles equality in an algebraic sum. Not checking that the three angles add to 180.

Looking ahead: the next lesson defines the quadrilaterals, and the one after finds that their angles add to 360. Grade 9 proves the triangle facts formally and adds the exterior angle theorem. Final questions follow.
