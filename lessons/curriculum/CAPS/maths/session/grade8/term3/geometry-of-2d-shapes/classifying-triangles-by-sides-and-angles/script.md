# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines triangles by their sides and angles. A triangle is a closed figure with three straight sides and three interior angles, named by its vertices. By sides, a scalene triangle has no equal sides, an isosceles triangle has at least two equal sides, and an equilateral triangle has three equal sides. By angles, an acute-angled triangle has three acute angles, a right-angled triangle has one right angle, and an obtuse-angled triangle has one obtuse angle. Each triangle carries one classification of each kind, and the definitions are used as reasons. The anchor figures are a scalene triangle with sides 5, 7 and 9, an isosceles triangle with two sides of 6, an equilateral triangle of side 4, and a right-angled triangle with legs 3 and 4.

## Subtopic: What a Triangle Is and How It Is Named

A triangle is a closed figure bounded by three straight line segments. The segments are its sides, the points where they meet are its vertices, and the angles inside at the vertices are its interior angles. Three sides, three vertices, three angles. A triangle is named by its vertices in any order: triangle ABC, written with a small triangle symbol before the letters.

The side opposite a vertex is often named by the lower-case version of the vertex letter: side a is opposite vertex A, and so on. Equally, a side can be named by its endpoints: side BC is the side joining B and C, which is the side opposite A. The angle at a vertex is named with that vertex in the middle — angle BAC — or, since a triangle has only one angle at each vertex, by the single letter with a hat: A with a hat.

Marks on a diagram carry the facts. Equal sides are marked with the same number of short dashes across them: one dash on each of two sides means those two sides are equal. Equal angles are marked with the same number of arcs. A right angle is marked with a small square. These marks are the definitions made visible; read them before anything else.

Not every three segments make a triangle. Three segments of lengths 2, 3 and 7 cannot close, because 2 plus 3 is 5, which is less than 7: the two short sides cannot reach each other across the long side. The rule is that the sum of any two sides must be greater than the third. Lengths 5, 7 and 9 work: 5 plus 7 is 12, which exceeds 9; and the other two sums exceed the remaining sides too. Lengths 3, 4 and 7 fail, because 3 plus 4 is exactly 7 and the figure collapses to a straight line.

The three interior angles of every triangle add to 180 degrees, which is the subject of the next lesson and is used here only as a fact: it is why a triangle cannot contain two right angles or two obtuse angles — there would be no room left for the third.

Triangles are drawn in any orientation. A triangle resting on a vertex rather than a side is still a triangle; the classifications that follow depend on the sides and angles themselves, never on which way up the figure is drawn. Questions on naming follow now.

## Subtopic: Classifying by Sides: Scalene, Isosceles, Equilateral

By their sides, triangles fall into three kinds. A scalene triangle has no two sides equal; all three sides have different lengths, and all three angles are different too. Sides 5, 7 and 9: scalene. No dashes on the diagram.

An isosceles triangle has at least two sides equal. The two equal sides are the legs; the third side is the base; the angle between the legs is the apex angle; the two angles at the base are the base angles. A triangle with two sides of 6 and a base of 4 is isosceles. The diagram shows one dash on each leg. The base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal — a fact the next lesson investigates and this lesson uses as part of the definition's consequences.

An equilateral triangle has all three sides equal. Side 4, side 4, side 4. Three dashes, one on each side. Every angle of an equilateral triangle is 60 degrees, since the three angles are equal and add to 180. An equilateral triangle is also isosceles, because it has at least two equal sides — it satisfies the definition — but it is called equilateral because that is the more specific name. Scalene, isosceles and equilateral are listed as three kinds, and in classification questions a triangle with three equal sides is called equilateral, not isosceles, even though both are true.

Classification by sides can be read from lengths or from marks. Given lengths 8, 8, 8: equilateral. Given 8, 8, 5: isosceles. Given 8, 6, 5: scalene. Given a diagram with two single dashes: isosceles, whatever the lengths look like, because the diagram is not to scale and the dashes are the fact.

Isosceles triangles need not sit on their base. The equal sides may be at the bottom, or one may be horizontal; the apex may point down or sideways. Find the two equal sides from the marks and name the base as the third side, wherever it lies.

The word scalene does not mean irregular or ugly; it means only that no two sides match. A right-angled triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 is scalene. Questions on sides follow.

## Subtopic: Classifying by Angles: Acute-Angled, Right-Angled, Obtuse-Angled

By their angles, triangles also fall into three kinds. An acute-angled triangle has all three angles acute, each less than 90 degrees. Angles 60, 70 and 50: acute-angled. An equilateral triangle, with three angles of 60, is acute-angled.

A right-angled triangle has one angle of exactly 90 degrees. The side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse, always the longest side; the other two sides are the legs, and they are perpendicular to each other. Legs 3 and 4 with hypotenuse 5 is the best-known example. The two angles other than the right angle add to 90, since all three add to 180; they are complementary, and both are acute.

An obtuse-angled triangle has one angle greater than 90 degrees. Angles 110, 40 and 30: obtuse-angled. The other two angles must be acute and must add to less than 90. The longest side is opposite the obtuse angle.

A triangle cannot have two right angles, because 90 plus 90 is already 180 and the third angle would be zero. It cannot have two obtuse angles, nor a right angle and an obtuse angle, for the same reason. So the classification by angles is decided by the largest angle alone: acute means largest under 90, right means largest exactly 90, obtuse means largest over 90.

Reading the type from a diagram: a small square means right-angled. A marked angle over 90 means obtuse-angled. No square and no obtuse angle marked means you need the values — the diagram is not to scale, and an angle that looks like 90 is not 90 unless marked.

Reading the type from the three angles when only two are given: find the third by subtraction from 180, then look at the largest. Angles 35 and 55: the third is 180 minus 35 minus 55, which is 90. Right-angled. Angles 20 and 50: the third is 110. Obtuse-angled. Angles 60 and 65: the third is 55. Acute-angled.

The angle classification says nothing about the sides, and the side classification says nothing directly about the angles; a triangle gets one name from each list, and the next section pairs them up. Questions on angles follow.

## Subtopic: Combining the Two Classifications

Every triangle has one name by sides and one by angles, and the two names are combined: a right-angled isosceles triangle, an obtuse-angled scalene triangle, an acute-angled isosceles triangle. Some combinations are common, some are possible but unusual, and some are impossible.

Scalene pairs with anything. A scalene acute-angled triangle: sides 5, 6, 7. A scalene right-angled triangle: sides 3, 4, 5. A scalene obtuse-angled triangle: sides 4, 5, 8, where the angle opposite the 8 is obtuse.

Isosceles pairs with anything too. An isosceles acute-angled triangle: legs 6, base 4, apex angle about 39 degrees. An isosceles right-angled triangle: the right angle is the apex, the base angles are each 45 degrees, and the legs are equal — the shape of a set square cut from a square along its diagonal. An isosceles obtuse-angled triangle: apex 120, base angles 30 each — the obtuse angle must be the apex, because the two base angles are equal and two obtuse angles are impossible.

Equilateral pairs with acute-angled only. Every angle is 60, so an equilateral triangle is always acute-angled; a right-angled equilateral triangle or an obtuse-angled equilateral triangle cannot exist.

So the full set of possible combinations is seven: scalene with each of the three angle types, isosceles with each of the three, and equilateral acute-angled. Two combinations are impossible: equilateral right-angled and equilateral obtuse-angled.

Describing a triangle fully means giving both names where the question asks for a classification, or giving the one name the question asks for. Classify by sides and angles: isosceles, right-angled. Classify by sides: isosceles. Match the answer to the question.

Sketching to a description is the reverse skill. Sketch an obtuse-angled isosceles triangle: draw a wide apex angle, make the two sides from it equal with dashes, join the base. Sketch a right-angled scalene triangle: a right angle with legs of visibly different lengths, hypotenuse joining them, no dashes. The sketch need not be accurate, but it must show the marks that make the classification true. Questions on combining follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same definitions as stories about a road sign, a roof and a set square, a learner sorting a box of cardboard triangles for a Grade 4 class, and a carpenter who checks a corner with nothing but a tape measure — stories about recognising a triangle's type from the marks it carries.

## Subtopic: The Road Sign, the Roof and the Set Square

A learner walking home starts noticing triangles and sorting them. The yield sign at the corner is a triangle pointing down, every side the same length and every corner the same. She measures one later from a photograph against the known width: all three sides equal. Equilateral, and so acute-angled, every angle 60. The sign is designed that way so that it reads the same whichever side you approach from, though the point-down orientation is what drivers learn.

The gable end of the house next door is a triangle: the two sloping roof edges and the top of the wall. The two slopes are built to the same pitch and meet at the ridge, so the two sloping sides are equal: isosceles, with the wall as base and the ridge as apex. The pitch is gentle, perhaps 25 degrees at each base angle, so the apex is 180 minus 25 minus 25, which is 130: obtuse. An isosceles obtuse-angled triangle, the commonest roof shape in the suburb.

In her school bag is a set square — the 45-degree one. Two equal legs meeting at a right angle, hypotenuse across. Isosceles right-angled, base angles 45 and 45. Her other set square, the 60-30 one, has a right angle too but its legs are different lengths, since the angles opposite them are 60 and 30: scalene right-angled.

A triangular slice of pizza box lid, torn off: three different sides, no right angle, one wide corner. Scalene obtuse-angled. A paper aeroplane's wing: scalene acute-angled, because all three corners are sharp and the sides all differ.

She writes the list. Equilateral acute: the yield sign. Isosceles obtuse: the roof. Isosceles right: the 45 set square. Scalene right: the 60-30 set square. Scalene obtuse: the pizza box. Scalene acute: the wing. Isosceles acute: she has to look for one, and finds it in the pediment above the library door, where the roof is steep enough that the apex is under 90.

Seven triangles, seven combinations, and no equilateral with a right or obtuse angle anywhere, because none can exist. She also notes the marks she used: measured equal sides for isosceles and equilateral; the square corner of the set squares for right-angled; the wide corner for obtuse. The marks, not the look, decided every case. Questions on the walk home follow.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Box of Cardboard Triangles

A Grade 8 is asked to sort a box of cardboard triangles for a Grade 4 teacher, who wants them in labelled bags by type. There are twenty-four triangles of different sizes and colours, and the Grade 4s have scribbled on some, so the sorting has to go by measurement, not appearance.

He sets up two tests. Sides: measure all three with a ruler; three equal, equilateral; two equal, isosceles; none equal, scalene. Angles: check the largest corner against the corner of a sheet of paper; fits exactly, right-angled; smaller, acute-angled; wider, obtuse-angled. Every triangle gets one result from each test.

The first triangle has sides 10, 10 and 10 centimetres. Equilateral. The largest corner is smaller than the paper corner. Acute. Bag: equilateral acute-angled. The second has sides 12, 12 and 17; two equal, isosceles; the corner between the two equal sides fits the paper corner exactly. Isosceles right-angled. The third has sides 9, 12 and 15; scalene; the corner opposite the 15 fits the paper corner. Scalene right-angled.

He hits a triangle whose sides measure 10, 10 and 10,2. He decides it is isosceles, not equilateral, because the third side is measurably different; then he realises the Grade 4s cut it badly from an equilateral template, and that for the Grade 4 bag the intention matters less than the measurement. It goes in the isosceles acute bag with a note.

A triangle with sides 7, 7 and 13 has a very wide apex. Isosceles obtuse-angled. A thin sliver with sides 5, 11 and 14: scalene obtuse-angled, the wide corner opposite the 14. He checks that each of the two short sides together exceed the long one — 5 plus 11 is 16, more than 14 — so it really is a triangle and not a near-miss.

At the end he has seven bags and one empty label he had prepared in advance: equilateral right-angled. Nothing went in it, and nothing ever could. He writes on the label why: three equal sides force three equal angles of 60, so no right angle is possible. The Grade 4 teacher keeps the empty bag as a talking point.

His method for the Grade 4s, written on the lid: measure the sides, count the equals; find the biggest corner, compare with a paper corner; two words per triangle. Questions on sorting follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for classifying triangles. One: read the marks — dashes for equal sides, arcs for equal angles, a square for a right angle — and any given lengths or angles. Two: classify by sides — scalene, no equal sides; isosceles, at least two; equilateral, three — using the most specific name. Three: classify by angles — acute-angled, right-angled, obtuse-angled — by the largest angle, finding the third angle by subtraction from 180 if two are given. Four: combine the two names if asked for both. Five: for a sketch, draw the marks that make the description true. Six: remember the impossible pairs — equilateral right-angled, equilateral obtuse-angled — and the reason: three equal sides force three angles of 60.

A typical examination item gives a diagram with marks and asks for a classification by sides and angles. Two words, read from the marks, not from the picture.

Another gives three side lengths. Count the equal ones. If asked whether the lengths can form a triangle at all, check that each pair of sides sums to more than the third.

Another gives two angles and asks for the type by angles. Subtract to get the third, then look at the largest.

Another asks for a definition. State it exactly: an isosceles triangle is a triangle with at least two equal sides; a right-angled triangle is a triangle with one angle of 90 degrees.

Another asks for a sketch of a stated type, with marks. Draw it, mark it, and label the vertices.

Another asks whether a described triangle can exist. Use the angle sum: two angles of 95 cannot; sides 2, 3, 7 cannot.

Errors to avoid. Calling an equilateral triangle isosceles in a classification. Deciding right-angled from appearance without a square mark. Assuming the base of an isosceles triangle is at the bottom. Forgetting that the hypotenuse is opposite the right angle, not merely the longest-looking side. Claiming a triangle with two obtuse angles.

Looking ahead: the next lesson investigates the angle facts in triangles — the sum of 180, the 60s of the equilateral, the equal base angles of the isosceles — by construction and measurement, and uses them in calculations. Grade 9 proves them and uses them in congruence. Final questions follow.
