# Part 1 — Expert

This session identifies and describes the properties of congruent and similar shapes. Two figures are congruent when they have the same shape and the same size: corresponding sides are equal and corresponding angles are equal, and one figure can be moved onto the other by sliding, turning or flipping. Two figures are similar when they have the same shape but not necessarily the same size: corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are in the same ratio, called the scale factor, so that one is an enlargement or reduction of the other. Congruent figures are similar with scale factor 1. The anchor figures are two triangles with sides 3, 4, 5, a triangle with sides 6, 8, 10, rectangles of 2 by 3 and 4 by 6, and a rectangle of 3 by 5.

## Subtopic: Congruent Shapes: Same Shape and Same Size

Two figures are congruent when one is an exact copy of the other: the same shape and the same size. Place one on the other, possibly after sliding it, turning it or flipping it over, and they match edge for edge. The symbol is three horizontal lines, like an equals sign with an extra bar: triangle ABC is congruent to triangle DEF.

Congruence means that every part matches. Corresponding sides are equal: AB equals DE, BC equals EF, CA equals FD. Corresponding angles are equal: the angle at A equals the angle at D, B equals E, C equals F. Corresponding means in the matching position, and the order of the letters in the statement carries that information: in triangle ABC congruent to triangle DEF, A matches D, B matches E and C matches F, in that order. Writing the letters in the wrong order is a wrong statement, even if the triangles are congruent, because it pairs the wrong parts.

Two triangles with sides 3, 4 and 5 are congruent, wherever they are on the page and however they are turned. A triangle with sides 3, 4, 5 drawn point-up and another drawn point-down, mirror images of each other, are congruent: flipping is allowed. Congruence is about the figure itself, not its position or orientation.

Congruent figures have equal perimeters and equal areas, since every length matches. The converse fails: two figures with equal areas need not be congruent — a 2 by 6 rectangle and a 3 by 4 rectangle both have area 12 and are not the same shape.

Testing for congruence in Grade 8 means comparing all the sides and all the angles. For triangles, Grade 9 shows that three matching sides are enough, or two sides and the included angle, or two angles and a side — the congruence conditions. For now, check everything and state what matches.

Where congruence appears. The two triangles made by a diagonal of a parallelogram are congruent, which is why opposite sides are equal. Identical tiles are congruent. The two halves of a symmetric figure, either side of its line of symmetry, are congruent, with one flipped onto the other. Questions on congruence follow now.

## Subtopic: Similar Shapes: Same Shape, Any Size

Two figures are similar when they have the same shape but not necessarily the same size: one is an enlargement or a reduction of the other. The symbol is three vertical lines: triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF. As with congruence, the letter order pairs the corresponding parts.

Similar figures have two properties. Corresponding angles are equal — the same angles as the original, so the shape is unchanged. Corresponding sides are in the same ratio — every side of the second figure is the same multiple of the matching side of the first. That multiple is the scale factor.

A triangle with sides 3, 4, 5 and a triangle with sides 6, 8, 10. Ratios of corresponding sides: 6 over 3 is 2; 8 over 4 is 2; 10 over 5 is 2. All equal, so the sides are in the same ratio, scale factor 2, and the triangles are similar. Their angles match: both are right-angled with the same two acute angles, since a triangle's angles are fixed by its side proportions.

A rectangle 2 by 3 and a rectangle 4 by 6. Ratios: 4 over 2 is 2; 6 over 3 is 2. Similar, scale factor 2; the angles are all 90 in both, so they match automatically. A rectangle 2 by 3 and a rectangle 3 by 5: ratios 3 over 2 is 1,5 and 5 over 3 is about 1,67. Not equal, so not similar, even though every angle is 90. For quadrilaterals, equal angles are not enough; the side ratios must match too. For triangles, equal angles alone are enough, because the angles fix the shape — Grade 9 proves it.

Scale factor less than 1 means a reduction: the 6, 8, 10 triangle reduced to 3, 4, 5 has scale factor one half. Scale factor exactly 1 means the figures are congruent: congruent figures are a special case of similar figures.

Perimeter scales by the scale factor: the 3, 4, 5 triangle has perimeter 12 and the 6, 8, 10 has perimeter 24, twice. Area scales by the square of the scale factor — 6 and 24, four times — which Term 4 investigates under enlargement.

Testing for similarity: pair up the corresponding sides, shortest with shortest and so on, divide each pair the same way round, and check that every quotient is the same; then check the corresponding angles are equal. Questions on similarity follow.

## Subtopic: Scale Factor and Missing Lengths

Once two figures are known to be similar, the scale factor finds any missing side. Triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF, with AB equal to 4, BC equal to 6, and DE equal to 10. First the scale factor from a known pair: DE over AB is 10 over 4, which is 2,5. Then EF, the side corresponding to BC, is 6 times 2,5, which is 15. If CA is 5, then FD is 5 times 2,5, which is 12,5.

The direction of the ratio matters. From the small to the large, multiply by the scale factor; from the large to the small, divide by it, or multiply by its reciprocal. If FD is 12,5 and the scale factor from ABC to DEF is 2,5, then CA is 12,5 divided by 2,5, which is 5. Decide which figure is the original and which the image, and keep that direction for every side.

Pair sides correctly. In triangles, the corresponding sides are opposite the corresponding equal angles; if the triangles are not labelled in matching order, find the equal angles first, or match the shortest side with the shortest and the longest with the longest. In a diagram where a small triangle sits inside a larger one sharing a vertex and with parallel bases, the sides along the shared arms correspond and the parallel bases correspond.

A photograph 10 centimetres by 15 is enlarged so that its short side is 25. Scale factor 25 over 10, which is 2,5. The long side becomes 15 times 2,5, which is 37,5 centimetres. A map at a scale of 1 to 50 000 shows two towns 4 centimetres apart; the real distance is 4 times 50 000, which is 200 000 centimetres, or 2 kilometres. A scale is a scale factor written as a ratio.

Checking. Every pair of corresponding sides must give the same quotient. If three pairs give 2,5 and one gives 2,4, either a side was mis-paired or the figures are not similar. Angles must match too, and in a diagram they are often marked with matching arcs to show which vertices correspond.

Writing the solution: state that the triangles are similar and why, if that is given; write the scale factor with its pair; multiply or divide for each missing side; give units. Questions on scale factors follow.

## Subtopic: Which Shapes Are Always Similar, and Which Are Not

Some families of shapes are similar to every other member of the family, because the definition fixes the proportions. Any two squares are similar: all angles 90, all sides equal, so the ratio of any side to any corresponding side is the same single number. Any two circles are similar, with scale factor the ratio of the radii. Any two equilateral triangles are similar: all angles 60, all sides equal within each. Any two regular polygons with the same number of sides — regular pentagons, regular hexagons — are similar.

Other families are not. Two rectangles need not be similar: 2 by 3 and 3 by 5 have equal angles but different proportions. Two rhombuses need not be similar: equal sides within each, but one may have angles of 60 and 120 and the other 80 and 100. Two isosceles triangles need not be similar: one tall and thin, one short and wide. Two right-angled triangles need not be similar: 3, 4, 5 and 5, 12, 13 have different acute angles. The definition of these families leaves a proportion or an angle free, and the free part can differ.

For triangles specifically, equal angles are enough for similarity: if two triangles have the same three angles, their sides are automatically in the same ratio. So two right-angled triangles that share one acute angle are similar; two isosceles triangles with the same apex angle are similar; any triangle and the triangle cut off it by a line parallel to one side are similar. For shapes with four or more sides, equal angles are not enough — the rectangles show it — and the side ratios must be checked separately.

Congruence versus similarity for the same families. Two squares of side 5 are congruent; two squares of sides 5 and 7 are similar but not congruent. Two circles of radius 3 are congruent; radius 3 and radius 6, similar with scale factor 2. Two equilateral triangles of side 4: congruent. Congruence needs the size to match as well.

A useful way to say it: similar shapes agree on everything except size; congruent shapes agree on size as well. The families that are always similar are exactly those whose definition leaves only size free. Questions on always-similar families follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas as stories about a photograph enlarged for a wall and one stretched to fit a screen, a set of identical paving tiles laid every way up, and a learner who used a shadow and a stick to measure a flagpole — stories about what stays the same when a shape is copied, turned or scaled.

## Subtopic: The Photograph Enlarged and the Photograph Stretched

A family has a favourite photograph, 10 centimetres by 15, and wants it big for the lounge wall. The print shop offers an enlargement with the short side at 50 centimetres. A Grade 8 in the family checks the long side: scale factor 50 over 10, which is 5; long side 15 times 5, which is 75 centimetres. The enlarged print, 50 by 75, is similar to the original — every length five times, every angle unchanged, every face the right shape. On the wall, nobody can tell it was ever small.

Her cousin has the same photograph as a phone wallpaper and complains that everyone looks too thin. She checks his settings. The phone screen is not in the ratio 2 to 3, and the phone has been told to fill the screen, so it has stretched the picture more one way than the other: the short side by a factor of 2,4 and the long side by a factor of 1,6. Two different scale factors. The result is not similar to the original: the angles have changed, a square frame in the background has become a non-square rectangle, and the faces are the wrong shape. A stretch with different factors in two directions is not an enlargement, and the image is not similar to the original.

She fixes it by choosing fit instead of fill. Now the phone uses one scale factor for both directions, the picture is similar to the original, and there are blank bars at the top and bottom where the screen is left over. Her cousin would rather have the bars than thin relatives.

The difference between the two cases is the whole content of similarity. One scale factor for every length keeps every angle and every proportion: similar. Different factors in different directions change proportions and angles: not similar, even though it is still recognisably the same photograph.

She finds the same distinction on a photocopier. The 141 percent button enlarges A4 to A3, one factor in both directions — similar, with scale factor 1,41, which is the square root of 2, so the area doubles exactly. A broken machine that enlarged 141 percent one way and 100 percent the other would produce a page similar to nothing.

The lounge print goes up, 50 by 75. The phone shows the picture with bars. Both are similar to the original; one is congruent to nothing but itself, and the stretched version is quietly deleted. Questions on the photographs follow.

## Subtopic: Paving Tiles and the Flagpole's Shadow

A paving contractor unloads two hundred tiles, each the same irregular pentagon cut from the same mould. The apprentice is told to lay them in the pattern on the plan, which shows tiles pointing in every direction, some flipped over. He asks whether flipped tiles are still the same tile. The contractor says: same mould, same tile. Every tile is congruent to every other — identical sides, identical angles — and congruence does not care which way up a thing lies. Sliding, turning and flipping are exactly the moves that take one congruent figure onto another. The pattern works because every tile is congruent; a tile from a different mould, even one similar to the others but a little larger, would not fit.

Meanwhile a learner at the school across the road has been asked to find the height of the flagpole without climbing it. She stands a metre stick upright in the sun and measures its shadow: 1,5 metres. She measures the flagpole's shadow at the same moment: 9 metres. The sun's rays are parallel, so the stick and its shadow make a right-angled triangle with the same angle at the shadow's tip as the flagpole and its shadow make. Two right-angled triangles sharing an acute angle: similar.

Scale factor from the stick's triangle to the pole's: 9 over 1,5, which is 6. The pole's height is the stick's height times 6: 1 metre times 6, which is 6 metres. She checks the other way: the ratio of height to shadow should be the same in both triangles — 1 over 1,5 for the stick, 6 over 9 for the pole; both equal two thirds. Similar triangles, consistent ratios, a flagpole measured with a stick.

She repeats it an hour later, when the shadows are shorter: stick 1 metre, shadow 1,2 metres; pole shadow 7,2 metres. Scale factor 7,2 over 1,2, which is 6 again; pole 6 metres again. The triangles have changed shape — a steeper sun — but each pair at each moment is similar, and the pole has not grown.

The contractor's tiles and the learner's triangles are the two ends of the lesson. Congruent: same mould, any orientation, exact fit. Similar: same angles, one scale factor, and a length you cannot reach calculated from one you can. Questions on tiles and shadows follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for congruence and similarity. One: identify corresponding vertices — by letter order in the statement, by matching angle marks, or by matching shortest to shortest and longest to longest sides. Two: for congruence, check that every corresponding side is equal and every corresponding angle is equal; orientation and flipping are irrelevant. Three: for similarity, check that corresponding angles are equal and compute the ratio of each pair of corresponding sides the same way round; all ratios equal means similar, and the common value is the scale factor. Four: for a missing length in similar figures, find the scale factor from a known pair, then multiply from the original to the image or divide from the image to the original. Five: remember the families — squares, circles, equilateral triangles always similar; rectangles, rhombuses, isosceles and right-angled triangles not necessarily; for triangles alone, equal angles suffice. Six: give units and check that every ratio agrees.

A typical examination item shows two triangles with sides marked and asks whether they are congruent, similar or neither, with a reason. Compare sides: all equal, congruent; all in one ratio, similar; otherwise neither.

Another gives two similar triangles with some sides and asks for the missing ones. Scale factor, then multiply or divide.

Another asks for a scale factor and the enlarged perimeter. Perimeter multiplies by the factor.

Another asks for a statement such as all rectangles are similar: true or false, with an example. False; 2 by 3 and 3 by 5.

Another describes shadows or a scale drawing. Set up the similar triangles or the scale, find the factor, calculate.

Errors to avoid. Pairing sides in the wrong order. Mixing directions, multiplying when you should divide. Declaring quadrilaterals similar from angles alone. Concluding similarity from equal perimeters or areas. Saying flipped figures are not congruent. Forgetting that congruent figures are also similar.
