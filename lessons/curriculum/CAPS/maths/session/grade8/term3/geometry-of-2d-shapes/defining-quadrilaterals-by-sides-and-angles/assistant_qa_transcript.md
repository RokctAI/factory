### subtopic_2 — Trapezium and Parallelogram
**Assistant:** Why does CAPS define a trapezium as having at least one pair of parallel sides rather than exactly one?

**Tutor:** Because the inclusive definition makes the family tree work cleanly. With at least one pair, every parallelogram is a trapezium, so anything proved about trapeziums — such as the two angles at the ends of a leg adding to 180 — applies automatically to parallelograms, rectangles, rhombuses and squares as well, and the area formula for a trapezium gives the parallelogram's area as a special case. With exactly one pair, parallelograms would be excluded, the tree would have a gap, and every statement about trapeziums would need a separate clause for parallelograms. The inclusive choice mirrors the other definitions: a rectangle is a parallelogram with at least the property of a right angle, and a square is not excluded from being a rectangle because it has extra equal sides. Some older or overseas textbooks use the exclusive definition, so you may meet both; in a CAPS examination, use the inclusive one and, if a question seems to hinge on the difference, state the definition you are using.

### subtopic_3 — Rectangle, Rhombus and Square
**Assistant:** If a square is a rectangle, why is it wrong to answer rectangle when asked to classify a square?

**Tutor:** It is not wrong in the sense of being false — the square genuinely is a rectangle — but it is incomplete, and classification questions ask for the most specific true name. Rectangle tells the reader there are four right angles and leaves the side lengths open; square adds that the four sides are equal. Giving the less specific name when the marks show the more specific one withholds information the figure provides, and examiners mark it as not fully correct. The same convention runs through the whole tree: a figure with two pairs of parallel sides and a right angle is called a rectangle, not a parallelogram, even though parallelogram is true. The exception is when the question asks something other than a classification — is this figure a rectangle, yes or no — in which case yes is the complete answer for a square. Read what the question asks for: a name, or a yes or no about a particular name.

### subtopic_4 — The Kite and the Family Tree
**Assistant:** A kite with sides 3, 3, 5, 5: how do I know the equal angles are at the vertices where a 3 meets a 5?

**Tutor:** From the kite's line of symmetry. The diagonal joining the vertex where the two 3s meet to the vertex where the two 5s meet is a line of symmetry: folding along it lands one 3 on the other 3 and one 5 on the other 5, so the two halves match exactly. The two remaining vertices — each where a 3 meets a 5 — are mirror images of each other under that fold, so the angles at them are equal. The angles at the ends of the symmetry diagonal, by contrast, are each bisected by the fold rather than matched to each other, and they are generally different: the angle between the two short sides is wider than the angle between the two long sides. A rhombus is the special kite in which all four sides are equal; then both diagonals are lines of symmetry, both pairs of opposite angles are equal, and the figure is also a parallelogram, which is why it sits under both kite and parallelogram in the tree.

### subtopic_5 — The Gate That Sagged
**Assistant:** Why does a quadrilateral with fixed side lengths not have fixed angles, when a triangle does?

**Tutor:** Because three sides leave no freedom and four sides leave one. When you construct a triangle from three given sides with compasses, the arcs from the two ends of the base cross at exactly one point above the base, so the third vertex has only one possible position and the angles are forced. With four given sides, you can fix three vertices and then the fourth is at the crossing of two arcs again — but the third vertex itself was not forced: the angle at the first corner can be opened or closed, moving the third vertex along a circle, and for each position there is a valid fourth vertex. So a four-sided frame has one degree of freedom, which is the sag. A diagonal brace removes that freedom by dividing the quadrilateral into two triangles, each with three fixed sides and therefore fixed angles. This is why trusses, gates and bicycle frames are built from triangles, and why the next lesson's angle-sum argument splits a quadrilateral along a diagonal into two triangles.
