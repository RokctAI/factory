# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates the interior angle sum of quadrilaterals and the sides and opposite angles of parallelograms. Measurement of constructed quadrilaterals suggests an angle sum of 360 degrees, explained by a diagonal dividing the figure into two triangles each summing to 180. Measurement of constructed parallelograms suggests that opposite sides are equal, opposite angles are equal and adjacent angles are supplementary, explained by co-interior and alternate angles between the parallel sides. The facts are applied as reasons — angle sum of a quadrilateral, opposite sides of a parallelogram, opposite angles of a parallelogram, co-interior angles — to calculate unknowns, including algebraic ones. The anchor figures are a constructed irregular quadrilateral, a parallelogram with sides 7 and 4 and one angle of 70 degrees, and a trapezium with parallel sides.

## Subtopic: Investigating the Angle Sum of a Quadrilateral

Construct any quadrilateral — four straight sides, no particular shape — and measure its four interior angles with a protractor. A careful construction gives something like 112, 84, 97 and 67. Add: 112 plus 84 is 196; plus 97 is 293; plus 67 is 360. Construct a second, long and thin, and a third with one very wide angle. Measure and add: 360 each time, within a degree or two of protractor error. Thirty learners, thirty quadrilaterals, thirty totals clustering around 360.

The explanation uses the triangle fact. Draw one diagonal of the quadrilateral ABCD, say AC. It splits the figure into two triangles, ABC and ACD. The angles of triangle ABC add to 180; the angles of triangle ACD add to 180. Every interior angle of the quadrilateral is either an angle of one triangle — B and D — or is split by the diagonal into two parts, one in each triangle — A and C. So the four angles of the quadrilateral together equal all six angles of the two triangles, which is 180 plus 180, giving 360. The written reason when the fact is used: angle sum of a quadrilateral, or interior angles of a quadrilateral.

This is also why the torn-corner trick from the triangle lesson gives a full turn for a quadrilateral: four corners filling 360 degrees around a point.

The argument needs the diagonal to lie inside the figure, which it does for the quadrilaterals in Grade 8 — convex ones, where every interior angle is less than 180. A quadrilateral with a reflex angle, shaped like an arrowhead, still has an angle sum of 360, using the other diagonal, but it is not met in this grade.

Calculations follow. Three angles of a quadrilateral are 80, 95 and 110; the fourth is 360 minus their sum. 80 plus 95 is 175; plus 110 is 285; 360 minus 285 is 75. Fourth angle 75 degrees, angle sum of a quadrilateral. A quadrilateral with three right angles has a fourth of 360 minus 270, which is 90 — so it is a rectangle, since a quadrilateral with four right angles has both pairs of opposite sides parallel by co-interior angles.

The fact extends: a pentagon splits into three triangles from one vertex, so its angles add to 540; a hexagon into four, 720. Each extra side adds a triangle and 180 degrees. Grade 8 needs the quadrilateral; the pattern is a look ahead. Questions on the angle sum follow now.

## Subtopic: Investigating the Sides of a Parallelogram

Construct a parallelogram: draw a line, mark a 7-centimetre side AB on it; at A draw a second line at 70 degrees and mark 4 centimetres to D; through D draw a line parallel to AB using a set square slid along a ruler; through B draw a line parallel to AD the same way; call their crossing C. Measure DC and BC. DC measures 7 centimetres, the same as AB; BC measures 4, the same as AD. Construct another with different lengths and angle; the same happens. The opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal in length.

The explanation uses triangles and will be a full congruence proof in Grade 9. Draw the diagonal AC. In triangles ABC and CDA, the angle BAC equals the angle DCA, alternate angles, AB parallel to DC; the angle BCA equals the angle DAC, alternate angles, AD parallel to BC; and AC is shared. Two angles and the included side match, so the triangles are congruent — identical — and their remaining sides match: AB equals CD and BC equals DA. Grade 8 records the fact and uses it: opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal. Written reason: opposite sides of a parallelogram.

The converse also holds and is useful for recognition: a quadrilateral whose opposite sides are both equal is a parallelogram.

Calculations. A parallelogram has sides AB equal to 7 and AD equal to 4. Then DC is 7 and BC is 4, opposite sides of a parallelogram. Its perimeter is 7 plus 4 plus 7 plus 4, which is 22 — or twice the sum of two adjacent sides, 2 times 11.

Algebra. A parallelogram has AB marked 3x minus 2 and DC marked 2x plus 5. Opposite sides are equal: 3x minus 2 equals 2x plus 5, so x equals 7, and each of those sides is 3 times 7 minus 2, which is 19; check 2 times 7 plus 5 is 19.

The special parallelograms inherit the fact. A rectangle's opposite sides are equal — the 6 by 4. A rhombus and a square go further: all four sides equal, by definition.

Equal opposite sides are what let a parallelogram be built from two identical pairs of rails, and what the sagging gate kept when it lost its right angles. Questions on sides follow.

## Subtopic: Investigating the Angles of a Parallelogram

Return to the constructed parallelogram ABCD with the angle at A equal to 70 degrees. Measure the other three. The angle at C measures 70, the same as A. The angles at B and D each measure 110. Construct another parallelogram with a different angle; again the opposite angles match and the adjacent ones add to 180. The opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal; the adjacent angles are supplementary.

The explanation is the parallel-line pairs. The angles at A and B are co-interior between the parallel sides AD and BC, with AB as the transversal, so A plus B is 180. The angles at B and C are co-interior between AB and DC, with BC as the transversal, so B plus C is 180. Both A and C equal 180 minus B, so A equals C. Likewise B equals D. Written reasons: co-interior angles, AD parallel to BC, for the sum to 180; opposite angles of a parallelogram, for the equality.

Calculations. One angle of a parallelogram is 70. The opposite angle is 70, opposite angles of a parallelogram. Each adjacent angle is 180 minus 70, which is 110, co-interior angles. Check: 70 plus 110 plus 70 plus 110 is 360, angle sum of a quadrilateral.

Algebra. Opposite angles are marked x plus 30 and 2x minus 60. Equal: x plus 30 equals 2x minus 60, so x equals 90, and each is 120. The adjacent angles are 60 each. Adjacent angles marked 3x and x plus 20: co-interior, 4x plus 20 equals 180, 4x equals 160, x equals 40; angles 120 and 60.

The special cases. A rectangle has a right angle; the adjacent angles are 180 minus 90, which is 90, so all four are right angles — this is why one right angle defines the rectangle. A rhombus has two acute angles and two obtuse angles, equal in opposite pairs, unless it is a square.

A parallelogram's angles are fully determined by any one of them: one value gives the opposite, and 180 minus it gives the other two. A trapezium is less generous: only the pairs at the ends of each leg add to 180, and the opposite angles need not be equal. Questions on angles of a parallelogram follow.

## Subtopic: Calculating in Quadrilaterals with Reasons

The facts combine in multi-step calculations, each step written as statement, value, reason.

A quadrilateral has angles 3x, 2x plus 10, x plus 50 and 4x. Angle sum: 3x plus 2x plus 10 plus x plus 50 plus 4x equals 360. Collect: 10x plus 60 equals 360, so 10x equals 300, x equals 30. Angles: 90, 70, 80 and 120. Check: 90 plus 70 is 160; plus 80 is 240; plus 120 is 360.

A trapezium ABCD has AB parallel to DC, angle A equal to 115 and angle B equal to 80. The angle D is co-interior with A along the leg AD, so D is 180 minus 115, which is 65. The angle C is co-interior with B along BC, so C is 180 minus 80, which is 100. Check: 115 plus 80 plus 65 plus 100 is 360.

A kite has equal angles at the two vertices where unequal sides meet. If the angles at the ends of the symmetry diagonal are 40 and 100, the other two angles share 360 minus 140, which is 220, equally: 110 each.

A rhombus has one angle of 50. Being a parallelogram, its opposite angle is 50 and its other two are 130 each. Its diagonals are lines of symmetry, so each diagonal bisects the angles it passes through: the diagonal through the 50-degree vertices makes angles of 25 with the sides there.

A parallelogram has a side extended past a vertex to form an exterior angle of 65. The interior angle at that vertex is 180 minus 65, which is 115, angles on a straight line; the opposite interior angle is 115, opposite angles of a parallelogram; the other two are 65 each, co-interior angles.

Sides and angles together. A parallelogram has perimeter 30 and one side 9. The opposite side is 9, so the other two sides total 30 minus 18, which is 12, and each is 6. If it is also given that one angle is 90, it is a rectangle 9 by 6.

In every case the reasons are drawn from a short list: angle sum of a quadrilateral; opposite sides of a parallelogram; opposite angles of a parallelogram; co-interior angles with the parallel sides named; angles on a straight line; base angles of an isosceles triangle when a diagonal creates one. Mark each found value on the diagram and finish with the 360 check. Questions on calculating follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same facts as stories about four torn corners that make a full turn, a mechanic's parallelogram jack that keeps a car level however high it lifts, and a learner who tiled a floor with any quadrilateral at all — stories about why 360 is the number and why parallelograms keep their promises.

## Subtopic: Four Torn Corners

The class that tore the corners off triangles is handed a second sheet. Draw any quadrilateral, the teacher says — a boring one, a wild one, anything with four straight sides. Cut it out. Tear off the four corners. Fit them together at a point.

Thirty desks. On every one, the four corners go all the way round: a full turn, no gap, no overlap. A learner with a long thin quadrilateral has two enormous corners and two tiny ones, and the tiny ones slot into what the enormous ones leave. A learner with a quadrilateral so close to a square that it might be one has four corners that are nearly identical quarter-turns.

The teacher asks for the reason, and a hand goes up: it is two triangles. Draw a diagonal and the quadrilateral is two triangles; each triangle's corners make a half-turn; two half-turns make a full turn. The teacher draws it: the diagonal from one corner to the opposite one, and the six triangle-angles that together are exactly the four quadrilateral-angles, two of them cut in two by the diagonal.

Then the teacher takes a different approach to the same fact. She hands out a pile of identical irregular quadrilaterals cut from card — all the same shape, an ugly one with no equal sides and no equal angles — and asks the class to tile the desk with them, no gaps, no overlaps. It takes a while. The trick turns out to be rotating each tile a half-turn relative to its neighbour, so that at every point where four tiles meet, the four different corners of the shape come together — one of each. Since the four corners add to 360, they fit around the point exactly. Any quadrilateral tiles the plane, which is a strange fact to hold in your hands.

The torn corners and the tiling are the same fact twice: the four angles fill a full turn. The diagonal is the reason it must be so.

In the exercise books it goes down in examination form: the interior angles of a quadrilateral add to 360 degrees; reason, angle sum of a quadrilateral; explanation, a diagonal divides it into two triangles. And a note that the teacher's ugly quadrilateral covered the whole desk. Questions on the torn corners follow.

## Subtopic: The Parallelogram Jack

A mechanic lifts a car with a scissor jack: two pairs of arms hinged into a parallelogram, with a screw across the middle. As the screw turns, the arms swing, the parallelogram gets taller and narrower, and the top plate rises — but the plate stays flat and parallel to the ground the whole way up. A learner on holiday work asks why it does not tilt.

The mechanic lets him work it out. The four arms are the four sides, and the two pairs of arms are equal — that is how the jack was made. Opposite sides equal: a parallelogram, by the converse fact, and therefore the top arm pair is always parallel to the bottom. The ground plate is fixed horizontal; the top plate, parallel to it, is horizontal too, at every height. The angles change as it lifts — the acute angles at the ends open towards 90, the obtuse angles at top and bottom close towards 90 — but the parallelism never goes.

The learner measures the angle at one end of the jack with the car partly raised: 50 degrees. Without touching anything else, he writes the other three: the opposite end is also 50, opposite angles of a parallelogram; the top and bottom are each 180 minus 50, which is 130, co-interior angles. Sum 360, as a check. He turns the screw a few times and measures again: 70 at the end. Then 70, 110, 110. The pattern holds at every height.

He also notices when the jack is strongest. At its lowest, the end angles are small and the arms nearly flat, and the screw works hard against a long lever. As the arms steepen, lifting gets easier. At a square — four right angles, the rhombus position, since all four arms happen to be the same length on this jack — the geometry is most compact. The mechanic says that is why the instructions say not to use the jack fully extended or nearly flat.

The learner's note for his geometry book: the jack is a rhombus, since all four arms are equal; it is a parallelogram at every height; its opposite angles are equal; its adjacent angles add to 180; and the plate stays level because opposite sides of a parallelogram are parallel, whatever the angles do. The facts of the lesson are the reason a car can be lifted level with one hand. Questions on the jack follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for quadrilateral angle and side calculations. One: read the marks — arrowheads, dashes, squares — and the given values; name the figure if the marks allow. Two: the facts and their written reasons: the interior angles add to 360, angle sum of a quadrilateral; in a parallelogram, opposite sides equal, opposite sides of a parallelogram; opposite angles equal, opposite angles of a parallelogram; adjacent angles supplementary, co-interior angles with the parallel sides named. Three: in a trapezium, only the leg-end pairs are co-interior; in a kite, the angles where unequal sides meet are equal; in a rhombus, all four sides are equal and the diagonals bisect the angles. Four: for algebraic unknowns, choose the equation that matches the fact — a sum to 360, an equality, a sum to 180 — solve, substitute, check. Five: for perimeters, use equal opposite sides. Six: mark each found value and check the four angles add to 360.

A typical examination item gives three angles of a quadrilateral and asks for the fourth with a reason. Subtract from 360.

Another gives one angle of a parallelogram and asks for the rest. Opposite equal; adjacent 180 minus.

Another gives algebraic angles of a quadrilateral. Sum to 360, solve, substitute.

Another gives algebraic opposite sides or opposite angles of a parallelogram. Set equal, solve.

Another gives a trapezium with two angles on the same parallel side and asks for the others. Leg-end pairs add to 180.

Another asks why the angle sum is 360, or why opposite angles of a parallelogram are equal. Diagonal into two triangles; co-interior pairs with the same neighbour.

Errors to avoid. Using 180 for a quadrilateral. Treating a trapezium's opposite angles as equal. Setting adjacent angles of a parallelogram equal instead of summing to 180. Forgetting that the perimeter uses both pairs of equal sides. Omitting reasons or the parallel lines in a co-interior reason. Not checking the 360.

Looking ahead: the next lesson defines congruent and similar shapes, the ideas behind why the two triangles in a parallelogram match and why a scaled copy keeps its angles. Grade 9 proves the parallelogram properties through congruence and adds the diagonals. Final questions follow.
