### subtopic_1 — Planning a Route Through a Multi-Step Diagram
**Assistant:** How do I know which route to take when there are several possible chains?

**Tutor:** Any valid chain earns full marks, so choose the shortest one you can name every step of. Work backwards from the target: ask which angle would give the target in one step — its vertically opposite partner, its straight-line neighbour, or its corresponding, alternate or co-interior partner across the parallels — and then ask the same of that angle, until you land on a given. Usually two or three steps suffice. If a route stalls because the two angles you want to link are not a named pair, do not force it; back up and try a different partner of the target. Writing the route forwards from the given then reads cleanly. When the problem has many unknowns, the order is set by ease: find the angles one step from a given first, mark them on the diagram, and treat them as new givens. Two routes to the same angle are not wasted; the second is a check, and in an examination the first correct route is what is marked.

### subtopic_2 — Equations from Angles on a Straight Line and Vertically Opposite Angles
**Assistant:** Why does the question want the angles when x is the thing I solved for?

**Tutor:** Because x is a label of convenience and the angles are the geometry. The expressions 2x plus 10 and 3x minus 40 describe two angles; x is just the number that makes those expressions true in this diagram, and on its own it tells a reader nothing about the figure. Reporting the angles — 94 and 86 degrees — states what the diagram actually contains and allows the check that they add to 180, which the value x equals 42 cannot show directly. Examiners allocate marks separately: for the equation and its reason, for solving to x, and for the angles; stopping at x forfeits the last set. The habit to build is to read the question's last line before writing — does it ask for x, for the angles, or for both — and to end with exactly that, each angle written with its degree sign, followed by a one-line check against the fact you used.

### subtopic_4 — Auxiliary Lines and Proving Lines Parallel
**Assistant:** Is drawing an auxiliary line cheating? The line is not in the original diagram.

**Tutor:** No; it is a standard and accepted move, as long as you state it. Geometry permits constructing any line whose existence is guaranteed — through a given point there is exactly one line parallel to a given line — and reasoning about the enlarged figure. The step to write is: draw a line through the bend parallel to the given parallel lines. After that, every angle pair you quote genuinely exists in the figure you have drawn, so your reasons are valid. What would be cheating is quoting an alternate pair without the line, because then the two angles are not between two parallel lines cut by one transversal and the reason does not apply. Auxiliary lines appear throughout geometry: extending a side of a triangle to show the exterior angle, dropping a perpendicular, joining two points. The rule is the same each time — construct, state the construction, then reason.

### subtopic_6 — The Bent Pipe Between Two Walls
**Assistant:** What if the given angles are on the other side of the bend, so the parts subtract instead of adding?

**Tutor:** Then the auxiliary line still works, but the arithmetic changes to a difference, and the diagram tells you which. If the path bends towards the parallels rather than away from them — so that one leg of the path doubles back — the angle at the bend on the side facing the parallels is not split into two pieces by the auxiliary line; instead, one alternate angle lies inside the other, and the bend angle is the larger alternate angle minus the smaller. For example, leaving the top line at 110 degrees measured on one side and meeting the bottom line at 30 on the same side gives a bend angle of 110 minus 30, which is 80, if the geometry puts the 30 inside the 110. The safe method is not to memorise add or subtract but to draw the auxiliary line, mark each alternate angle where it actually sits, and read off from the figure whether the wanted angle is the union of the two pieces or the gap between them. A diagram drawn large enough to see this is worth the extra half-minute.
