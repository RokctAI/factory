# Part 1 — Expert

This session solves geometric problems using the relationships between pairs of angles. Multi-step problems are approached by identifying the given angles, choosing a chain of relationships that reaches the required angle, and writing each step with its value and reason. Unknowns given as algebraic expressions produce linear equations from the facts that angles on a straight line sum to 180 degrees, vertically opposite angles are equal, and the parallel-line pairs are equal or supplementary. The converse relationships prove lines parallel, and an auxiliary line through a vertex resolves diagrams in which a bent line crosses parallel lines. The anchor diagrams are a single transversal with algebraic angles, two transversals through parallel lines, and a step-shaped path between parallel lines.

## Subtopic: Planning a Route Through a Multi-Step Diagram

A multi-step angle problem gives one or two angles and asks for one that is several relationships away. The method has four parts. Read the diagram: mark every given angle, note the parallel arrowheads, the right-angle squares, the straight lines that continue through points. Name the target: which angle is asked for, and where does it sit. Plan the route: which known angle is one relationship away from the target, and which is one away from that, until the chain reaches a given. Write the chain forwards from the given to the target, each step with a value and a reason.

Take two parallel lines AB and CD with a transversal EF cutting AB at G and CD at H. Angle AGE, above the top line on the left, is given as 52 degrees. The target is angle GHD, below the bottom line on the right. Plan: angle AGE and angle BGH are vertically opposite, so angle BGH is 52. Angle BGH and angle GHD are alternate angles — a Z between the parallels — so angle GHD is 52. Two steps, two reasons, done. Alternatively: angle AGE and angle CHG are corresponding, so angle CHG is 52; angle CHG and angle GHD are vertically opposite, so angle GHD is 52. Two routes, same answer; the agreement is a check.

Now suppose the target is angle GHC, below the bottom line on the left. A first attempt: angle AGE equals 52, given; angle EGB equals 180 minus 52, which is 128, angles on a straight line; but angle EGB and angle GHC are not a standard pair, so the route stalls. A better plan: angle BGH equals 52, vertically opposite angles; angle BGH and angle GHC are co-interior, so angle GHC equals 180 minus 52, which is 128, co-interior angles, AB parallel to CD. The second plan reaches the target in two clean steps. Planning before writing avoids the dead end.

Every step must rest on a stated fact, and the fact must apply to the two angles named. Vertically opposite needs the same two lines through one point. Corresponding, alternate and co-interior need the two parallel lines and the same transversal. If you cannot name the pair, you have not got a step.

When the diagram has several unknowns, find them in order of ease — the one nearest a given first — and reuse each found angle as a new given. Mark each found value on your copy of the diagram as you go; a diagram that fills in as you work is the best tool there is. Questions on planning follow now.

## Subtopic: Equations from Angles on a Straight Line and Vertically Opposite Angles

When an angle is given as an expression, the relationship produces an equation. Two adjacent angles on a straight line are marked 2x plus 10 and 3x minus 40, in degrees. Angles on a straight line add to 180: so 2x plus 10 plus 3x minus 40 equals 180. Collect: 5x minus 30 equals 180. Add 30: 5x equals 210. Divide: x equals 42. The question usually wants the angles, not x. The first is 2 times 42 plus 10, which is 84 plus 10, giving 94. The second is 3 times 42 minus 40, which is 126 minus 40, giving 86. Check: 94 plus 86 is 180.

Vertically opposite angles marked 3x plus 15 and 4x minus 20. Vertically opposite angles are equal: 3x plus 15 equals 4x minus 20. Subtract 3x: 15 equals x minus 20. Add 20: x equals 35. Each angle is 3 times 35 plus 15, which is 105 plus 15, giving 120. Check with the other expression: 4 times 35 minus 20 is 140 minus 20, which is 120. The two adjacent angles at that crossing are each 180 minus 120, which is 60.

Angles around a point marked x, 2x and 3x. Angles around a point add to 360: 6x equals 360, so x equals 60. The angles are 60, 120 and 180 — and the 180 means two of the arms form a straight line, which the diagram should show.

Complementary angles at a perpendicular: 90 equals x plus 2x minus 15, so 3x minus 15 equals 90, 3x equals 105, x equals 35. Angles 35 and 55.

The written form matters. State the relationship in words as the reason, write the equation, solve in clear lines, then substitute to find the angles and check they satisfy the original fact. An answer of x equals 42 alone, when the angles were asked for, loses the marks for the angles; angles given without the equation lose the method marks.

Watch for an answer that is impossible: a negative angle, or an angle over 180 where the diagram shows less than a straight angle. That signals a wrong relationship — perhaps 360 was used for a straight line — not a strange diagram. Questions on equations follow.

## Subtopic: Equations from Parallel-Line Pairs

The parallel-line relationships produce equations in the same way, with the reason naming the pair and the parallel lines. Corresponding and alternate angles give an equation of the form expression equals expression; co-interior angles give expression plus expression equals 180.

Co-interior angles between parallel lines PQ and RS are marked x plus 20 and 2x plus 10. Co-interior angles are supplementary: x plus 20 plus 2x plus 10 equals 180. So 3x plus 30 equals 180, 3x equals 150, x equals 50. The angles are 50 plus 20, which is 70, and 2 times 50 plus 10, which is 110. Check: 70 plus 110 is 180.

Alternate angles marked 5x minus 10 and 3x plus 30. Alternate angles are equal: 5x minus 10 equals 3x plus 30. Subtract 3x: 2x minus 10 equals 30. Add 10: 2x equals 40. So x equals 20, and each angle is 5 times 20 minus 10, which is 90. Both angles are right angles, so this transversal happens to be perpendicular to the parallel lines — a fact the diagram would not have shown, since diagrams are not to scale.

Corresponding angles marked 4x and 3x plus 25: 4x equals 3x plus 25, x equals 25, each angle 100.

A mixed problem: a transversal crosses parallel lines; at the top crossing one angle is 3x; at the bottom crossing, the co-interior partner is x plus 40. Equation: 3x plus x plus 40 equals 180, so 4x equals 140, x equals 35. The angles are 105 and 75. The remaining six angles at the two crossings are then each 105 or 75, by vertically opposite angles and angles on a straight line.

The choice of equation depends entirely on identifying the pair correctly. Writing expression equals expression for a co-interior pair, or expression plus expression equals 180 for an alternate pair, gives a confident wrong answer. Find the letter — F, Z or U — before writing anything, and quote it in the reason.

As always, the parallel lines must be given or marked. If they are not, there is no equation from these pairs, and the problem must be solved — or shown to be unsolvable — from the single-crossing facts alone. Questions on parallel-line equations follow.

## Subtopic: Auxiliary Lines and Proving Lines Parallel

Some diagrams have no transversal that crosses both parallel lines in one straight piece. A bent line — a path with a corner — runs from one parallel line to the other, and the angle at the corner is wanted. Two parallel horizontal lines; a path leaves the top line making 40 degrees with it, bends at a point between the lines, and meets the bottom line making 30 degrees with it. Find the angle at the bend, on the side facing the parallels.

Draw an auxiliary line: a third line through the bend, parallel to the other two. It splits the angle at the bend into two parts. The upper part is alternate to the 40 at the top line, so it is 40, alternate angles. The lower part is alternate to the 30 at the bottom line, so it is 30. The angle at the bend is 40 plus 30, which is 70.

The auxiliary line is a legitimate move: state it — draw a line through the bend parallel to the given lines — and then every angle pair you use exists. Without it, the problem has no standard pair to quote. The same device handles a path that bends outward, where the angle at the bend is a difference rather than a sum, and a path with two bends, which needs two auxiliary lines.

The converse relationships run the parallel-line facts backwards. If a transversal makes corresponding angles that are equal, then the two lines are parallel. If alternate angles are equal, the lines are parallel. If co-interior angles add to 180, the lines are parallel. These are used to prove that two lines are parallel from measured or given angles.

A diagram shows two lines crossed by a transversal; the angle at the top crossing, above-left, is 112 degrees; the angle at the bottom crossing, above-left, is 112 degrees. Those angles are in corresponding positions and equal, so the lines are parallel — corresponding angles equal. Another diagram: the co-interior angles are 100 and 80. Sum 180, so the lines are parallel — co-interior angles supplementary. A third: the co-interior angles are 100 and 85. Sum 185, not 180, so the lines are not parallel.

The structure of a proof of parallelism: identify the pair by position; state their values; state that they are equal, or that they sum to 180; conclude the lines are parallel, giving the converse reason. If the condition fails, conclude that the lines are not parallel, and say which pair fails. Questions on auxiliary lines and proving parallel follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problem solving as stories about a roof truss checked with two angles and no ladder, a learner who proved the school's new parking bays were parallel, and a bent pipe between two parallel walls — stories about getting from what is known to what is wanted, one reason at a time.

## Subtopic: The Roof Truss Checked from the Ground

A builder is told that the two long rafters of a new roof truss should be parallel to each other, and that the horizontal ceiling beam and the slanted brace crossing them have been fitted. He has no ladder tall enough, but he has a protractor on a long pole and a Grade 8 nephew who has just finished the straight-lines topic.

The brace crosses both rafters — a transversal across what should be parallel lines. From the ground, the builder can read the angle between the brace and the lower rafter on the left side above the rafter: 64 degrees. He can also read the angle between the brace and the upper rafter, on the left side above that rafter: 64 degrees. The nephew points out that those two are in corresponding positions. Equal corresponding angles mean the lines are parallel. The rafters are parallel, proved from the ground with two readings.

The builder wants a second check. He can read one more angle: between the brace and the upper rafter on the right side, below the rafter — the co-interior partner of the 64 at the lower rafter. The reading is 116. The nephew adds: 64 plus 116 is 180, co-interior angles supplementary, lines parallel. Two independent checks, one conclusion.

Then the builder asks for every other angle at the two crossings, for the fitter's notes. At each crossing, four angles: 64 and 116 alternating. The nephew writes them with reasons: 64, given; 116, angles on a straight line; 64, vertically opposite; 116, vertically opposite. Same at the upper crossing by corresponding angles. Eight angles, two values.

The next truss has the brace fitted at a different slant, and the builder reads 58 at the lower rafter and 60 at the upper, in corresponding positions. Not equal, so not parallel; the rafters are two degrees off, and the top one must be adjusted before the sheeting goes on. The nephew notes that a two-degree error over a six-metre rafter puts the far end about twenty centimetres out of line — something the roofer would have found much later and much more expensively.

The builder's summary for his notebook: read two corresponding angles; equal means parallel; or read two co-interior angles; 180 means parallel; anything else, fix the rafter. Questions on the roof truss follow.

## Subtopic: The Bent Pipe Between Two Walls

A plumber must run a pipe from a point on one wall to a point on the opposite, parallel wall of a narrow passage. The pipe cannot go straight because a beam is in the way, so it will have one bend. The pipe leaves the first wall at 35 degrees to the wall, and must arrive at the second wall at 55 degrees to it. The plumber needs the angle of the bend to order the right fitting, and fittings come in fixed angles: 45, 60, 90, 120, 135.

A learner on work experience draws the passage as two parallel lines with the pipe as a bent path between them, marks 35 at the first wall and 55 at the second, and draws a line through the bend parallel to the walls. The angle at the bend, on the side facing along the passage, splits into two: the part next to the first wall's line is alternate to the 35, so it is 35; the part next to the second wall's line is alternate to the 55, so it is 55. The angle at the bend is 35 plus 55, which is 90. A 90-degree fitting.

The plumber is suspicious that it came out so neatly, so the learner checks a different way. At the first wall, the angle between the pipe and the wall on the other side is 180 minus 35, which is 145. At the second wall, 180 minus 55, which is 125. The bend angle measured on the outside — the reflex side — would be 360 minus 90, which is 270, and the learner notes that the two pieces of pipe, the two walls and the bend form a closed shape whose angles must add correctly. The check is consistent.

Then the beam is found to be further along, and the pipe must leave the first wall at 35 but arrive at the second at 40. The learner redraws: 35 plus 40 is 75. No 75-degree fitting exists. Options: two bends, or change the arrival angle so the bend is a stock size — arriving at 55 gives 90, arriving at 25 gives 60. The plumber chooses two bends, and the learner draws two auxiliary lines and finds both bend angles the same way.

The passage walls, the learner notes, were assumed parallel. He checks with the plumber's long level across the passage at two places: same width both ends. Parallel confirmed, so the alternate-angle reasoning stands. Questions on the bent pipe follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for angle problems. One: mark the givens, arrowheads and right angles on the diagram; name the target. Two: plan a chain of named pairs from a given to the target — straight line, vertically opposite, corresponding, alternate, co-interior — and write it forwards, each line a statement, a value and a reason that names any parallel lines. Three: for algebraic angles, state the fact, write the equation — expression plus expression equals 180 for straight-line and co-interior pairs, expression equals expression for vertically opposite, corresponding and alternate pairs, sum equals 360 around a point — solve, substitute to get the angles, and check. Four: for a bent path between parallels, draw an auxiliary parallel line through the bend and say so. Five: to prove lines parallel, show equal corresponding or alternate angles or supplementary co-interior angles, and give the converse as the reason. Six: check every answer by a second route or by the 360 at a crossing.

A typical examination item: parallel lines, a transversal, one angle given, find a lettered angle with reasons. Two or three steps.

Another: adjacent angles on a straight line given as expressions; find x and the angles. Equation, solve, substitute, check the sum.

Another: a diagram with marked angles and no arrowheads; prove the lines parallel or show they are not. Identify a pair, compare, conclude with the converse.

Another: a path with a bend between parallels; find the bend angle. Auxiliary line, two alternate angles, add.

Another: a diagram with several unknowns labelled a, b, c, d; find all with reasons. Nearest to the given first, reuse each result.

Errors to avoid. Quoting a parallel-line reason without parallel lines. Expression equals expression for a co-interior pair. Stopping at x when the angles are asked. Omitting the auxiliary line and quoting a pair that does not exist. Concluding parallel from vertically opposite angles, which say nothing about parallelism. Measuring the diagram.

Looking ahead: the next topic applies these same reasons inside triangles and quadrilaterals, where the interior angles add to 180 and 360. Grade 9 uses them in congruence and similarity proofs. Final questions follow.
