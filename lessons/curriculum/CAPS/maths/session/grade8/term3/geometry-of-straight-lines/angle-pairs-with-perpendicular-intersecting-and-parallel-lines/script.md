# Part 1 — Expert

This session recognises and describes the pairs of angles formed by perpendicular lines, intersecting lines and parallel lines cut by a transversal. Angles are named by three letters with the vertex in the middle and classified by size. Adjacent angles on a straight line are supplementary, angles around a point sum to 360 degrees, and vertically opposite angles at intersecting lines are equal; perpendicular lines meet at right angles. When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal, alternate angles are equal and co-interior angles are supplementary, and each relationship carries a standard written reason. The anchor diagrams are two lines crossing at a point, a pair of perpendicular lines, and two parallel lines with one transversal.

## Subtopic: Naming and Classifying Angles

An angle is formed where two rays or line segments meet at a point called the vertex. It is named by three letters, with the vertex in the middle: the angle at B between the arms BA and BC is written angle ABC or angle CBA, and spoken as angle A B C. When only one angle sits at a vertex, the single letter with a hat — B with a hat — is allowed. When several angles share a vertex, the three-letter name is compulsory, because B with a hat would be ambiguous. In examinations, write the three letters unless the diagram marks the angle with its own symbol such as x or a small number.

Angles are measured in degrees, and a full turn is 360 degrees. Classify by size. An acute angle is less than 90 degrees. A right angle is exactly 90 degrees, marked with a small square in the corner. An obtuse angle is more than 90 and less than 180. A straight angle is exactly 180: the two arms form a straight line. A reflex angle is more than 180 and less than 360. A revolution is 360.

Two lines that meet at a right angle are perpendicular, written with the symbol that looks like an upside-down T: AB is perpendicular to CD. Perpendicular lines make four right angles at their intersection, since the angles on each side of a straight line add to 180 and each is 90.

Lines that never meet, however far extended, are parallel, written AB parallel to CD and marked in diagrams with matching arrowheads on the two lines. A line that crosses two or more other lines is a transversal.

Diagrams are not drawn to scale unless the question says so. Never measure with a protractor to find an answer; use the marked values and the relationships in this lesson. A diagram that looks like a right angle is not a right angle unless the square is marked or the question says perpendicular.

Equal angles are marked with matching arcs — one arc, two arcs — and equal lengths with matching dashes. Read the marks before reading the numbers; they carry information the numbers do not. Questions on naming and classifying follow now.

## Subtopic: Angles on a Straight Line and Around a Point

Two angles that share a vertex and an arm, and lie on opposite sides of the shared arm, are adjacent angles. When the outer arms of two adjacent angles form a straight line, the two angles add to 180 degrees. The reason, written exactly: angles on a straight line. If one angle on a straight line is 110 degrees, the other is 180 minus 110, which is 70. Three or more adjacent angles along a straight line also add to 180, same reason.

Two angles that add to 180 are called supplementary, whether or not they are adjacent. Two angles that add to 90 are complementary. If AB is perpendicular to CD and a third line through the intersection makes an angle of 35 degrees with AB, the angle between that line and CD is 90 minus 35, which is 55; the reason is that the two angles make up a right angle, or complementary angles.

Angles that fill the whole space around a point add to 360 degrees. The reason: angles around a point, or angles at a point. If three angles around a point are 120, 95 and x, then x is 360 minus 120 minus 95: 360 minus 120 is 240; 240 minus 95 is 145. So x is 145 degrees.

These two facts are the engine of nearly every angle calculation. Write the relationship as an equation, with the reason beside it, then solve. For an unknown on a straight line next to 48 degrees: x plus 48 equals 180, angles on a straight line; x equals 132.

A common error is to use 180 for angles around a point or 360 for a straight line. Check the diagram: does the collection of angles make a straight line, half a turn, or a full turn. Another error is to assume two angles are adjacent when they do not share an arm; adjacent means side by side with a common arm, not merely near each other.

The reason is part of the answer. In Grade 8 geometry, a correct number with no reason, or with the wrong reason, loses marks; a correct reason with an arithmetic slip keeps most of them. Learn the phrases: angles on a straight line; angles around a point; complementary angles; vertically opposite angles — the last of which is next. Questions on straight lines and points follow.

## Subtopic: Vertically Opposite Angles at Intersecting Lines

When two straight lines cross, four angles are formed at the intersection. The angles opposite each other — sharing the vertex but no arm, so that each is formed by the extensions of the other's arms — are vertically opposite angles, and they are equal. Vertically here means at the vertex, not up and down. The reason, written exactly: vertically opposite angles.

Why they are equal. Call the four angles a, b, c, d going round the point. Angles a and b are on a straight line, so a plus b is 180. Angles b and c are on a straight line too, so b plus c is 180. Both a and c equal 180 minus b, so a equals c. The same argument gives b equals d. Vertically opposite angles are equal because each is supplementary to the same neighbour.

So two crossing lines give two pairs of equal angles, and any adjacent pair adds to 180. If one of the four angles is 70 degrees, its vertically opposite partner is 70, and the other two are each 180 minus 70, which is 110. The four add to 70 plus 70 plus 110 plus 110, which is 360, as angles around a point should.

When the two lines are perpendicular, all four angles are 90, and the vertically opposite pairs are equal trivially. Perpendicular lines are the special case of intersecting lines where adjacent angles are equal as well as supplementary.

Recognising vertically opposite angles in a cluttered diagram: they are the angles in the X shape formed by two straight lines, top and bottom, or left and right. Check that both lines are actually straight through the point — two segments meeting at a point without continuing make no vertically opposite angles. Check too that the angles are formed by the same two lines; in a diagram with three lines through a point, an angle and the one across from it may be bounded by different lines and are then not vertically opposite.

Write the reasoning as a chain. Angle one equals 70 degrees, given. Angle three equals 70 degrees, vertically opposite angles. Angle two equals 180 minus 70, which is 110 degrees, angles on a straight line. Angle four equals 110 degrees, vertically opposite angles. Every line a statement, a value and a reason. Questions on vertically opposite angles follow.

## Subtopic: Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal

Two parallel lines cut by a transversal make eight angles, four at each intersection. Each intersection on its own has the straight-line and vertically opposite relationships already covered. The new relationships connect the two intersections, and they hold only because the lines are parallel.

Corresponding angles are in the same position at each intersection: both above the parallel line and to the right of the transversal, for instance. Corresponding angles are equal. The shape to see is a letter F, possibly turned or flipped, with the two angles tucked into the F's two corners. Reason: corresponding angles, with the parallel lines named — corresponding angles, AB parallel to CD.

Alternate angles are on opposite sides of the transversal and between the parallel lines. Alternate angles are equal. The shape is a letter Z, or an S, with the angles in the Z's two inside corners. Reason: alternate angles, AB parallel to CD.

Co-interior angles are on the same side of the transversal and between the parallel lines. Co-interior angles add to 180 degrees — supplementary, not equal. The shape is a letter U or C, with the angles inside the U's two corners. Reason: co-interior angles, AB parallel to CD.

Only one angle needs to be known. If a transversal makes an angle of 65 degrees with the upper parallel line, then at the lower line the corresponding angle is 65, the alternate angle is 65, and the co-interior angle is 180 minus 65, which is 115. The four angles at the lower intersection are 65, 115, 65, 115, exactly as at the upper. With parallel lines, every one of the eight angles is either equal to the given angle or supplementary to it.

The reason must name the parallel lines, because the relationship depends on them. Without parallel lines, corresponding angles are not equal and the F, Z and U shapes mean nothing. Check the arrowheads in the diagram or the words in the question before quoting any of these three reasons.

Angles outside the parallel lines on the same side of the transversal — co-exterior — are also supplementary, and alternate exterior angles are equal; these follow from the three main relationships and vertically opposite angles, and are rarely needed as separate reasons in Grade 8. Questions on parallel lines follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same angle pairs as stories about a level crossing where a road meets twin railway tracks, a tiler who lays a diagonal row across parallel skirting, and a learner who found the letters F, Z and U hiding in every parallel-line diagram — stories about seeing the shapes before measuring anything.

## Subtopic: The Level Crossing

A road crosses a double railway line at a level crossing. The two tracks are parallel — they must be, or the trains would collide — and the road is a transversal. A learner waiting at the boom with her uncle's bakkie counts the angles while the train passes.

Where the road meets the first track, four angles. The road is not perpendicular to the track; it comes in at a slant, and the warning sign says the crossing angle is 70 degrees. So at the first track: 70 degrees between the road and the track on the left ahead, and its vertically opposite partner, 70 on the right behind; the other two, on a straight line with 70, are each 110.

Where the road meets the second track, twenty metres on, the same four. The angle in the same position as the first 70 — left ahead — is corresponding to it: 70 degrees, because the tracks are parallel. The angle between the tracks on the right at the second crossing and between the tracks on the left at the first crossing form a Z: alternate angles, both 70. The two angles between the tracks on the same side of the road form a U: co-interior, 70 and 110, adding to 180.

Her uncle asks why it matters. She points at the barrier arm, which must swing down parallel to the track. If the arm's hinge makes 70 degrees with the road, then — corresponding angles — the arm is parallel to the track only if the track makes 70 degrees with the road too, which it does. A fitter installing the boom uses exactly that: match the corresponding angle and the arm is parallel. The relationship runs both ways, which is the subject of the next lesson.

She also notices what is not there. The two tracks are parallel, so eight angles, two values. If a siding branched off at a different angle, it would not be parallel, and none of the F, Z, U reasons would apply to it; its angles with the road would have to be measured or given separately.

The train passes, forty-one wagons. The boom lifts, swinging through 90 degrees — a right angle, perpendicular to the road when up, parallel to the track when down. Perpendicular lines are not in the parallel-line diagram, but she has found them anyway. Questions on the level crossing follow.

## Subtopic: The Tiler and the Letters F, Z and U

A tiler lays a diagonal border across a room whose two long skirting boards are parallel. His apprentice is told to cut the end tiles so the border meets both skirtings cleanly, and reaches for a protractor at the far wall. The tiler stops him: you already measured the near wall; the far wall is the same.

The border meets the near skirting at 50 degrees, measured on the left. The tiler draws the room on a scrap of board: two parallel lines, a slanted line across them. He marks the 50 and asks the apprentice to find the letters.

An F: the 50 at the near wall and the angle in the same position at the far wall sit in the two corners of an F whose spine is the border. Corresponding angles, equal: the far-wall angle on the left is 50. Cut that tile at 50 too.

A Z: the 50 at the near wall on the left, inside the skirtings, and the angle at the far wall on the right, inside the skirtings, sit in a Z. Alternate angles, equal: 50. So the right-hand cut at the far wall is also 50, which saves the apprentice a second measurement.

A U: the near-wall angle on the right, inside, and the far-wall angle on the right, inside, sit in a U. Co-interior, adding to 180. The near-wall angle on the right is 180 minus 50, which is 130, on a straight line with the 50; so the far-wall angle on the right inside is 180 minus 130, which is 50 — agreeing with the Z. Two reasons, one answer; that agreement is the check.

The apprentice asks how to find the letters when the diagram is upside down or the transversal slopes the other way. The tiler turns the scrap of board through a half turn: the F is now upside down and backwards, but still an F — two angles in the same position relative to their own crossing. The Z is now an S; still a Z. The U is now an n; still a U. The letters are about position, not orientation.

The tiler's last rule: before using any letter, look for the arrowheads or ask whether the two lines are really parallel. Skirting boards usually are. Old houses sometimes are not, and then the far wall needs its own protractor. The apprentice cuts eight tiles without going back to the far wall, and they all fit. Questions on the tiler follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for angle pairs. One: name angles with three letters, vertex in the middle, and read the marks — right-angle squares, arrowheads for parallel, arcs for equal. Two: at any single intersection, use angles on a straight line, adding to 180; angles around a point, adding to 360; and vertically opposite angles, equal. Perpendicular lines give right angles. Three: across parallel lines, identify the pair by position — corresponding, F, equal; alternate, Z, equal; co-interior, U, supplementary — and quote the reason with the parallel lines named. Four: write every step as statement, value and reason. Five: check by a second route where possible, and check that the angles at any intersection add to 360.

A typical examination item shows two lines crossing with one angle given and asks for the others with reasons. Vertically opposite for the equal one; angles on a straight line for the two supplementary ones.

Another shows parallel lines, a transversal and one angle, and asks for a named angle with a reason. Identify the pair by position, state equal or supplementary, calculate, and write the reason including the parallel lines.

Another asks you to name the type of angle pair — just the name — for two marked angles. Say corresponding, alternate, co-interior, vertically opposite, or adjacent on a straight line, and say whether they are equal or supplementary.

Another gives a diagram without arrowheads and asks for an angle. Use only the single-intersection facts; do not assume parallel.

Errors to avoid. Vertex letter not in the middle. Using 360 for a straight line. Calling alternate angles supplementary or co-interior angles equal. Quoting a parallel-line reason when the lines are not marked parallel. Omitting the reason. Measuring the diagram.

Looking ahead: the next lesson uses these relationships to solve multi-step problems with algebraic unknowns and to prove lines parallel. Grade 9 extends the same reasons to triangles, quadrilaterals and congruence proofs. Final questions follow.
