### subtopic_1 — Naming and Classifying Angles
**Assistant:** When is it acceptable to write a single letter with a hat instead of three letters?

**Tutor:** Only when exactly one angle sits at that vertex in the diagram, so that the single letter cannot refer to anything else. In a triangle ABC with no extra lines through the vertices, B with a hat is unambiguous and is standard. At the intersection of two lines there are four angles at the one vertex, so a single letter would be meaningless, and the three-letter name — or the label the diagram gives, such as x or a numbered angle — is required. Examiners mark the naming: a vertex letter not in the middle, or a single letter where four angles meet, is a lost mark even if the value is right. The safest habit is to use three letters whenever a diagram has more than one angle at the vertex, and to copy exactly the notation the question uses. When you introduce your own labels for angles the question has not named, say so — let the angle at the top left be y — before using them in your reasoning.

### subtopic_3 — Vertically Opposite Angles at Intersecting Lines
**Assistant:** How can I tell vertically opposite angles from alternate angles? Both are equal pairs.

**Tutor:** By where they sit. Vertically opposite angles are at a single intersection — one vertex — and are formed by the same two lines crossing, lying across the vertex from each other in an X. Alternate angles are at two different intersections, one on each of the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal and between the parallels, forming a Z. The quick test is to count the vertices: one vertex, vertically opposite; two vertices, a parallel-line relationship. The difference matters for the reason you quote, because vertically opposite angles are equal whether or not any lines are parallel, while alternate angles are equal only when the two lines are parallel, and the reason must say so. A frequent slip in parallel-line diagrams is to call an alternate pair vertically opposite because both are equal and the diagram is busy; checking that the two angles sit at different crossings prevents it.

### subtopic_4 — Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal
**Assistant:** Are co-interior angles ever equal?

**Tutor:** Only in the special case where each is 90 degrees, which happens when the transversal is perpendicular to the parallel lines. In general co-interior angles add to 180 and are not equal: one is acute and the other obtuse, such as 65 and 115. The reason is their position — same side of the transversal, between the parallels, in a U shape — which makes one of them the straight-line partner of the other's corresponding angle. If a question says two co-interior angles are equal, the conclusion is that each is 90 and the transversal is perpendicular to both parallel lines. Conversely, if a diagram shows co-interior angles that look equal but neither is marked as a right angle, do not assume they are equal: the diagram is not to scale. The three parallel-line pairs are best remembered as two equal pairs, F and Z, and one supplementary pair, U, and the U is the one that catches people out.

### subtopic_6 — The Tiler and the Letters F, Z and U
**Assistant:** What if I cannot see the letters because the diagram is turned or the transversal slopes the other way?

**Tutor:** Rotate the page, or rotate the diagram in your mind, until one of the parallel lines is horizontal; the letters then appear in their familiar or mirrored forms, and a mirrored or upside-down F, Z or U is still the same pair. If that does not help, fall back on the definitions, which do not depend on orientation. Corresponding angles are in the same position at their respective crossings — both above-left, say — so compare positions. Alternate angles are on opposite sides of the transversal and both between the parallel lines. Co-interior angles are on the same side of the transversal and both between the parallels. A useful trick is to copy the diagram and shade the two angles in question, then ask: same crossing or different, same side of the transversal or opposite, inside the parallels or outside. Those three answers identify the pair without any letter. The letters are a memory aid; the positions are the mathematics.
