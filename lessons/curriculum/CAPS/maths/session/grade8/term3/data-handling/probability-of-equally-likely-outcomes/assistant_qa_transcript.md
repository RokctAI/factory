### subtopic_1 — Outcomes, Sample Spaces and Equally Likely
**Assistant:** Why list each marble separately when I only care about the colour?

**Tutor:** Because the formula needs equally likely outcomes in the denominator, and the colours are not equally likely while the marbles are. There are three colours but ten marbles; if you took colour as the outcome you would say P(red) is one third, which is wrong, since only three of the ten marbles are red. Each marble, mixed in the bag and drawn blind, has the same chance as every other, so marbles are the right unit to count: three red marbles out of ten gives three tenths. You do not have to write out red one, red two, red three every time once the idea is clear — writing three over ten is enough — but you must count marbles, not categories. The same principle appears everywhere: on an unequal spinner you count degrees, not colours; in a raffle you count tickets, not people, so that a person with three tickets has three times the chance. Ask what the equally likely units are, and let those be the outcomes.

### subtopic_2 — The Probability Formula
**Assistant:** Should I leave probability as a fraction or convert it to a decimal or percentage?

**Tutor:** Leave it as a simplified fraction unless the question asks for another form. The fraction is exact: one sixth is exactly one sixth, while 0,17 is a rounded approximation and 16,7% is rounded too. The fraction also shows the counting — three over ten says three favourable out of ten — which an examiner can follow and award method marks for even if the simplification slips. When the question says give your answer as a decimal or as a percentage, convert: divide numerator by denominator for the decimal, and multiply that by a hundred for the percentage, so three eighths is 0,375 and 37,5%. If the decimal recurs, round to the number of places asked for and say so. In reports written for the public, percentages are common because people read them easily — a 30% chance of rain — but in mathematics the fraction is the home form, and the others are translations of it.

### subtopic_4 — Listing Outcomes for Two-Step Situations
**Assistant:** How do I know I have listed all the two-step outcomes without missing any?

**Tutor:** Use a system, then a count. The system is a grid or an ordered list: fix the first step's outcome and run through every outcome of the second step before moving on. For a coin and a die, write H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6, then T1 to T6; for two dice, a six-by-six grid with the first die down the side and the second across the top. Then count the outcomes and compare with the product of the step counts: two times six should give twelve, six times six should give thirty-six. If the list is short, something is missing; if it is long, something is repeated. Keep the two steps distinguishable — first coin and second coin, even if they look identical — so that HT and TH are both present. A tree diagram does the same job and is the standard tool in Grade 9: a branch for each first-step outcome, and from each a branch for each second-step outcome, with the ends of the branches being the full list.

### subtopic_6 — The Board Game That Was Never Fair
**Assistant:** If the spinner's colours are not equally likely, how can probability be calculated at all?

**Tutor:** By finding a finer set of outcomes that are equally likely and counting those. A spinner's pointer is equally likely to stop at any angle, so every degree of the 360 is an equally likely outcome, and a sector's probability is its angle over 360. Red at 150 degrees has probability 150 over 360, which simplifies to five twelfths. The same idea rescues many unfair-looking situations: a bag with different numbers of each colour is handled by counting marbles; a raffle where one person holds several tickets is handled by counting tickets; a dartboard's rings by comparing areas. What cannot be rescued this way is a situation with no underlying symmetry at all — a drawing pin, a weather forecast, a cricket match — because there is no finer unit that is equally likely. For those, probability must be estimated from repeated trials, which is relative frequency, the subject of the next lesson. The test is whether you can name a unit — degree, marble, ticket, square centimetre — of which every one has the same chance.
