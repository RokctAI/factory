### subtopic_1 — Tally Marks and Frequency Tables for Categories
**Assistant:** Why not just sort the forms into piles and count each pile?

**Tutor:** Piles work for a handful of forms and fail for a survey, for three reasons. First, piles are not a record: once the forms are counted and put away, there is nothing on paper showing how each count was reached, so a doubtful total cannot be checked without redoing everything. A tally table is the record, and a row can be recounted on its own. Second, piles get disturbed — a door, a breeze, a classmate — and a disturbed pile must be rebuilt from the start, while a tally with ticked-off forms can be resumed at the next form. Third, counting a pile of twenty-three items is a long run of counting where one slip changes the answer, whereas tally marks grouped in fives are read as four fives and three, which is almost impossible to misread. The piles-and-counts method also tempts you to skip the total check, because the piles look complete; the tally table forces the check by putting the frequencies in one column that must sum to the number of forms.

### subtopic_3 — Constructing a Stem-and-Leaf Display
**Assistant:** What do I do with a value like 100 when my other stems are single digits?

**Tutor:** Give it its own stem of 10, with leaf 0, and keep every stem between the lowest and highest, even if some are empty. If the marks run from 12 to 100 out of 100, the stems are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10, and a learner who scored 100 appears as 10 | 0. The key must then show that the stem can have two digits: 10 | 0 means 100 and 2 | 3 means 23. The stem is the number with its last digit removed, however many digits remain, so the method does not change. Alternatively, if all the values are three-digit — times of 102 to 148 seconds — the stems are 10 to 14 and the leaves are the units, so the display is just as compact. What you must not do is write 100 as stem 1 and leaf 00, because leaves are single digits, or leave 100 off because it does not fit; every observation must appear, and the count of leaves must still equal the number of observations.

### subtopic_4 — Reading Stem-and-Leaf Displays and Grouping into Intervals
**Assistant:** How do I choose the width of the class intervals?

**Tutor:** Start from the range: highest value minus lowest, which for the marks is 48 minus 12, giving 36. Decide how many intervals would show the shape — five to eight is usual for a class-sized data set — and divide the range by that number to get a rough width: 36 divided by 5 is about 7, so a tidy width of 10 giving four intervals, or 5 giving eight intervals, are both reasonable. Choose a round width — 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 — because readers understand round intervals and because the boundaries then fall on numbers people recognise. Start the first interval at a round number at or below the minimum, such as 10 rather than 12. Make every interval the same width, and extend the last one to include the maximum. For whole-number data write the intervals as 10 to 19, 20 to 29; for measured data write 10 up to but not including 20. Finally, tally and check that the frequencies sum to the total; if a value sits exactly on a boundary and you are unsure where it went, your intervals are not written clearly enough.

### subtopic_6 — A Stem-and-Leaf on the Whiteboard at the Cricket
**Assistant:** Why keep an empty stem? Would the display not be neater without it?

**Tutor:** The empty stem is information: it says that no observation fell in that range, and that gap is part of the shape of the data. On the cricket board, the empty 4 row tells the coach that nobody scored in the forties, which is different from nobody scoring in the forties being hidden because the row was removed. If the empty stem were deleted, the 5 row would sit directly under the 3 row and a reader would see a smooth tail where there is actually a jump from 34 to 51. The display's power is that the length of each row is a bar in a sideways bar chart, and bar charts need their zero-height bars in place for the horizontal scale to be honest. The same applies to grouped frequency tables: an interval with frequency zero stays in the table with a zero, not a blank line and not a missing row. Neatness that hides a gap is not neatness; it is a misleading picture.
