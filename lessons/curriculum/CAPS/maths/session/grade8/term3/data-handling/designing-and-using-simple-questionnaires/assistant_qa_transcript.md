### subtopic_2 — Options That Do Not Overlap and Leave No Gaps
**Assistant:** How do I decide how many intervals to use for a numerical question?

**Tutor:** Between four and six is usual for a short questionnaire. Fewer than four lumps very different respondents together — someone spending R1 with someone spending R19 — and hides the shape of the data; more than six makes the form slow and leaves many intervals nearly empty. Start by estimating the smallest and largest answers you expect, including zero, and the range most people fall into, perhaps from a quick pilot. Place narrower intervals where most answers will land and wider ones towards the extremes, and finish with an open top interval such as R200 or more so nobody is left without a box. Keep the boundaries tidy — multiples of 5, 10, 20 or 50 — because respondents judge their own spending in round figures. Use the up to but not including phrasing or whole-number ranges so each boundary belongs to one interval. Remember that the intervals you choose now are the groups you will tally and graph later, so choose them with the final frequency table in mind.

### subtopic_3 — Neutral, Single and Unambiguous Wording
**Assistant:** Is a question leading just because it mentions a specific issue, like healthy eating?

**Tutor:** Not by itself. A question is leading when its wording tells the respondent which answer is expected or approved, usually through loaded adjectives, assumptions or an appeal to agree. Would you buy fruit if the tuck shop sold it at R5 mentions healthy food but pushes nobody; the respondent can say no without feeling judged. Do you agree the tuck shop should finally offer healthy food instead of junk is leading: agree invites agreement, finally implies overdue, and junk condemns the alternative. Test by reading the stem and options aloud and asking whether a respondent with the opposite view would feel comfortable ticking their honest answer. If one option sounds like the right answer and another sounds shameful, redraft. Describe the options in plain nouns — fruit, chips, sweets — and let the data, not the adjectives, make the argument afterwards.

### subtopic_4 — Ordering, Trialling and Recording Responses
**Assistant:** What should I do with forms that are only partly filled in?

**Tutor:** Keep them, number them, and record each question separately. A form with the spending question blank still has a valid answer to the travel question, so it counts for travel and is recorded as no response for spending. Add a no response row to every tally table so that the counts for each question add up to the number of forms returned; that keeps the totals honest and shows which questions people avoided. If many forms skip the same question, that is evidence the question was faulty — too personal, a gap in the options, or unclear — and it should be reported as such. Do not guess the missing answer or fill it in with the most common response, because that invents data. Do not throw the whole form away either, because that discards good answers along with the gap and can bias the rest if the people who skipped differ from those who did not. Report the response rate for the form as a whole and, where it matters, for the individual question.

### subtopic_6 — The Hole in the Spending Question
**Assistant:** Why does missing data from the big spenders matter so much more than missing data at random?

**Tutor:** Because the gap was not random: it fell on one particular part of the population — those spending over R100 — and removed them from the data in a block. Any summary calculated from what remains, such as an average or a most common interval, describes the small and medium spenders only, while the report will claim to describe all food-court customers. That is bias, the same fault as a sample drawn from one suburb, created not by who was asked but by who could not answer. Random missing data — a few forms dropped in a puddle, say — removes a little of every kind of respondent and leaves the proportions roughly intact, so the summaries shift only slightly. Systematic missing data shifts them in one direction every time. The defence is an exhaustive set of options, especially an open top interval and a zero option, so that nobody is pushed out of the question by its design; and, when gaps do appear, reporting them openly rather than averaging over what is left.
