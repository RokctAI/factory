# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the data handling cycle: posing a question, identifying the population, selecting a sample and choosing sources. A statistical question is one whose answer varies from person to person or place to place and so needs data rather than a single fact. The population is the whole group the question is about; a sample is the part of that group actually measured, and it must be large enough and chosen without favouring any part of the population if its results are to speak for the whole. Data collected first-hand is primary; data taken from an existing source such as Statistics South Africa or a municipal report is secondary. The anchor examples are school water use, taxi fares on a route and household recycling.

## Subtopic: Posing a Statistical Question

Every data project begins with a question, and the quality of the question decides the quality of everything after it. A statistical question is one whose answer is spread across many people or many measurements. How tall is the tallest learner in Grade 8 has one answer and needs no statistics. How tall are Grade 8 learners is a statistical question, because the answer is a collection of heights that varies, and we need data to describe it.

CAPS asks you to pose questions on social, economic and environmental issues, so the question should be about something that matters beyond the classroom. Social: how many learners in the school walk more than thirty minutes to get here. Economic: how much does a family in our suburb spend on electricity in a month. Environmental: how many litres of water does a household in the town use each day during a water restriction.

A good question is specific. It names the group, the quantity and the time or place. How much do people spend on food is vague: which people, which food, over what period. How much did households in this ward spend on groceries in August is answerable. Specific questions produce data that can be counted, measured or categorised; vague questions produce arguments.

A good question also says what kind of data it wants. Some questions produce numbers that are measured: height in centimetres, water in litres, fares in rand. Some produce numbers that are counted: number of siblings, number of taxis taken. Some produce categories: preferred snack, mode of transport, yes or no. Knowing the kind of data in advance tells you how it will be recorded and summarised later in the term.

Finally, a good question is one that data can actually answer. Should the school ban plastic bottles is an opinion question. How many plastic bottles are thrown away at the school in a week is a data question, and its answer can inform the opinion. Statistics gathers evidence; the decision is made afterwards, with the evidence in hand. Questions on posing questions follow now.

## Subtopic: Population and Sample

The population is the whole group the question is about. If the question is how far Grade 8 learners at our school travel, the population is every Grade 8 learner at the school — perhaps two hundred of them. If the question is how much households in the town spend on electricity, the population is every household in the town — perhaps twenty thousand. The population is defined by the question, and it is not necessarily people: if the question is how long light bulbs from a factory last, the population is every bulb the factory makes.

A sample is the part of the population from which data is actually collected. Asking all two hundred Grade 8 learners is possible and is called a census of that population. Asking all twenty thousand households is not practical: it costs too much, takes too long, and the answers would be out of date before the survey finished. So a sample is taken — say four hundred households — and the results from the sample are used to say something about the whole population.

The word for this is inference: from the sample to the population. If 30% of the four hundred sampled households use prepaid electricity, we infer that about 30% of all twenty thousand households do. The inference is only trustworthy if the sample represents the population, which is the subject of the next section.

Be precise with the words. The population is not the population of the country; it is the group in the question. The sample is not a single person; it is the whole set of people or items measured. A sample of one is anecdote, not data. And the result from a sample is an estimate of the population value, not the population value itself: a different sample of four hundred would give a slightly different percentage.

Two quantities you will meet later in the term describe the two groups: a value calculated from the whole population is a parameter; the same value calculated from a sample is a statistic. In Grade 8 the vocabulary you need is population, sample, census and representative, and the skill is naming the population and the sample in any described survey. Questions on populations and samples follow.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Representative Sample

A sample is representative when every part of the population has a fair chance of appearing in it, in roughly the proportions it has in the population. A sample is biased when some part of the population is more likely to be chosen than another, so the sample leans one way. Bias is not about the sample being small; it is about who was left out or over-included.

Consider the electricity question with a sample taken only in one wealthy suburb. Those households use more electricity and are more likely to be on a monthly account than prepaid. The sample would overstate spending for the whole town. The fix is not more households from the same suburb; it is households from every kind of area in the town.

Consider a school survey done by asking the learners in the library at break. Learners in the library are more likely to be those who read, who do homework at school, or who avoid the field. Whatever the question, the answers lean towards that group. Asking the first twenty learners through the gate leans towards early risers and those who live nearby. Asking only your friends leans towards people like you.

The standard fix is random selection: every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen. In practice, number the population — a class list, a list of erven, a list of taxis on the route — and pick numbers using a random method: a hat, a dice, a spreadsheet's random function. Random does not mean careless; it means a method with no preference.

Sample size matters too, after fairness. A larger random sample gives an estimate closer to the population value; a sample of five learners can be wildly off even if chosen randomly, while a random sample of eighty from two hundred is usually close. The Grade 8 rules of thumb: choose randomly first, then choose as many as time and cost allow, and never trade fairness for size — a biased sample of a thousand is worse than a random sample of fifty.

Watch also for self-selection: a poster inviting learners to fill in a form attracts those who care about the topic. Volunteers are not a random sample, and surveys built on them systematically favour strong opinions. Questions on representative samples follow.

## Subtopic: Primary and Secondary Sources of Data

Once the question and the population are set, decide where the data will come from. Primary data is collected by you, for this question, directly from the population or sample: a questionnaire handed out at school, a count of bottles in the bins, a measurement of the water meter each morning. Secondary data already exists, collected by someone else for their own purpose, and you use it: Statistics South Africa's census and household surveys, a municipality's water and electricity reports, the weather service's rainfall records, a newspaper's published table.

Primary data fits your question exactly, because you designed the collection. It is also fresh. But it costs time and effort, your sample is limited to what you can reach, and mistakes in your method become mistakes in your data. Secondary data is cheap and often covers a far larger population than you could ever reach — the whole country, every year since 1996. But it was collected for someone else's question, so its categories may not match yours, it may be old, and you must trust that it was collected properly.

Choosing a source means matching it to the question. How many litres of water do households in this town use: the municipality bills every household monthly, so a secondary source exists and is better than any sample you could take. What snacks would Grade 8 learners buy at the tuck shop: nobody has that data, so it must be primary. How has the town's population changed since 2001: Statistics South Africa, secondary. How long do learners in this class spend on homework: primary.

Whatever the source, record where the data came from. For primary data, note who was asked, when, how many and how they were chosen. For secondary data, note the organisation, the publication, the year, and the population it covers. A result without its source cannot be checked, and an examiner will ask for the source as part of the method.

Judge the reliability of a secondary source. A national statistics agency, a university study or an official report is reliable. A social media post, an advertisement or a single news headline is not reliable on its own: find the original source behind it. The number alone is never enough; the number plus its source is data. Questions on sources follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas as stories about a tuck shop that nearly stocked the wrong snacks, a radio phone-in that misjudged a whole city, and a learner who used last year's census instead of knocking on two thousand doors — stories in which who was asked mattered more than what was asked.

## Subtopic: The Tuck Shop Survey That Nearly Went Wrong

A Grade 8 class is given the job of advising the tuck shop on what to stock. The first question they write is: what food do learners like. A learner points out that everybody likes everything, and that the tuck shop needs to know what learners would buy, with the money they actually have, at break. So the question becomes: which of these five snacks would you buy at break most often, and how much do you spend at the tuck shop in a week. The group is the school's learners — the population — all nine hundred of them.

Nine hundred is too many to ask in one break, so the class decides to sample. The first plan is to ask everyone in the queue at the tuck shop. Someone objects: the queue holds the learners who already buy from the tuck shop, and leaves out those who bring lunch or have no money, who are exactly the learners the tuck shop wants to attract. Second plan: ask the Grade 8s, because they are easy to reach. Objection: Grade 8 pocket money and Grade 12 pocket money are not the same, and a Grade 8 sample would stand for the school badly.

Third plan: take the school's register, which numbers every learner, and pick ninety numbers from a hat — ten percent of the population, every grade with an equal chance, every learner with an equal chance. Random. The class checks afterwards that the ninety includes learners from each grade in roughly the right proportion, and it does.

They also decide where the data comes from. The snack preference must be primary — nobody has that data — so they ask their ninety. The weekly spending could be primary too, but the tuck shop's own till records show how much is spent in total each week, which is a secondary source that covers the whole population without asking anyone, so they use both and compare.

The results surprise them: the most popular snack among the ninety is not the one the queue would have suggested, because the learners who do not buy now would come for fruit and cheaper items. The tuck shop changes its stock. The lesson the class takes away: the question had to be sharpened twice, the population had to be named, two sample plans had to be thrown out for bias, and the source had to be chosen for each quantity separately. Questions on the tuck shop follow.

## Subtopic: Spotting Biased Samples in Everyday Claims

A radio station runs a phone-in during the morning show: should the city raise the electricity tariff. Nine out of ten callers say no. The presenter announces that ninety percent of the city opposes the increase. Think about who called. People who listen to that station at that hour; people with airtime to spare; people who feel strongly enough to phone. The population the presenter named is the whole city; the sample is a few dozen angry listeners who chose themselves. The number may be correct for the callers and tells us almost nothing about the city.

A fitness app reports that its users walk an average of eleven thousand steps a day and concludes that South Africans are active. The sample is people who bought a fitness app, which is already a group that walks more than most. The population claimed is all South Africans. The leap from one to the other is the bias.

A learner wants to know how much time Grade 8s spend on social media and asks the eleven friends in a chat group. The friends are in the chat group because they use social media. The result will be high, and it says nothing about the learner in the class without a phone.

A municipality wants to know whether residents are satisfied with refuse collection and puts a survey form on its website. Residents whose refuse is collected on time rarely visit the website to say so. The form collects complaints, which is useful, but it does not measure satisfaction across the population.

In each case, ask the same three questions. What population does the claim speak about. Who was actually in the sample. Could the way people got into the sample be connected to how they would answer. If the answer to the third is yes, the sample is biased and the claim does not follow, however large the number.

Then ask what a fair sample would have looked like. For the tariff: a random sample of households from the billing list, phoned or visited. For the steps: a random sample of adults given a pedometer for a week. For the social media: a random sample of the whole grade from the register. For refuse: a random sample of addresses visited in person. Fair samples cost more, which is why the biased versions are so common; recognising them is the first defence. Questions on everyday bias follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for the first stage of the data cycle. One: pose a statistical question on a social, economic or environmental issue that names the group, the quantity and the time or place, and decide whether the data will be measured, counted or categorical. Two: name the population — the whole group the question is about. Three: decide between a census and a sample; if a sample, choose it randomly from a complete list of the population, as large as time and cost allow, and state how it was chosen. Four: choose the source — primary if you must collect it, secondary if a reliable body already has it — and record the source. Five: check for bias by asking whether the way members entered the sample is connected to their likely answers.

A typical examination item gives a scenario and asks you to identify the population, the sample and whether the sample is representative, with a reason. A learner asks the forty learners in her class whether they recycle at home, to find out about recycling in the town. Population: households in the town. Sample: the forty learners' households. Not representative: all from one school and one age group, and clustered in the areas the school serves; a random sample of addresses across the town would be better.

Another item asks for a better question. How do people feel about the dam: vague. Improve to: do you support the proposed dam on the river, yes or no, asked of residents of the affected villages, in this month.

Another asks primary or secondary with a reason. Rainfall in the town for the last twenty years: secondary, from the weather service, because it exists and covers more years than you could measure. Litres of water your household uses this week: primary, read from the meter daily.

Errors to avoid. Calling the sample the population. Saying a sample is random because it was chosen without thinking; random means a method giving equal chances. Believing a large sample cannot be biased. Forgetting to state the source. Posing a question that asks for an opinion when the task wants data.

Looking ahead: the next lesson designs the questionnaire that collects the primary data, and later lessons organise, summarise and interpret it. Grade 9 extends the same cycle with larger data sets and critical reading of published statistics. Final questions follow.
