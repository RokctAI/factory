# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces theoretical probability for situations with equally likely outcomes. The sample space is the complete list of outcomes; when each outcome is equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of outcomes, a value from 0 for an impossible event to 1 for a certain event, expressible as a fraction, decimal or percentage. The probabilities of all outcomes in a sample space sum to 1, and the probability of an event not happening is 1 minus the probability that it does. The anchor situations are a fair coin, a fair six-sided die, an eight-sector spinner and a bag of ten marbles.

## Subtopic: Outcomes, Sample Spaces and Equally Likely

An experiment in probability is anything with an uncertain result: tossing a coin, rolling a die, spinning a spinner, drawing a marble from a bag. Each possible result is an outcome. The list of all possible outcomes is the sample space, and listing it completely is the first skill: nothing missing, nothing repeated.

A coin: two outcomes, heads and tails. A standard die: six outcomes, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. A spinner divided into eight equal sectors numbered 1 to 8: eight outcomes. A bag with three red, five blue and two green marbles, one drawn without looking: ten outcomes, because each marble is a separate outcome even though several share a colour — red one, red two, red three, blue one, and so on. Listing marbles individually rather than by colour is what makes the counting work.

Outcomes are equally likely when there is no reason for any one to happen more often than any other. A fair coin is symmetric: heads and tails are equally likely. A fair die is a cube with identical faces: each face is equally likely. Equal sectors on a spinner, identical marbles in a bag thoroughly mixed — equally likely. The word fair in a question is the signal that the outcomes are equally likely.

Not every sample space has equally likely outcomes. A drawing pin lands point up or point down, but the two are not equally likely, because the pin is not symmetric. A weather forecast's rain or no rain are two outcomes that are not equally likely. For such cases, probability must be estimated from repeated trials, which is the next lesson. This lesson is about situations where symmetry gives equal likelihood, so probability can be calculated by counting.

An event is a set of outcomes we are interested in. Rolling an even number is the event made of the outcomes 2, 4 and 6. Drawing a red marble is the event made of the three red outcomes. Drawing a marble that is not green is the event made of eight outcomes: the three red and five blue. An event can be a single outcome — rolling a 4 — or many, or all, or none.

Before any calculation, write the sample space and circle the outcomes in the event. Questions on outcomes and sample spaces follow now.

## Subtopic: The Probability Formula

When all outcomes are equally likely, the probability of an event is the number of outcomes in the event divided by the total number of outcomes in the sample space. Write it as P of the event, in words or with the event in brackets.

A fair die. P(rolling a 4): one favourable outcome out of six, so one sixth. P(even): the outcomes 2, 4 and 6 are three out of six, which is three sixths, simplifying to one half. P(more than 4): the outcomes 5 and 6, two out of six, which is one third. P(at least 4): 4, 5 and 6, three out of six, one half. Notice the difference between more than and at least: more than 4 excludes the 4; at least 4 includes it.

The bag with three red, five blue and two green. P(red): three out of ten. P(blue): five out of ten, which is one half. P(green): two out of ten, one fifth. P(not green): eight out of ten, four fifths. P(red or green): five out of ten, one half.

The eight-sector spinner numbered 1 to 8. P(landing on 3): one eighth. P(odd): 1, 3, 5, 7 — four out of eight, one half. P(a multiple of 3): 3 and 6 — two out of eight, one quarter. P(a number less than 9): all eight out of eight, which is 1.

Always write the fraction with the total as the denominator first, then simplify. Three sixths becomes one half; writing one half straight away is fine once you are fluent, but the unsimplified fraction shows the count and earns method marks.

A probability can be given as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage. Three tenths is 0,3 or 30%. One eighth is 0,125 or 12,5%. One sixth is a recurring decimal, 0,1666..., so leave it as a fraction unless the question asks for a rounded decimal — then 0,17 to two decimal places. The fraction form is usually the most exact and the one to prefer.

The denominator counts outcomes, not colours or categories. P(red) is three tenths because there are ten marbles, not one third because there are three colours. The colours are not equally likely; the marbles are. Questions on the formula follow.

## Subtopic: The Probability Scale and Certain, Impossible and Complementary Events

Every probability is a number from 0 to 1. An event that cannot happen has probability 0: rolling a 7 on a standard die, drawing a yellow marble from a bag with no yellow marbles. An event that must happen has probability 1: rolling a number less than 7, drawing a marble that is red, blue or green. Everything else lies between. There is no such thing as a probability of 1,2 or of negative one tenth; if a calculation gives one, the count is wrong.

Picture a line from 0 on the left to 1 on the right. Impossible at 0, certain at 1, an even chance at one half in the middle. Unlikely events sit between 0 and one half; likely events between one half and 1. The words are not precise, but placing a probability on the line is a quick check on sense: P(red) at 0,3 is unlikely but far from impossible; P(not green) at 0,8 is likely.

The probabilities of all the outcomes in a sample space add to 1, because exactly one of them must happen. For the die: six outcomes, each one sixth, total six sixths, which is 1. For the bag: P(red) plus P(blue) plus P(green) is three tenths plus five tenths plus two tenths, which is ten tenths, 1. That total is a check on a list of probabilities: if the colours in a bag are given with probabilities adding to 0,9, a colour is missing or a number is wrong.

The complement of an event is the event not happening. If P(green) is two tenths, P(not green) is 1 minus two tenths, which is eight tenths — the same answer as counting the eight non-green marbles. The complement rule is useful when the event is easier to count than its opposite. P(not rolling a 6) is 1 minus one sixth, which is five sixths, far quicker than listing 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5.

Some language to read carefully. At least one means one or more. Neither red nor blue means green. A number that is prime on a die means 2, 3 or 5, three outcomes, one half. A number that is a factor of 6 means 1, 2, 3 or 6, four outcomes, two thirds. Translate the words into a list of outcomes before counting, and the formula does the rest. Questions on the scale and complements follow.

## Subtopic: Listing Outcomes for Two-Step Situations

Some simple situations involve two steps, and the sample space must list every combination. Tossing two coins: the first can land heads or tails, and for each the second can land heads or tails. Outcomes: HH, HT, TH, TT — four, all equally likely if both coins are fair. The common mistake is to list three outcomes — two heads, one of each, two tails — and treat them as equally likely. They are not: one of each can happen two ways, HT and TH, so it is twice as likely as two heads.

With the four outcomes listed, probabilities follow. P(two heads): one out of four, one quarter. P(exactly one head): HT and TH, two out of four, one half. P(at least one head): HH, HT, TH, three out of four. P(no heads): TT, one quarter — and indeed one minus three quarters.

Tossing a coin and rolling a die: two outcomes for the coin, six for the die, so twelve combined outcomes: H1, H2, H3, H4, H5, H6, T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6. P(heads and a 6): one out of twelve. P(tails and an even number): T2, T4, T6, three out of twelve, one quarter. P(a 3, whatever the coin): H3 and T3, two out of twelve, one sixth — the same as P(3) on the die alone, as it should be, because the coin cannot change the die.

A systematic way to list: write the outcomes of the first step down the side and the second step across the top of a grid, and fill in each cell. For two dice the grid has thirty-six cells, and it answers questions like P(the two dice show the same number): six cells on the diagonal out of thirty-six, one sixth. Grade 8 stays with small grids; the method is the point.

Order matters in the listing when the two steps are distinguishable — first coin and second coin — because HT and TH are different outcomes. Counting them as one is the error that makes one of each look as likely as two heads.

Always count the total from the completed list, and check that it equals the product of the step counts: two times two is four; two times six is twelve; six times six is thirty-six. Questions on two-step listing follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same probability as stories about a raffle with a hundred tickets, a board game that was never fair, and a Grade 8 who worked out why a bag of sweets kept giving her the flavour she did not want — stories about counting the ways before trusting your luck.

## Subtopic: The Raffle with a Hundred Tickets

A school fundraiser sells a hundred raffle tickets, numbered 1 to 100, for a bicycle. One ticket is drawn from a drum. A Grade 8 buys ticket 47 and her brother buys tickets 12, 13 and 14, and they argue about whose chance is better.

The sample space is the hundred tickets, each as likely as any other if the drum is well mixed. Her probability: one favourable ticket out of a hundred, one hundredth, which is 0,01 or 1%. His: three out of a hundred, three hundredths, 3%. Three times hers, exactly as he paid three times as much. Consecutive numbers make no difference; three tickets scattered anywhere would give the same three hundredths.

Their friend has a theory that ticket 100 never wins because it is on the bottom of the pile. If the drum is mixed, ticket 100 is one of a hundred equally likely outcomes, one hundredth like all the rest. If the drum is not mixed, no ticket's probability can be calculated by counting, because the outcomes are no longer equally likely — and that is a complaint to the organisers, not a strategy.

The organisers announce a second prize: any ticket ending in 7 wins a cake. The Grade 8 lists the tickets ending in 7: 7, 17, 27, 37, 47, 57, 67, 77, 87, 97 — ten of them. P(cake) for a single ticket: ten out of a hundred, one tenth. Her ticket 47 is one of them, so she has a one hundredth chance of the bicycle and, in the separate cake draw, a one tenth chance of the cake. Her brother's tickets 12, 13 and 14 end in 2, 3 and 4; his P(cake) is zero in the cake draw.

She also works out P(the bicycle goes to a ticket number greater than 90): tickets 91 to 100 are ten, one tenth. P(a multiple of 10): 10, 20, up to 100, ten tickets, one tenth. P(a two-digit number): 10 to 99 is ninety tickets, nine tenths. P(not a two-digit number): one minus nine tenths, one tenth — the single-digit tickets 1 to 9 and the ticket 100, which is ten tickets, agreeing.

The draw happens. Ticket 63 wins the bicycle; the cake goes to ticket 7. Nobody in the family wins anything, which, at one hundredth and three hundredths, is what the probabilities said was most likely. Questions on the raffle follow.

## Subtopic: The Board Game That Was Never Fair

A family board game uses a spinner to decide who starts. The spinner is a circle divided into four sectors labelled with the players' colours — red, blue, green, yellow — and the youngest always seems to lose the spin. One evening the Grade 8 in the family measures the sectors with a protractor. Red: 150 degrees. Blue: 90. Green: 60. Yellow: 60. Total 360, as it should be. The sectors are not equal, and yellow is the youngest's colour.

If the sectors were equal, each would be 90 degrees and each colour would have probability one quarter. With these sectors, the probabilities follow the angles: P(red) is 150 out of 360, which is five twelfths; P(blue) is 90 out of 360, one quarter; P(green) is 60 out of 360, one sixth; P(yellow) is 60 out of 360, one sixth. Red is two and a half times as likely as yellow. The sum: five twelfths plus three twelfths plus two twelfths plus two twelfths is twelve twelfths, 1.

The spinner's outcomes are not equally likely, so the simple formula — one colour out of four — gives the wrong answer. But the angle approach works because the spinner is still symmetric in a finer way: every degree of the circle is equally likely, and there are 360 of them. Counting degrees instead of colours restores equally likely outcomes. P(red) is favourable degrees over total degrees.

The family wants a fair spinner. Option one: redraw the sectors at 90 degrees each. Option two: use a fair die — 1 and 2 for red, 3 and 4 for blue, 5 for green, 6 for yellow — but that is unfair in a different way, giving red and blue one third each and green and yellow one sixth each. Option three: a die with 1 red, 2 blue, 3 green, 4 yellow, and spin again on 5 or 6; each colour then has an equal chance on any spin that counts. Option three is fair.

The Grade 8 also settles an old family argument. The game has a bag of sixteen tokens: eight bronze, five silver, three gold. P(gold) on a single draw is three sixteenths, under one fifth; her sister's complaint that she never gets gold is mostly correct about the odds, not about the bag being rigged.

The lesson the family takes: fair is a claim to check, by measuring or counting, not an assumption; and when the obvious outcomes are not equally likely, look for finer outcomes — degrees, individual tokens — that are. Questions on the board game follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for probability with equally likely outcomes. One: list the sample space completely — every outcome once, individual items rather than categories, every combination for two-step situations, checking the count against the product of step counts. Two: confirm the outcomes are equally likely — the word fair, equal sectors, a mixed bag — or find finer outcomes that are, such as degrees or individual tokens. Three: translate the event's words into a list of outcomes, reading at least, more than, not, neither carefully. Four: write P(event) as favourable over total, then simplify, and convert to a decimal or percentage only if asked. Five: check the answer lies between 0 and 1 and makes sense on the scale; use the complement, 1 minus P, when the opposite event is easier to count; check that a full list of outcome probabilities sums to 1.

A typical examination item gives a bag or spinner and asks for several probabilities in fraction form. Write the total once, then each count over it. P(red) three tenths, P(not red) seven tenths, P(red or blue) eight tenths.

Another asks for a probability as a decimal and a percentage. Three eighths is 0,375, which is 37,5%. Divide, then multiply by a hundred.

Another gives probabilities for all colours but one and asks for the missing one. Subtract the sum from 1.

Another describes two coins or a coin and a die and asks for a probability. List the outcomes in full — HH, HT, TH, TT — and count; never assume one of each is a single outcome.

Another asks whether a described game is fair. Compute each player's probability of winning; fair means equal probabilities.

Errors to avoid. Counting categories instead of outcomes. Missing or duplicating outcomes in two-step lists. Confusing more than with at least. Probabilities greater than 1. Forgetting to simplify, or simplifying wrongly. Writing a probability as a ratio such as 3 to 7 when a fraction is asked for.

Looking ahead: the next lesson runs the experiments — many tosses, many spins — and compares the relative frequency observed with the probability calculated here. Grade 9 extends to tree diagrams and compound events. Final questions follow.
