### subtopic_1 — Relative Frequency from a Series of Trials
**Assistant:** Is relative frequency just another name for probability?

**Tutor:** No, and the distinction is the heart of this lesson. Probability is a number decided by the structure of the situation before anything happens: a fair coin has P(heads) of one half whether or not it is ever tossed. Relative frequency is a number measured from a particular series of trials after they happen: twenty-seven heads in sixty tosses gives 0,45, and a second sixty tosses might give 0,52. Probability is fixed; relative frequency varies from experiment to experiment and depends on how many trials were run. They are connected because relative frequency settles towards probability as the number of trials grows, which is why a long series of trials is used to estimate a probability that cannot be calculated. But a relative frequency from a short series is not the probability, and even from a long series it is an estimate of the probability, not the thing itself. Keep the two words separate in your writing: predicted or theoretical for probability, observed or experimental for relative frequency.

### subtopic_2 — Predicting the Number of Occurrences
**Assistant:** What should I do when the expected number of occurrences is not a whole number?

**Tutor:** Report it as it is, and interpret it as a long-run average. One sixth of 100 rolls is 16,67 sixes, and that is the right expected value even though a count in any single experiment must be a whole number. It means that if the hundred-roll experiment were repeated many times, the average number of sixes per experiment would be about 16,67; in any one run you would see 14, or 17, or 20. If a question asks how many sixes you would expect, 16,67 or about 17 is acceptable, and about 17 is the practical answer; do not round silently to 16 or 17 in a calculation that continues, because the rounding error compounds. The same applies when working backwards: forty draws to expect twelve reds is exact, but if the arithmetic gave 38,4 draws you would say about 38 or 39, and state that the expectation is approximate. The expected value is a prediction about the average, and averages need not be whole numbers even when the things being averaged are.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Relative Frequency with Probability
**Assistant:** How big a difference between relative frequency and probability should make me suspicious?

**Tutor:** It depends on the number of trials, and that dependence is the whole point. With sixty coin tosses, counts of heads from about 22 to 38 — relative frequencies roughly 0,37 to 0,63 — are ordinary chance variation around 0,5, so 27 heads is unremarkable. With six hundred tosses the ordinary band shrinks to roughly 270 to 330 heads, so 0,45 and 0,55 would then be mildly surprising and 0,37 would be strong evidence of bias. A rough rule for Grade 8: in a short series of a few dozen trials, differences of a tenth or more are common; in a series of several hundred, differences of more than about five hundredths start to need an explanation; in thousands, a few hundredths is suspicious. Grade 11 and beyond give exact tools for this. For now, state both numbers, state the number of trials, and use graded language: with so few trials the difference is unremarkable; with this many trials the difference suggests the object is not fair. Never declare proof either way.

### subtopic_5 — The Class That Tossed Until the Board Ran Out
**Assistant:** If a coin has landed tails five times, is heads not due next?

**Tutor:** No. Each toss is independent: the coin has no memory and no obligation to balance the books, so the probability of heads on the sixth toss is still one half. The feeling that heads is due is called the gambler's fallacy, and it comes from confusing two true facts. It is true that in a long series the relative frequency of heads settles towards one half. It is false that this happens by the coin correcting earlier runs; it happens because the earlier run becomes a smaller and smaller fraction of the growing total. Five tails is a lot in five tosses; it is almost nothing in five hundred, where it is swamped rather than cancelled. Runs of five are also far more common than intuition says — in three hundred tosses there will typically be several — so a run is not evidence that anything unusual is happening. The practical consequence is that no betting strategy based on what came before can beat a fair coin, which is why casinos are happy to display the recent results of their roulette wheels.
