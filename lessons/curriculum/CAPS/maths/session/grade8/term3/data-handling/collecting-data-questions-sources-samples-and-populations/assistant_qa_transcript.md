### subtopic_1 — Posing a Statistical Question
**Assistant:** What makes a question statistical rather than just a question with an answer?

**Tutor:** A question is statistical when its answer is not one fact but a spread of values across many people, places or items, so that data must be collected and described to answer it. How tall is the tallest learner has a single answer: find that learner and measure. How tall are Grade 8 learners has two hundred answers that differ, and the honest reply is a description of the spread — a typical height, the shortest and tallest, how bunched or scattered the heights are. The difference matters because it tells you whether you need the data cycle at all. A statistical question also needs to be specific enough to collect for: it names the group, the quantity and the time or place, and it points towards a kind of data — measured, counted or categorical — so you know how the answers will be recorded. Opinion questions such as should the school ban plastic bottles are not statistical, but a statistical question about bottle counts can supply evidence for that debate.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing a Representative Sample
**Assistant:** If I pick learners randomly, can the sample still turn out to be unrepresentative?

**Tutor:** Yes, by chance, especially if the sample is small. Random selection gives every learner an equal chance, but it does not guarantee that a particular sample of ten will have the right mix of grades or genders; you could draw eight Grade 12s by luck. What random selection does guarantee is that there is no systematic lean: on average, over many samples, the mix is right, and as the sample grows the mix in any single sample gets closer to the population's. That is why the advice is fairness first, then size: random selection removes bias, and a larger sample reduces the chance variation. A biased method, by contrast, leans the same way every time, and making the sample larger just makes the lean more confident. In practice, after drawing a random sample, check it against what you know about the population — the proportion in each grade, for instance — and if it is badly off by bad luck, draw again or draw more. State in your method that you did so.

### subtopic_4 — Primary and Secondary Sources of Data
**Assistant:** Can a secondary source be biased too, or is bias only about my own sampling?

**Tutor:** A secondary source can be biased in exactly the same way, because someone else collected it from some sample using some method, and that method may have favoured part of the population. A survey published by a company that sells a product, a poll run on a website, a set of figures from an organisation with something to prove — each may rest on a self-selected or convenience sample. That is why the reliability of the source matters and why you record it. A national statistics agency uses carefully designed random samples and publishes its method; a social media poll does not. When using secondary data, ask the same three questions as for your own: which population does it claim to describe, who was actually in the sample, and could entry into the sample be linked to the answers. Also check the date and the definitions, because a category such as household or employed may be defined differently from the way your question needs it. Secondary data saves effort; it does not save you from judging the method behind it.

### subtopic_6 — Spotting Biased Samples in Everyday Claims
**Assistant:** Why is a bigger sample not enough to fix bias?

**Tutor:** Because bias is a lean built into the method of choosing, and the method does not change when you apply it to more people. If the radio phone-in ran for a week instead of an hour and collected a thousand callers instead of fifty, the callers would still be listeners with airtime and strong feelings, and the thousand would lean the same way as the fifty. The estimate becomes more precise — more confident — about the wrong population. Size helps with a different problem: chance variation in a fair sample, where a random fifty might by luck be slightly off and a random five hundred will be closer. So the two tools address two separate faults: random selection addresses bias, and sample size addresses chance error. Fixing one does nothing for the other. A small random sample is an honest estimate with wide uncertainty; a large biased sample is a confident statement about a group other than the one claimed. Of the two, the honest estimate is the useful one.
