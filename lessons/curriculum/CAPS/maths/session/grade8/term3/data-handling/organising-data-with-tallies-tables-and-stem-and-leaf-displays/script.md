# Part 1 — Expert

This session organises and records data using tally marks, frequency tables and stem-and-leaf displays, and groups numerical data into class intervals. Raw data is counted into tallies in groups of five, converted to a frequency table whose frequencies sum to the total number of observations, and, for numerical data, arranged in a stem-and-leaf display that keeps every value while showing the distribution's shape. Larger numerical data sets are grouped into equal-width, non-overlapping, exhaustive intervals. The anchor data sets are the transport modes of thirty learners and the test marks out of fifty of twenty learners.

## Subtopic: Tally Marks and Frequency Tables for Categories

Raw data is the pile of answers exactly as collected: thirty forms, each with a tick beside walk, taxi, car or bus. To organise it, make a table with one row per category and go through the forms one at a time, making a tally mark in the matching row for each. Tally marks are grouped in fives: four vertical strokes and a fifth across them. Grouping in fives means that a row can be counted at a glance — three groups and two singles is seventeen — and that losing your place is unlikely.

Work through the forms in order and tick each form off as it is counted, so none is counted twice or skipped. Do not sort the forms into piles first and count the piles; that is faster for small sets but loses the record of each form's tally, and the piles get knocked over. The tally table is the record.

When all forms are tallied, write the count for each row in a frequency column. Frequency means how many times that category occurred. For the thirty learners: walk twelve, taxi nine, car six, bus three. The frequencies must add up to the number of forms: twelve plus nine plus six plus three is thirty. If they do not, something was counted twice or missed, and the forms must be re-tallied. This check is the single most important habit in the whole lesson.

The finished table has three columns — category, tally, frequency — and a total row. Give it a heading that says what was counted and how many: Mode of transport to school, thirty Grade 8 learners. A table without a heading is a list of numbers with no meaning.

The table already answers questions the raw forms could not. The most common mode is walking; the least common is the bus; more learners walk than use taxis and cars combined, since twelve is more than nine plus six, no — nine plus six is fifteen, which is more than twelve, so walking is the most common single mode but not the majority. Reading carefully from the table avoids exactly that kind of slip. Questions on tallies and frequency tables follow now.

## Subtopic: Frequency Tables for Numerical Data

Numerical data is tallied the same way, but the rows are values rather than categories. Twenty learners wrote a test out of fifty. The raw marks, in the order collected: 23, 31, 45, 12, 38, 27, 31, 44, 19, 36, 28, 31, 40, 22, 35, 17, 29, 33, 48, 30. Twenty numbers in no order; the eye cannot find the most common or the spread.

If the values are few and repeat often — number of siblings, shoe size, number of taxis taken — list each value in a row and tally. Number of siblings for a class might give rows 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 or more, with frequencies that sum to the class size. This is an ungrouped frequency table, and it keeps every value exactly.

If the values are many and rarely repeat, as test marks out of fifty are, a row per value would give almost forty rows most with a frequency of zero or one, which organises nothing. Two tools handle this: the stem-and-leaf display, which keeps every value, and the grouped frequency table, which trades the exact values for a shape. Both are in the sections that follow.

Before either, a first step that is always worth taking with numerical data: order it. Write the marks from smallest to largest: 12, 17, 19, 22, 23, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 31, 31, 33, 35, 36, 38, 40, 44, 45, 48. Count them: twenty, the same as the raw list, so nothing was dropped. Ordered data shows the lowest mark at once, twelve, and the highest, forty-eight; it shows that thirty-one appears three times; and it makes every later step — grouping, finding the middle — far easier.

Record the units and what the numbers are: marks out of fifty, for twenty learners. A column of numbers with no units could be marks, minutes or rand, and a table that does not say is incomplete.

Whatever the layout, the check is the same: the frequencies, or the number of ordered values, must equal the number of observations collected. Twenty forms, twenty values, every time. Questions on numerical tables follow.

## Subtopic: Constructing a Stem-and-Leaf Display

A stem-and-leaf display splits each number into a stem — the leading digit or digits — and a leaf — the final digit. For two-digit marks, the stem is the tens digit and the leaf is the units digit: the mark 23 has stem 2 and leaf 3. The display lists the stems in a column, draws a vertical line, and writes each leaf on its stem's row.

For the twenty test marks, the stems are 1, 2, 3 and 4. Go through the ordered list and place each leaf. Stem 1: leaves 2, 7, 9, from the marks 12, 17 and 19. Stem 2: leaves 2, 3, 7, 8, 9. Stem 3: leaves 0, 1, 1, 1, 3, 5, 6, 8. Stem 4: leaves 0, 4, 5, 8. Count the leaves: three, five, eight, four — twenty in total. The check holds.

Leaves on each row are written in increasing order, with a space between them so that each leaf is clearly one digit. A repeated value gets a repeated leaf: the three marks of 31 give three leaves of 1 on stem 3. Nothing is lost; every original mark can be read back from the display.

A key is compulsory. Write it under or beside the display: 2 | 3 means 23. Without the key, the same display could show 230 or 2,3 or 2 300, and the reader cannot know. If the data were three-digit, such as times in seconds from 102 to 148, the stems would be 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and the leaves the units: key 12 | 3 means 123. If the data were decimals such as 4,7 and 5,2, the stem is the whole-number part and the leaf the tenths: key 4 | 7 means 4,7.

If one stem would have far too many leaves and others almost none, the stems can be split: 3 for leaves 0 to 4 and 3 again for leaves 5 to 9. That is optional in Grade 8; the single-stem version is standard.

Build the display in two passes if the data is not yet ordered: first place leaves as they come, then rewrite each row with leaves in order. Working from the ordered list avoids the second pass. Either way, count the leaves at the end against the number of observations. Questions on constructing displays follow.

## Subtopic: Reading Stem-and-Leaf Displays and Grouping into Intervals

A stem-and-leaf display is read in both directions. Read along a row to recover values: stem 4 with leaves 0, 4, 5, 8 is the marks 40, 44, 45 and 48. Read the shape down the column of leaves: the longest row is stem 3, so most marks were in the thirties; the rows thin out above and below, so the data bunches in the thirties and tails off towards twelve and forty-eight. The lowest value is the first leaf of the first stem, twelve; the highest is the last leaf of the last stem, forty-eight. Counting leaves gives how many learners scored in each ten-mark band without any further table.

The display is a picture and a table at once, which is why it is favoured for data sets of twenty to fifty values. Beyond that, the grouped frequency table takes over.

To group data, choose class intervals: ranges of values that are equal in width, do not overlap, and together cover every value. For the marks, intervals of ten are natural: 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39, 40 to 49. Written for whole-number marks, the intervals meet without overlapping. For measured data with decimals, write them as 10 up to but not including 20, and so on, exactly as in questionnaire design.

Tally the ordered marks into the intervals. 10 to 19: 12, 17, 19 — three. 20 to 29: 22, 23, 27, 28, 29 — five. 30 to 39: 30, 31, 31, 31, 33, 35, 36, 38 — eight. 40 to 49: 40, 44, 45, 48 — four. Total twenty. The grouped table has the same counts as the stem-and-leaf rows, because stems of ten and intervals of ten are the same grouping; the difference is that the table has forgotten the individual marks and kept only the counts.

Choosing the interval width. Five to eight intervals usually show the shape well. Find the range — highest minus lowest, forty-eight minus twelve is thirty-six — and divide by the number of intervals you want; round to a convenient width such as 5, 10, 20 or 50. Start the first interval at a round number at or below the lowest value. Every value must fall in exactly one interval; a value equal to a boundary must have one home, which the up to but not including wording guarantees.

Grouping loses detail: the table says eight marks were in the thirties but not which. That is the price of a clear shape, and it is why the raw or ordered data is kept. Questions on reading and grouping follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same organising as stories about a cashier who counts coins in fives, a stem-and-leaf display drawn on a whiteboard during a cricket match, and a water-meter log that only made sense once it was grouped — stories where order revealed what the heap was hiding.

## Subtopic: The Cashier Who Counts in Fives

A cashier at a spaza shop closes the till at the end of the day with a drawer full of coins. She does not count them one by one into a running total, because a customer at the door would make her lose the number. She sorts them into piles by value and stacks each pile in fives: five R2 coins, five R2 coins, five R2 coins, and two left over. Three stacks and two: seventeen coins. She writes 17 beside R2 on a slip, and does the same for each coin. Then she multiplies and adds. If the slip's totals do not match the day's takings, she recounts the stacks — not the heap.

That is a tally table. The coin values are the categories, the stacks of five are the groups of tally marks, the slip is the frequency column, and the day's takings are the total that the frequencies must agree with. She has been doing data handling for years without calling it that.

Her nephew is in Grade 8 and brings home the thirty transport forms from his class. She watches him sort them into four piles and count the piles. The piles slide; he counts walk twice; the total comes to thirty-two from thirty forms. She shows him the slip. One row for each answer, one mark per form, marks in fives, tick the form when it is counted. He does it again: walk twelve, taxi nine, car six, bus three. Twelve and nine is twenty-one, and six is twenty-seven, and three is thirty. The forms are counted.

Then he tries her trick on numbers. His class's test marks out of fifty are on a sheet in the order the teacher marked them. He copies them in order from smallest to largest first, counting to make sure there are still twenty, and then tallies them into tens: the tens are the piles, and the marks are the coins. Three in the tens, five in the twenties, eight in the thirties, four in the forties.

The cashier's rule, which is now his: never count the heap. Sort into rows, mark in fives, write the frequency, add the frequencies and compare with the total you started with. If the sum disagrees, recount the rows, because the rows are small and the heap is big. Questions on the cashier follow.

## Subtopic: A Stem-and-Leaf on the Whiteboard at the Cricket

A school cricket coach keeps a whiteboard in the pavilion. During a Saturday match she writes each batter's score as they come in, but not in a list: she draws a vertical line, writes 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 down the left for the tens, and writes each score's units digit on its row. A score of 7 goes on the 0 row as a 7. A score of 23 goes on the 2 row as a 3. A score of 51 goes on the 5 row as a 1.

By the end of the innings the board reads: 0 row, leaves 0, 2, 4, 7, 8; 1 row, leaves 1, 5, 9; 2 row, leaves 3, 6; 3 row, leaf 4; 4 row, nothing; 5 row, leaf 1. Eleven batters, eleven leaves: five, three, two, one, zero, one. Nobody needs to add anything up to see the story. Most of the team scored under ten; three got into the teens; two into the twenties; one made 34; and one made 51. The 4 row is empty, which is itself information — nobody was out in the forties — and an empty stem is kept in the display so the gap shows.

The coach's key is written in the corner — 2 | 3 means 23 — because a parent once read a 5 row leaf as a score of five hundred and one and asked which record had been broken.

The coach uses the board in two directions. Reading across, she can tell a batter his exact score. Reading down, she sees the shape: a long row at the bottom and a thin tail at the top, meaning the batting relied on one or two players. Next week, if the rows at 1 and 2 are longer and the 0 row shorter, the team has improved, and the board shows it without a single calculation.

She also explains why she does not just write the scores in a column. A column of eleven numbers needs to be read and compared; the stem-and-leaf has already been read and compared by the act of placing each leaf on its row. Organising data is not a chore done after collecting it; done well, it is the first step of understanding it. Questions on the whiteboard follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for organising data. One: make a table with a row for every category or value, go through the raw data once, one tally mark per observation in fives, ticking off each observation. Two: write the frequency for each row and add the frequencies; the sum must equal the number of observations. Three: for numerical data, order the values first and count them. Four: for twenty to fifty numerical values, draw a stem-and-leaf display with stems in a column, leaves in increasing order, repeats repeated, empty stems kept, and a key. Five: for larger sets or when only the shape is needed, choose five to eight equal-width intervals that do not overlap and cover every value, tally into them, and total. Six: give every table and display a heading with what was measured, the units and how many.

A typical examination item gives raw numerical data and asks for a stem-and-leaf display with a key. Order the data, count it, place leaves, count leaves, write the key. Marks are given for the key and for correctly ordered leaves, and lost for a missing value — so count.

Another gives a stem-and-leaf display and asks questions: how many values, the lowest, the highest, how many above a given value, which stem has most. Count leaves; read ends; count leaves at or beyond the value, being careful about whether above includes the value itself.

Another gives raw data and intervals and asks for a grouped frequency table. Tally carefully at boundaries — a mark of 30 belongs to 30 to 39, not 20 to 29 — and check the total.

Another asks you to choose intervals. State the range, choose a round width giving five to eight intervals, start at a round number at or below the minimum, and write the intervals so every value has one home.

Errors to avoid. Frequencies that do not sum to the total. Leaves not in order. No key. Dropping repeated values. Removing an empty stem. Intervals that overlap at a boundary or stop below the maximum. A table with no heading or units.

Looking ahead: the next lesson summarises organised data with the mean, median, mode and range, all of which are far easier to find from an ordered list or a stem-and-leaf than from a heap. Grade 9 draws histograms from grouped tables and compares distributions. Final questions follow.
