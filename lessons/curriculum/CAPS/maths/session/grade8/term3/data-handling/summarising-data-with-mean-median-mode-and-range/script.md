# Part 1 — Expert

This session summarises data with measures of central tendency and dispersion. The mean is the total divided by the number of values; the median is the middle value of the ordered data, or the mean of the two middle values when the count is even; the mode is the most frequent value. The range is the largest value minus the smallest, and the extremes are those two values themselves. Each measure is calculated from raw lists, frequency tables and stem-and-leaf displays, and the effect of an extreme value on each is examined. The anchor data sets are five learners' pocket money, the twenty test marks organised in the previous lesson, and a small firm's monthly salaries.

## Subtopic: The Mean

The mean is the number most people call the average. Add every value and divide by how many values there are. Five learners' weekly pocket money, in rand: 20, 35, 50, 15, 30. Total: 20 plus 35 is 55; plus 50 is 105; plus 15 is 120; plus 30 is 150. Five values. Mean: 150 divided by 5, which is 30. The mean pocket money is R30.

The mean has a meaning beyond the formula: it is the value every learner would have if the total were shared equally. If the five pooled their money and split it evenly, each would get R30. That picture explains the mean's behaviour: every value contributes to the total, so every value moves the mean, and a very large value moves it a great deal.

The mean need not be one of the values, and it need not be a whole number. The twenty test marks from the previous lesson total 619, and 619 divided by 20 is 30,95. No learner scored 30,95; the mean is a summary, not an observation. Keep the decimal; rounding to 31 is acceptable only if the question says so, and then state that you rounded.

Mean from a frequency table. If a value occurs several times, multiply the value by its frequency rather than adding it repeatedly. Siblings: 0 occurs 4 times, 1 occurs 9 times, 2 occurs 6 times, 3 occurs 1 time. Total siblings: 0 times 4 is 0; 1 times 9 is 9; 2 times 6 is 12; 3 times 1 is 3; sum 24. Number of learners: 4 plus 9 plus 6 plus 1, which is 20. Mean: 24 divided by 20, which is 1,2 siblings. The divisor is the number of learners, twenty, not the number of rows, four — the most common error with tables.

Working backwards. If the mean of six values is 14, their total is 6 times 14, which is 84. If five of them are 10, 12, 15, 16 and 17, which total 70, the sixth is 84 minus 70, which is 14. Mean times count gives the total; that identity solves every missing-value problem.

Units follow the data: rand, marks, siblings. State them. Questions on the mean follow now.

## Subtopic: The Median

The median is the middle value when the data is in order. Order first, always; the median of an unordered list is meaningless. Pocket money ordered: 15, 20, 30, 35, 50. Five values; the middle is the third: 30. The median pocket money is R30, the same as the mean in this case — but only by coincidence of this particular data.

With an odd number of values, the median is the single middle value. Its position is found by adding one to the count and halving: for five values, six halved is three, so the third value. For twenty-one values, the eleventh. For ninety-nine, the fiftieth.

With an even number of values there is no single middle; the median is the mean of the two middle values. Six values: 4, 7, 9, 12, 15, 20. The middle pair is the third and fourth, 9 and 12; the median is their mean, which is 21 divided by 2, giving 10,5. For twenty values the middle pair is the tenth and eleventh. The twenty test marks in order have 31 as both the tenth and eleventh values, so the median mark is 31.

From a stem-and-leaf display, count leaves from the top until you reach the middle position. Twenty leaves: the tenth and eleventh. Stem 1 has three, stem 2 has five, making eight; the ninth, tenth and eleventh leaves are the first three on stem 3: 0, 1, 1. The tenth and eleventh are 31 and 31. Median 31, the same answer, found faster.

The median's character: it depends only on the position of the middle, not on the size of the values at the ends. Replace the top mark of 48 with 100 and the median is still 31, because the eleventh value has not moved. That makes the median the right summary when the data has an extreme value or two that would drag the mean — house prices, salaries, waiting times.

Check that the number of values below the median equals the number above it, allowing for ties. Five values: two below the third, two above. That symmetry is what middle means. Questions on the median follow.

## Subtopic: The Mode, the Range and the Extremes

The mode is the value that occurs most often. Shoe sizes of seven learners: 5, 6, 6, 7, 7, 7, 8. Size 7 occurs three times, more than any other: the mode is 7. In the twenty test marks, 31 occurs three times and no other mark more than once: the mode is 31. The mode is the only measure that works for categorical data: the mode of the transport survey is walking, with twelve of thirty — there is no mean or median mode of transport.

A data set can have two modes if two values tie for most frequent — it is bimodal — or no mode at all if every value occurs once. Say so: no mode is a valid answer; do not invent one. The mode can be misleading for small numerical sets, where a value occurring twice by chance becomes the mode, so it is most useful for large sets and for categories.

The range is a measure of dispersion — of spread — rather than of centre. Range is the largest value minus the smallest. Pocket money: 50 minus 15, which is 35, so the range is R35. Test marks: 48 minus 12, which is 36 marks. Shoe sizes: 8 minus 5, which is 3. The range is one number, not two: writing the range as 12 to 48 is giving the extremes, not the range.

The extremes are the smallest and largest values themselves: the minimum and the maximum. For the marks, the extremes are 12 and 48. Reporting the extremes alongside the range tells the reader where the spread sits as well as how wide it is: a range of 36 from 12 to 48 is a different class from a range of 36 from 60 to 96.

The range is sensitive to a single unusual value, since it is calculated from the two most unusual values of all. One learner scoring 2 in a class whose other marks run from 40 to 48 gives a range of 46 that describes nobody. Grade 9 introduces measures of spread that resist this; in Grade 8, report the range, state the extremes, and comment if one extreme is far from the rest.

A full summary of a data set in Grade 8 gives mean, median, mode, range and extremes, each with units, and a sentence saying which measure of centre best represents the data and why. Questions on mode, range and extremes follow.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Measure and the Effect of an Extreme Value

The three measures of centre agree when the data is roughly symmetric and disagree when it is not, and the disagreement is informative. A small firm has five employees with monthly salaries, in rand: 8 000, 9 000, 9 500, 10 000 and 60 000 — four workers and the owner. Total: 8 000 plus 9 000 is 17 000; plus 9 500 is 26 500; plus 10 000 is 36 500; plus 60 000 is 96 500. Mean: 96 500 divided by 5, which is 19 300. Median: the third of the five ordered values, 9 500. Mode: none. Range: 60 000 minus 8 000, which is 52 000.

Which number describes a typical salary at the firm. The mean, R19 300, is higher than what four of the five people earn; nobody earns anything near it. The median, R9 500, is what a typical worker earns. The owner's salary — the extreme value, sometimes called an outlier — has pulled the mean up by nearly ten thousand rand and left the median untouched.

Rule of thumb. Use the mean when the data is spread fairly evenly with no extreme values, because it uses every value and is the basis of most later statistics. Use the median when there are extreme values or the data is bunched at one end with a long tail, because it reports the middle person. Use the mode for categorical data, or when the question is literally which value is most common — the shoe size to stock most of, the most popular snack.

Understand how each measure moves. Add a constant to every value and all three measures rise by that constant. Add one very large value: the mean rises, the median moves at most one position, the mode usually does not change, and the range jumps. Remove the largest value: the mean falls, the median shifts half a position, the range shrinks.

Reporting honestly. A company advertising that its average salary is R19 300 is not lying, but it is choosing the measure that flatters. An examiner may ask which measure a particular person would prefer to quote and why: the owner quotes the mean; a union quotes the median. Being able to answer that is understanding the measures, not just calculating them.

Always state which measure you are using. The word average on its own is ambiguous, and in an examination it usually means the mean unless the question specifies. Questions on choosing a measure follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same summaries as stories about a soccer team arguing over its average goals, a street where one house changed the average price of every other, and a shoe shop that stocks by mode and not by mean — stories about which single number tells the truth.

## Subtopic: The Team That Argued About Its Average

An under-fourteen soccer team plays ten matches and the striker claims the team averages three goals a game. The goalkeeper disagrees: most games they score one. The coach writes the goals on the board, in order of the matches: 1, 0, 2, 1, 1, 3, 1, 0, 1, 20. The twenty was against a side that arrived with eight players.

Order them: 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 3, 20. Ten values. Total: 0 and 0 and five ones make 5; plus 2 is 7; plus 3 is 10; plus 20 is 30. Mean: 30 divided by 10, which is 3. The striker is right about the mean. Median: the fifth and sixth of ten ordered values, both 1; their mean is 1. Mode: 1, which occurs five times. Range: 20 minus 0, which is 20. Extremes: 0 and 20.

The goalkeeper is right about what a typical match looks like: the team scores one goal. The mean of 3 exists only because of one freak match. If the team drops that match and looks at the other nine — total 10, mean a little over 1 — the striker's number collapses. The median and mode did not need the match dropped; they were never fooled by it.

The coach asks a sharper question: which number should the team plan around. If they expect three goals a game they will play carelessly and lose one–nil. If they expect one, they will defend properly. The median and mode are the planning numbers. The mean is the number for the end-of-season poster.

The coach also points out what the range says. A range of 20 does not mean the team's scoring varies wildly from week to week; it means one match was unlike all the others. Stating the extremes, 0 and 20, and noting that nine of ten matches fell between 0 and 3, is the honest description of spread.

Then the coach gives the team the general lesson. When someone quotes an average, ask which one. When a data set has one value far from the rest, the mean follows that value and the median does not. And always write the numbers down in order before anyone argues, because the argument on the bus was about a list nobody had actually looked at. Questions on the team follow.

## Subtopic: The Street Where One House Moved Every Average

Eight houses on a street sold last year. Seven went for prices between R800 000 and R1 100 000; one, a large house on a double plot, went for R4 000 000. A newspaper reports the average house price on the street as nearly R1,4 million, and a family that had been saving for a house there panics.

An estate agent explains with the figures, in thousands of rand: 800, 850, 900, 950, 1 000, 1 050, 1 100, 4 000. Total: the first seven sum to 6 650; plus 4 000 is 10 650. Mean: 10 650 divided by 8, which is 1 331,25 — about R1,33 million. Median: eight values, so the mean of the fourth and fifth, 950 and 1 000, which is 975 — R975 000. Mode: none; every price is different. Range: 4 000 minus 800, which is 3 200 — a range of R3,2 million, driven entirely by one sale. Extremes: R800 000 and R4 000 000.

The family can afford R1 million. Against the mean, they are below average and the street looks out of reach. Against the median, they are above the middle house and can afford more than half the houses that sold. The median is the number that answers their question: what does a typical house here cost.

The agent adds that every measure except the median and the mode was moved by the one sale. Without it, the seven remaining prices have a mean of 950 — 6 650 divided by 7 — a median of 950, and a range of only 300. One house changed the mean by nearly four hundred thousand rand and the range tenfold, and did not change the median by more than a step.

This is why property reports, salary surveys and income statistics quote the median. Wherever a few values can be far larger than the rest — prices, incomes, waiting times, goals in a freak match — the median describes the middle case and the mean describes the total divided out, which is a different thing.

The family buys a house at R980 000, a little above the median, and the newspaper's number is still printed on the sign at the end of the road. Questions on the street follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for summarising a data set. One: order the data and count it; write the count. Two: mean — total divided by count; from a frequency table, multiply each value by its frequency, add, and divide by the total frequency. Three: median — the middle value for an odd count, the mean of the two middle values for an even count; locate it by position in the ordered list or by counting leaves in a stem-and-leaf display. Four: mode — the most frequent value or category; report two modes or no mode where that is the case. Five: range — maximum minus minimum, one number; extremes — the minimum and maximum themselves. Six: state units and say which measure of centre represents the data best and why, naming any extreme value and its effect.

A typical examination item gives seven to twelve values and asks for all five measures. Order, count, total, divide, find the middle, find the repeats, subtract the extremes. Show the ordered list and the total; marks are given for them.

Another gives a frequency table — number of pets against number of learners — and asks for the mean. Multiply each value by its frequency, sum, and divide by the number of learners, not the number of rows.

Another gives a mean and asks for a missing value. Mean times count is the total; subtract the known values. If the mean of five tests is 62 and four scores are 55, 60, 68 and 70, the total is 310, the four known sum to 253, and the fifth is 57.

Another asks which measure is most appropriate for a described data set, with a reason. Extreme values present: median, because the mean is pulled. Categories: mode. Even data, no extremes: mean, because it uses every value.

Errors to avoid. Finding the median of unordered data. Dividing by the number of rows in a frequency table. Giving the range as two numbers. Inventing a mode when none exists. Calling the mean the average without saying so when the question distinguishes. Forgetting units.
