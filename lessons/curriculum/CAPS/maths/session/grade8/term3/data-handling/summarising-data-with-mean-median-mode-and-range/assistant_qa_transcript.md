### subtopic_1 — The Mean
**Assistant:** Why is the mean of a frequency table not just the mean of the values in the first column?

**Tutor:** Because the first column lists each value once, while the data contains each value as many times as its frequency says. If nine learners have one sibling and only one learner has three, the data holds nine ones and a single three; averaging the column 0, 1, 2, 3 would treat the single three as equal in weight to the nine ones, and would give 1,5 instead of the true 1,2. The frequency is the weight. Multiplying each value by its frequency reproduces the full list's total without writing the list out — nine ones contribute nine, six twos contribute twelve — and the sum of the frequencies reproduces the count of the full list. Divide total by count as always. The check is to imagine expanding the table back into the twenty individual values; the mean of that list is what you are calculating, and the table is only a compact way of holding it.

### subtopic_2 — The Median
**Assistant:** If there are two middle values, why take their mean rather than just picking one?

**Tutor:** Because the median is defined as the point with half the data below it and half above, and with an even count that point lies between the two middle values rather than on either. Picking the lower would put one more value above than below; picking the higher would do the reverse. The midpoint of the two — their mean — is the only value that splits the data exactly in half, and it is also fair in the sense that it does not favour the smaller or larger side. For 9 and 12 the median is 10,5, even though 10,5 is not a value in the data, exactly as the mean need not be a data value. When the two middle values are equal, as with the twenty test marks where both are 31, the median is simply that value. The rule does not change: it is always the mean of the two middle values; it just happens that their mean equals them.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing a Measure and the Effect of an Extreme Value
**Assistant:** Is an extreme value a mistake that should be removed before calculating?

**Tutor:** Not automatically. An extreme value is simply a value far from the rest; whether it is a mistake depends on where it came from. The owner's salary of R60 000 is real, and removing it would misdescribe the firm's payroll total. A test mark of 480 out of 50 is a recording error — probably 48 — and should be corrected, with a note. The first step is to check the source: the form, the register, the meter reading. If the value is genuine, keep it, report it as an extreme, and choose the measure of centre that describes the typical case despite it — usually the median — while reporting the mean as well if the total matters. If it is an error and cannot be corrected, remove it and say so, giving the count you actually used. What is never acceptable is quietly dropping a genuine value because it spoils the answer you wanted; the extreme is part of the data, and the honest response is to pick the right measure, not to edit the facts.

### subtopic_6 — The Street Where One House Moved Every Average
**Assistant:** If the median is so much more honest, why do we bother with the mean at all?

**Tutor:** Because the mean carries information the median throws away: it is linked to the total. If you know the mean price and the number of sales you know the total value of sales on the street; if you know the mean salary and the headcount you know the payroll; if you know the mean goals and the matches you know the goals scored all season. The median tells you none of that. The mean also uses every value, so for data without extremes it is the more stable summary — two random samples from the same population will usually have means closer together than medians. And the mean is the foundation of nearly everything in later statistics, from standard deviation to the estimates you will meet in Grade 9 and beyond. So the two are not rivals; they answer different questions. The median asks what the middle case is; the mean asks what the total shared equally is. The street's mean of about R1,33 million is a true statement about total money changing hands; it is simply the wrong answer to a family asking what a typical house costs.
