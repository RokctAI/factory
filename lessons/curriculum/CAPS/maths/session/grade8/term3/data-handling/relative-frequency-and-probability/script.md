# Part 1 — Expert

This session relates relative frequency to probability. The relative frequency of an event is the number of trials in which it occurred divided by the number of trials, and the predicted number of occurrences in a series of trials is the probability multiplied by the number of trials. Relative frequency varies from experiment to experiment, especially in short series, and approaches the theoretical probability as the number of trials grows; where outcomes are not equally likely, relative frequency from a long series is the only available estimate of probability. The anchor experiments are sixty coin tosses, a hundred and twenty die rolls, and a drawing pin dropped two hundred times.

## Subtopic: Relative Frequency from a Series of Trials

A trial is one performance of an experiment: one toss of a coin, one roll of a die, one draw from a bag with the marble put back. A series of trials is many performances, recorded. The relative frequency of an event is the number of trials in which the event occurred divided by the total number of trials.

A coin is tossed sixty times and lands heads twenty-seven times. Relative frequency of heads: 27 out of 60, which is twenty-seven sixtieths, simplifying to nine twentieths, which is 0,45 or 45%. The relative frequency of tails is the rest: 33 out of 60, eleven twentieths, 0,55. The two add to 1, as they must, since every trial was one or the other.

A die is rolled a hundred and twenty times and the results tallied: 1 came up 18 times, 2 came up 22 times, 3 came up 19 times, 4 came up 21 times, 5 came up 17 times, 6 came up 23 times. Check: 18 plus 22 is 40; plus 19 is 59; plus 21 is 80; plus 17 is 97; plus 23 is 120. The relative frequency of a 6 is 23 out of 120, about 0,19. The relative frequency of an even number is the trials showing 2, 4 or 6: 22 plus 21 plus 23, which is 66, out of 120, which is 0,55.

Relative frequency is a fraction between 0 and 1, like a probability, and can be given as a fraction, decimal or percentage, like a probability. But it is a different kind of number. Probability is calculated before the experiment from the structure of the situation; relative frequency is measured after the experiment from what happened. Probability does not change if the experiment is repeated; relative frequency usually does.

Record a series of trials in a tally table exactly as survey data is recorded: one row per outcome, a tally mark per trial, frequency, and a check that the frequencies sum to the number of trials. Then add a column for relative frequency, frequency over total, and check that these sum to 1. Questions on relative frequency follow now.

## Subtopic: Predicting the Number of Occurrences

Probability lets us predict, before the trials, roughly how many times an event should occur. Expected number of occurrences equals the probability multiplied by the number of trials. A fair coin tossed sixty times: P(heads) is one half, so we expect one half of 60, which is 30 heads. A fair die rolled a hundred and twenty times: P(6) is one sixth, so we expect one sixth of 120, which is 20 sixes. P(even) is one half, so 60 even rolls. A bag with three red marbles out of ten, drawn fifty times with replacement: P(red) is three tenths, so we expect three tenths of 50, which is 15 reds.

Expected does not mean guaranteed. The coin experiment gave 27 heads, not 30; the die gave 23 sixes, not 20, and 66 evens, not 60. Those are the results the probability said to expect roughly, and they are roughly right. A result of 55 heads in sixty tosses would be far from the prediction and would make us suspect the coin; 27 is within the ordinary wobble of chance.

The expected number may not be a whole number. Rolling a die 100 times, the expected number of sixes is one sixth of 100, which is 16,67 — about seventeen. Nobody rolls two thirds of a six; the expected value is a long-run average, and in any one experiment the count is a whole number near it.

The prediction also runs the other way, to answer how many trials are needed. If a raffle ticket has probability one hundredth and you want to expect one win, you need a hundred tickets or a hundred draws. If P(red) is three tenths and you want to expect twelve reds, you need 12 divided by three tenths, which is 40 draws.

Predicted relative frequency is simply the probability. Before sixty tosses, we predict a relative frequency of heads of one half; after them, we measure 0,45. Comparing the two is the subject of the next section. Writing the prediction down before the experiment is the discipline that makes the comparison honest: a prediction made after seeing the results is not a prediction. Questions on predicting follow.

## Subtopic: Comparing Relative Frequency with Probability

Relative frequency and probability are compared by putting them side by side in the same form. Coin: predicted one half, 0,5; observed 27 out of 60, 0,45. Difference 0,05. Die, a 6: predicted one sixth, about 0,167; observed 23 out of 120, about 0,192. Difference about 0,025. Even: predicted 0,5; observed 0,55. Difference 0,05. In each case the observed is near the predicted but not equal.

Why not equal. Chance. Each trial is independent of the last, and a fair coin has no memory; it is not obliged to even things out in the next ten tosses. Over sixty tosses, counts anywhere from about 22 to 38 heads are quite ordinary. So a relative frequency of 0,45 is no evidence against fairness. Only a result far outside the ordinary band — say 50 heads in 60 — would be.

Why near. The law of large numbers: as the number of trials grows, the relative frequency settles closer and closer to the probability. Ten tosses can easily give 7 heads, a relative frequency of 0,7. A hundred tosses rarely give 70 heads. A thousand tosses almost never give 700. The count of heads keeps wandering, but as a fraction of the growing total it wanders less. Picture a graph of relative frequency against number of tosses: wild swings at the left, settling into a narrowing band around 0,5 as it moves right.

This gives the practical rules. A short series tells you little; do not conclude a die is loaded from twelve rolls. A long series is the evidence; two hundred rolls with a 6 coming up 60 times — relative frequency 0,3 against a probability of 0,167 — would be suspicious. Pool results: if every learner in a class tosses sixty times and the class combines, the two thousand tosses give a far steadier relative frequency than any one learner's.

The comparison also runs backwards: when the probability is unknown because the outcomes are not equally likely, the relative frequency from a long series is the best estimate of the probability. A drawing pin dropped two hundred times lands point up 128 times: the estimated probability of point up is 128 out of 200, which is 0,64. No formula could have given that number, and more drops would refine it. Questions on comparing follow.

## Subtopic: Fair or Loaded? Using Relative Frequency as Evidence

A common examination task gives the results of a series of trials and asks whether the object seems fair. The method is to compare each outcome's relative frequency with its probability under fairness, and to judge the differences against the size of the series.

A die is rolled 60 times: 1 came up 9 times, 2 came up 11 times, 3 came up 8 times, 4 came up 10 times, 5 came up 12 times, 6 came up 10 times. Expected under fairness: one sixth of 60, which is 10 each. The counts range from 8 to 12, all within two of ten. For sixty rolls, that is ordinary; the die seems fair.

Another die is rolled 600 times: 1 came up 70 times, 2 came up 80 times, 3 came up 75 times, 4 came up 85 times, 5 came up 90 times, 6 came up 200 times. Expected: one sixth of 600, 100 each. Five outcomes are below 100 and the 6 is at 200, double. The relative frequency of a 6 is 200 out of 600, one third, against a probability of one sixth. In six hundred rolls such a gap does not happen by chance; the die is loaded towards 6.

The size of the series changes the judgement. Twelve rolls with four sixes is a relative frequency of one third too, but twelve rolls are far too few to say anything; four sixes in twelve is unremarkable. Six hundred rolls with two hundred sixes is damning. Same relative frequency, different evidence, because of the number of trials.

A spinner with four colours is spun 80 times: red 38, blue 14, green 15, yellow 13. If the sectors were equal, expected 20 each. Red at 38 is nearly double; the others are below. Relative frequency of red: 38 out of 80, 0,475, against an equal-sector probability of 0,25. Either the sectors are unequal — measure them — or the spinner is sticky. Either way, equal sectors is not supported.

State conclusions in the right language. The evidence suggests the die is loaded; the results are consistent with a fair coin. Never say the die is proven fair — a loaded die can produce ordinary-looking results in a short run — and never conclude bias from a handful of trials. Relative frequency is evidence whose weight grows with the number of trials. Questions on fair or loaded follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same comparison as stories about a class that tossed coins until the chalkboard ran out, a bottle top that no formula could predict, and a goalkeeper who kept a diary of penalties — stories about how often luck really is luck.

## Subtopic: The Class That Tossed Until the Board Ran Out

A Grade 8 class is told that a fair coin lands heads half the time, and a learner at the back says that is not what happens in real life, because he once got five tails in a row. The teacher hands out thirty coins and says: ten tosses each, write your heads on the board.

Thirty learners, ten tosses each. The board fills with thirty small numbers: mostly fours, fives and sixes, a few threes and sevens, one 2, one 8 and one 9. The learner at the back got 2 heads in ten, a relative frequency of 0,2, and one learner got 9 of 10, a relative frequency of 0,9. The individual results are all over the place, from 0,2 to 0,9. Each learner's ten tosses tell almost nothing.

Then the teacher adds the column. The total of the thirty numbers is 150 heads out of 300 tosses. Relative frequency 0,5, exactly the probability — a coincidence that it is exact, but not a coincidence that it is close. The class had, between them, run a long series, and the long series settled.

The teacher then goes further. Adding the results ten learners at a time: the first ten learners had 51 heads out of 100, relative frequency 0,51; the first twenty, 99 out of 200, 0,495; all thirty, 150 out of 300, 0,5. The teacher sketches the graph: relative frequency against number of tosses, swinging between 0,2 and 0,9 in the first ten tosses of any learner, then narrowing, then hugging 0,5.

The learner at the back asks about his five tails in a row. The teacher asks the class whether anyone else had a run of four or five the same. Half the hands go up. In three hundred tosses, runs of five happen many times; they feel unusual to the person tossing and are entirely ordinary to the board. The coin has no memory, and a run of tails does not make heads more likely next time — that belief has a name, the gambler's fallacy, and it has cost people a great deal of money.

The class copies down the rule. One learner's ten tosses are a story. Thirty learners' three hundred tosses are evidence. The probability was right all along; it just needed enough trials to show itself. Questions on the class follow.

## Subtopic: The Bottle Top and the Goalkeeper's Diary

Two situations where no formula gives the probability, and relative frequency is the only way in.

A learner wants the probability that a plastic bottle top lands face up when dropped. The top is not symmetric — a cap with a rim — so face up and face down are not equally likely, and favourable over total is useless. She drops it two hundred times and tallies: face up 74, face down 126. Check: 200. Relative frequency of face up: 74 out of 200, which is 0,37. Her estimate of the probability of face up is 0,37. She drops it another two hundred times: 71 face up. Four hundred drops, 145 face up, 0,3625. The estimate steadies around 0,36. Nobody could have calculated that; the shape of the cap decides it, and only the trials reveal it.

She uses the estimate to predict. If the cap is dropped 1 000 times, expect about 0,36 of 1 000, which is 360 face up. Her prediction is as good as her estimate, which is as good as her four hundred trials; a thousand more drops would sharpen it.

A goalkeeper keeps a diary of every penalty she faces for two seasons: 40 penalties. She records where the kicker aimed — left, centre, right — and whether she saved it. Aimed left: 18. Centre: 6. Right: 16. Relative frequencies: left 18 out of 40, 0,45; centre 6 out of 40, 0,15; right 16 out of 40, 0,4. Equally likely would be one third each, about 0,33; the data says kickers favour the sides and avoid the centre. She also records saves: 9 out of 40, relative frequency 0,225.

The goalkeeper now uses relative frequency as probability for the next season. If she faces 20 penalties, she expects about 0,45 of 20, which is 9 to her left, 3 to the centre and 8 to her right. She also knows that forty penalties is a modest series, and that a new kicker with different habits is not a coin — the trials are not identical, which is a limitation she notes.

Both stories end the same way. When symmetry gives a probability, relative frequency confirms it. When symmetry gives nothing, relative frequency replaces it, and the more trials, the better the replacement. Questions on the bottle top and the diary follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for relative frequency and probability. One: record a series of trials in a tally table with a row per outcome; frequencies sum to the number of trials. Two: relative frequency of an event is its frequency divided by the number of trials; the relative frequencies of all outcomes sum to 1. Three: predicted number of occurrences is probability times number of trials; predicted relative frequency is the probability itself. Four: compare observed relative frequency with probability in the same form — both decimals or both fractions — and judge the difference against the length of the series: short series wander widely, long series settle. Five: where outcomes are not equally likely, estimate probability by the relative frequency of a long series, and use that estimate to predict further trials.

A typical examination item gives trial results and asks for the relative frequency of an event. Frequency over trials, simplified or as a decimal as asked. Twenty-three sixes in 120 rolls: 23 over 120, about 0,19.

Another asks how many times an event is expected in a given number of trials. Probability times trials: one sixth of 300 is 50.

Another asks you to compare an experiment's result with the theoretical probability and comment. Give both numbers, state the difference, and attribute it to chance variation in a short series or to possible bias in a long one, using the words consistent with or suggests.

Another gives relative frequencies from an experiment with an unfair object and asks for the probability. The relative frequency from the longest available series is the estimate; say that more trials would improve it.

Another asks what happens to relative frequency as trials increase. It approaches the probability.

Errors to avoid. Dividing by the number of outcomes instead of the number of trials. Treating a relative frequency of 0,45 as proof of an unfair coin. Expecting exactly the predicted count. Believing a run of one outcome makes the other due. Using favourable over total for a non-symmetric object.

Looking ahead: the Grade 8 data cycle is complete — question, collect, organise, summarise, and now chance. Grade 9 adds tree diagrams, two-way tables and compound probabilities, and continues the comparison of experiment with theory. Final questions follow.
