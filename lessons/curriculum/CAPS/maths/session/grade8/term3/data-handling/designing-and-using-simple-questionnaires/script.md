# Part 1 — Expert

This session designs simple questionnaires with multiple-choice responses, the main instrument for primary data collection in the data handling cycle. A closed question offers a fixed set of responses so that answers can be counted; the options must be mutually exclusive, so that each respondent fits exactly one, and exhaustive, so that every respondent fits somewhere. Questions must be neutral, single-topic and unambiguous, numerical options are grouped into non-overlapping intervals, and the whole instrument is trialled on a few people before it is used. The anchor instrument is the tuck shop questionnaire from the previous lesson, with questions on snack preference, weekly spending and travel time.

## Subtopic: Closed Questions and Why Options Must Be Fixed

A questionnaire is a list of questions given to every member of the sample in the same words. There are two kinds of question. An open question lets the respondent write anything: what do you think of the tuck shop. A closed question offers a fixed list of responses and asks the respondent to choose: which one of these five snacks would you buy most often at break. CAPS Grade 8 uses closed questions with multiple-choice responses, and for a good reason: closed responses can be counted. Forty answers of fruit are forty tally marks; forty paragraphs of opinion are forty things to interpret, and two people would interpret them differently.

A closed question has three parts. The question itself, in plain words. The response options, each in a box or beside a letter. An instruction: tick one box, or tick all that apply. The instruction matters because tick one and tick all that apply produce different data and are counted differently.

The options must be fixed before the survey starts and must be the same on every copy. If one copy lists chips, fruit, sweets and another lists chips, fruit, sweets, yoghurt, the two sets of answers cannot be combined. Once the options are set, do not change them halfway through.

Options should be worded as the respondents themselves would say them. Fruit, not fresh produce; taxi, not public road transport. A respondent who has to stop and work out what an option means may guess, and guesses are not data.

Closed questions do have a cost: they can only find what the designer thought to list. If the most wanted snack is one you did not offer, the questionnaire cannot discover it. The usual safeguard is one last option — other, please specify — which keeps the question closed for counting while leaving a door open. If many respondents write the same thing under other, the next version of the questionnaire adds it as an option. Questions on closed questions follow now.

## Subtopic: Options That Do Not Overlap and Leave No Gaps

The options of a tick-one question must satisfy two conditions. They must be mutually exclusive: no respondent can honestly tick two. And they must be exhaustive: every respondent can honestly tick one. Break either condition and the data is damaged before it is collected.

Overlap. How do you get to school: walk, taxi, bus, car, with a friend. A learner who comes by car with a friend fits two boxes. Fix: make the categories about one thing — the vehicle — and drop with a friend, or ask it as a separate question.

Gaps. How much do you spend at the tuck shop in a week: R0 to R10, R11 to R20, R21 to R30. A learner who spends R45 has no box. A learner who spends R10,50 has no box either, because the options are whole rand. Fix: add a top category — more than R30 — and define the boundaries so that every amount lands somewhere: less than R10; R10 up to but not including R20; R20 up to but not including R30; R30 or more. The phrase up to but not including is what makes the intervals meet without overlapping.

Boundaries that overlap are a common mistake with numbers: 0 to 10, 10 to 20, 20 to 30. Where does ten go. A respondent who spends exactly R10 can tick two boxes, and two respondents in the same position may tick different ones. The fix is either to use the up to but not including wording or to write 0 to 9, 10 to 19, 20 to 29 for whole-number data.

Exhaustive also means covering the respondents who do not fit the frame. How many times a week do you buy from the tuck shop should allow never. Which snack would you buy most often should allow I would not buy anything. Leaving these out forces people to lie or to skip the question, and skipped questions are missing data.

A simple test for a question's options: imagine five very different respondents — one who never does the thing, one who does it a great deal, one who is exactly on a boundary, one who does two things at once, one who is unusual — and check each has exactly one box. If any has none or two, redesign. Questions on options follow.

## Subtopic: Neutral, Single and Unambiguous Wording

The wording of a question changes the answers it collects. Three faults to avoid: leading questions, double-barrelled questions and ambiguous questions.

A leading question pushes the respondent towards an answer. Do you agree that the tuck shop's overpriced junk food should be replaced with healthy options. The words overpriced and junk tell the respondent what to think. Neutral version: which of these should the tuck shop sell more of, with the options listed plainly. Leading also hides in the options: offering healthy fruit versus greasy chips loads the choice. Describe options without adjectives.

A double-barrelled question asks two things at once and gets one answer. Do you buy from the tuck shop and are you happy with the prices. A learner who buys but is unhappy cannot answer yes or no honestly. Split it: do you buy from the tuck shop, yes or no; if yes, how do you find the prices, with options. Watch for the word and in a question stem: it is the usual sign.

An ambiguous question means different things to different people. Do you often buy from the tuck shop: often might be daily to one learner and weekly to another, so the yes answers cannot be compared. Replace vague frequency words with counted options: never; once or twice a week; three or four times a week; every day. Do you spend a lot at the tuck shop has the same problem; ask for a rand interval instead.

Time frames should be stated. How much do you spend at the tuck shop could mean per day, per week or since Grade 7. Write in a typical school week. Terms should be defined if they could be misread: by snack we mean anything bought at break, including drinks.

Avoid negatives and double negatives: do you not disagree with the proposal confuses everyone. Avoid asking for information people cannot know accurately: how many rand have you spent at the tuck shop this year is a guess. Ask what people can recall: last week.

Finally, respect privacy and keep the questionnaire anonymous if any question is sensitive — pocket money, home circumstances. People answer honestly when no name is attached, and honest answers are the point. Questions on wording follow.

## Subtopic: Ordering, Trialling and Recording Responses

With the questions written, assemble the questionnaire. Keep it short: for a Grade 8 survey, five to eight questions that take under three minutes. Long questionnaires are abandoned halfway, and half-finished forms are missing data. Start with the easiest, least personal questions — grade, how you travel — and put anything sensitive — pocket money — near the end, after the respondent is comfortable. Put a one-line heading saying what the survey is for and that it is anonymous.

Number the questions. Put the instruction with each: tick one box. Use boxes or circles for the options rather than asking the respondent to write a letter, because a tick is faster and harder to misread. Leave an other, please specify line where the options might not be complete.

Before the real survey, run a trial — a pilot — on four or five people who are like the sample but not in it. Watch them fill it in. Where they hesitate, the question is unclear. Where they ask what a word means, define it. Where they tick two boxes, the options overlap. Where they leave a question out, there may be a gap or the question may be too personal. Then fix the questionnaire. A pilot costs ten minutes and saves the whole survey; a fault found after ninety forms are in cannot be fixed.

Decide before collecting how the responses will be recorded. The standard tool is a tally table with one row per option per question, filled in by going through the forms one at a time. Later lessons turn tallies into frequency tables, stem-and-leaf displays and summaries. Design the recording table now, from the questionnaire, so that every option has a row, including other and no response.

Keep the forms. If a tally looks wrong, the forms are the only way to recount. Number each form as it comes in so that none is counted twice or missed.

Record how the data was collected alongside the data: how many forms were given out, how many came back complete, who was sampled and how, and the date. The response rate — forms returned out of forms given — is itself a piece of information, because a low rate hints that those who answered may differ from those who did not. Questions on assembling and trialling follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same craft as stories about a questionnaire that asked two things in one breath, a spending question with a hole in it that forty learners fell through, and a trial run on five classmates that saved a whole survey — stories about the boxes nobody could tick.

## Subtopic: The Question That Asked Two Things

The tuck shop group from the previous lesson drafts its questionnaire in one afternoon and feels pleased. Question three reads: do you buy from the tuck shop and would you buy fruit if it were sold. Yes or no. They print ninety copies.

The first respondent, a Grade 11, holds the pen over the boxes and asks: I do buy, but I would not buy fruit, so which. The group looks at the question and sees the word and sitting in the middle like a fork in the road. Two questions have been welded into one, and the two possible answers cannot carry four possible situations: buy and would, buy and would not, do not buy and would, do not buy and would not. The fourth kind of learner is exactly the one the tuck shop wants to attract, and the question cannot even see them.

They stop and redraft. Question three: do you buy from the tuck shop in a typical week. Options: never; once or twice; three or four times; every day. Question four: if fruit were sold at R5 a piece, would you buy it at least once a week. Yes; no; not sure. Now the Grade 11 ticks every day and no, and a learner who never buys can tick never and yes, and the tuck shop can count how many non-buyers fruit would bring in.

While they are redrafting they read every question aloud, listening for the word and. Question six: do you think the tuck shop is good value and well run. Welded again. They split it. Question seven: how long do you spend in the tuck shop queue. The options are short, medium and long, and the group realises that one learner's long is another's short, so the options become under two minutes; two to five minutes; more than five minutes — with the up to but not including understood at the boundary, stated as two minutes or more up to five.

The ninety printed copies go in the recycling, which is the environmental issue from another survey. The group prints again and runs a pilot first this time. The lesson: every and in a question stem is a fork; split it, and let the respondent walk down one road at a time. Questions on the welded question follow.

## Subtopic: The Hole in the Spending Question

Another class surveys weekly spending at a shopping centre's food court for an economics project. Their question: how much do you spend here in a week. Options: R0 to R20; R20 to R50; R50 to R100. They collect one hundred and twenty forms in a Saturday morning.

Tallying on Monday, they hit trouble three ways. Eleven forms have two ticks, all at the boundaries: people who spend exactly R20 or R50 ticked both boxes they fit. Forty forms have no tick at all, and when the group reads the comments scrawled beside the question — I spend about R150; way more than that — they realise that regular food court customers spend more than R100, and the top option left them nowhere to go. And a few forms have a tick plus the note never, just walking through, because the options had no zero and the first box, R0 to R20, lumps someone who spends nothing with someone who spends R19.

Fifty-one forms out of one hundred and twenty are unusable or doubtful on that question — more than forty percent of the data gone. Worse, the forty who skipped are the big spenders, so the remaining data understates spending. A gap at the top is not just missing data; it is biased missing data.

The redesign. Options: nothing; less than R20; R20 up to but not including R50; R50 up to but not including R100; R100 up to but not including R200; R200 or more. Six options, every rand amount in exactly one, including zero, including the big spenders. A pilot on five shoppers finds nobody hesitating.

They also add a line to the heading: in a typical week, counting food and drinks bought in this food court only, because two pilot respondents asked whether the cinema counted.

The group goes back the next Saturday. This time three forms out of one hundred and thirty have problems, and all three are forms left blank entirely. The data on spending now has a top interval with twenty-two forms in it, which the first design had made invisible. The lesson: imagine the extremes before you print — the person who spends nothing and the person who spends the most — and give both a box; and make every boundary belong to exactly one interval. Questions on the spending hole follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for designing a questionnaire. One: start from the statistical question and the population; each item on the form must help answer that question. Two: write closed questions with fixed options; include an instruction — tick one — on each. Three: make options mutually exclusive and exhaustive: no overlaps, no gaps, include never, nothing, other and not sure where they could apply; for numbers, use intervals with up to but not including boundaries and an open top interval. Four: word neutrally, one topic per question, no vague frequency words, with the time frame stated. Five: order easy to sensitive, keep it short, state the purpose and anonymity. Six: pilot on a few people and fix. Seven: design the tally table before collecting, number the forms, and record the response rate.

A typical examination item shows a flawed question and asks what is wrong and how to fix it. Do you agree that learners waste too much time on their phones. Leading, through the word waste; also ambiguous, because too much is undefined. Fix: how many hours do you spend on your phone on a typical school day, with intervals: less than one; one up to but not including two; two up to but not including four; four or more.

Another shows age intervals 10 to 15, 15 to 20, 20 to 25 and asks for the fault. Overlap at 15 and 20; fix with 10 to 14, 15 to 19, 20 to 24 for whole-year ages, and a top interval of 25 or older if the population includes them.

Another asks you to write one closed question with suitable options for a given purpose. Give the question, three to six options, and the instruction. Check the five imagined respondents.

Errors to avoid. Options that overlap at a boundary. No top interval. No never or nothing. The word and in a stem. Loaded adjectives. Asking for what people cannot recall. Skipping the pilot. Changing options after some forms are in.

Looking ahead: the next lesson records the responses in tally and frequency tables and stem-and-leaf displays, and groups measured data into intervals — the same intervals you have just learnt to design. Grade 9 extends to survey design with larger samples and compares results across groups. Final questions follow.
