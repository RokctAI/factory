### subtopic_2 — Volume: Cross-Section Times Length
**Assistant:** Why does cross-section times length work for every prism?

**Tutor:** Because a prism is the same all the way through. Slice it anywhere parallel to its ends and the slice has exactly the same shape and area as the end. So the prism can be thought of as a stack of identical thin slices, like a loaf of sliced bread where every slice is the same shape. If each slice is 1 centimetre thick and has area A square centimetres, its volume is A cubic centimetres, and a prism 10 centimetres long holds 10 such slices, so its volume is A times 10. Thinner slices give the same result, because the number of slices goes up as their thickness goes down. For a rectangular box this is length times breadth times height, the familiar counting of cubes in layers. For a triangular prism the slices are triangles, each of area half base times height. The same reasoning will work in Grade 9 for a cylinder, whose slices are circles of area pi r squared. What makes it fail is a shape that changes along its length, such as a pyramid or a cone, which narrows to a point; those have different formulae, met in later grades. So the first question with any solid is whether every cross-section is the same.

### subtopic_3 — Capacity: Millilitres and Litres
**Assistant:** Why does a cubic centimetre hold exactly one millilitre?

**Tutor:** Because that is how the millilitre is defined in the metric system. When the system was designed, the litre was set as the volume of a cube 10 centimetres on each edge, which is 1 000 cubic centimetres, and the millilitre, one thousandth of a litre, therefore equals 1 cubic centimetre exactly. It was a deliberate choice to link length, volume and capacity so that conversions would be simple. The link extends to mass as well, approximately: 1 millilitre of water has a mass of about 1 gram, so 1 litre is about 1 kilogram and 1 cubic metre of water is about 1 000 kilograms, a metric tonne. That is how the fish tank's 54 litres of water became about 54 kilograms. These neat relationships are one of the great advantages of the metric system: a container's dimensions in centimetres give its capacity in millilitres by a single multiplication, with no conversion factor at all. Remember that the link is between cubic centimetres and millilitres; a cubic metre is a much larger unit and holds 1 000 litres, so a tank measured in metres needs its volume multiplied by 1 000 to give litres.

### subtopic_4 — Triangular Prisms
**Assistant:** How do I know which measurement in a triangular prism is the length?

**Tutor:** Find the two identical triangles first; the length of the prism is the distance between them, measured along the edges that join corresponding corners. In a tent standing on the ground, the triangles are the front and back openings, and the length is how far it is from the front to the back. In a diagram, the triangles are often drawn at the ends, and the length is the long edge running between them, but a prism can also be drawn lying on a triangular face, so look for the matching pair of triangles rather than relying on orientation. Once you have the triangles, separate their measurements into two kinds: the base and perpendicular height, which give the triangle's area; and the three sides, which give the widths of the rectangular faces. The perpendicular height is often not a side at all, but a dashed line inside the triangle. A typical diagram might show a triangle with base 2, height 1,5 and slanting sides 1,8, and a length of 3. The volume uses 2, 1,5 and 3; the rectangles use 2, 1,8, 1,8 and 3. Labelling each number on a sketch before calculating prevents most errors.

### subtopic_6 — The Camping Club's Tent and the Juice Carton
**Assistant:** Why does a bigger carton use less card per millilitre?

**Tutor:** Because when a shape is enlarged, its volume grows faster than its surface area. Under an enlargement by a scale factor k, every length is multiplied by k, every area by k squared, and every volume by k cubed. To hold four times as much juice, the carton's volume must be multiplied by 4, so k cubed is 4 and k is the cube root of 4, about 1,59. The card, being an area, is multiplied by k squared, about 2,52. So four times the juice needs only about two and a half times the card. Per millilitre, the large carton uses about 2,52 divided by 4, roughly 63 percent as much card as the small one. This is why bulk packaging is cheaper per unit, why large animals lose heat more slowly than small ones, and why a big block of ice melts more slowly than the same mass of crushed ice. In practice, manufacturers do not always enlarge exactly, but the general principle holds: larger, more compact containers have less surface for each unit of volume.
