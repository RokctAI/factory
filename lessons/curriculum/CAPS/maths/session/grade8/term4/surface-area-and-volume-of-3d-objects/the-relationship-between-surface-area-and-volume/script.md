# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the interrelationship between surface area and volume. Rectangular prisms with the same volume can have very different surface areas: arranging 24 unit cubes as a long rod gives a surface area of 98 square units, but as a 2 by 3 by 4 block only 52. The more compact and cube-like the shape, the smaller its surface area for a given volume. Conversely, a fixed amount of surface encloses the most volume when the shape is compact. When every length is multiplied by a scale factor k, surface area is multiplied by k squared and volume by k cubed, so larger objects have less surface for each unit of volume: a cube of edge 1 has 6 square units of surface per cubic unit, a cube of edge 6 only 1. The anchor examples are boxes built from 24 and 36 unit cubes and cubes of edge 1 to 6.

## Subtopic: Same Volume, Different Surface Area

Take 24 cubes, each 1 centimetre on every edge. Whatever box they are packed into, the volume is 24 cubic centimetres. But the surface area depends on the arrangement.

Lay them in a single row: a rod 1 by 1 by 24. Surface area: 2 times the bracket 1 times 1 plus 1 times 24 plus 1 times 24, which is 2 times 49, which is 98 square centimetres.

A 1 by 2 by 12 slab: 2 times the bracket 2 plus 12 plus 24, which is 76.

A 1 by 3 by 8 slab: 2 times the bracket 3 plus 8 plus 24, which is 70.

A 1 by 4 by 6 slab: 2 times the bracket 4 plus 6 plus 24, which is 68.

A 2 by 2 by 6 block: 2 times the bracket 4 plus 12 plus 12, which is 56.

A 2 by 3 by 4 block: 2 times the bracket 6 plus 8 plus 12, which is 52.

Same 24 cubic centimetres every time, but the surface area ranges from 98 down to 52 — the rod needs nearly twice as much wrapping as the block.

The reason is hidden faces. Each cube has 6 faces, 144 faces in all among 24 cubes. Wherever two cubes touch, two faces are hidden inside the box and do not count as surface. The more cubes press against each other, the more faces are hidden and the smaller the surface. In the rod each cube touches at most two others; in the compact block, many cubes touch three or four.

The pattern: the closer the box is to a cube, with its three dimensions as equal as possible, the smaller its surface area. Twenty-four is not a perfect cube, so no arrangement is a true cube; 2 by 3 by 4 is the most nearly equal and has the least surface.

Try 36 cubes. The rod 1 by 1 by 36 has surface area 146. The block 3 by 3 by 4, the most cube-like, has surface area 2 times the bracket 9 plus 12 plus 12, which is 66. And 64 cubes can make a perfect cube, 4 by 4 by 4, with surface area 96, which is less than any other arrangement of 64.

So volume alone does not decide surface area. To know how much material a box needs, you must know its shape, not just how much it holds. Questions on same volume, different surface follow now.

## Subtopic: Same Surface Area, Different Volume

The relationship also runs the other way. A fixed amount of material can enclose different volumes, depending on the shape it is folded into.

Two boxes with the same surface area. A 1 by 1 by 5 box: surface area 2 times the bracket 1 plus 5 plus 5, which is 22 square centimetres; volume 5 cubic centimetres. A 1 by 2 by 3 box: surface area 2 times the bracket 2 plus 3 plus 6, which is 22 square centimetres; volume 6 cubic centimetres. Same material, but the second box holds 20 percent more.

A sheet-metal workshop has a square sheet of steel 30 centimetres on each side and must make an open-topped tray by cutting a square from each corner and folding up the sides. Cut 5-centimetre squares: the base is 20 by 20 and the sides are 5 high; volume 20 times 20 times 5, which is 2 000 cubic centimetres. Cut 2-centimetre squares: base 26 by 26, height 2; volume 1 352. Cut 10-centimetre squares: base 10 by 10, height 10; volume 1 000. All three trays use material from the same sheet, but 5-centimetre corners give the most volume. Too shallow and the tray holds little; too deep and the base becomes tiny.

The pattern is the mirror image of the previous section: for a fixed amount of surface, the most compact shape encloses the most volume. Among closed rectangular boxes made from the same area of material, the cube holds the most.

This is why so many containers that must be strong and cheap are close to compact shapes, and why nature favours rounded forms. A sphere, in fact, is even more compact than a cube: it encloses the most volume for its surface of any shape at all, which is why soap bubbles and raindrops are round. But spheres are hard to stack and store, so boxes are a practical compromise, and boxes near the shape of a cube are the most economical boxes.

Real packaging is not always cube-shaped, for good reasons: cereal boxes are tall and thin so that the large front panel can carry advertising and so that they fit on a shelf; a pizza box is flat because a pizza is flat. Economy of material is one consideration among several.

In summary: fixing volume, compact shapes minimise surface area; fixing surface area, compact shapes maximise volume. Both statements describe the same relationship. Questions on same surface, different volume follow now.

## Subtopic: Scaling Up: Squares and Cubes

What happens to surface area and volume when a solid is enlarged? From the transformation lessons, lengths are multiplied by the scale factor k and areas by k squared. Volumes, built from three lengths, are multiplied by k cubed.

Watch it on cubes. A cube of edge 1: surface area 6, volume 1. Edge 2: surface area 6 times 4, which is 24; volume 8. Edge 3: surface area 54; volume 27. Edge 4: surface area 96; volume 64. Edge 5: surface area 150; volume 125. Edge 6: surface area 216; volume 216.

From edge 1 to edge 2, k is 2: surface area times 4, volume times 8. From edge 1 to edge 3: surface area times 9, volume times 27. Volume grows much faster than surface area.

Why k cubed? A box 5 by 3 by 2 enlarged by 2 becomes 10 by 6 by 4. Its volume goes from 30 to 240, which is 8 times, because each of the three dimensions was doubled: 2 times 2 times 2. Each unit cube of the original becomes a 2 by 2 by 2 block of 8 unit cubes. The surface goes from 62 to 248, which is 4 times, because each face is an area, doubled in two directions.

The consequence: for every cubic unit of volume, a larger object has less surface. The ratio of surface area to volume for the cubes above: 6 to 1, then 24 to 8, which is 3; 54 to 27, which is 2; 96 to 64, which is 1,5; 150 to 125, which is 1,2; 216 to 216, which is 1. For a cube, the ratio is 6 divided by the edge, falling steadily as the cube grows. A cube of edge 10 has only 0,6 square units of surface for each cubic unit.

The opposite happens when an object is cut up. A cube of edge 6 centimetres has surface area 216 and volume 216. Cut it into 27 cubes of edge 2 centimetres: the volume is unchanged, 27 times 8, which is 216, but the surface is 27 times 24, which is 648 square centimetres — three times as much. Every cut exposes new faces.

Use the factors to predict without recalculating. A box with surface area 62 and volume 30 is enlarged by 3. New surface area: 62 times 9, which is 558. New volume: 30 times 27, which is 810. Questions on scaling follow now.

## Subtopic: Why the Ratio Matters

The surface-area-to-volume ratio explains a great many everyday facts, because exchanges between an object and its surroundings happen through its surface, while the object's contents are its volume.

Heat. A hot object loses heat through its surface; the amount of heat it holds depends on its volume. A large object has relatively little surface for its volume, so it cools slowly; a small object cools quickly. A big pot of stew stays warm much longer than a single spoonful from it. Small animals lose body heat fast and must eat often; large animals keep warm more easily.

Melting and dissolving. Ice melts at its surface. Crushed ice has far more surface than a single block of the same volume, so it melts much faster. Sugar granules dissolve faster than a sugar cube, and fine powder faster still. Chopping vegetables small makes them cook faster, because heat enters through more surface.

Packaging. Material costs depend on surface area; what is sold depends on volume. Bigger and more compact packages use less material per unit of product, which is one reason bulk packs cost less per kilogram.

Drying. Washing spread out flat dries faster than washing bundled in a heap, because spreading increases the exposed surface.

Living things use the relationship in both directions. Lungs and intestines are folded into enormous surfaces packed into small volumes, because they must absorb as much as possible. Elephants in hot climates have large thin ears that add surface for losing heat. Animals curl into a ball to keep warm, reducing their exposed surface.

To compare two objects, compute the ratio for each: surface area divided by volume. The larger the ratio, the more surface each unit of volume has, and the faster it exchanges heat, water or anything else with its surroundings. Compare a 2 by 3 by 4 block, ratio 52 divided by 24, about 2,17, with a 1 by 1 by 24 rod, ratio 98 divided by 24, about 4,08. The rod would cool, melt or dry nearly twice as fast.

When describing the relationship in words, three statements cover it. For a fixed volume, compact shapes have the least surface. Enlarging an object multiplies surface area by k squared and volume by k cubed. So larger objects have a smaller ratio of surface area to volume, and smaller or more spread-out objects have a larger one. Questions on why the ratio matters follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same relationship as stories about a soap factory choosing the cheapest carton for twelve bars, a braai where a block of ice outlasted a bag of ice cubes, and a learner who discovered why newborn babies are wrapped so warmly — stories about how much outside there is for each bit of inside.

## Subtopic: The Soap Factory's Carton

A small soap factory in Pinetown packs its bars twelve to a cardboard carton for delivery to shops. Each bar is a rectangular prism 9 centimetres long, 6 centimetres wide and 3 centimetres thick, volume 162 cubic centimetres. Twelve bars make 1 944 cubic centimetres, whatever the arrangement. The question is which arrangement needs the least cardboard.

The production manager asks a Grade 8 learner on a school visit to help. The learner lists arrangements: how many bars along the length, how many across the width, how many stacked up, with the three numbers multiplying to 12.

All twelve in a single row, end to end: a carton 108 by 6 by 3 centimetres. Surface area: 2 times the bracket 648 plus 324 plus 18, which is 2 times 990, which is 1 980 square centimetres.

Two along, two across, three high: a carton 18 by 12 by 9 centimetres. Surface area: 2 times the bracket 216 plus 162 plus 108, which is 2 times 486, which is 972 square centimetres.

One along, four across, three high: 9 by 24 by 9. Surface area: 2 times the bracket 216 plus 81 plus 216, which is 1 026.

Two along, three across, two high: 18 by 18 by 6. Surface area: 2 times the bracket 324 plus 108 plus 108, which is 1 080.

The 18 by 12 by 9 carton, the most nearly cubic, needs 972 square centimetres of card — less than half of the 1 980 for the single row. The learner finds two other arrangements that also give 972, and they turn out to be the same 18 by 12 by 9 carton with the bars packed differently.

The manager is impressed but asks one more question: why not wrap each bar separately? A single bar has surface area 2 times the bracket 54 plus 27 plus 18, which is 198 square centimetres, so twelve separate wrappers would need 2 376, more than twice the best carton. Packing together hides faces.

The factory makes 500 cartons a day. Switching from the long carton the old supplier used — which happened to be the 9 by 24 by 9 shape, 1 026 square centimetres — to the 18 by 12 by 9 carton saves 54 square centimetres each, which is 27 000 square centimetres, or 2,7 square metres, of cardboard every day. Over a working year of about 250 days, that is 675 square metres.

The learner's report ends with the rule she used: for a fixed volume, the most compact box uses the least material. Questions on the soap carton follow.

## Subtopic: The Ice at the Braai and the Newborn's Blanket

At a family braai in Johannesburg on a hot December afternoon, two cooler boxes are packed with drinks. One gets a single block of ice, 10 by 10 by 20 centimetres, frozen in an old ice-cream tub. The other gets a bag of small ice cubes, each 2 centimetres on a side, with the same total volume. By evening, the cubes have melted into water while the block is still half there. A Grade 8 learner at the braai works out why.

The block's volume is 10 times 10 times 20, which is 2 000 cubic centimetres. Its surface area is 2 times the bracket 100 plus 200 plus 200, which is 1 000 square centimetres.

Each small cube has volume 8 cubic centimetres, so the bag holds 2 000 divided by 8, which is 250 cubes. Each cube has surface area 6 times 4, which is 24 square centimetres, so together they have 250 times 24, which is 6 000 square centimetres — six times the surface of the block. The cubes do not all touch the warm air and drinks equally, since they pack against each other, but far more ice is exposed. Ice melts at its surface, so the cubes melted much faster. They also cooled the drinks faster at first, which is why people like ice cubes in a glass.

The surface-area-to-volume ratios tell the same story in one number each: the block has 1 000 divided by 2 000, which is 0,5 square centimetres of surface per cubic centimetre; each small cube has 24 divided by 8, which is 3. Six times as much.

The same evening, the learner's aunt arrives with her newborn baby, tightly wrapped in a blanket despite the warm weather. The learner asks why. Her aunt, a nurse, explains that newborns lose heat quickly, and the learner connects it to the ice. A baby is much smaller than an adult, and a smaller body has more skin for each kilogram of body. If a baby were roughly an adult scaled down by a factor of about one third in every length, its skin area would be about one ninth of the adult's but its volume only about one twenty-seventh: three times as much skin for each unit of body. Heat escapes through skin, so the baby cools about three times as fast, and the blanket slows it down.

Real babies are not exact scale models — their heads are larger in proportion, for example — but the reasoning is why hospitals put hats on newborns and keep them close to their mothers. The learner writes it in her notebook beside the ice: small things have more outside for their inside. Questions on the ice and the blanket follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The relationship in three statements. One: rectangular prisms with the same volume can have different surface areas, and the most compact, cube-like shape has the least. Two: prisms with the same surface area can have different volumes, and the most compact shape encloses the most. Three: when every length is multiplied by k, surface area is multiplied by k squared and volume by k cubed, so larger objects have a smaller surface-area-to-volume ratio and cutting an object into pieces increases its surface without changing its volume.

The method for comparing arrangements. One: list the possible dimensions — for unit cubes, the sets of three whole numbers whose product is the volume. Two: calculate each surface area with 2 times the bracket l b plus l h plus b h. Three: compare, and identify the most compact as the smallest.

The method for scaling questions. Find k from matching lengths. Multiply surface area by k squared and volume by k cubed. Do not recalculate from scratch unless asked to check.

The method for ratio questions. Divide surface area by volume for each object. A larger ratio means more surface per unit of volume: faster heating, cooling, melting, drying or dissolving.

A typical examination item gives a number of cubes and asks for the arrangement with the least surface area, or asks learners to complete a table of dimensions and surface areas. Show each calculation.

Another asks what happens to the surface area and volume of a cube when its edge is doubled or tripled. State the multipliers 4 and 8, or 9 and 27, and give a reason.

Another asks for an explanation of an everyday observation — why crushed ice melts faster, why bulk packs use less packaging — using surface area and volume. Name the relationship and support it with numbers if possible.

Errors to avoid. Assuming that the same volume means the same surface area. Multiplying volume by k squared instead of k cubed. Confusing the ratio's direction: surface area divided by volume, not volume divided by surface area. Forgetting that cutting changes surface but not volume.

Looking ahead: the next lessons solve multi-step problems with surface area, volume and capacity, and convert between cubic units and capacity units, where the factors are the length factors cubed. Final questions follow.
