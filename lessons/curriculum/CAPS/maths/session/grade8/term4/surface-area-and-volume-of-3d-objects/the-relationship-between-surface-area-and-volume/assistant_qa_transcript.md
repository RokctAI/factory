### subtopic_1 — Same Volume, Different Surface Area
**Assistant:** How can I find all the boxes that can be made from a given number of cubes?

**Tutor:** List the ways of writing the number as a product of three whole numbers, keeping them in order from smallest to largest so that you do not count the same box twice. For 24, start with 1 as the smallest: 1 times 1 times 24, 1 times 2 times 12, 1 times 3 times 8, 1 times 4 times 6. Then 2 as the smallest: 2 times 2 times 6, 2 times 3 times 4. Then 3 as the smallest would need the other two to multiply to 8 with neither less than 3, which is impossible, so you stop. That gives six boxes. For each, calculate 2 times the bracket l b plus l h plus b h. A quick way to spot the best before calculating is to look for the three numbers closest to each other: for 24, that is 2, 3 and 4. If the number is a perfect cube, such as 27 or 64, the cube itself is the best. Turning a box on its side does not create a new box, because its dimensions and surface area stay the same, which is why keeping the numbers in order matters. For larger numbers the list gets longer, but the closest-together rule still points straight to the answer.

### subtopic_3 — Scaling Up: Squares and Cubes
**Assistant:** Why does volume get multiplied by k cubed but surface area only by k squared?

**Tutor:** Because volume involves three lengths and surface area only two. A box's volume is length times breadth times height; enlarging by k multiplies each of the three, so the product is multiplied by k times k times k. Each face's area is one length times another, so it is multiplied by k times k, and the total surface, a sum of such areas, is multiplied by k squared too. You can see it with unit cubes: enlarge a single unit cube by 2 and it becomes a 2 by 2 by 2 block containing 8 unit cubes, while each of its faces becomes a 2 by 2 square of 4 unit squares. The units confirm it as well: cubic centimetres for volume, square centimetres for area. The difference between k squared and k cubed is what drives everything in this lesson. Since k cubed grows faster than k squared for any k greater than 1, enlarging an object always gives it more volume relative to its surface, so its surface-area-to-volume ratio falls by a factor of k. For a cube of edge s the ratio is exactly 6 divided by s: 6 for s equal to 1, 3 for s equal to 2, 1 for s equal to 6.

### subtopic_4 — Why the Ratio Matters
**Assistant:** If a big object has a smaller ratio, does it have less surface than a small object?

**Tutor:** No, it has more surface in total, but less surface for each unit of volume. A cube of edge 6 has 216 square centimetres of surface, far more than the 6 of a cube of edge 1. But it also has 216 cubic centimetres of volume, compared with 1. So each cubic centimetre of the big cube has, on average, 1 square centimetre of surface to exchange heat through, while the small cube's single cubic centimetre has 6. What matters for cooling, melting or drying is how fast the whole object changes, and that depends on how much surface there is compared with how much material must be heated, cooled or melted. The big cube has 216 times as much ice to melt but only 36 times as much surface to melt it through, so it takes longer. This is why the ratio is the right tool: it compares the two quantities rather than looking at either alone. When answering questions, be careful to say a smaller ratio, or less surface per unit of volume, rather than less surface, because the total surface of a larger object is always greater.

### subtopic_6 — The Ice at the Braai and the Newborn's Blanket
**Assistant:** Is a baby really like a scaled-down adult?

**Tutor:** Not exactly, and the reasoning in the story was deliberately a rough model. A baby's proportions differ from an adult's: the head is much larger relative to the body, the legs are shorter, and the body is rounder. So multiplying an adult's measurements by one third does not produce a baby. But the main conclusion does not depend on exact proportions. Any object that is much smaller than another, while being roughly similar in overall shape, will have a larger surface-area-to-volume ratio. A newborn of about 3,5 kilograms has, according to medical sources, roughly three times as much skin area per kilogram as an adult, which is the figure the scale model predicted. That is why newborns lose heat so quickly, and why the large head, which has a lot of surface, is covered with a hat. Mathematical models in science are often like this: simplified on purpose so that the main effect can be calculated, then checked against real measurements. The scale model explains why the effect exists and roughly how big it is, and the real measurements confirm it.
