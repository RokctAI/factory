# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the surface area, volume and capacity of cubes and rectangular prisms, and extends them to triangular prisms. The surface area of a prism is the sum of the areas of its faces, found most reliably from its net. The volume of any prism is the area of its cross-section times its length: for a rectangular prism, length times breadth times height; for a cube of edge s, s cubed; for a triangular prism, half the base times the height of the triangle, times the length of the prism. Volume is measured in cubic units, and capacity in millilitres and litres, with 1 cubic centimetre holding 1 millilitre and 1 000 cubic centimetres holding 1 litre. The anchor solids are a cube of edge 4, a box 5 by 3 by 2, and a triangular prism with a right-angled cross-section of sides 3, 4 and 5 and length 10.

## Subtopic: Faces, Nets and the Surface Area of Rectangular Prisms

A prism is a solid with two identical, parallel ends joined by flat faces. Cut it anywhere parallel to the ends and the cross-section is the same shape. A rectangular prism — a box — has rectangles at its ends and four rectangles around its sides: six faces in all. A cube is a rectangular prism whose six faces are identical squares.

The surface area is the total area of all the faces. It is what you would need to wrap the solid completely, with no overlaps: the cardboard of a box, the paint on a cupboard, the glass of a tank.

The net makes it visible. Unfold a box along its edges and lay it flat: the net is six rectangles joined edge to edge. The surface area of the box is the area of the net.

A box 5 centimetres long, 3 centimetres wide and 2 centimetres high. Its faces come in three pairs. Top and bottom: each 5 by 3, area 15, pair 30. Front and back: each 5 by 2, area 10, pair 20. The two ends: each 3 by 2, area 6, pair 12. Total: 30 plus 20 plus 12, which is 62 square centimetres. As a formula: twice the sum of length times breadth, length times height and breadth times height — 2 times the bracket 15 plus 10 plus 6, which is 2 times 31, which is 62.

A cube of edge 4 centimetres has six faces, each 4 by 4, area 16. Surface area: 6 times 16, which is 96 square centimetres. As a formula: 6 s squared.

A larger box, 25 by 12 by 8 centimetres. Pairs: 25 times 12 is 300, 25 times 8 is 200, 12 times 8 is 96. Surface area: 2 times 596, which is 1 192 square centimetres.

Open boxes need care. A box without a lid has only five faces. A fish tank 60 by 30 by 40 centimetres, open at the top: the base, 60 times 30, which is 1 800; front and back, 60 times 40, two of them, 4 800; the ends, 30 times 40, two of them, 2 400. Total: 9 000 square centimetres of glass. With a lid it would be 10 800.

Surface area is measured in square units, because it is an area, even though the object is three-dimensional. A good check is to picture the net and count six faces, or five if one is missing, and confirm that the faces come in matching pairs. Questions on surface area follow now.

## Subtopic: Volume: Cross-Section Times Length

Volume is the amount of space inside a solid, measured in cubic units: cubic centimetres, cubic metres. A cubic centimetre is a cube 1 centimetre on each edge, about the size of a sugar cube.

For a rectangular prism, count cubes. A box 5 by 3 by 2 centimetres has a bottom layer of 5 rows of 3, which is 15 cubes. There are 2 layers, so 30 cubes: the volume is 30 cubic centimetres. Length times breadth times height.

A cube of edge 4: 4 times 4 times 4, which is 64 cubic centimetres — 4 cubed, which is why the third power is called the cube.

The general principle for every prism: volume is the area of the cross-section times the length. The cross-section is the shape of the end. For the box standing on its 5 by 3 base, the cross-section area is 15 and the height is 2, so the volume is 30. Stand it on another face and the cross-section and length change, but the product does not: 5 by 2 is 10, times 3 is 30.

This principle is what lets us extend to other prisms, because it does not depend on the cross-section being a rectangle. Think of the prism as a stack of thin identical slices, each the shape of the cross-section; the volume is the area of one slice times the number of slices, which is the length.

The box 25 by 12 by 8 centimetres: 25 times 12 is 300, times 8 is 2 400 cubic centimetres. The fish tank 60 by 30 by 40: 1 800 times 40, which is 72 000 cubic centimetres.

Working backwards. A box has volume 360 cubic centimetres, length 12 and breadth 6. Then the base area is 72, and the height is 360 divided by 72, which is 5 centimetres. A cube has volume 125 cubic centimetres; its edge is the cube root of 125, which is 5 centimetres.

Volume and surface area are different measurements of the same object and must not be confused. The box 5 by 3 by 2 has volume 30 cubic centimetres and surface area 62 square centimetres. Different numbers, different units, different questions: how much fits inside, against how much material wraps the outside.

Units must match before multiplying. A box 1,2 metres by 50 centimetres by 40 centimetres: convert to 1,2 by 0,5 by 0,4 metres, volume 0,24 cubic metres, or to 120 by 50 by 40 centimetres, volume 240 000 cubic centimetres. Both are correct. Questions on volume follow now.

## Subtopic: Capacity: Millilitres and Litres

Capacity is the volume a container can hold, usually of a liquid, and is measured in millilitres, litres and kilolitres. Capacity and volume are linked by one exact fact: 1 cubic centimetre holds 1 millilitre.

So 1 000 cubic centimetres holds 1 000 millilitres, which is 1 litre. A litre is the capacity of a cube 10 centimetres on each edge, because 10 times 10 times 10 is 1 000. A 1-litre carton of milk is close to that shape.

And 1 cubic metre holds 1 000 litres, which is 1 kilolitre. A cubic metre is a cube 100 centimetres on each edge: 100 times 100 times 100 is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres, which is 1 000 000 millilitres, which is 1 000 litres. Municipal water is billed in kilolitres, so one kilolitre on the bill is one cubic metre of water.

The fish tank 60 by 30 by 40 centimetres has volume 72 000 cubic centimetres, so its capacity is 72 000 millilitres, which is 72 litres. Filled to 5 centimetres below the rim, the water is 60 by 30 by 35, which is 63 000 cubic centimetres, or 63 litres.

The box 25 by 12 by 8 centimetres: 2 400 cubic centimetres, so 2,4 litres.

A water tank 2 metres by 1,5 metres by 1 metre: 3 cubic metres, so 3 000 litres, or 3 kilolitres.

Working backwards from a capacity. A rectangular container must hold 5 litres and has a base 25 by 20 centimetres. 5 litres is 5 000 cubic centimetres, and the base area is 500 square centimetres, so the depth of 5 litres is 5 000 divided by 500, which is 10 centimetres.

Capacity uses the inside measurements. A tank whose walls are 1 centimetre thick, with outside measurements 62 by 32 by 41 centimetres, has inside measurements 60 by 30 by 40 if it is open at the top — 2 centimetres less in length and breadth, for two walls, but only 1 centimetre less in height, for the floor. Questions usually give inside measurements directly, but read carefully.

Remember the chain: cubic centimetres equal millilitres; divide by 1 000 for litres; cubic metres equal kilolitres; multiply by 1 000 for litres. Questions on capacity follow now.

## Subtopic: Triangular Prisms

A triangular prism has two identical triangles as its ends and three rectangles joining them. A tent, a roof space and a chocolate bar in a triangular box are triangular prisms.

Volume: the same principle — cross-section area times length. Take a prism whose ends are right-angled triangles with legs 3 and 4 centimetres and hypotenuse 5, and whose length is 10 centimetres. The cross-section area is half of 3 times 4, which is 6 square centimetres. The volume is 6 times 10, which is 60 cubic centimetres. It is exactly half of the 3 by 4 by 10 box, 120, which makes sense: slice that box along a diagonal of its end and you get two of these prisms.

The length of a triangular prism is the distance between the two triangles, not a side of the triangle. Identify the triangles first, find their area, then multiply by the distance between them. A common error is to multiply all three numbers on the diagram together and forget the half.

Surface area: five faces, from the net — two triangles and three rectangles. The two triangles: 6 each, 12 together. The three rectangles each have the prism's length, 10, and one side of the triangle as their width: 3 by 10, 4 by 10 and 5 by 10, areas 30, 40 and 50, which add to 120. Total surface area: 12 plus 120, which is 132 square centimetres.

There is a shortcut for the rectangles: together they form one long strip whose width is the perimeter of the triangle, 3 plus 4 plus 5, which is 12, and whose length is 10, so 120. Surface area of any prism: two ends plus perimeter of the end times length.

For a triangle that is not right-angled, the slanting sides may have to be found with the theorem of Pythagoras. A tent has an isosceles triangular end with base 2 metres and height 1,5 metres, and is 3 metres long. Volume: half of 2 times 1,5, which is 1,5 square metres, times 3, which is 4,5 cubic metres. For the surface, each slanting side of the triangle runs 1 metre across and 1,5 metres up, so it is the square root of 1 plus 2,25, the square root of 3,25, about 1,80 metres. The perimeter of the end is about 2 plus 1,80 plus 1,80, which is 5,61 metres. Surface area: two ends, 3 square metres, plus 5,61 times 3, about 16,82, total about 19,82 square metres including the floor.

Use the perpendicular height of the triangle for its area and the slanting sides for the rectangles. Mixing them up is the commonest error with triangular prisms. Questions on triangular prisms follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same measurements as stories about a learner setting up a fish tank and working out how many litres of water it holds and how much glass it is made of, a camping club sewing a tent and filling it with sleepers, and a juice carton that holds a little more than its label says — stories about the outside and the inside of a box.

## Subtopic: Setting Up the Fish Tank

A learner in Gqeberha is given a second-hand fish tank for her birthday. It is a glass rectangular prism with no lid, and its inside measurements are 60 centimetres long, 30 centimetres wide and 40 centimetres high. Before buying fish, she must work out how much water it holds, how much the full tank weighs, and how big a lid to have cut.

Capacity. The volume is 60 times 30 times 40, which is 72 000 cubic centimetres, so the tank holds 72 litres when full to the brim. The pet shop advises leaving 5 centimetres of air at the top and putting 5 centimetres of gravel on the bottom. That leaves water 30 centimetres deep, with volume 60 times 30 times 30, which is 54 000 cubic centimetres, or 54 litres. The fish shop's guideline is about one small fish per 4 litres, so roughly 13 small fish.

Weight. A litre of water has a mass of about 1 kilogram, so 54 litres of water weighs about 54 kilograms. The gravel layer is 60 by 30 by 5, which is 9 000 cubic centimetres; gravel weighs about 1,6 kilograms per litre, so about 14,4 kilograms. With the glass, the full tank weighs well over 70 kilograms. She decides the old bookshelf will not do and asks her father for a proper stand.

Surface area. The glass has five panels: the base, 60 by 30, which is 1 800 square centimetres; front and back, 60 by 40, each 2 400; two ends, 30 by 40, each 1 200. Total: 1 800 plus 4 800 plus 2 400, which is 9 000 square centimetres. She needs this to buy the right amount of background film for the back panel only: 2 400 square centimetres, which is 0,24 square metres.

The lid. A lid covers the open top, 60 by 30 centimetres, area 1 800 square centimetres, or 0,18 square metres. The glass shop charges R450 per square metre for cut glass, so the lid costs 0,18 times R450, which is R81.

Water changes. The shop advises changing a quarter of the water each week. A quarter of 54 litres is 13,5 litres. Her bucket holds 9 litres, so she needs one full bucket and half of a second.

She writes all the figures on a card taped to the side of the stand: 72 litres to the brim, 54 litres of water, 9 000 square centimetres of glass, 13,5 litres a week. Her father says it is the best-planned fish tank in the street. Questions on the fish tank follow.

## Subtopic: The Camping Club's Tent and the Juice Carton

A school camping club in the Drakensberg foothills decides to sew its own tents. Each tent is a triangular prism: the ends are isosceles triangles 2 metres across the base and 1,5 metres high, and the tent is 3 metres long. The canvas covers the two ends and the two sloping sides; a separate groundsheet covers the floor.

The club treasurer works out the canvas. Each sloping side of the triangle runs from a corner of the base up to the peak: 1 metre across, half the base, and 1,5 metres up. By the theorem of Pythagoras, it is the square root of 1 plus 2,25, the square root of 3,25, about 1,80 metres. Each sloping roof panel is therefore 1,80 by 3 metres, about 5,41 square metres; two of them, about 10,82. Each end is a triangle of area half of 2 times 1,5, which is 1,5 square metres; two ends, 3. Canvas: about 13,82 square metres per tent. The groundsheet is 2 by 3, which is 6 square metres.

A member asks how many people can sleep in it. The volume is half of 2 times 1,5, times 3, which is 4,5 cubic metres. But volume is not the right measure for sleeping; floor area is. Each sleeping bag needs about 0,8 metres by 2 metres. The bags lie along the 3-metre length, each 0,8 metres wide, and the floor is 2 metres wide: 2 divided by 0,8 is 2,5, so 2 sleepers fit side by side with room for kit. The volume matters for warmth instead: a smaller air space warms up faster.

The club orders canvas for 4 tents: 4 times 13,82, which is about 55,3 square metres, plus 10 percent for seams, about 60,8, so they buy 61 square metres.

At the camp, a learner looks at a juice carton labelled 250 millilitres. It measures 4 centimetres by 6,3 centimetres by 10 centimetres. Its volume is 4 times 6,3 times 10, which is 252 cubic centimetres: 252 millilitres. The carton holds a little more than the label, leaving a small air gap so it can be opened without spilling. Its surface area is 2 times the bracket 25,2 plus 40 plus 63, which is 2 times 128,2, which is 256,4 square centimetres of card.

A 1-litre carton, the learner calculates, would need four times the volume. If its shape were an enlargement of the small carton, the scale factor would be the cube root of 4, about 1,59, and the card would be 1,59 squared, about 2,52 times as much — not 4 times. A bigger box uses less card per millilitre. That idea is the subject of the next lesson. Questions on the tent and the carton follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

Surface area. Sum of the areas of all the faces, found from the net. Rectangular prism: 2 times the bracket l b plus l h plus b h. Cube: 6 s squared. Triangular prism: two triangles plus three rectangles, or two ends plus the perimeter of the end times the length. Leave out any open face. Units: square units.

Volume. Area of the cross-section times the length of the prism. Rectangular prism: l times b times h. Cube: s cubed. Triangular prism: half base times height of the triangle, times the length. Units: cubic units.

Capacity. 1 cubic centimetre is 1 millilitre; 1 000 cubic centimetres is 1 litre; 1 cubic metre is 1 000 litres, which is 1 kilolitre. Use inside measurements.

The method. One: identify the solid and its cross-section. Two: sketch the net if surface area is wanted, and label each face's dimensions. Three: put all lengths in the same unit. Four: calculate, showing each face or the cross-section and length. Five: convert to capacity if asked. Six: give the unit — square for surface area, cubic or litres for volume and capacity.

A typical examination item gives a box and asks for its surface area and volume. Two calculations, two different units.

Another gives a triangular prism with the triangle's sides and height and the prism's length. Use the height for the triangle's area, the sides for the rectangles.

Another gives a tank and asks how many litres it holds, or how deep a given amount of water would be. Volume to capacity, or capacity divided by base area.

Another gives the volume and two dimensions and asks for the third. Divide the volume by the product of the two.

Another asks for the cost of painting or covering: surface area times the rate.

Errors to avoid. Confusing surface area with volume. Forgetting the half in a triangular prism. Using the slanting side as the triangle's height. Counting the open top of a tank. Mixing units. Converting cubic centimetres to litres by dividing by 100 instead of 1 000.
