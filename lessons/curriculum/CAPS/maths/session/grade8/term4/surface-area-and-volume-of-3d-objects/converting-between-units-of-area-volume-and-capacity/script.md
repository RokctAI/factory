# Part 1 — Expert

This session converts between the SI units of area, volume and capacity. Area factors are the squares of length factors: 1 square centimetre is 100 square millimetres, 1 square metre is 10 000 square centimetres, 1 square kilometre is 1 000 000 square metres. Volume factors are the cubes of length factors: 1 cubic centimetre is 10 times 10 times 10, which is 1 000 cubic millimetres, and 1 cubic metre is 100 times 100 times 100, which is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres. Capacity is linked to volume exactly: 1 millilitre is 1 cubic centimetre, 1 litre is 1 000 millilitres or 1 000 cubic centimetres, and 1 kilolitre is 1 000 litres or 1 cubic metre. Converting to a smaller unit multiplies; converting to a larger unit divides. The anchor examples are a sugar cube of 1 cubic centimetre, a 1-litre cube 10 centimetres on each edge, and a 1-kilolitre cube 1 metre on each edge.

## Subtopic: Why Volume Factors Are Cubed

In the area lesson, a square 1 centimetre on each side turned out to hold 100 square millimetres, because it is 10 millimetres in two directions. A cube adds a third direction.

Take a cube 1 centimetre on each edge — about the size of a sugar cube. Its volume is 1 cubic centimetre. Measure it in millimetres: each edge is 10 millimetres. Fill it with tiny cubes 1 millimetre on each edge. The bottom layer has 10 rows of 10, which is 100 tiny cubes. There are 10 layers. So the cube holds 10 times 10 times 10, which is 1 000 cubic millimetres. 1 cubic centimetre equals 1 000 cubic millimetres.

A cube 1 metre on each edge is 100 centimetres on each edge, so it holds 100 times 100 times 100, which is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres. 1 cubic metre equals one million cubic centimetres. In cubic millimetres, it is 1 000 times 1 000 times 1 000, which is 1 000 000 000 — one thousand million.

The pattern across the three kinds of measurement:
millimetres to centimetres: length factor 10, area factor 100, volume factor 1 000;
centimetres to metres: length factor 100, area factor 10 000, volume factor 1 000 000;
metres to kilometres: length factor 1 000, area factor 1 000 000.
Cubic kilometres are rarely used except for very large things such as the water in a big lake.

The rule: for areas, square the length factor; for volumes, cube it. It is the same rule as scaling a solid by k: surface area by k squared, volume by k cubed. Changing the unit is like enlarging the measuring grid.

The commonest error is to use the length or area factor for a volume. Saying 1 cubic metre is 100 cubic centimetres, or 10 000, gives answers that are ten thousand or a hundred times too small. Picture a cubic metre: a box you could sit inside with your knees up. A cubic centimetre is a sugar cube. A hundred sugar cubes would barely cover a saucer; a million is right.

Moving the decimal comma: volume factors of 1 000 and 1 000 000 move it 3 and 6 places — three times the 1 and 2 places of the matching length conversions. Questions on why volume factors are cubed follow now.

## Subtopic: Converting Cubic Units Down and Up

The direction rule is the same as for lengths and areas. To a smaller unit, the number grows: multiply. To a larger unit, the number shrinks: divide.

Cubic centimetres to cubic millimetres: multiply by 1 000. 2,5 cubic centimetres is 2 500 cubic millimetres. 0,04 cubic centimetres is 40 cubic millimetres.

Cubic millimetres to cubic centimetres: divide by 1 000. 4 500 cubic millimetres is 4,5 cubic centimetres. 750 cubic millimetres is 0,75 cubic centimetres.

Cubic metres to cubic centimetres: multiply by 1 000 000. 0,75 cubic metres is 750 000 cubic centimetres. 1,44 cubic metres is 1 440 000 cubic centimetres.

Cubic centimetres to cubic metres: divide by 1 000 000. 72 000 cubic centimetres is 0,072 cubic metres. 2 500 000 cubic centimetres is 2,5 cubic metres.

Moving the comma: 3 places for 1 000, 6 places for 1 000 000. To multiply 0,75 by 1 000 000, move the comma 6 places right, filling with zeros: 750 000. To divide 72 000 by 1 000 000, move it 6 places left: 0,072.

Chaining through more than one unit multiplies the factors. Cubic metres to cubic millimetres: times 1 000 000, then times 1 000, a total of times 1 000 000 000. A brick of 0,001 cubic metres is 1 000 cubic centimetres, which is 1 000 000 cubic millimetres.

The route through dimensions works for volumes too, and avoids large factors. A box 20 centimetres by 10 centimetres by 5 centimetres has volume 1 000 cubic centimetres. In cubic metres: either 1 000 divided by 1 000 000, which is 0,001, or convert the dimensions to 0,2 by 0,1 by 0,05 metres and multiply: 0,001 cubic metres. The same.

Comparing volumes requires a common unit. Which is larger: 0,003 cubic metres or 2 800 cubic centimetres? 0,003 cubic metres is 3 000 cubic centimetres, so the first. Order 1,2 cubic centimetres, 1 500 cubic millimetres and 0,0000011 cubic metres: in cubic millimetres they are 1 200, 1 500 and 1 100. Smallest first: 0,0000011 cubic metres, 1,2 cubic centimetres, 1 500 cubic millimetres.

After every conversion, check the direction: going to a smaller unit, the number must have grown. Questions on converting cubic units follow now.

## Subtopic: Capacity Units and the Bridge to Volume

Capacity units — millilitres, litres, kilolitres — measure how much a container holds, usually of a liquid. They are related to each other by factors of 1 000: 1 litre is 1 000 millilitres, and 1 kilolitre is 1 000 litres. These are ordinary metric prefixes, milli for one thousandth and kilo for a thousand, so the factors are not squared or cubed.

The bridge to volume is exact. 1 millilitre is 1 cubic centimetre. Therefore 1 litre, which is 1 000 millilitres, is 1 000 cubic centimetres — a cube 10 centimetres on each edge. And 1 kilolitre, 1 000 litres, is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres, which is 1 cubic metre — a cube 1 metre on each edge.

Three pairs to remember: millilitre and cubic centimetre; litre and 1 000 cubic centimetres, or one cubic decimetre if you meet that unit; kilolitre and cubic metre.

Millilitres to litres: divide by 1 000. 350 millilitres is 0,35 litres. 3 200 millilitres is 3,2 litres.

Litres to millilitres: multiply by 1 000. 2,5 litres is 2 500 millilitres. 0,25 litres is 250 millilitres.

Litres to kilolitres: divide by 1 000. 2 400 litres is 2,4 kilolitres. Kilolitres to litres: multiply by 1 000. 18 kilolitres is 18 000 litres.

Across the bridge. A container of 3 200 cubic centimetres holds 3 200 millilitres, which is 3,2 litres. A tank of 2,4 cubic metres holds 2,4 kilolitres, which is 2 400 litres. A medicine bottle labelled 100 millilitres holds 100 cubic centimetres.

The same quantity can be written many ways: 1,44 cubic metres, 1,44 kilolitres, 1 440 litres, 1 440 000 millilitres, 1 440 000 cubic centimetres. Choose the one that suits the context: a water tank in litres or kilolitres, a cool drink in millilitres, a swimming pool in cubic metres or kilolitres.

A common error is to convert cubic metres to litres by multiplying by 1 000 000, confusing the cubic-centimetre factor with the litre factor. Remember: cubic metre to litre is times 1 000, because a cubic metre is a kilolitre. Another is to treat millilitres and cubic millimetres as the same: they are not — a millilitre is a cubic centimetre, which is 1 000 cubic millimetres. A drop of water is about 50 cubic millimetres, or 0,05 millilitres. Questions on capacity units follow now.

## Subtopic: Mixed Conversions and Choosing the Unit

Problems often mix length, area, volume and capacity in one calculation. The safe approach is to settle on one unit system at the start.

A fish tank is 60 centimetres by 30 centimetres by 40 centimetres. Work in centimetres: volume 72 000 cubic centimetres, which is 72 000 millilitres, which is 72 litres. Or work in metres: 0,6 by 0,3 by 0,4 is 0,072 cubic metres, which is 0,072 kilolitres, which is 72 litres. Both routes arrive at 72 litres; the centimetre route uses the millilitre bridge, the metre route the kilolitre bridge.

A tank 1,5 metres by 1,2 metres by 0,8 metres: 1,44 cubic metres, which is 1 440 litres.

A carton of juice 4 centimetres by 6,3 centimetres by 10 centimetres: 252 cubic centimetres, which is 252 millilitres, or 0,252 litres.

How many 250-millilitre glasses can be filled from a 2-litre bottle? Convert to the same unit: 2 litres is 2 000 millilitres, and 2 000 divided by 250 is 8 glasses.

How many 5-millilitre medicine doses are in a 0,1-litre bottle? 0,1 litres is 100 millilitres, so 100 divided by 5 is 20 doses.

A pool holds 60 cubic metres. How long to fill at 40 litres per minute? 60 cubic metres is 60 000 litres. 60 000 divided by 40 is 1 500 minutes, which is 25 hours.

Choosing units for an answer: use the one that gives a manageable number for the context. Drinks and medicine in millilitres. Buckets, tanks and fuel in litres. Municipal water and swimming pools in kilolitres or cubic metres. Concrete, soil and sand in cubic metres. Very small solid volumes, such as a drop or a grain, in cubic millimetres.

A complete table to keep. Length: 10 millimetres in a centimetre, 100 centimetres in a metre, 1 000 metres in a kilometre. Area: 100 square millimetres in a square centimetre, 10 000 square centimetres in a square metre, 1 000 000 square metres in a square kilometre. Volume: 1 000 cubic millimetres in a cubic centimetre, 1 000 000 cubic centimetres in a cubic metre. Capacity: 1 000 millilitres in a litre, 1 000 litres in a kilolitre. Bridges: 1 cubic centimetre is 1 millilitre; 1 cubic metre is 1 kilolitre.

Every factor in the table follows from the length factors and the two bridges. If you remember those and the rule — square for area, cube for volume — you can rebuild the rest. Questions on mixed conversions follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same conversions as stories about a household reading its municipal water bill in kilolitres, a nurse measuring a child's medicine in millilitres from a syringe marked in cubic centimetres, and a farmer working out how many litres her dam holds — stories about the same water measured in very different units.

## Subtopic: Reading the Water Bill

A family in Mangaung receives its monthly municipal water bill, and the Grade 8 learner in the house is asked to explain it. The bill shows a meter reading at the start of the month and at the end, in kilolitres: 1 284 at the start and 1 302 at the end. Consumption: 1 302 minus 1 284, which is 18 kilolitres.

The learner converts. 18 kilolitres is 18 000 litres. Over a 30-day month that is 18 000 divided by 30, which is 600 litres a day. The family has five members, so about 120 litres per person per day.

To make it real, she thinks in buckets. A 25-litre bucket: 600 divided by 25 is 24 buckets a day. In cubic metres, 18 kilolitres is 18 cubic metres — a cube about 2,6 metres on each edge, since 2,6 cubed is about 17,6. The month's water would fill a small room.

The bill explains that the first 6 kilolitres per household each month are free under the government's free basic water policy, and the rest is charged by the kilolitre on a rising scale. So 12 of the 18 kilolitres are charged.

She looks for the biggest uses. A bath of about 150 litres is 0,15 kilolitres. A 5-minute shower at 10 litres per minute is 50 litres, a third of a bath. If the five family members switch from a daily bath to a daily 5-minute shower, they save 100 litres each per day, 500 litres a day, or 15 000 litres in a 30-day month — 15 kilolitres, most of the bill's consumption. In practice not everyone bathes daily, so the real saving is smaller, but the direction is clear.

A dripping tap is another. One drip per second, each drop about 0,05 millilitres, is 0,05 times 86 400 seconds a day, which is 4 320 millilitres, about 4,3 litres a day — about 130 litres a month. Small, but fixing it costs only a washer.

The toilet cistern holds 9 litres per flush. Twenty flushes a day across the family is 180 litres, which is 0,18 kilolitres, nearly a third of daily use. A brick-sized plastic bottle of water in the cistern, about 1 litre, saves 1 litre per flush, 20 litres a day, 600 litres a month.

The learner writes her summary on the fridge in three units: 18 kilolitres a month, 600 litres a day, 24 buckets a day. Her father says the buckets make it real. Questions on the water bill follow.

## Subtopic: The Syringe and the Farm Dam

At a clinic in Upington, a nurse prepares a child's antibiotic. The prescription says 7,5 millilitres twice a day for 7 days, and the oral syringe she gives the parent is marked in cubic centimetres. A learner accompanying her younger brother asks whether the markings are wrong.

They are not. One millilitre is exactly one cubic centimetre, so 7,5 millilitres is 7,5 cubic centimetres, and the parent fills the syringe to the 7,5 mark. Medical equipment often uses cubic centimetres, sometimes written cc, interchangeably with millilitres.

The bottle holds 100 millilitres. The course needs 7,5 times 2 times 7, which is 105 millilitres. The learner points out the problem: one bottle is 5 millilitres short. The nurse checks and agrees, and adds a second bottle. Without the conversion, nobody would have noticed until the last day.

The learner also works out how much the child swallows in cubic millimetres, out of curiosity: 7,5 cubic centimetres is 7 500 cubic millimetres per dose. The number is large but the dose is small, because a cubic millimetre is tiny.

The same weekend, the learner visits an aunt who farms near the Orange River. The farm has an earth dam for irrigation, roughly a rectangular prism 40 metres long, 25 metres wide and, on average, 3 metres deep. The aunt asks how much water it holds when full.

Volume: 40 times 25 times 3, which is 3 000 cubic metres. That is 3 000 kilolitres, which is 3 000 000 litres. In megalitres, a unit farmers and water authorities use, where a megalitre is a million litres, it is 3 megalitres.

The aunt irrigates an orchard with drip lines that deliver 12 000 litres a day in summer. 3 000 000 divided by 12 000 is 250 days, if no water evaporated or seeped away and no rain or river water refilled it. In reality evaporation in the Northern Cape summer is high: the surface of the dam is 40 times 25, which is 1 000 square metres, and if 8 millimetres evaporates on a hot day, that is 1 000 times 0,008, which is 8 cubic metres, or 8 000 litres a day — two thirds as much as the orchard uses. The learner suggests shade-cloth covers, which some farmers use, and the aunt says she has been meaning to price them.

Two very different scales — 7,5 millilitres and 3 000 000 litres — but the same chain of conversions links them: cubic centimetres to millilitres, millilitres to litres, litres to kilolitres, kilolitres to cubic metres. Questions on the syringe and the dam follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The facts. Length: 10, 100, 1 000 between millimetres, centimetres, metres and kilometres. Area: square the length factor — 100, 10 000, 1 000 000. Volume: cube the length factor — 1 000 between cubic millimetres and cubic centimetres, 1 000 000 between cubic centimetres and cubic metres. Capacity: 1 000 between millilitres and litres, and between litres and kilolitres. Bridges: 1 cubic centimetre is 1 millilitre; 1 000 cubic centimetres is 1 litre; 1 cubic metre is 1 kilolitre, which is 1 000 litres.

The method. One: identify the starting and target units and whether they measure length, area, volume or capacity. Two: choose the factor — length, squared or cubed — or the bridge. Three: decide the direction: to a smaller unit multiply, to a larger unit divide. Four: move the decimal comma the right number of places. Five: check that the number grew if the unit shrank. Six: when dimensions are known, converting them first and then calculating is often simplest.

A typical examination item gives a table to complete: 2,5 cubic centimetres in cubic millimetres; 0,75 cubic metres in litres; 3 200 millilitres in litres; and so on. Show the factor used.

Another gives a container's dimensions and asks for its capacity in litres. Calculate the volume, then cross the bridge: cubic centimetres to millilitres to litres, or cubic metres to kilolitres to litres.

Another asks how many smaller portions fit in a larger quantity: glasses from a bottle, doses from a bottle, buckets from a tank. Convert to one unit, then divide.

Another asks for an explanation of a factor: why 1 cubic metre is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres. Draw the cube, 100 centimetres on each edge, and multiply 100 by 100 by 100.

Errors to avoid. Using an area factor for a volume. Multiplying cubic metres by 1 000 000 to get litres. Treating a millilitre as a cubic millimetre. Dividing when you should multiply. Mixing units in a division.

Looking back over the term: Pythagoras gave lengths, transformations moved and scaled shapes, area and perimeter measured them, and surface area and volume extended the measuring into three dimensions. The thread through all of it is the scale-factor rule: lengths by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed. Final questions follow.
