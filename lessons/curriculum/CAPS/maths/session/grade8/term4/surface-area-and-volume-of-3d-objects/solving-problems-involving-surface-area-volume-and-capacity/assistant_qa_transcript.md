### subtopic_1 — Filling Tanks: Volume, Capacity and Rate
**Assistant:** Why do I convert to litres before dividing by the tap's rate?

**Tutor:** Because the rate is in litres per minute, and you can only divide a quantity by a rate when both use the same unit. The tank's volume of 3 cubic metres and a rate of 12 litres per minute are in different units. Dividing 3 by 12 would give 0,25, which looks like a quarter of a minute and is wildly wrong, because a cubic metre is a thousand litres. Converting first, 3 cubic metres becomes 3 000 litres, and 3 000 divided by 12 gives 250 minutes, which is correct. You could equally convert the rate to cubic metres per minute, 0,012, and divide 3 by 0,012 to get 250; the answer is the same as long as both are in matching units. Converting the volume to litres is usually easier because rates for taps, pumps and hoses are almost always given in litres. A quick sense check also helps: a garden tap fills a 10-litre bucket in under a minute, so a 3 000-litre tank should take hours, not seconds. If your answer is far from that kind of estimate, look first for a missing factor of 1 000.

### subtopic_2 — Surface Meets Volume: Rain on a Roof
**Assistant:** Why is the rain collected from a sloping roof based on the ground area and not the roof's actual surface?

**Tutor:** Because rain falls straight down, so the amount a roof catches depends on how much ground it shelters, not on how steep it is. Imagine a roof over a 12 by 8 metre classroom. If it were flat, it would catch the rain falling on 96 square metres. Tilt it into a steep pitch and its sloping surface becomes larger, but it still shelters exactly the same 96 square metres of ground, and the rain that would have landed on that ground is all that it can catch. The extra surface area of the slope only means the same rain is spread more thinly over it. So for rainwater calculations, measure the length and breadth of the area the roof covers, usually the building's outside dimensions plus any overhang. The sloping surface area does matter for other jobs: roofing sheets, paint, or the weight of tiles depend on the actual surface, which is larger than the plan area. This is a good example of reading a problem carefully to decide which area it needs, because the same roof has two different areas for two different purposes.

### subtopic_3 — Packing Boxes: Volume Is Not Enough
**Assistant:** How do I know which orientation of the boxes is best without trying all six?

**Tutor:** Look for the orientation in which each box dimension divides exactly, or nearly exactly, into a carton dimension. For boxes 20 by 15 by 8 and a carton 60 by 40 by 30, notice that 20 divides 60, 8 divides 40, and 15 divides 30. Matching them up gives 3 times 5 times 2, which is 30, the same as the volume limit, so it must be the best. When no orientation divides perfectly, the best one wastes the least space along each side, and a little trial is unavoidable; in an examination, two or three sensible orientations usually reveal it. A good order is to place the largest box dimension against the carton dimension it divides best, and then check the other two. The volume division always gives an upper limit, so once an orientation reaches that limit, you can stop. If the best orientation falls well short of the limit, mixing orientations within one carton can sometimes fit a few more, but that is beyond what Grade 8 questions expect. State the orientation you used and the count it gives, so the marker can follow.

### subtopic_6 — The Clinic Ramp and the Bedroom Walls
**Assistant:** Why was the ramp's sloping surface only slightly longer than its horizontal run?

**Tutor:** Because the slope is very gentle. The sloping surface is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose legs are the horizontal run of 6 metres and the rise of 0,5 metres. By the theorem of Pythagoras the hypotenuse is the square root of 36 plus 0,25, which is the square root of 36,25, about 6,02 metres. The rise is so small compared with the run that squaring it adds only 0,25 to the 36, and the square root barely changes. A steep ramp would be different: a rise of 3 metres over a run of 4 metres gives a sloping length of 5 metres, a quarter more than the run. So for gentle slopes such as wheelchair ramps, driveways and roads, the sloping length is almost the same as the horizontal distance, and a quick estimate can use the horizontal length; for steep slopes such as ladders or roof pitches, the theorem of Pythagoras is essential. In this problem, the small difference still matters for buying paint, but only by about 0,02 metres in length, or about 0,02 square metres in area for each metre of width, which is well within the margin of the paint tin.
