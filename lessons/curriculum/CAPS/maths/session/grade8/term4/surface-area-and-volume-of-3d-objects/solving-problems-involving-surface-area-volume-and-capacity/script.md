# Part 1 — Expert

This session solves problems, with and without a calculator, involving surface area, volume and capacity. Each problem is read for whether it concerns a surface, such as paint, wrapping or a roof, or a volume, such as water, concrete or packing space, or both. Volumes are converted to capacities using 1 cubic metre equals 1 000 litres and 1 000 cubic centimetres equals 1 litre. Rates such as litres per minute or square metres per litre turn the measurement into a time or a quantity to buy, and whole items are rounded up. When packing boxes into a carton, the number that fits depends on the orientation and must be checked dimension by dimension, not only by dividing volumes. The anchor problems are a tank 2 by 1,5 by 1 metres, rain of 25 millimetres on a 60 square metre roof, and a carton 60 by 40 by 30 centimetres.

## Subtopic: Filling Tanks: Volume, Capacity and Rate

A problem about filling or emptying a container has three parts: the volume, converted to a capacity, and a rate that turns the capacity into a time.

A rectangular water tank is 2 metres long, 1,5 metres wide and 1 metre deep. Its volume is 2 times 1,5 times 1, which is 3 cubic metres. Since 1 cubic metre holds 1 000 litres, the tank holds 3 000 litres.

A tap delivers 12 litres per minute. Time to fill the empty tank: 3 000 divided by 12, which is 250 minutes. Convert to hours: 250 divided by 60 is 4 remainder 10, so 4 hours and 10 minutes.

If the tank is already one third full, the remaining two thirds is 2 000 litres, which takes 2 000 divided by 12, about 166,7 minutes — about 2 hours and 47 minutes.

Working backwards: a pump empties the full tank in 2 hours. Its rate is 3 000 litres in 120 minutes, which is 25 litres per minute.

Depth problems. If 1 200 litres are in the tank, how deep is the water? 1 200 litres is 1,2 cubic metres, and the base is 2 times 1,5, which is 3 square metres. Depth: 1,2 divided by 3, which is 0,4 metres, or 40 centimetres. In general, depth is volume divided by base area — the volume formula run backwards.

Smaller containers use cubic centimetres and millilitres. A jug 10 by 10 by 25 centimetres holds 2 500 cubic centimetres, which is 2,5 litres. Filling the 3 000-litre tank with it would take 3 000 divided by 2,5, which is 1 200 jugfuls — a good reason to use a hosepipe.

Units must be consistent throughout. The tank in metres gives cubic metres; convert to litres before dividing by a rate in litres per minute. Mixing cubic metres with litres per minute would give an answer a thousand times too small.

Without a calculator, choose an order of operations that keeps the numbers friendly: 3 000 divided by 12 is 3 000 divided by 4, which is 750, then divided by 3, which is 250.

Every answer should be written in the units of the question and in a sentence: the tank holds 3 000 litres and takes 4 hours and 10 minutes to fill at 12 litres per minute. Questions on filling tanks follow now.

## Subtopic: Surface Meets Volume: Rain on a Roof

Some problems use a surface to find a volume. Rainwater harvesting is the clearest example: rain falls on a roof, and the volume collected is the area of the roof times the depth of rain.

Rainfall is measured as a depth: 25 millimetres of rain means that, if none ran away or soaked in, the ground would be covered by water 25 millimetres deep. On a flat surface, the volume of rain is the area times that depth, a prism of water.

A house has a roof that covers 60 square metres of ground. For a sloping roof, what counts is the area of ground it covers, its plan area, because rain falls vertically. In a storm of 25 millimetres, the rain landing on it is 60 square metres times 0,025 metres, which is 1,5 cubic metres, which is 1 500 litres. The depth had to be converted to metres before multiplying by square metres.

Gutters and pipes lose a little, so in practice perhaps 80 to 90 percent reaches the tank. At 85 percent, 1 500 times 0,85 is 1 275 litres.

How much rain fills a 2 000-litre tank from this roof, with no losses? The depth needed is 2 cubic metres divided by 60 square metres, about 0,0333 metres, which is about 33 millimetres of rain.

Over a month with 80 millimetres of rain, the roof collects 60 times 0,08, which is 4,8 cubic metres, or 4 800 litres. A 2 000-litre tank overflows; the household either uses the water as it arrives or buys a bigger tank.

The same surface-times-depth reasoning works for other layers: a concrete slab, a layer of sand in a sandpit, a coat of paint. A floor slab 5 by 3 metres and 10 centimetres thick: 15 square metres times 0,1 metres, which is 1,5 cubic metres of concrete. Sand 30 centimetres deep in a sandpit of area 4,52 square metres: about 1,36 cubic metres.

The general principle: a thin layer is a prism whose cross-section is the surface and whose length is the thickness. Area in square metres times thickness in metres gives cubic metres. Keep the units in step, and convert millimetres or centimetres of depth to metres before multiplying by an area in square metres. Questions on rain and layers follow now.

## Subtopic: Packing Boxes: Volume Is Not Enough

How many small boxes fit into a large carton? Dividing the volumes gives an upper limit, but boxes are rigid, so the true number depends on how they line up.

A carton is 60 by 40 by 30 centimetres, volume 72 000 cubic centimetres. Small boxes are 10 by 10 by 15 centimetres, volume 1 500. Dividing: 72 000 by 1 500 is 48. Check the fit: along 60, 60 divided by 10 is 6; along 40, 40 divided by 10 is 4; along 30, 30 divided by 15 is 2. 6 times 4 times 2 is 48. They fit exactly, with no gaps, because each dimension divides evenly.

Now boxes 20 by 15 by 8 centimetres, volume 2 400. Dividing volumes: 72 000 by 2 400 is 30. But do 30 fit? Try lining the 20 along the 60, the 15 along the 40 and the 8 along the 30. 60 divided by 20 is 3. 40 divided by 15 is 2,67, so only 2 fit, with a gap. 30 divided by 8 is 3,75, so 3 fit. 3 times 2 times 3 is 18 — far fewer than 30.

Turn the boxes. The 20 along the 60, the 8 along the 40, the 15 along the 30: 3, then 40 divided by 8 is 5, then 30 divided by 15 is 2. 3 times 5 times 2 is 30. A perfect fit.

Of the six possible orientations, the counts are 14, 18, 20, 24, 28 and 30. The best is 30, matching the volume limit only because, in that orientation, every dimension divides exactly. The worst wastes more than half the carton.

The method. Take each dimension of the small box in turn against each dimension of the carton, divide, and keep only the whole number — round down, because a part box does not fit. Multiply the three whole numbers. Try the orientations, at least the sensible ones, and take the best. Use the volume division as a check: the count can never exceed it.

This is a case where rounding down is correct, the opposite of buying tiles. Boxes that fit is a counting question about whole objects inside a fixed space; boxes to buy is a question about covering a need. Always ask which kind of question it is.

A related problem: how many cubes of edge 5 centimetres fit in the carton? 12 along 60, 8 along 40, 6 along 30: 576 cubes, equal to 72 000 divided by 125, because all three dimensions are multiples of 5.

In practice, packers also care about stability and labels facing outward, but the mathematics of fitting is the dimension-by-dimension division. Questions on packing follow now.

## Subtopic: Painting and Covering: Surface Area With Gaps and Rates

Painting a room, tiling a pool, or wrapping a parcel are surface-area problems, usually with parts left out and a rate to apply at the end.

A bedroom is 4 metres long, 3,5 metres wide and 2,7 metres high. The four walls are to be painted, not the floor or ceiling. The walls are two pairs of rectangles: 4 by 2,7, two of them, and 3,5 by 2,7, two of them. Together: 2 times the bracket 4 plus 3,5, times 2,7 — the perimeter of the floor times the height — which is 15 times 2,7, which is 40,5 square metres.

Subtract the openings. A door 0,9 by 2,1 metres, 1,89 square metres. A window 1,5 by 1,2 metres, 1,8 square metres. Wall to paint: 40,5 minus 1,89 minus 1,8, which is 36,81 square metres.

Two coats are needed, so the area to cover is 73,62 square metres. The paint covers 10 square metres per litre, so 7,362 litres are needed. Paint is sold in 4-litre tins: 7,362 divided by 4 is about 1,84, so 2 tins.

The perimeter-times-height shortcut for the walls of a room works because the four walls, unfolded, form one long rectangle whose length is the perimeter of the floor. It is the same shortcut as for the rectangles around a prism.

Covering the inside of an open swimming pool, 8 metres by 4 metres and 1,5 metres deep, with tiles: the floor, 32 square metres, plus the four walls, the perimeter 24 times 1,5, which is 36 square metres. Total 68 square metres. No top.

Wrapping: a gift box 30 by 20 by 10 centimetres has surface area 2 times the bracket 600 plus 300 plus 200, which is 2 200 square centimetres. Wrapping paper must overlap, so allow extra — perhaps 10 to 20 percent — and remember the paper comes in rectangular sheets that may not match the net exactly.

Costs. If tiles cost R220 per square metre, the pool costs 68 times R220, which is R14 960 in tiles.

Without a calculator: 15 times 2,7 is 15 times 2 plus 15 times 0,7, which is 30 plus 10,5, which is 40,5. 68 times 220 is 68 times 200 plus 68 times 20, which is 13 600 plus 1 360, which is 14 960.

The routine: identify the faces to cover, leave out what is not covered, subtract openings, multiply by the number of coats or layers, apply the coverage rate, round up whole items. Questions on painting and covering follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problems as stories about a school vegetable garden that runs on rainwater from a classroom roof, a clinic that needed a concrete wheelchair ramp, and a learner who painted her bedroom with exactly the right number of tins — stories about turning cubes and faces into litres, bags and rand.

## Subtopic: The School Garden's Rainwater Tank

A primary school in Pretoria starts a vegetable garden, but the municipal water bill is already high, so the governing body decides to collect rainwater from the roof of one classroom block. A Grade 8 learner from the high school next door helps plan it as a community project.

The classroom roof slopes, but the learner measures the ground it covers: 12 metres by 8 metres, a plan area of 96 square metres. In a typical summer storm of 20 millimetres, the roof catches 96 times 0,02, which is 1,92 cubic metres, or 1 920 litres. In an average December, Pretoria gets roughly 110 millimetres of rain, so the roof catches about 96 times 0,11, which is 10,56 cubic metres, about 10 560 litres.

The school has budget for one 5 000-litre tank. The learner points out that a single good month would fill it twice over; the rest would overflow. But the dry winter months matter more: the tank should be full when the rain stops.

How long will a full tank last? The garden is 15 beds, each 3 metres by 1 metre, 45 square metres in total. A gardener advises watering about 3 litres per square metre per day in hot weather, so 135 litres per day, rounded to 150 to allow for spillage. 5 000 divided by 150 is about 33,3, so the tank supplies about 33 days of watering without rain.

The tank is a cylinder, but the supplier's brochure gives its footprint: it needs a level base about 1,9 metres across. The school will build a concrete base slab 2 metres by 2 metres and 15 centimetres thick: 2 times 2 times 0,15, which is 0,6 cubic metres of concrete. Concrete costs about R1 600 per cubic metre delivered, so the base costs about R960.

The tank stands on the base, and a full tank holds 5 000 litres of water, about 5 000 kilograms. Spread over the 4 square metres of the slab, that is 1 250 kilograms on each square metre. The builder confirms that 15 centimetres of concrete on compacted ground carries it comfortably.

The gutters along one side of the roof are 12 metres long and drain into a downpipe. The learner checks that the downpipe can cope: in a heavy storm of 30 millimetres in an hour, the roof sheds 96 times 0,03, which is 2,88 cubic metres, or 2 880 litres in an hour, which is 48 litres per minute — within the rating of a standard downpipe.

At the official opening, the principal reads the learner's figures to the parents: 96 square metres of roof, 5 000 litres of storage, 33 days of watering, 0,6 cubic metres of concrete. Questions on the rainwater tank follow.

## Subtopic: The Clinic Ramp and the Bedroom Walls

A community clinic in Polokwane needs a concrete wheelchair ramp from the car park up to its front door, which is 50 centimetres above the ground. Building guidelines recommend a gentle slope of about 1 in 12: for every 12 units along, the ramp rises 1. So the ramp must run 0,5 times 12, which is 6 metres horizontally. It will be 1,2 metres wide.

The ramp is a solid triangular prism. Its cross-section, seen from the side, is a right-angled triangle with a horizontal leg of 6 metres and a vertical leg of 0,5 metres. Area: half of 6 times 0,5, which is 1,5 square metres. The prism's length is the width of the ramp, 1,2 metres. Volume: 1,5 times 1,2, which is 1,8 cubic metres of concrete. At R1 600 per cubic metre, about R2 880.

The sloping top surface will be painted with non-slip paint. Its length is the hypotenuse of the triangle: the square root of 36 plus 0,25, the square root of 36,25, about 6,02 metres — barely longer than the horizontal run, because the slope is so gentle. The surface is 6,02 by 1,2, about 7,22 square metres. One litre of non-slip paint covers 5 square metres, so about 1,44 litres for one coat; two coats need about 2,89 litres, so a 5-litre tin, with plenty left for touch-ups.

The volunteers who mix the concrete want to know how many wheelbarrow loads it is. A builder's wheelbarrow holds about 65 litres, which is 0,065 cubic metres. 1,8 divided by 0,065 is about 27,7, so 28 loads — a full morning's work.

One of the volunteers, a Grade 8 learner, goes home that evening and paints her bedroom, using the same thinking. The room is 4 by 3,5 metres with walls 2,7 metres high. The walls, unfolded, form a strip 15 metres long and 2,7 high: 40,5 square metres. The door and window take away 3,69 square metres, leaving 36,81. Two coats: 73,62 square metres. At 10 square metres per litre, she needs 7,362 litres. The shop sells 4-litre tins at R389 and 1-litre tins at R119. Two 4-litre tins cost R778 and give 8 litres. One 4-litre tin and four 1-litre tins would cost R865 and also give 8 litres. She buys the two large tins.

Before buying, she checks her figure by estimating: the walls are about 15 metres around and under 3 metres high, so about 40 square metres, less a bit for the door and window, times 2 coats is about 75, which needs about 7,5 litres. Her calculation agrees. Questions on the ramp and the walls follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for surface area, volume and capacity problems. One: read and sketch, labelling all dimensions. Two: decide whether the question concerns a surface — paint, tiles, wrapping, a roof catching rain — or a volume — water, concrete, sand, packing space — or both. Three: choose the faces or the cross-section, leaving out open faces and subtracting openings. Four: put all lengths in one unit, converting millimetres of rain or centimetres of thickness to metres when the area is in square metres. Five: calculate. Six: convert volumes to capacities: 1 cubic metre is 1 000 litres, 1 000 cubic centimetres is 1 litre. Seven: apply rates — litres per minute, square metres per litre, rand per cubic metre — and round items to buy up and items that fit down. Eight: answer in a sentence with units, and check against an estimate.

Useful shortcuts. The walls of a room, or the rectangular faces of a prism, have total area equal to the perimeter of the base times the height. A layer has volume equal to its area times its thickness. Depth of liquid is volume divided by base area. Time to fill is capacity divided by rate.

A typical examination item gives a tank and asks for its capacity in litres and the time to fill it at a given rate. Volume, litres, divide by rate, convert minutes to hours.

Another gives a room and asks how many tins of paint to buy. Walls minus openings, times coats, divided by coverage, round up.

Another asks how many boxes fit in a carton. Dimension by dimension, round down, multiply; compare with the volume ratio.

Another uses a triangular prism — a ramp, a roof space, a tent — for volume or surface area. Half base times height for the triangle.

Errors to avoid. Using surface area when volume is needed, or the reverse. Forgetting to convert cubic metres to litres. Mixing millimetres with metres. Counting the open top of a tank or pool. Not subtracting doors and windows. Dividing volumes for a packing question without checking the fit. Rounding down a number of tins.

Looking ahead: the final lesson of the term converts between cubic units and capacity units in detail — cubic millimetres, cubic centimetres, cubic metres, millilitres, litres and kilolitres — completing the set of conversions used in all these problems. Final questions follow.
