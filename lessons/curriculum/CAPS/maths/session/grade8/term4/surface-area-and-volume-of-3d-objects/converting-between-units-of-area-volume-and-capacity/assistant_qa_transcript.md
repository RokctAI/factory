### subtopic_1 — Why Volume Factors Are Cubed
**Assistant:** How do I remember which factor to use for length, area and volume?

**Tutor:** Remember only the length factors and one rule: use the length factor once for length, twice for area, three times for volume. Between millimetres and centimetres the length factor is 10, so the area factor is 10 times 10, which is 100, and the volume factor is 10 times 10 times 10, which is 1 000. Between centimetres and metres the length factor is 100, so area uses 100 times 100, which is 10 000, and volume uses 100 times 100 times 100, which is 1 000 000. The little 2 or 3 in the unit tells you how many times: square centimetres has a 2, so use the factor twice; cubic centimetres has a 3, so use it three times. If you are unsure, picture the shape: a square 1 centimetre on each side holds 10 rows of 10 square millimetres, and a cube 1 centimetre on each edge holds 10 layers of those. Capacity units are the exception: millilitres, litres and kilolitres are linked by 1 000 each time, because milli and kilo are prefixes meaning a thousandth and a thousand, not powers of a length. Their link to volume comes through the bridge: a millilitre is a cubic centimetre.

### subtopic_3 — Capacity Units and the Bridge to Volume
**Assistant:** If a cubic metre is a million cubic centimetres, why is it only a thousand litres?

**Tutor:** Because a litre is not a cubic centimetre; it is a thousand of them. A cubic metre contains 1 000 000 cubic centimetres, and since each cubic centimetre is 1 millilitre, that is 1 000 000 millilitres. Grouping those millilitres into litres, a thousand at a time, gives 1 000 000 divided by 1 000, which is 1 000 litres. So both statements are true at once: 1 cubic metre is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres and also 1 000 litres. The confusion comes from forgetting which small unit matches which: the cubic centimetre matches the millilitre, not the litre. The litre matches a cube 10 centimetres on each edge, and ten of those fit along each edge of a cubic metre, so 10 times 10 times 10, which is 1 000, litres fit inside a cubic metre. A good way to keep it straight is to picture the three cubes: the sugar-cube millilitre, the milk-carton litre, and the sit-inside-it kilolitre. Each is 10 times bigger on each edge than the one before, so a thousand times bigger in volume.

### subtopic_5 — Reading the Water Bill
**Assistant:** Why does the water bill use kilolitres instead of litres?

**Tutor:** Because household water use is large enough that litres give awkward numbers. A family using 600 litres a day uses about 18 000 litres a month and more than 200 000 litres a year; in kilolitres those are 18 and about 219, much easier to read on a bill and a meter. A kilolitre is also exactly a cubic metre, which suits the engineers who design pipes, reservoirs and treatment works, since their calculations are naturally in metres. And it makes the free basic water allowance easy to state: 6 kilolitres per household per month, which is 6 000 litres, or about 200 litres a day. When a bill is hard to picture, converting back to litres and then to something familiar, like buckets or baths, makes it meaningful, which is what the learner did. Water authorities and dams use even bigger units: the megalitre, a million litres, which is 1 000 cubic metres, and for large dams the million cubic metres. The unit is always chosen to keep the numbers manageable for the people who use them, and converting between them is just a matter of factors of 1 000.

### subtopic_6 — The Syringe and the Farm Dam
**Assistant:** How did the learner work out the evaporation from a depth in millimetres?

**Tutor:** By treating the evaporated water as a thin layer, a flat prism whose cross-section is the surface of the dam and whose thickness is the depth lost. The surface is 40 metres by 25 metres, which is 1 000 square metres. A depth of 8 millimetres must be converted to metres before multiplying by square metres: 8 millimetres is 0,008 metres. The layer's volume is 1 000 times 0,008, which is 8 cubic metres. Since a cubic metre is a kilolitre, that is 8 kilolitres, or 8 000 litres. This is the same reasoning as rain falling on a roof, run in reverse: rain adds a layer, evaporation removes one. It shows why a shallow dam with a large surface loses a lot of water in a hot climate: evaporation depends on the surface area, while the water stored depends on the volume. A deep, narrow dam has less surface for its volume, which is the surface-area-to-volume idea from earlier in the topic. Farmers in dry areas sometimes cover dams with shade-cloth or floating covers to reduce the exposed surface, cutting evaporation sharply.
