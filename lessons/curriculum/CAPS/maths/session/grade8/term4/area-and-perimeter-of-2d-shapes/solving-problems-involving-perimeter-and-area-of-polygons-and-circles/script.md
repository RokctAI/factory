# Part 1 — Expert

This session solves problems, with and without a calculator, involving the perimeter and area of polygons and circles. Each problem is approached in the same way: read and sketch, identify the component shapes, decide which measurements are perimeters and which are areas, calculate each part, combine them by adding or subtracting, and answer the question in its context with units. Composite figures include paths around rectangles, running tracks made of a rectangle and two semicircles, rings between two circles, and squares with circles cut out. Without a calculator, pi is taken as 3,14 or twenty-two sevenths, or answers are left in terms of pi. Quantities of whole items such as tiles, boxes and rolls are always rounded up. The anchor problems are a garden 12 by 8 metres with a path 1 metre wide around it and a track with 80-metre straights and semicircular ends of radius 30 metres.

## Subtopic: A Routine for Problems: The Path Around the Garden

A problem in this topic is a short story with numbers. It does not say use the area formula; it says pave a path, or fence a field, or tile a floor. A routine turns the story into calculations.

One: read the whole problem and sketch it, labelling every measurement given. Two: identify the shapes — rectangles, triangles, circles, parts of circles. Three: decide what is wanted — a length around something, a surface to cover, or something derived from them such as a cost. Four: plan the steps, including any measurements that must first be found. Five: calculate, showing each step. Six: answer the question asked, in a sentence, with units.

A rectangular garden is 12 metres by 8 metres. A paved path 1 metre wide runs all the way around the outside. Find the area of the path and the cost of paving it at R165 per square metre.

Sketch: a rectangle 12 by 8 inside a larger rectangle. The path adds 1 metre on every side, so the outer rectangle is 12 plus 1 plus 1, which is 14 metres, by 8 plus 1 plus 1, which is 10 metres. The common error is adding only 1 to each dimension; the path is on both ends.

The path is the region between the two rectangles: enclose and subtract. Outer area: 14 times 10, which is 140 square metres. Garden area: 12 times 8, which is 96 square metres. Path area: 140 minus 96, which is 44 square metres.

Check by decomposition. The path is two strips along the long sides, each 14 by 1, area 28 together, and two strips along the short sides between them, each 8 by 1, area 16 together. Total 44 square metres.

Cost: 44 times R165, which is R7 260. Answer: the path has an area of 44 square metres and costs R7 260 to pave.

The problem might instead ask for the length of edging along the outside of the path: that is the outer perimeter, 2 times the bracket 14 plus 10, which is 48 metres. The same sketch serves both questions, but one is an area and the other a perimeter, and the routine's third step is where that choice is made.

Without a calculator, 44 times 165 can be done as 44 times 100, plus 44 times 60, plus 44 times 5: 4 400 plus 2 640 plus 220, which is 7 260. Breaking a multiplication into friendly parts is part of solving problems without a calculator. Questions on the routine follow now.

## Subtopic: Polygons and Circles Combined: The Running Track

Many real shapes join straight edges and curves. A running track is the standard example: two straight sides joined by two semicircular ends.

A school track has straights 80 metres long, and each end is a semicircle with radius 30 metres, measured to the inside edge. Find the distance around the inside edge and the area of the field it encloses.

Sketch: a rectangle 80 metres long and 60 metres wide — the width is the diameter of the semicircles, 2 times 30 — with a semicircle on each short end.

Perimeter: the two straights, 2 times 80, which is 160 metres, plus the two semicircles. Two semicircles of the same radius make one whole circle, so their total length is 2 pi times 30, which is 60 pi, about 188,50 metres. Inside edge: 160 plus 188,50, about 348,50 metres. The short sides of the rectangle are not part of the perimeter; they are inside the shape, where the semicircles join.

Area: the rectangle, 80 times 60, which is 4 800 square metres, plus the two semicircles, which make one circle of radius 30, area pi times 900, about 2 827,43 square metres. Total: about 7 627,43 square metres.

Recognising that two halves make a whole is a key move. Two semicircles of radius r: total length 2 pi r, total area pi r squared. Four quarter circles of radius r: likewise a whole circle.

Another combined shape: a window made of a rectangle 1,2 metres wide and 1,5 metres tall with a semicircle on top. The semicircle's diameter is 1,2 metres, so its radius is 0,6. The frame around the outside: the two sides and the bottom of the rectangle, 1,5 plus 1,5 plus 1,2, which is 4,2 metres, plus the curved top, half of 2 pi times 0,6, which is 0,6 pi, about 1,88 metres; total about 6,08 metres. The glass: the rectangle, 1,2 times 1,5, which is 1,8 square metres, plus the semicircle, half of pi times 0,36, about 0,57 square metres; total about 2,37 square metres.

The pattern for every combined shape: list the edges that form the outside boundary for the perimeter, and list the regions that make up the inside for the area. An edge shared between two pieces is never part of the perimeter. Questions on combined shapes follow now.

## Subtopic: Shaded Regions and Rings

A shaded region is the part of one shape left over when another is removed. Its area is almost always found by subtraction: the larger area minus the smaller.

A square of side 10 centimetres has the largest possible circle drawn inside it, touching all four sides. The shaded region is the four corners outside the circle. The circle's diameter equals the side, 10, so its radius is 5. Square: 100 square centimetres. Circle: 25 pi, about 78,54. Shaded: 100 minus 25 pi, about 21,46 square centimetres. Without a calculator, using 3,14: 100 minus 78,5, which is 21,5. In terms of pi: 100 minus 25 pi, exact.

The four corners make up about 21 percent of the square — a useful figure to remember: a circle fills about 79 percent of the square around it.

A ring, also called an annulus, is the region between two circles with the same centre. A ring has outer radius 7 centimetres and inner radius 4 centimetres. Area: pi times 7 squared minus pi times 4 squared, which is 49 pi minus 16 pi, which is 33 pi, about 103,67 square centimetres. Note that the answer is not pi times 3 squared, the area of a circle with the width of the ring; subtracting the radii first is a common and costly error.

The perimeter of a ring is the two circles together: 2 pi times 7 plus 2 pi times 4, which is 22 pi, about 69,12 centimetres.

A square of side 8 centimetres has a quarter circle of radius 8 drawn from one corner. The region inside the square but outside the quarter circle: 64 minus one quarter of pi times 64, which is 64 minus 16 pi, about 13,73 square centimetres.

A circle of radius 6 has a square inscribed in it, with its corners on the circle. The square's diagonals are diameters, 12 centimetres each. A square is a rhombus, so its area is half the product of its diagonals: half of 12 times 12, which is 72. The region inside the circle and outside the square: 36 pi minus 72, about 41,10 square centimetres.

In every case, decide which shape is the outer and which the inner, find both areas, and subtract. If the shaded part is made of several pieces, such as four corners, there is no need to find them separately: one subtraction finds them all together, and dividing by 4 gives one corner if needed. Questions on shaded regions follow now.

## Subtopic: Without a Calculator, and Rounding Whole Items Up

Some problems are set without a calculator. They are designed so that the arithmetic is manageable, and they reward three techniques.

First, choose pi to suit the numbers. If a radius or diameter is a multiple of 7, use twenty-two sevenths: a circle of radius 7 has circumference 2 times twenty-two sevenths times 7, which is 44, and area twenty-two sevenths times 49, which is 154. Otherwise use 3,14, or leave the answer in terms of pi when allowed: a circle of radius 5 has area 25 pi, no arithmetic needed.

Second, break multiplications into friendly parts. 3,14 times 25 is 3 times 25 plus 0,14 times 25: 75 plus 3,5, which is 78,5. 48 times 85 is 48 times 80 plus 48 times 5: 3 840 plus 240, which is 4 080.

Third, estimate first. A circle of radius 10 has area a little more than 3 times 100, so about 314; an answer of 31,4 or 3 140 is a decimal slip.

Many problems end not with an area but with a number of things to buy: tiles, boxes, rolls, tins, bags. These come in whole units, and buying too few means the job cannot be finished. So the count is always rounded up, even when the decimal part is small.

A floor 4,2 metres by 3,6 metres is to be tiled with square tiles 30 centimetres on a side. Floor area: 4,2 times 3,6, which is 15,12 square metres. One tile: 0,3 times 0,3, which is 0,09 square metres. Tiles needed: 15,12 divided by 0,09, which is 168. Tilers add about 10 percent for cutting and breakage: 168 times 1,1 is 184,8, rounded up to 185 tiles. Tiles come in boxes of 12: 185 divided by 12 is about 15,4, so 16 boxes. Fifteen boxes hold only 180 tiles, 5 short.

The same rule applies to paint: if the wall area needs 3,2 tins, buy 4. To rolls of fencing: if the perimeter needs 4,1 rolls, buy 5.

It does not apply to everything. A cost in rand is rounded to the nearest cent, not up. A measurement is rounded to the stated number of decimal places. The question to ask is whether the quantity must be a whole item bought from a shop.

Units must match before dividing. Tiles measured in centimetres and a floor in metres must be put into the same unit first; dividing 15,12 square metres by 900 square centimetres gives nonsense. Questions on these techniques follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same problem solving as stories about a family building a round rondavel and costing its floor and walls, a learner who tiled her grandmother's kitchen and bought exactly the right number of boxes, and a contractor who quoted a path by the perimeter instead of the area — stories about getting from a sketch to a shopping list.

## Subtopic: Building the Rondavel

A family in rural KwaZulu-Natal is building a rondavel, a round house with a thatched roof, beside their main home. The floor is a circle with an inside diameter of 6 metres, and the walls are 2,4 metres high. The eldest daughter, in Grade 8, is asked to work out the quantities for the builder's order.

Floor first. The floor needs a cement screed, priced by area at R85 per square metre including labour. The radius is 3 metres, so the floor area is pi times 9, about 28,27 square metres. The screed costs about 28,27 times R85, which is about R2 403,32.

The wall. The builder lays blocks by area of wall. The wall is a curved surface, but if it were unrolled it would be a rectangle whose length is the circumference and whose height is 2,4 metres. The circumference is pi times 6, about 18,85 metres. The wall area is 18,85 times 2,4, about 45,24 square metres. There is one door, 0,9 metres wide and 2,1 metres tall, which is 1,89 square metres, and two windows, each 0,6 by 0,9 metres, 0,54 square metres each. Openings total 1,89 plus 1,08, which is 2,97 square metres. The wall to build is about 45,24 minus 2,97, which is 42,27 square metres.

The builder uses about 12,5 blocks per square metre. 42,27 times 12,5 is about 528,4, rounded up to 529 blocks, and he adds 5 percent for breakages: 529 times 1,05 is about 555,45, so 556 blocks.

The foundation trench follows the outside of the wall. The wall is 0,2 metres thick, so the outside diameter is 6 plus 0,2 plus 0,2, which is 6,4 metres, and the outside circumference is pi times 6,4, about 20,11 metres. The trench runs around that circle.

Her uncle asks whether a square house with the same floor area would need more wall. A square of area 28,27 square metres has side the square root of 28,27, about 5,32 metres, and perimeter 4 times 5,32, about 21,27 metres, compared with 18,85 for the circle. The round house needs about 2,4 metres less wall for the same floor — one reason the circular plan is economical with materials. Of all shapes with a given area, the circle has the smallest perimeter.

The builder checks her figures and orders exactly what she listed. Questions on the rondavel follow.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen Floor and the Path Quotation

A learner in East London helps her grandmother retile the kitchen floor, which measures 4,2 metres by 3,6 metres, and replace the skirting board around the walls. The shop sells 30-centimetre square tiles in boxes of 12, and skirting in lengths of 2,4 metres.

She follows the routine. Tiles cover the floor: an area. Floor: 15,12 square metres. Each tile is 0,09 square metres, so 168 tiles exactly fill the floor if none is wasted. The shop assistant advises 10 percent extra for cutting at the walls: 184,8, which she rounds up to 185. At 12 per box, 185 divided by 12 is about 15,4, so 16 boxes, holding 192 tiles. The 7 left over will be kept for repairs.

She checks the tile count another way. Along the 4,2-metre wall, 4,2 divided by 0,3 is 14 tiles; along the 3,6-metre wall, 12 tiles. A grid of 14 by 12 is 168 tiles, agreeing with the area calculation — this time exactly, because both walls are whole multiples of 30 centimetres.

Skirting runs around the edge of the room: a perimeter. The perimeter is 2 times the bracket 4,2 plus 3,6, which is 15,6 metres. But there is a doorway 0,9 metres wide where no skirting goes, so she needs 15,6 minus 0,9, which is 14,7 metres. At 2,4 metres per length, 14,7 divided by 2,4 is about 6,125, so 7 lengths. Six would provide only 14,4 metres, 30 centimetres short.

At the same time, her grandmother has a quotation from a contractor for a new paved path, 1 metre wide, around a rectangular flower bed 12 metres by 8 metres. The quotation says 40 square metres of paving. The learner sketches it and recognises the problem from class: the outer rectangle is 14 by 10, so the path is 140 minus 96, which is 44 square metres, not 40.

She works out where 40 came from. The perimeter of the flower bed is 2 times 20, which is 40 metres, and a path 1 metre wide along 40 metres looks like 40 square metres. But that misses the four corner squares, each 1 by 1, where the path turns: 40 plus 4 is 44. The contractor had multiplied the inner perimeter by the width and forgotten the corners.

The contractor corrects the quote, and her grandmother says the corners would have been the first places to crack if he had run short of pavers. Questions on the kitchen and the path follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for perimeter and area problems. One: read the whole problem and sketch it with every measurement labelled. Two: identify the component shapes. Three: decide for each part whether a perimeter or an area is needed, using the context — fencing, edging, skirting and trim are perimeters; paving, tiling, painting and covering are areas. Four: find missing measurements, such as the outer dimensions of a path or the radius of a semicircle. Five: calculate each part, keeping full accuracy. Six: combine by adding regions, subtracting holes and shaded parts, and counting each outside edge once. Seven: convert to what the question asks — a cost, a count of items rounded up, a percentage. Eight: write the answer in a sentence with units, and check it against an estimate.

Useful facts. Two semicircles of the same radius make a circle. Four quarter circles make a circle. A path of width w around a rectangle adds 2 w to each dimension. The shaded area between a square and its largest inscribed circle is about 21 percent of the square. A ring's area is the difference of the circle areas, not the area of a circle with the difference of the radii.

A typical examination item gives a composite figure — a rectangle with semicircular ends, or a square with a circle removed — and asks for its perimeter and area. Show the parts.

Another gives a context and asks for a cost. Find the measure first, then multiply by the rate.

Another asks how many items to buy. Divide, then round up, and say why.

Another, without a calculator, asks for an answer in terms of pi or with a convenient value of pi. Choose twenty-two sevenths for multiples of 7.

Errors to avoid. Adding the path width once instead of twice. Including internal edges in a perimeter. Forgetting the straight edge of a semicircle or the corners of a path. Subtracting radii before squaring. Rounding down a number of items. Mixing metres and centimetres.

Looking ahead: the next lesson converts between square units — square millimetres, square centimetres, square metres and square kilometres — which is often needed when a problem mixes tile sizes in centimetres with room sizes in metres; then the topic moves into three dimensions with surface area and volume. Final questions follow.
