### subtopic_1 — A Routine for Problems: The Path Around the Garden
**Assistant:** Why does a path 1 metre wide add 2 metres to each side of the rectangle and not 1?

**Tutor:** Because the path runs along both ends of each dimension. Look at the length of 12 metres: the path sticks out 1 metre beyond the left end and 1 metre beyond the right end, so the outer length is 1 plus 12 plus 1, which is 14. The same happens to the width: 1 plus 8 plus 1, which is 10. A sketch makes this obvious, which is why the routine insists on one. If you add only 1 to each, you get 13 by 9, area 117, and a path of only 21 square metres, about half the true 44. The same reasoning applies to a frame around a picture, a border around a carpet, or a margin around a page: a border of width w adds 2 w to every dimension. Watch for problems where the path runs along only some sides, for example along the front and one side of a house; then the sketch shows which ends get the extra width and which do not. Another check: the path area should equal the outer perimeter of the garden times the width, plus the four corner squares, so 40 plus 4 is 44, agreeing with 140 minus 96.

### subtopic_2 — Polygons and Circles Combined: The Running Track
**Assistant:** In the running track, why are the short sides of the rectangle not included in the perimeter?

**Tutor:** Because they are not on the outside of the shape. The track is made by joining a semicircle to each short end of the rectangle, and the short sides become the straight edges where the semicircles attach. Once the pieces are joined, those edges are inside the figure, with field on both sides of them, so a runner going around the track never travels along them. The perimeter is only the boundary between the shape and everything outside it: the two long straights and the two curved ends. This is a general rule for every composite figure. When pieces are joined, each shared edge disappears from the perimeter, even though it was part of each piece's own perimeter. That is why perimeters of composite figures cannot be found by adding the perimeters of the pieces, while areas can be found by adding the areas of the pieces. For the track, adding the rectangle's perimeter of 280 metres to the circle's circumference of about 188,50 metres would give about 468,50, including the two 60-metre short sides that should not be there; removing them gives 348,50.

### subtopic_3 — Shaded Regions and Rings
**Assistant:** How do I tell which shape is subtracted from which in a shaded region?

**Tutor:** Ask which shape the shaded part lies inside, and which shape has been cut out of it. The shaded region always lies within the larger, outer shape, and outside the smaller, inner shape, so its area is the outer area minus the inner area. In the square with a circle inside, the shaded corners are inside the square but outside the circle: square minus circle. If instead the circle were drawn around the square, with the square's corners touching the circle, the shaded parts between them would be inside the circle and outside the square: circle minus square. For a ring, the shaded part is inside the big circle and outside the small one. If you are unsure, estimate: the shaded part must have a smaller area than the outer shape and a positive area, so a negative answer means the subtraction was the wrong way round. Sometimes a figure has several shapes removed, such as a rectangle with two circular holes; then subtract each hole. And sometimes the shaded part is made of pieces of different shapes added together, so sketch it, label each region as shaded or not, and build the calculation from the labels.

### subtopic_5 — Building the Rondavel
**Assistant:** Why does a circle have a shorter boundary than a square with the same area?

**Tutor:** Because a circle is the most compact shape possible: every point on its edge is the same distance from the centre, so there are no corners poking out that add boundary without adding much area. With the rondavel's floor of about 28,27 square metres, the circle needs about 18,85 metres of wall, while a square of the same area needs about 21,27 metres, roughly 13 percent more. A long thin rectangle of the same area, say 2 metres by about 14,14, would need about 32,27 metres of wall, far more. The pattern continues through regular polygons: a regular hexagon does better than a square, an octagon better still, and as the number of sides increases the shape approaches a circle and the perimeter approaches the circle's. This fact, that among all shapes with a given area the circle has the least perimeter, has been known since ancient times and appears in nature: soap bubbles and water drops pull themselves into round shapes to minimise their surface. For builders it means fewer blocks for the same floor space, which is part of why round houses have been built in many parts of the world, including the rondavels of southern Africa.
