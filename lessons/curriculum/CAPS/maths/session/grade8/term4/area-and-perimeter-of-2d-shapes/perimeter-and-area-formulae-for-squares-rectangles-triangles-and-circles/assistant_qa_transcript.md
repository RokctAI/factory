### subtopic_2 — Triangles: Base and Perpendicular Height
**Assistant:** How do I know which measurement is the perpendicular height when a triangle has several lengths marked?

**Tutor:** Look for the line that meets the base at a right angle, usually shown with a small square symbol, or described as perpendicular or as the height. The height is the straight-down distance from the vertex opposite the base to the line of the base. A slanting side joins the same vertex to an end of the base, but at an angle, so it is always longer than the height and gives too large an area. In a right-angled triangle the legs are perpendicular to each other, so one leg is the height for the other, and no extra line is needed: legs 6 and 8 give half of 6 times 8, which is 24. If a diagram shows a base of 14, slanting sides of 13 and 15, and a dashed line of 12 meeting the base at a right angle, the area uses 14 and 12, giving 84, and the 13 and 15 are needed only for the perimeter, which is 42. If you change which side you call the base, you must use the height perpendicular to that side; the area comes out the same either way. When no perpendicular height is given at all and the triangle is not right-angled, the theorem of Pythagoras can sometimes find it, as in the equilateral triangle problems from earlier this term.

### subtopic_3 — Circles: Circumference and Area
**Assistant:** When should I use 3,14, twenty-two sevenths, or the pi key?

**Tutor:** Use whatever the question tells you to use; if it says nothing, the calculator's pi key is the most accurate and is generally accepted, with the answer rounded to two decimal places. All three give slightly different answers because 3,14 and twenty-two sevenths are approximations of a number whose decimals never end. For a circle of radius 7, the circumference is about 43,98 with the pi key, 43,96 with 3,14, and exactly 44 with twenty-two sevenths. A marker following a memorandum built on 3,14 expects 43,96, so if the question specifies 3,14, use it even though the key is more accurate. Twenty-two sevenths is convenient when working without a calculator and the radius or diameter is a multiple of 7, because the 7 cancels. A fourth option is to leave pi as a symbol: 14 pi centimetres is exact, and questions often ask for answers in terms of pi for that reason. Whichever value you use, keep it throughout the question, and round only at the final step, because rounding halfway through and then multiplying makes the error grow.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing the Formula and Stating the Units
**Assistant:** Can two shapes have the same area but different perimeters?

**Tutor:** Yes, and very different ones. A rectangle 1 by 36 and a square 6 by 6 both have area 36 square units, but the long thin rectangle has perimeter 74 units and the square only 24. A rectangle 2 by 18 has perimeter 40, 3 by 12 has 30, and 4 by 9 has 26: as the rectangle becomes more square, the perimeter falls, and among all rectangles with a given area the square has the smallest perimeter. The reverse is true too: shapes with the same perimeter can have different areas. With 24 metres of fencing, a 1 by 11 pen encloses 11 square metres, a 3 by 9 pen 27, and a 6 by 6 pen 36. A circle does even better: 24 metres of fencing bent into a circle has radius 24 divided by 2 pi, about 3,82 metres, and encloses about 45,8 square metres. This is why perimeter and area must always be worked out separately, and why questions about the most efficient use of fencing or material are really about making the shape compact. It is also the idea behind the surface area and volume lessons later this term, where compact shapes use the least packaging.

### subtopic_6 — The Shade Sail and the Sandpit
**Assistant:** How did the treasurer know the barrier mistake came from the diameter?

**Tutor:** She compared the wrong figure with her correct one and asked what simple slip connects them. Her correct area was about 4,52 square metres, and the quotation said 18,10, which is almost exactly four times as much. A factor of 4 in a circle area has one very common cause: using the diameter where the radius belongs. The diameter is twice the radius, and squaring it gives four times the radius squared, so the area comes out four times too large. She confirmed it by calculating pi times 2,4 squared, which is about 18,10, matching the quotation exactly. The shade-sail error was diagnosed the same way: 48 is exactly twice 24, and a factor of 2 in a triangle area almost always means the half was forgotten. This is a powerful habit for checking anyone's work, including your own. When an answer is wrong, divide it by the right answer: a ratio of 2 suggests a missing half, a ratio of 4 suggests diameter for radius, a ratio of 100 or 10 000 suggests a unit conversion slip. The size of the error often names the mistake.
