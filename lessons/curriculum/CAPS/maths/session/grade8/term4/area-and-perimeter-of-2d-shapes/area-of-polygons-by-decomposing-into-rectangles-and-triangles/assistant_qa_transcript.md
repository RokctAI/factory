### subtopic_1 — Splitting and Adding: The L-Shape
**Assistant:** How do I decide where to make the cuts?

**Tutor:** Look for cuts that produce shapes whose every dimension you can find from the labels. In an L-shape, the natural cuts extend one of the inner edges straight across the shape, horizontally or vertically, so that both pieces are rectangles. Either choice works; pick the one where the missing lengths are easiest to work out. If the shape has a big notch, enclose and subtract is often quicker: one big rectangle minus one small rectangle. If there are slanting sides, cut perpendicular to the parallel sides so that the slanting parts become right-angled triangles, whose base and height are horizontal and vertical lengths you can find by subtraction. Avoid cuts that create a piece with a dimension you cannot determine, such as a triangle whose height is not given and cannot be worked out. Fewer pieces generally means fewer chances for error, but a decomposition into three easy pieces is better than two awkward ones. Finally, remember that the choice of cuts cannot change the answer, which is exactly why a second decomposition is such a good check: if your two totals disagree, look for the piece whose dimensions were misread.

### subtopic_3 — Decimal Dimensions and Rounding to Two Decimal Places
**Assistant:** Why is 4,995 rounded to two decimal places written as 5,00 and not 5?

**Tutor:** Because the question asked for two decimal places, and the zeros show that the answer is accurate to hundredths. Rounding 4,995 to two decimal places looks at the third decimal digit, which is 5, so the second digit goes up by one. The second digit is 9, so it becomes 10, which carries 1 into the first decimal place; that is also 9, so it carries again into the units, and 4 becomes 5. The result is 5,00. Writing just 5 would suggest the answer was rounded to the nearest whole number, which is less precise, and a marker looking for two decimal places may not award the mark. Trailing zeros after a decimal comma are meaningful in measurement: 5,00 square metres says the area is between 4,995 and 5,005, while 5 says only that it is between 4,5 and 5,5. The same applies to money, where R5,00 is the usual way to write five rand. So when a question specifies the number of decimal places, always write exactly that many, even if they are zeros. In the middle of a calculation, though, keep all the digits and do not round at all.

### subtopic_4 — Polygons on a Grid: Enclose and Subtract
**Assistant:** In enclose and subtract, how do I know the outside regions are all triangles?

**Tutor:** Look at where each vertex of the polygon touches the enclosing rectangle. When every side of the rectangle is touched by exactly one vertex, and the polygon is a quadrilateral, the four outside regions are each bounded by one side of the polygon and two half-sides of the rectangle meeting at a corner, so each is a right-angled triangle. That was the case for the quadrilateral with vertices 1 semicolon 2, 4 semicolon 1, 6 semicolon 4 and 3 semicolon 6. If two vertices lie on the same side of the rectangle, or a vertex sits in a corner of the rectangle, the regions change: some disappear, and some become a triangle joined to a rectangle. For example, a quadrilateral with vertices 1 semicolon 1, 6 semicolon 2, 5 semicolon 6 and 2 semicolon 5 sits in a 5 by 5 box, but the top-left outside region is a 1 by 1 square plus two triangles, not a single triangle, because the vertex 2 semicolon 5 does not touch the box. Counting only four triangles gives 17, while the true area is 16. The safe routine is to shade every outside region on the sketch and split each into right-angled triangles and rectangles before calculating.

### subtopic_5 — Paving the Irregular Yard
**Assistant:** Why did the homeowner apply the 5 percent allowance after subtracting the triangle and not before?

**Tutor:** Because the allowance is a percentage of the area that will actually be paved, so the area must be correct first. The allowance covers pavers that are cut and wasted at the edges, and it scales with the paved area: 5 percent of 101 square metres is 5,05, giving 106,05. Applying it to the full rectangle and then subtracting the triangle would give 105 times 1,05, which is 110,25, minus 4, which is 106,25: close, but slightly wrong, because it adds an allowance for the 4 square metres that will never be paved. The general rule is to finish the geometry before the money or the percentages: find the true area, then apply rates, allowances and prices. In this case the difference between the two orders is small, 0,2 square metres, but in larger jobs and with bigger allowances it can be significant. It is also clearer for anyone checking the work, because the area figure stands on its own and the allowance is a separate, visible step. The supplier's error was bigger, of course: using 105 instead of 101 overstated the order by about 4,2 square metres once the allowance was included.
