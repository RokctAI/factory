# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses formulae for the perimeter and area of squares, rectangles, triangles and circles. For a square of side s, the perimeter is 4 s and the area is s squared. For a rectangle of length l and breadth b, the perimeter is 2 times l plus b and the area is l times b. For a triangle, the perimeter is the sum of the three sides and the area is half the base times the perpendicular height. For a circle of radius r, the circumference is 2 pi r, or pi times the diameter, and the area is pi r squared. Perimeters are lengths, in units such as centimetres; areas are in square units such as square centimetres. The anchor shapes are a square of side 3,5 centimetres, a rectangle 2,4 by 1,8 metres, a triangle of base 10 and height 6, and a circle of radius 7.

## Subtopic: Squares and Rectangles

Perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a shape. Area is the amount of flat surface inside it, counted in squares. The two are measured differently: a perimeter in centimetres or metres, an area in square centimetres or square metres.

A square has four equal sides. If the side is s, the perimeter is s plus s plus s plus s, which is 4 s. The area is s times s, which is s squared. For a square of side 3,5 centimetres, the perimeter is 4 times 3,5, which is 14 centimetres, and the area is 3,5 times 3,5, which is 12,25 square centimetres.

A rectangle has two lengths and two breadths. If the length is l and the breadth is b, the perimeter is 2 l plus 2 b, which can be written 2 times the bracket l plus b: add the length and the breadth, then double. The area is l times b. For a rectangle 2,4 metres by 1,8 metres, the perimeter is 2 times 4,2, which is 8,4 metres, and the area is 2,4 times 1,8, which is 4,32 square metres.

The area formula comes from counting. A rectangle 4 by 3 can be covered by 3 rows of 4 unit squares, 12 squares in all; length times breadth counts the rows and columns at once. With decimal sides the same multiplication counts parts of squares.

A formula can be used backwards to find a missing side. A rectangle has area 60 square centimetres and length 12 centimetres. Area is length times breadth, so 60 equals 12 times b, and b is 60 divided by 12, which is 5 centimetres. Its perimeter is then 2 times 17, which is 34 centimetres.

A square has perimeter 36 metres. Then 4 s equals 36, so s is 9 metres, and the area is 81 square metres. A square has area 49 square metres; then s squared equals 49, so s is 7 metres, and the perimeter is 28 metres.

Before substituting, make sure all lengths are in the same unit. A rectangle 2 metres by 50 centimetres must become 2 metres by 0,5 metres, or 200 centimetres by 50 centimetres. Multiplying 2 by 50 and calling it 100 gives a meaningless number.

Write each calculation in three lines: the formula, the substitution, and the answer with its unit. That habit shows the marker your method and catches the commonest slip, which is using the area formula for a perimeter or the other way round. Questions on squares and rectangles follow now.

## Subtopic: Triangles: Base and Perpendicular Height

The perimeter of a triangle has no special formula: add the three sides. The area formula is half the base times the height, where the height is the perpendicular height — the distance from the base to the opposite vertex, measured at right angles to the base.

Why half? Any triangle fits inside a rectangle with the same base and the same height, and the triangle takes exactly half of that rectangle. Draw the height from the top vertex down to the base: it splits the rectangle into two smaller rectangles, and the triangle's sides cut each of those in half along a diagonal. So the triangle's area is half of base times height.

A triangle with base 10 centimetres and height 6 centimetres has area half of 10 times 6, which is 30 square centimetres. A triangle with base 8 and height 5,5 has area half of 44, which is 22 square units. A triangle with base 12,4 metres and height 7,5 metres has area half of 93, which is 46,5 square metres.

The height must be perpendicular to the base. A slanting side is not the height, unless the triangle has a right angle and the side is one of the two legs. In a right-angled triangle, the two legs are perpendicular, so either can be the base and the other the height. A right-angled triangle with legs 6 and 8 has area half of 48, which is 24 square units, and its hypotenuse, by the theorem of Pythagoras, is 10, so its perimeter is 6 plus 8 plus 10, which is 24 units. The equal numbers are a coincidence; the units are different.

Any side can be chosen as the base, as long as the height used is the one perpendicular to that side. The area comes out the same whichever pair is used.

In an obtuse-angled triangle, the height from the top vertex can fall outside the triangle, onto the base extended. The formula still works: use the base as given and the perpendicular height measured to the line of the base.

Working backwards: a triangle has area 22 square centimetres and base 8 centimetres. Half of 8 times h is 22, so 4 h is 22 and h is 5,5 centimetres. A triangle has area 30 square metres and height 12 metres; half of b times 12 is 30, so 6 b is 30 and b is 5 metres.

Remember to halve. Forgetting the half doubles the answer and is the most common error with triangles. Questions on triangles follow now.

## Subtopic: Circles: Circumference and Area

The perimeter of a circle is called its circumference. A circle is fixed by its radius r, the distance from the centre to the edge; the diameter d is twice the radius, the full width through the centre.

The circumference is pi times the diameter, or 2 times pi times the radius — the same thing, since d is 2 r. The area is pi times the radius squared. The number pi is about 3,14; its meaning is the subject of a later lesson in this topic.

A circle of radius 7 centimetres. Circumference: 2 times pi times 7, which is 14 pi. With the calculator's pi, that is about 43,98 centimetres; with 3,14 it is 43,96; with twenty-two sevenths it is exactly 44, which is why that fraction is popular when the radius is a multiple of 7. Area: pi times 7 squared, which is 49 pi. With the calculator, about 153,94 square centimetres; with 3,14, 153,86; with twenty-two sevenths, 154.

The three values differ slightly because 3,14 and twenty-two sevenths are approximations. Use the one the question specifies. If it says use the pi key, use the calculator; if it says take pi as 3,14, use 3,14. If it asks for the answer in terms of pi, leave pi as a symbol: 14 pi centimetres and 49 pi square centimetres are exact.

Watch for a diameter in the question. A circle of diameter 20 metres has radius 10 metres. Circumference: pi times 20, about 62,83 metres. Area: pi times 10 squared, which is 100 pi, about 314,16 square metres. Using 20 in the area formula would give 400 pi, four times too much — the most common circle error.

Square the radius before multiplying by pi. Pi times 7 squared means pi times 49, not pi times 7, squared.

Working backwards: a circle has circumference 18,85 centimetres. Then 2 pi r is 18,85, so r is 18,85 divided by 2 pi, which is about 3 centimetres. A circle has area 28,27 square centimetres; r squared is 28,27 divided by pi, about 9, so r is about 3.

Part circles follow from the whole. A semicircle of radius 10 has curved edge half of 2 pi times 10, which is 10 pi, about 31,42; its full perimeter adds the diameter, 20, for about 51,42 units; its area is half of 100 pi, about 157,08 square units. Forgetting the straight edge of a semicircle is another common slip. Questions on circles follow now.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Formula and Stating the Units

Most errors in this topic are not arithmetic but choice: the wrong formula, the wrong measurement, or the wrong unit. A short routine prevents them.

First, decide whether the question asks for a perimeter or an area. Words that point to perimeter: fence, border, edging, frame, trim, distance around, how far to walk around. Words that point to area: cover, paint, carpet, tile, grass, paper, fabric, space inside. A question about fencing a rectangular field 30 by 20 metres asks for the perimeter, 2 times 50, which is 100 metres; a question about planting grass on it asks for the area, 600 square metres.

Second, identify the measurements the formula needs. For a rectangle, length and breadth. For a triangle, a base and the perpendicular height to it — not a slanting side. For a circle, the radius — halve a diameter. Extra measurements in a diagram are often there to tempt you into using the wrong one.

Third, make the units agree before substituting. A rectangle 1,5 metres by 80 centimetres: use 1,5 and 0,8 metres, area 1,2 square metres, or 150 and 80 centimetres, area 12 000 square centimetres. Both are correct; mixing them is not.

Fourth, write the unit of the answer. A perimeter is in the length unit: millimetres, centimetres, metres, kilometres. An area is in square units: square millimetres, square centimetres, square metres. Writing square metres for a fence, or metres for a carpet, loses marks and signals confusion.

Fifth, check the size of the answer. A classroom floor of about 60 square metres is reasonable; 6 000 square metres is a sports field and 0,6 is a desk top. If the answer is far from a sensible estimate, re-check the units.

A summary table helps. Square: perimeter 4 s, area s squared. Rectangle: perimeter 2 times l plus b, area l times b. Triangle: perimeter the sum of the sides, area half b times h. Circle: circumference 2 pi r, area pi r squared.

One more distinction: for the same shape, perimeter and area do not move together in a simple way. A 1 by 36 rectangle and a 6 by 6 square both have area 36, but perimeters of 74 and 24. A long thin shape has a large perimeter for its area; a compact shape has a small one. Among rectangles of a given area, the square has the smallest perimeter. # Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same formulae as stories about the groundsman who marks out a soccer field and its centre circle, a crèche that needs a triangular shade sail and a round sandpit, and a learner who checked a quotation by doing the sums herself — stories about the lengths and areas that real jobs are priced by.

## Subtopic: Marking Out the Soccer Field

The groundsman at a community stadium in Gqeberha prepares the pitch for a league match. The pitch is a rectangle 105 metres long and 68 metres wide, the standard size used in professional football. He has a line-marking trolley that paints a stripe of white along the ground, and a fertiliser spreader that covers the grass.

For the boundary lines he needs the perimeter. Two lengths and two widths: 2 times the bracket 105 plus 68, which is 2 times 173, which is 346 metres. The trolley holds enough paint for about 400 metres, so one fill covers the boundary with some to spare.

For the fertiliser he needs the area: 105 times 68, which is 7 140 square metres. The bag says one kilogram covers 50 square metres, so he needs 7 140 divided by 50, which is about 143 kilograms.

The centre circle has radius 9,15 metres. The trolley must paint its circumference: 2 times pi times 9,15, which is about 57,49 metres. The groundsman sets up a rope from the centre spot, 9,15 metres long, and walks the trolley around at the end of it — the rope keeps the radius fixed, so the path is a circle.

A learner on a school visit asks how much grass is inside the centre circle. Pi times 9,15 squared: 9,15 squared is about 83,72, and times pi is about 263,02 square metres. She points out that the circle is less than 4 percent of the pitch — 263 out of 7 140.

Each penalty area is a rectangle 40,3 metres wide and 16,5 metres deep. Its painted outline uses three sides, since the goal line is already marked: 40,3 plus 16,5 plus 16,5, which is 73,3 metres. Its area is 40,3 times 16,5, which is about 664,95 square metres.

The learner then asks about the semicircle at the top of each penalty area, called the arc. It is part of a circle of radius 9,15 metres centred on the penalty spot, but only the part outside the penalty area is painted, so its length is less than half the circumference. The groundsman laughs and says he simply walks the rope again.

By the end of the morning the learner has added up all the lines: boundary, halfway line, centre circle, both penalty areas and goal areas, arcs and spots. Every one came from a perimeter or a circumference, and every bag of fertiliser from an area. Questions on the soccer field follow.

## Subtopic: The Shade Sail and the Sandpit

A crèche in Kimberley is raising money for two improvements: a triangular shade sail over the play area and a round sandpit. A supplier sends a quotation, and the crèche's treasurer — a parent who studied Grade 8 maths with her daughter that year — checks the numbers before paying.

The shade sail is a right-angled triangle with the two shorter sides 6 metres and 8 metres, fixed to two walls that meet at a corner. The quotation charges for fabric by area and for the reinforced hem by the length around the edge.

The treasurer works out the area: half of 6 times 8, which is 24 square metres. The two walls are perpendicular, so the 6-metre side is the height for the 8-metre base. The quotation says 48 square metres. She circles it: the supplier forgot the half and charged for the whole rectangle.

For the hem she needs the third side. By the theorem of Pythagoras, 36 plus 64 is 100, so the long edge is 10 metres, and the perimeter is 6 plus 8 plus 10, which is 24 metres. The quotation says 24 metres of hem. Correct.

The sandpit is a circle with a diameter of 2,4 metres. It needs a plastic edging strip around the outside and a weed-barrier sheet on the bottom. The radius is 1,2 metres. Edging: the circumference, 2 times pi times 1,2, which is about 7,54 metres. Barrier: the area, pi times 1,2 squared; 1,2 squared is 1,44, and times pi is about 4,52 square metres.

The quotation charges for 18,10 square metres of barrier. The treasurer spots the cause at once: pi times 2,4 squared is about 18,10. The supplier used the diameter instead of the radius, quadrupling the area. She also checks that the edging was not done from the radius alone: pi times the diameter, about 7,54 metres, agrees with her figure, so that line is correct.

She writes back with two corrections: shade-sail fabric 24 square metres, not 48, and barrier sheet 4,52 square metres, not 18,10. The supplier apologises and sends a new quotation, several hundred rand cheaper.

At the next parents' meeting she explains the two errors in one sentence each: the triangle needs a half, and the circle needs the radius. The crèche principal asks her to check every quotation from then on. Questions on the shade sail and sandpit follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for perimeter and area. One: decide whether the question asks for a perimeter or an area. Two: choose the formula. Square: perimeter 4 s, area s squared. Rectangle: perimeter 2 times l plus b, area l times b. Triangle: perimeter the sum of the sides, area half of base times perpendicular height. Circle: circumference 2 pi r or pi d, area pi r squared. Three: pick out the measurements the formula needs — perpendicular height, not a slanting side; radius, not diameter — and put them in the same unit. Four: write the formula, substitute, calculate. Five: use the value of pi the question gives, or leave the answer in terms of pi if asked. Six: round as instructed, usually to two decimal places, and state the unit — units for perimeter, square units for area. Seven: check that the size is sensible.

A typical examination item gives a labelled diagram and asks for both perimeter and area. Two calculations, two units.

Another gives the area and one dimension and asks for the other. Substitute what is known and solve: for a rectangle, divide; for a triangle, double the area then divide by the base; for a square, take the square root.

Another gives a circle's circumference or area and asks for the radius. Divide by 2 pi, or divide by pi and take the square root.

Another asks for the perimeter of a semicircle or quarter circle. Take the fraction of the circumference and add the straight edges.

Another asks for a cost: so many rand per metre of fencing or per square metre of carpet. Decide which measure the price uses before multiplying.

Errors to avoid. Using a slanting side as the height. Forgetting the half for a triangle. Using the diameter in pi r squared. Squaring after multiplying by pi instead of before. Forgetting the straight edge of a semicircle. Mixing centimetres and metres. Writing square units for a perimeter.
