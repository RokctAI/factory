# Part 1 — Expert

This session explores the relationships between radius, diameter and circumference, and between radius and area, and the meaning of pi. The diameter is twice the radius. For every circle, the circumference divided by the diameter is the same number, called pi, about 3,14159; so the circumference is pi times the diameter, or 2 pi times the radius. Pi is irrational: its decimal expansion never ends and never repeats, and 3,14 and twenty-two sevenths are only approximations. Cutting a circle into equal sectors and rearranging them gives a shape close to a parallelogram with base half the circumference and height the radius, so the area is pi times the radius squared. The anchor measurements come from round objects around a kitchen and a circle of radius 5 centimetres.

## Subtopic: Radius, Diameter and Circumference

A circle is the set of all points at the same distance from a fixed point, the centre. That distance is the radius. A straight line through the centre from one side of the circle to the other is a diameter, and it is made of two radii end to end, so the diameter is twice the radius: d equals 2 r, and r equals d divided by 2. A circle of radius 5 centimetres has diameter 10 centimetres; a circle of diameter 26 millimetres, the size of a five-rand coin, has radius 13 millimetres.

The circumference is the distance around the circle — its perimeter. It is clearly longer than the diameter: going around is further than going across. How much longer?

Look at a square drawn around a circle, touching it on all four sides. The square's side equals the diameter, so its perimeter is 4 times the diameter, and the circle's circumference, which stays inside the square, is less than that. Now look at a regular hexagon drawn inside the circle, its six corners on the circle. Each side of the hexagon equals the radius, because the hexagon splits into six equilateral triangles at the centre, so the hexagon's perimeter is 6 radii, which is 3 diameters. The circumference, which bends outside the hexagon, is more than that.

So the circumference is more than 3 diameters and less than 4. It is, in fact, a little more than 3: about 3,14 diameters, for every circle, whatever its size.

That ratio — circumference divided by diameter — is the same for a coin, a dinner plate, a car tyre and a stadium track, because all circles are similar: each is an enlargement of every other, and an enlargement multiplies the circumference and the diameter by the same factor, so their ratio does not change. The constant ratio is given a name, the Greek letter pi.

Therefore the circumference is pi times the diameter, and since the diameter is 2 r, it is also 2 times pi times r. For the circle of radius 5 centimetres, the circumference is 10 pi, about 31,42 centimetres.

The relationships chain together. From the radius, double to get the diameter, then multiply by pi to get the circumference. From the circumference, divide by pi to get the diameter, then halve to get the radius. A circle whose circumference is 31,4 centimetres, taking pi as 3,14, has diameter 31,4 divided by 3,14, which is 10 centimetres, and radius 5 centimetres. Questions on radius, diameter and circumference follow now.

## Subtopic: The Meaning of Pi

Pi is the number of diameters that fit around the circumference of any circle. Its value begins 3,14159265 and continues for ever.

It can be measured. Wrap a string around a tin, mark it, straighten it and measure: say 23,2 centimetres around a tin 7,4 centimetres across. Divide: about 3,135. A dinner plate 25 centimetres across has a rim of about 78,5 centimetres: 3,14. Measurements are never perfect, so each result is a little off, but they cluster around 3,14, and the average of many careful measurements gets closer.

It cannot be written exactly as a fraction. Pi is irrational, like the square root of 2 from the Pythagoras lessons: its decimal never ends and never falls into a repeating pattern. So every decimal or fraction used for pi is an approximation. 3,14 is accurate to two decimal places. Twenty-two sevenths, which is 3,142857 repeating, is slightly too large, but accurate to two decimal places as well, and convenient when a radius is a multiple of 7. The fraction 355 over 113 is accurate to six decimal places. Calculators store pi to about ten digits, more than enough for any practical measurement.

Mathematicians have known pi's approximate value for thousands of years. Ancient Egyptian and Babylonian scribes used values close to 3,16 and 3,125. Archimedes, around 250 BC, trapped pi between the perimeters of polygons with 96 sides drawn inside and outside a circle, showing it lies between 3 and ten seventy-firsts and 3 and one seventh. The hexagon argument from the previous section is the first step of his method.

When should you use which? If the question says use 3,14, use it. If it says use the pi key, use the calculator. If it asks for an exact answer, or an answer in terms of pi, keep the symbol: the circumference of a circle of radius 5 is exactly 10 pi, and 31,42 is only an approximation of it.

Pi is a number, not a unit and not a variable. It does not change from question to question, and it is not the circumference. Writing pi equals 3 is a common shortcut in rough estimates, and gives answers about 5 percent too small.

A useful sense of scale: pi is a little more than 3, so a circle's circumference is a little more than three times its diameter. A round table 1 metre across needs a little more than 3 metres of edging, about 3,14 metres. Questions on the meaning of pi follow now.

## Subtopic: Why the Area Is Pi Times the Radius Squared

The area formula can be seen by cutting and rearranging, without any advanced mathematics.

Cut a circle of radius r into 16 equal slices, like a pizza. Lay them in a row, alternating point up and point down, so that they fit together side by side. The result looks roughly like a parallelogram with wavy top and bottom edges. Its height is the length of each slice from the point to the crust: the radius r. Its base is made of the crusts along the bottom: half of the slices point up, so their crusts form the bottom edge, and that is half of the circumference, which is half of 2 pi r, which is pi r.

Cut the circle into more and thinner slices — 32, 64, a thousand — and the wavy edges become flatter and the shape becomes closer and closer to a rectangle with height r and base pi r. The area of that rectangle is pi r times r, which is pi r squared. The area of the slices has not changed in the rearrangement, so the area of the circle is pi r squared.

The argument also shows how the two formulae are connected: the area uses the circumference. Half the circumference times the radius gives the area. For radius 5: half of 10 pi is 5 pi, times 5 is 25 pi, about 78,54 square centimetres.

A second way to see the size of the area. Draw a square on the radius: its area is r squared. The circle fits inside a square of side 2 r, area 4 r squared, so the circle's area is less than 4 r squared. It is more than the area of the square drawn inside it with its corners on the circle, which is 2 r squared. Pi r squared, about 3,14 r squared, sits between them, as it must.

Use the formula with the radius, not the diameter, and square before multiplying by pi. For radius 5: 5 squared is 25, times pi is 25 pi. For diameter 5: radius 2,5, squared 6,25, times pi is 6,25 pi, about 19,63 square centimetres.

Area grows with the square of the radius. A circle of radius 10 has area 100 pi, four times the area of a circle of radius 5, while its circumference, 20 pi, is only twice as long. This is the scale-factor rule from the enlargement lessons: circumference is a length and scales by k, area scales by k squared. Questions on the area of a circle follow now.

## Subtopic: Moving Between Radius, Circumference and Area

With the relationships in place, any one of radius, diameter, circumference and area determines the other three.

From the radius: diameter 2 r, circumference 2 pi r, area pi r squared. Radius 4: diameter 8, circumference 8 pi, about 25,13; area 16 pi, about 50,27.

From the circumference: divide by 2 pi for the radius. A circular running track with an inside edge of 400 metres would have radius 400 divided by 2 pi, about 63,66 metres. A tin with a label 25,13 centimetres long wrapped exactly around it has radius about 4 centimetres.

From the area: divide by pi, then take the square root. A circle of area 50,27 square centimetres: 50,27 divided by pi is about 16,00, whose square root is 4, so the radius is 4 centimetres. A round garden bed of area 1 000 square metres has radius the square root of 1 000 divided by pi, about the square root of 318,31, which is about 17,84 metres, and its edge is 2 pi times 17,84, about 112,09 metres.

From the area to the circumference, go through the radius: area to radius, then radius to circumference. There is no shortcut that avoids the square root.

The effect of changing the radius. Double the radius: the diameter and circumference double; the area becomes four times as large. Triple the radius: circumference three times, area nine times. Halve the radius: circumference halves, area quarters.

Comparing circles by ratio, without pi. Two circles with radii 3 and 5 have circumferences in the ratio 3 to 5 and areas in the ratio 9 to 25. One pizza 28 centimetres across and another 14 centimetres across: the larger has 4 times the area — four small pizzas fit the same topping as one large.

Work exactly and round at the end. If the radius is found from a circumference as a decimal and then used for an area, keep the full calculator value or work in terms of pi. Rounding the radius to 3,8 when it is 3,82 and then squaring makes the error grow.

A table with four columns — radius, diameter, circumference, area — and one row for each circle is a good way to organise practice and examination work: fill in the given value and work along the row in both directions. Questions on moving between measurements follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same relationships as stories about a learner who measured every round thing in her kitchen and found the same number five times, a trundle wheel built to measure a school field in metres, and a cyclist who counted wheel turns to measure a road — stories about the one number hidden in every circle.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen Investigation

For a Grade 8 investigation, a learner in Mahikeng is asked to find pi from measurements of round objects at home. She collects five: a dinner plate, a tin of beans, a plastic bucket, a five-rand coin and a coffee mug. For each one she measures the diameter with a ruler, straight across through the centre, and the circumference by wrapping a strip of paper tightly around it, marking where it overlaps, and measuring the strip flat.

Her table. Plate: diameter 25 centimetres, circumference 78,5. Tin: 7,4 and 23,2. Bucket rim: 30 and 94,5. Coin: 2,6 and 8,2. Mug: 8 and 25,1.

She divides circumference by diameter for each. Plate: 78,5 divided by 25 is 3,14. Tin: about 3,135. Bucket: 3,15. Coin: about 3,154. Mug: about 3,1375. Five different objects, sizes ranging from a coin to a bucket, and every ratio is between 3,13 and 3,16.

The average of her five ratios is about 3,143, within 0,002 of the true value. She writes in her report that the ratio does not depend on the size of the circle, which is the definition of pi.

She then thinks about her errors. The coin gives the biggest one, because a measuring error of 1 millimetre is a large fraction of a 26-millimetre diameter, but a tiny fraction of a 30-centimetre bucket. Large objects give more reliable ratios. The strip of paper also adds a little, since it has thickness and wraps slightly outside the object. Her teacher adds a comment: that is exactly the reasoning a scientist uses.

For the second part of the investigation she tests the area formula. She draws a circle of radius 5 centimetres on centimetre-squared paper and counts squares: 60 whole squares inside and 28 part squares on the edge. Counting each part square as a half, she estimates 60 plus 14, which is 74 square centimetres. The formula gives 25 pi, about 78,54. Her count is within about 6 percent; most of the part squares are more than half inside, so counting them as halves undercounts, and she notes that a finer grid would come closer.

Finally she cuts a paper circle into 16 slices and arranges them alternately into the wavy near-parallelogram, glues it into her report, and measures it: about 15,7 centimetres long and 5 centimetres tall. 15,7 is half of 10 pi; 15,7 times 5 is 78,5. The area formula, glued into her report, in her own handwriting. Questions on the kitchen investigation follow.

## Subtopic: The Trundle Wheel and the Bicycle

A school in Mthatha needs to measure distances on its sports field for athletics practice, and the Grade 8 class is asked to build a trundle wheel: a wheel on a handle that clicks once every full turn, so that the number of clicks is the distance in metres.

For one click per metre, the wheel's circumference must be exactly 1 metre. The learners work backwards: circumference is pi times diameter, so the diameter is 1 divided by pi, about 0,318 metres, or 31,8 centimetres. The radius is half that, about 15,9 centimetres. They cut a disc of plywood with a radius of 15,9 centimetres, fix it to a broom handle, and attach a strip of plastic that clicks against a nail once per turn.

They test it along a 50-metre tape measure: 50 clicks. Then along a 100-metre straight: 100 clicks, plus a little. One learner calculates the circumference of their actual wheel: 2 times pi times 0,159 is about 0,999 metres, so after 100 turns they have gone about 99,9 metres. The small error comes from rounding the radius. They accept it for training runs.

Meanwhile a learner who cycles to school wonders how far she rides each day. Her bicycle wheel has a diameter of 66 centimetres. One turn of the wheel moves the bicycle forward by the circumference: pi times 66, about 207,35 centimetres, just over 2 metres.

She clips a small magnet to a spoke and a counter to the frame. On her way to school the counter reads 1 450 turns. The distance is 1 450 times 207,35 centimetres, about 300 660 centimetres, which is about 3 007 metres — just over 3 kilometres.

Working backwards, she wants to know how many turns make 1 kilometre: 100 000 centimetres divided by 207,35, about 482 turns. A bicycle computer bought from a shop asks for the wheel size when it is set up for exactly this reason: it counts turns and multiplies by the circumference.

Her brother, who has a smaller bicycle with 50-centimetre wheels, rides beside her. For every turn of her wheel his must turn 66 divided by 50, which is 1,32 times, because the circumferences are in the same ratio as the diameters. His wheels spin faster to keep up; pi cancels out of the comparison entirely. Questions on the trundle wheel and bicycle follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The relationships. Diameter is twice the radius. Pi is the circumference divided by the diameter, the same for every circle, about 3,14159, irrational. Circumference is pi d or 2 pi r. Area is pi r squared, which is half the circumference times the radius.

The method for circle questions. One: identify what is given — radius, diameter, circumference or area — and convert to the radius first. From a diameter, halve. From a circumference, divide by 2 pi. From an area, divide by pi and take the square root. Two: from the radius, calculate whatever is asked. Three: use the value of pi specified, or the calculator's, or leave the answer in terms of pi. Four: round only at the end and state the unit.

A typical examination item asks for the circumference and area from a radius or a diameter. Two formulae, two units.

Another gives the circumference and asks for the area, or the reverse. Go through the radius.

Another asks what happens to the circumference and area when the radius is doubled or halved. Circumference by 2, area by 4; or circumference by one half, area by one quarter.

Another asks the learner to explain the meaning of pi. State that it is the ratio of the circumference to the diameter, the same for all circles, and that it is irrational, so 3,14 and twenty-two sevenths are approximations.

Another asks for an explanation of the area formula. Describe the slices rearranged into a near-rectangle with height r and base pi r.

Errors to avoid. Confusing radius and diameter. Using pi as exactly 3,14 when the question asks for an exact answer. Squaring the diameter in the area formula. Taking pi r and squaring it all. Forgetting to take the square root when working back from an area. Rounding the radius before using it again.
