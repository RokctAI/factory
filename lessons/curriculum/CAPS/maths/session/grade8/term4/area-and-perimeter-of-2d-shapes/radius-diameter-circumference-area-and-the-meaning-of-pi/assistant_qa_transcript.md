### subtopic_2 — The Meaning of Pi
**Assistant:** If pi never ends, how can anyone know so many of its digits?

**Tutor:** Because there are methods that produce the digits one after another with certainty, even though the list never ends. Archimedes used polygons: a polygon drawn inside a circle has a perimeter less than the circumference, one drawn outside has a perimeter more, and as the number of sides increases the two perimeters squeeze closer together, trapping pi between them. With 96 sides he showed that pi lies between 3 and ten seventy-firsts and 3 and one seventh, which fixes the first two decimal places, 3,14. Later mathematicians found formulae — long sums of fractions that add up to pi — which can be computed to any accuracy, and modern computers have used them to calculate trillions of digits. None of this contradicts irrationality: irrational means that the digits never stop and never settle into a repeating block, not that they cannot be calculated. Each digit is completely determined; we simply cannot write them all down, because there is no last one. For practical purposes very few digits are needed: 3,14159 is enough for engineering, and about forty digits would measure the visible universe to the width of an atom.

### subtopic_3 — Why the Area Is Pi Times the Radius Squared
**Assistant:** In the slices argument, the shape is wavy, not a real rectangle. Is the formula only approximate then?

**Tutor:** No, the formula is exact; it is the picture that is approximate. With 16 slices the rearranged shape has bumpy top and bottom edges and slanting ends, so it is only roughly a rectangle. With 64 slices the bumps are smaller and the ends nearly vertical. With 1 000 slices you could not see the difference from a rectangle. The key point is that the area of the slices never changes, whatever their number, because they are the same pieces of the same circle, while the shape they make gets as close to a rectangle of height r and base pi r as you like. Since the circle's area is fixed and the rectangle's area is pi r squared, and the two can be made to differ by less than any amount you name, they must be equal. This idea of getting closer and closer without end is the basis of calculus, which you will meet in Grade 12 and beyond, and it is how mathematicians prove that the formula is exactly right. The kitchen investigation's paper version measured about 15,7 by 5 centimetres, giving 78,5, very close to the exact 25 pi of about 78,54.

### subtopic_4 — Moving Between Radius, Circumference and Area
**Assistant:** Why must I take a square root to find the radius from the area, but not from the circumference?

**Tutor:** Because the circumference depends on the radius directly, while the area depends on the radius squared. The circumference formula 2 pi r multiplies the radius by a number, so undoing it means dividing by that number: r is the circumference divided by 2 pi. The area formula pi r squared multiplies the radius by itself and then by pi, so undoing it takes two steps in reverse order: first divide by pi to get r squared, then take the square root to get r. Forgetting the square root is a common error and gives a hugely wrong answer: for an area of 50,27 square centimetres, dividing by pi gives about 16, and the radius is 4, not 16. A quick sense check catches it: a radius of 16 would give an area of about 804, far larger than 50. The difference also explains the scaling behaviour. Because area involves the radius squared, doubling the radius quadruples the area, and conversely, to double the area you multiply the radius by only the square root of 2, about 1,41.

### subtopic_6 — The Trundle Wheel and the Bicycle
**Assistant:** Why did the trundle wheel need a diameter of 31,8 centimetres rather than a round number?

**Tutor:** Because the requirement was a round number for the circumference, exactly 1 metre, and the circumference and the diameter cannot both be round numbers: they differ by the factor pi, which is irrational. Working backwards, the diameter must be 1 metre divided by pi, about 0,3183 metres, and no amount of careful cutting makes that a whole number of centimetres. The class rounded the radius to 15,9 centimetres, which gives a circumference of about 0,999 metres, an error of about 1 millimetre per turn, or 10 centimetres over 100 metres. For training runs that is acceptable; for official measurements, commercial trundle wheels are made more precisely. The same thing appears in reverse with the bicycle: the manufacturer chose a round diameter of 66 centimetres, and so the distance per turn, about 207,35 centimetres, is not round. Whenever you design something circular, you choose which measurement should be the neat one, and pi makes the other one awkward. That is also why answers in terms of pi are useful: 1 divided by pi metres is exact, while 31,8 centimetres is not.
