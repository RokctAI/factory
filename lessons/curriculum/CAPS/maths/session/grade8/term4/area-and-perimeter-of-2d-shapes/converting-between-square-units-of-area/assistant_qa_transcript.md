### subtopic_1 — Why Area Factors Are Squared
**Assistant:** Why do we square the length factor and not just multiply by it?

**Tutor:** Because an area has two dimensions, and the unit change applies to both of them. Picture 1 square metre as a square with sides of 1 metre. In centimetres, each side is 100 centimetres. The number of square centimetres inside is the number of rows times the number of columns: 100 rows of 100, which is 10 000. Multiplying by 100 only once would count a single row of 100 square centimetres, a strip 1 centimetre wide across the square, and ignore the other 99 rows. The same logic applies to any rectangle: an area of 4,2 by 3,6 metres becomes 420 by 360 centimetres, and the area 15,12 square metres becomes 151 200 square centimetres, because both the length and the breadth were multiplied by 100. You can also see it in the units themselves: a square metre is metre times metre, and replacing each metre with 100 centimetres gives 100 centimetres times 100 centimetres, which is 10 000 square centimetres. When we reach volume, there are three dimensions, so the length factor is used three times: a cubic metre is 100 times 100 times 100, which is 1 000 000 cubic centimetres.

### subtopic_2 — Converting Down and Converting Up
**Assistant:** How do I remember whether to multiply or divide?

**Tutor:** Think about the size of the unit and the size of the number. The same area measured with a smaller unit needs more of them, so the number gets bigger: multiply. Measured with a larger unit, it needs fewer, so the number gets smaller: divide. A floor of 15,12 square metres is a lot of square centimetres, 151 200, because each square centimetre is tiny; it is a very small number of square kilometres, 0,00001512, because a square kilometre is huge. If you are ever unsure, convert a simple case you can picture: 1 square metre, which you know is 10 000 square centimetres. Since going from square metres to square centimetres turned 1 into 10 000, that direction multiplies. Then apply the same operation to your actual number. A final check after every conversion is to ask whether the answer is bigger or smaller than the starting number and whether that makes sense for the change of unit. Converting 4,2 square metres and getting 0,00042 square centimetres fails this check at once, because square centimetres are smaller units and the number should have grown.

### subtopic_3 — Convert the Lengths First
**Assistant:** Is it better to convert the lengths first or to convert the area at the end?

**Tutor:** Both give the same answer, so choose whichever is less likely to go wrong in the particular problem. Converting the lengths first uses only the familiar length factors, 10, 100 and 1 000, which most people remember reliably; it is the safest route when the dimensions are given, and especially when they are in different units, such as a tile in centimetres and a floor in metres. Converting the area at the end is necessary when you are given only an area, such as a farm of 450 hectares or a screen of 105 square centimetres, with no dimensions to convert. It is also convenient when the area has already been calculated and only the unit needs changing. For a circle, converting the radius first and then calculating works perfectly, as does calculating the area and then multiplying by the area factor; what does not work is converting the area with a length factor. A good habit is to do it both ways on practice questions, because the agreement builds confidence in the area factors, and then in examinations use whichever is quicker.

### subtopic_6 — Solar Panels on the Roof
**Assistant:** If the roof area divided by the panel area gives about 20, why can only 16 panels fit?

**Tutor:** Because dividing areas assumes the panels could be cut up and rearranged to fill every gap, like pouring paint. Real panels are rigid rectangles of fixed size, so they must be laid out in rows and columns, and the leftover strips at the edges are wasted. Along the 8-metre edge, four panels take up about 6,9 metres, and the remaining 1,1 metres is too short for another panel. Across the 5-metre edge, four rows take about 4,6 metres, and the remaining 0,4 metres is too short for another row. Those unused strips add up to about 9 square metres of roof, which is why 16 panels cover only about 31,2 of the 40 square metres. Turning the panels the other way gives only 14, so the best layout has to be found by trying both orientations. The area division is still useful as an upper limit: you can never fit more than about 20, so if a layout seems to give more, something is wrong. Tiles behave differently because they can be cut to fit the edges, which is why the area method works for tiling with an allowance for waste, but not for panels, solar or otherwise.
