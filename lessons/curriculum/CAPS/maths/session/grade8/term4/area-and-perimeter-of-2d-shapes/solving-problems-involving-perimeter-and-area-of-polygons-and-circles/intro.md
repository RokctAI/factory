Today the formulae become tools for real jobs: paths around gardens, running tracks with curved ends, shaded regions between a square and a circle, floors to tile and walls to edge. A problem rarely names the formula; it describes a situation, and the work is to sketch it, see which shapes are hiding in it, decide whether each part is a perimeter or an area, and then turn the answer into what was actually asked — a cost, a number of tiles, a number of boxes. By the end you will plan and solve multi-step problems involving polygons and circles, work with and without a calculator, round up correctly when buying whole items, and present a solution a reader can follow.
