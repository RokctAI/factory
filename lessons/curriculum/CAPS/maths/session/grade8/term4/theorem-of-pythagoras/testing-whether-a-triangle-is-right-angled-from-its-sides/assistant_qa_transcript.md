### subtopic_1 — The Converse of the Theorem
**Assistant:** Why do we need a separate converse? Is it not obvious that the theorem works both ways?

**Tutor:** It is not automatic, because a statement and its converse are logically different and one can be true while the other is false. All squares have four right angles is true; all shapes with four right angles are squares is false, since rectangles qualify. So the fact that right-angled triangles satisfy the equation does not by itself guarantee that triangles satisfying the equation are right-angled; that needs its own argument. The argument is short: given sides a, b, c with a squared plus b squared equal to c squared, build a right-angled triangle with legs a and b; by the theorem its hypotenuse is c; so the built triangle and the given one have the same three sides and are congruent, and the given one therefore has the right angle. In Grade 8 the converse is accepted as a fact, but you should name it correctly when you use it — converse of the Theorem of Pythagoras — because the direction of reasoning is from sides to angle, which is the opposite of the theorem's. Examiners look for the word converse in exactly these problems.

### subtopic_2 — Applying the Test to Decimals, Fractions and Multiples
**Assistant:** If the numbers are messy decimals, how exact does the comparison have to be?

**Tutor:** Exact, in principle; the triangle is right-angled only if the two quantities are precisely equal. In practice there are two situations. In a textbook or examination problem the numbers are chosen so that the comparison is exact when calculated without rounding: 0,81 plus 1,44 is exactly 2,25, which is exactly 1,5 squared, and a calculator or careful hand arithmetic shows it. Do not round intermediate values, and do not accept 2,24 as equal to 2,25; a near miss means not right-angled. In a measured situation — the builder's tape, the frame's diagonal — the numbers carry measurement error, so a difference smaller than the measurement precision cannot be distinguished from equality, and the honest conclusion is that the corner is square to within the accuracy of the tape. The builder's 5,06 against 5,00 is a real difference, six centimetres over five metres, well beyond tape error, so she moves the peg; a reading of 5,002 would be square for any practical purpose. Say which situation you are in: exact mathematics or measurement with stated precision.

### subtopic_3 — Reading the Inequality: Acute or Obtuse
**Assistant:** Why does a longer third side mean a wider angle?

**Tutor:** Picture two rods of fixed lengths a and b hinged at one end, with the free ends joined by an elastic third side. Close the hinge to a small angle and the free ends come close together, so the third side is short. Open the hinge wider and the free ends separate, so the third side lengthens, reaching its maximum of a plus b when the hinge is opened flat to 180 degrees. The third side's length rises steadily as the angle between a and b opens from 0 to 180. At one particular angle, 90 degrees, the third side's square is exactly a squared plus b squared by the theorem. Below 90 the third side is shorter than that, so its square is less than the sum; above 90 it is longer, so its square is more. That is why the inequality reads the angle: longest squared below the sum, acute; above, obtuse. Grade 11 makes this exact with the cosine rule, which adds a term depending on the angle; for now the hinge picture is the reason.

### subtopic_6 — The Frame That Was Not Square and the Pitch That Was
**Assistant:** The frame's two diagonals were 50,8 and 49,2. Why do they tell the same story from different sides?

**Tutor:** Because a frame with four equal-opposite sides is a parallelogram, and a parallelogram's two diagonals span different pairs of corners: one spans the two obtuse corners and the other the two acute ones. In a true rectangle all four corners are 90 and both diagonals are the same, 50 for a 30 by 40 frame. When the frame is pushed out of true, two opposite corners open past 90 and the other two close below 90 — adjacent angles still add to 180, so what one gains the next loses. The diagonal across an obtuse corner is longer than 50 and its square exceeds 2 500; the diagonal across an acute corner is shorter and its square falls below 2 500. So measuring both diagonals is a double check: they should be equal for a rectangle, and if they differ, the longer one tells you which corners are too wide. The groundsman used the same double check on the pitch. The quickest test of any supposed rectangle, from a frame to a foundation, is to compare the two diagonals; if they match, and one of them also satisfies the converse with two adjacent sides, the figure is a rectangle.
