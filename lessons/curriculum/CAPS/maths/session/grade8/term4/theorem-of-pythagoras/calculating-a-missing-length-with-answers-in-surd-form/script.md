# Part 1 — Expert

This session calculates a missing length in a right-angled triangle, leaving irrational answers in surd form. The hypotenuse is found from the two legs by adding their squares and taking the square root; a leg is found from the hypotenuse and the other leg by subtracting squares. Answers that are not perfect squares are written as surds, simplified by extracting the largest square factor, and converted to decimals only when asked. The method is applied to diagonals of squares and rectangles, heights of isosceles and equilateral triangles found by dropping a perpendicular, and contextual problems with ladders and distances. The anchor problems are legs 2 and 3, hypotenuse 10 with leg 6, hypotenuse 7 with leg 4, a square of side 80 and an equilateral triangle of side 80.

## Subtopic: Finding a Hypotenuse and Finding a Leg

Two kinds of missing length appear in a right-angled triangle, and they need opposite operations.

Finding the hypotenuse from two legs: add the squares. Legs 6 and 8: c squared equals 36 plus 64, which is 100; c equals 10. Legs 2 and 3: c squared equals 4 plus 9, which is 13; c equals the square root of 13, which is not a whole number and is left as the square root of 13 — about 3,61 if a decimal is wanted.

Finding a leg from the hypotenuse and the other leg: subtract the squares. The theorem says a squared plus b squared equals c squared, so a squared equals c squared minus b squared — the hypotenuse squared minus the known leg squared. Hypotenuse 10, leg 6: a squared equals 100 minus 36, which is 64; a equals 8. Hypotenuse 13, leg 5: a squared equals 169 minus 25, which is 144; a equals 12. Hypotenuse 7, leg 4: a squared equals 49 minus 16, which is 33; a equals the square root of 33, about 5,74.

The decision rule: is the missing side the hypotenuse — opposite the right angle — or a leg. Hypotenuse missing: add. Leg missing: subtract, and always subtract the leg's square from the hypotenuse's square, never the other way round, because the hypotenuse is the longest side and its square is the largest. A negative result under the square root means the subtraction was done backwards or the wrong side was taken as hypotenuse.

Write three lines whichever case it is. Line one: the theorem arranged for the unknown. Line two: substitution and arithmetic down to a single number under the square. Line three: the square root with units, exact or rounded as asked.

Hypotenuse 25, leg 7: a squared equals 625 minus 49, which is 576; a equals 24. Hypotenuse 5, leg 2: a squared equals 25 minus 4, which is 21; a equals the square root of 21. Legs 1 and 1: c equals the square root of 2. Legs 5 and 5: c squared equals 50; c equals the square root of 50, which the next section simplifies.

Check every answer for sense: the hypotenuse must be longer than either leg and shorter than their sum; a leg must be shorter than the hypotenuse. An answer that breaks these was computed with the wrong operation. Questions on hypotenuse and leg follow now.

## Subtopic: Surds and Simplifying Them

A surd is a square root that cannot be written as a whole number or a fraction: the square root of 2, the square root of 13, the square root of 33. Its decimal never ends and never repeats, so no decimal is exact; the surd itself is the exact value. The square root of 2 is about 1,414, but 1,414 squared is 1,999396, not 2. Leaving an answer in surd form means writing the square root sign and the number, and that is what the syllabus asks for unless a decimal is requested.

Some surds simplify. If the number under the root has a square factor, that factor's root comes outside. The square root of 50: 50 is 25 times 2, and 25 is a square, so the square root of 50 equals the square root of 25 times the square root of 2, which is 5 times the square root of 2 — written five root two. Check: 5 squared times 2 is 25 times 2, which is 50.

The square root of 72: 72 is 36 times 2, so six root two. The square root of 48: 48 is 16 times 3, so four root three. The square root of 20: 4 times 5, so two root five. The square root of 200: 100 times 2, ten root two. The square root of 12: 4 times 3, two root three.

Always take out the largest square factor. The square root of 72 could be written as the square root of 9 times 8, giving three root eight — but 8 still has the square factor 4, so three root eight becomes three times two root two, six root two. Going straight to 36 times 2 is quicker. The square factors to look for are 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144.

Some surds do not simplify, because the number under the root has no square factor other than 1: the square root of 13, of 33, of 21, of 2, of 3, of 5, of 6, of 7, of 10, of 11, of 14, of 15. Leave them as they are.

A surd times a whole number is written with the number in front: five root two, not root two times five. A surd is never left with a perfect square inside — the square root of 25 is 5, not a surd — and never with a decimal inside if it can be avoided.

Decimals when asked: the square root of 50 is about 7,07; five root two is 5 times 1,414, also about 7,07. State the rounding: to two decimal places. Questions on surds follow.

## Subtopic: Diagonals, Heights and Dropped Perpendiculars

Many figures hide a right-angled triangle, and finding it is the skill.

The diagonal of a rectangle is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose legs are the two sides. Rectangle 3 by 5: diagonal squared equals 9 plus 25, which is 34; diagonal equals the square root of 34. Rectangle 6 by 8: diagonal 10.

The diagonal of a square of side s is the hypotenuse of a triangle with legs s and s: diagonal squared equals 2 times s squared, so the diagonal is s root two. Side 80: the diagonal is 80 root two, about 113,1. Side 1: root two. Every square's diagonal is root two times its side — the glazier's first missing length.

The height of an isosceles triangle is found by dropping a perpendicular from the apex to the base. By symmetry it meets the base at its midpoint, so the triangle splits into two congruent right-angled triangles, each with the leg as hypotenuse, half the base as one leg, and the height as the other. Isosceles triangle with legs 13 and base 10: half the base is 5; height squared equals 169 minus 25, which is 144; height 12.

The height of an equilateral triangle is the same construction with legs equal to the side. Side 80: half the base is 40; height squared equals 6 400 minus 1 600, which is 4 800; height equals the square root of 4 800, which is the square root of 1 600 times 3, that is 40 root three, about 69,3. The glazier's second missing length. In general an equilateral triangle of side s has height s over 2 times root three.

The distance between two points on a grid is a hypotenuse: from the point 1 semicolon 2 to the point 4 semicolon 6, the horizontal change is 3 and the vertical change is 4, so the distance is 5. From the origin to the point 2 semicolon 3, the distance is the square root of 13.

A kite or rhombus has diagonals that cross at right angles, so each quarter is a right-angled triangle with half-diagonals as legs and a side as hypotenuse. A rhombus with diagonals 12 and 16 has half-diagonals 6 and 8 and side 10.

The habit: look for the right angle, or create one with a perpendicular; name the hypotenuse; decide add or subtract; three lines. Questions on diagonals and heights follow.

## Subtopic: Problems in Context and Choosing the Form of the Answer

Real problems give the right-angled triangle in words, and the first step is to draw it. A ladder 5 metres long leans against a wall with its foot 1,5 metres from the base of the wall. How high up the wall does it reach. The ladder is the hypotenuse; the ground distance is a leg; the height is the other leg. Height squared equals 25 minus 2,25, which is 22,75; height equals the square root of 22,75, about 4,77 metres. A decimal is appropriate here, because a wall height in surd form helps nobody; round to two decimal places and give units.

A ship sails 12 kilometres east and then 9 kilometres north. How far is it from its start. The two legs are 12 and 9, meeting at a right angle; the distance is the hypotenuse: 144 plus 81 is 225, square root 15 kilometres.

A rectangular field is 60 metres by 80 metres. A learner walks diagonally across it instead of along two sides. How much shorter is the diagonal. Diagonal squared equals 3 600 plus 6 400, which is 10 000; diagonal 100 metres. Along two sides is 140 metres; the saving is 40 metres.

A television screen is described by its diagonal, 100 centimetres, and is 80 centimetres wide. Its height squared equals 10 000 minus 6 400, which is 3 600; height 60 centimetres — a 3, 4, 5 triangle scaled by 20.

A tent pole 2 metres tall is held by a rope pegged 1 metre from its base. The rope's length squared equals 4 plus 1, which is 5; the rope is root five metres, about 2,24. For buying rope, the decimal; for an examination answer, root five unless told otherwise.

Choosing the form. If the question says leave your answer in surd form or give the exact answer, write the simplified surd. If it says correct to one decimal place or to the nearest centimetre, round. If it says nothing, the exact surd is safest, with the decimal alongside. In a context where something must be cut, bought or built, the decimal is the usable answer, but the surd is still the mathematics.

Units: the squares are in square units during the working; the square root returns to the original unit. Write it. Questions on context follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same calculations as stories about the glazier's two missing lengths from the house-shaped window, a ladder that was long enough only on paper, and a learner who refused to round the square root of two — stories about lengths you can calculate exactly but never measure exactly.

## Subtopic: The Glazier's Two Lengths

The house-shaped window from Term 3 — a square of side 80 centimetres with an equilateral triangle on top — went to the glazier with two lengths marked to follow: the diagonal bar across the square and the height of the triangle. The learner who left them now has the theorem.

The diagonal of the square. It cuts the square into two right-angled triangles with legs 80 and 80. Diagonal squared equals 6 400 plus 6 400, which is 12 800. Diagonal equals the square root of 12 800. Simplify: 12 800 is 6 400 times 2, and 6 400 is 80 squared, so the diagonal is 80 root two. For the glazier: 80 times 1,4142, which is about 113,1 centimetres. He cuts the bar at 113,1 and it fits with a millimetre to spare for the glazing rubber.

The height of the triangle. Drop a perpendicular from the peak to the middle of the top side of the square; it splits the equilateral triangle into two right-angled triangles with hypotenuse 80 and base 40. Height squared equals 6 400 minus 1 600, which is 4 800. Height equals the square root of 4 800, which is 1 600 times 3 under the root, so 40 root three. For the glazier: 40 times 1,7321, about 69,3 centimetres. The total height of the window is the square's 80 plus the triangle's 69,3, about 149,3 centimetres — which is why the frame did not fit the 145-centimetre opening the carpenter had first measured, and the opening had to be enlarged.

The glazier also wants the length of the two sloping roof edges as glass: 80 each, no theorem needed. But he wants to know the length of a glazing bar from the peak straight down to a bottom corner of the square, for a decorative design. That is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose legs are the full height, 80 plus 40 root three, and half the width, 40. Height squared equals 40 squared plus the quantity 80 plus 40 root three squared — awkward in surd form, so the learner uses decimals: 149,28 squared is about 22 285; plus 1 600 is 23 885; square root about 154,5 centimetres. The decorative bar is cut at 154,5. The learner notes that this is a case where the exact surd is messy and a decimal to one place is the sensible answer.

The glazier's sheet is finished: 80 root two for the diagonal, 40 root three for the height, both with decimals; 154,5 for the long bar. The learner writes the exact forms first and the decimals second, because the exact forms are the ones that will still be right if anyone ever recuts the window at a different size — scale the side, and the surds scale with it. Questions on the glazier follow.

## Subtopic: The Ladder That Was Long Enough on Paper

A painter needs to reach a gutter 4,5 metres above the ground. He has a 5-metre ladder and reasons that 5 is more than 4,5, so the ladder is long enough. His assistant, who is in Grade 8, points out that a ladder standing straight up against a wall is a ladder about to fall, and that the safety guide says the foot must be one quarter of the ladder's length from the wall — 1,25 metres for a 5-metre ladder.

She draws the triangle: hypotenuse 5, ground leg 1,25, height unknown. Height squared equals 25 minus 1,5625, which is 23,4375. Height equals the square root of 23,4375, about 4,84 metres. The ladder reaches 4,84, the gutter is at 4,5, and the painter needs about half a metre above the point he is working on to hold the ladder safely. So 4,84 is just enough, with 34 centimetres to spare rather than the 50 he would like.

The painter suggests pulling the foot in to 1 metre. She recalculates: 25 minus 1, which is 24; square root about 4,90. Six centimetres gained, and the ladder is now steeper than the guide allows. Not worth it.

He suggests a 6-metre ladder from the hire shop. Foot at 1,5 metres: height squared equals 36 minus 2,25, which is 33,75; height about 5,81. Comfortable. She notes that the extra metre of ladder gave almost a full metre of height, because at this angle the ladder is nearly vertical and most of its length goes upward.

Then she tests his original reasoning. If the foot were at 3 metres — a very shallow angle — the 5-metre ladder would reach only the square root of 25 minus 9, which is 4 metres. The reach depends on the angle; the ladder's length alone decides nothing. On paper 5 is more than 4,5; on the wall the reach is a leg, and the leg is always less than the hypotenuse.

She writes him a table for the van: ladder length, foot distance at one quarter, reach. 4 metres, 1 metre, square root of 15, about 3,87. 5 metres, 1,25 metres, about 4,84. 6 metres, 1,5 metres, about 5,81. 8 metres, 2 metres, square root of 60, which is two root fifteen, about 7,75. The surds are exact; the decimals are what he reads on the job. Questions on the ladder follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for a missing length. One: draw or identify the right-angled triangle; mark the right angle; name the hypotenuse as the side opposite it. Two: decide which side is missing. Hypotenuse: add the squares of the legs. Leg: subtract the known leg's square from the hypotenuse's square. Three: write three lines — the theorem arranged for the unknown, the substitution reduced to one number, the square root with units. Four: if the number under the root is a perfect square, give the whole number; if not, give the surd, simplified by taking out the largest square factor, and a decimal only if asked, with the rounding stated. Five: in figures without a visible right angle, create one — a diagonal of a rectangle or square, a perpendicular from the apex of an isosceles or equilateral triangle to the midpoint of the base, half-diagonals of a rhombus or kite. Six: check that the hypotenuse is the longest side and that the answer fits the picture.

A typical examination item gives two legs and asks for the hypotenuse in surd form. Add, root, simplify.

Another gives the hypotenuse and a leg and asks for the other leg to one decimal place. Subtract, root, round.

Another gives a rectangle or square and asks for its diagonal. Legs are the sides.

Another gives an isosceles triangle's legs and base and asks for its height or area. Drop the perpendicular, halve the base, subtract.

Another is a word problem: ladder, ship, field, screen. Draw, label, decide, three lines, units, sensible form.

Another asks you to simplify a surd. Largest square factor out.

Errors to avoid. Adding when a leg is missing. Subtracting the wrong way round. Stopping at the squared value. Leaving the square root of 50 unsimplified when surd form is asked. Rounding too early. Halving the wrong quantity when dropping a perpendicular. Forgetting units. Writing root two as 1,41 and calling it exact.

Looking ahead: the next topic, transformation geometry, moves points and shapes on the Cartesian plane, and distances between points are hypotenuses found exactly this way. Grade 9 takes the theorem into three dimensions and coordinate geometry. Final questions follow.
