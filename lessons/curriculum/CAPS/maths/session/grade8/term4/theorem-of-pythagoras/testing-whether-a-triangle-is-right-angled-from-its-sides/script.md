# Part 1 — Expert

This session determines whether a triangle is right-angled from the lengths of its three sides, using the converse of the Theorem of Pythagoras: if the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, the triangle is right-angled, with the right angle opposite the longest side; otherwise it is not. The test is applied to integer, decimal and fractional side lengths and to multiples of known triples, and the direction of any inequality is used to classify the triangle further as acute- or obtuse-angled. The anchor triangles have sides 7, 24, 25; 5, 6, 8; 4, 5, 6; and 0,9, 1,2, 1,5.

## Subtopic: The Converse of the Theorem

The Theorem of Pythagoras runs from the angle to the sides: if a triangle is right-angled, then the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides. The converse runs the other way: if the square of the longest side of a triangle equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, then the triangle is right-angled, and the right angle is opposite the longest side.

Both are true, and the converse is what lets three lengths decide an angle. Its justification: suppose a triangle has sides a, b and c with a squared plus b squared equal to c squared. Construct a right-angled triangle with legs a and b; by the theorem its hypotenuse squared is a squared plus b squared, which is c squared, so its hypotenuse is c. The constructed triangle has sides a, b, c — the same three sides as the given triangle — and triangles with the same three sides are congruent. So the given triangle has the constructed triangle's right angle. Grade 8 records the converse as a fact and uses it; the congruence step is formal in Grade 9.

The test, step by step. Identify the longest side; call it c. Square it. Square the other two and add. Compare. Equal: right-angled, right angle opposite c. Not equal: not right-angled.

Sides 7, 24, 25. Longest 25; 25 squared is 625. The others: 49 plus 576 is 625. Equal, so right-angled, with the right angle between the 7 and the 24 — opposite the 25.

Sides 5, 6, 8. Longest 8; 64. Others: 25 plus 36 is 61. Not equal, so not right-angled.

Sides 6, 8, 10. Longest 10; 100. Others: 36 plus 64 is 100. Right-angled.

The written reason for a yes: converse of the Theorem of Pythagoras, or sides satisfy Pythagoras. For a no: the sides do not satisfy the Theorem of Pythagoras, with the two numbers shown.

The comparison must be set up with the longest side alone. Squaring a leg and comparing it with the other two squares added gives a meaningless result; 5, 12, 13 tested as 25 against 144 plus 169 would wrongly say not right-angled. Find the longest first, always. Questions on the converse follow now.

## Subtopic: Applying the Test to Decimals, Fractions and Multiples

The test is arithmetic, so it works for any numbers. Sides 0,9, 1,2 and 1,5. Longest 1,5; squared, 2,25. Others: 0,81 plus 1,44 is 2,25. Equal: right-angled. These are 3, 4, 5 each multiplied by 0,3, and multiples of triples are always right-angled.

Sides 2,5, 6 and 6,5. Longest 6,5; squared, 42,25. Others: 6,25 plus 36 is 42,25. Right-angled; these are half of 5, 12, 13.

Sides 1, 1 and the square root of 2. Longest is the square root of 2, whose square is 2. Others: 1 plus 1 is 2. Right-angled — the half-square. When a side is given as a surd, squaring it is easy: the square of the square root of 2 is 2, of the square root of 5 is 5. Sides 1, 2 and the square root of 5: 5 against 1 plus 4, right-angled. Sides 2, 3 and the square root of 13: 13 against 4 plus 9, right-angled.

Fractions. Sides three halves, 2 and five halves. Longest five halves; squared, twenty-five quarters. Others: nine quarters plus 4, which is nine quarters plus sixteen quarters, twenty-five quarters. Right-angled — again 3, 4, 5 halved.

Spotting a multiple saves work. Sides 15, 36, 39 are 3 times 5, 12, 13: right-angled. Sides 21, 72, 75 are 3 times 7, 24, 25: right-angled. Sides 12, 16, 20 are 4 times 3, 4, 5: right-angled. If the ratio of the sides matches a known triple, the triangle is right-angled; if unsure, square and check anyway.

Large numbers. Sides 20, 21, 29: 841 against 400 plus 441, which is 841. Right-angled, and 20, 21, 29 is a triple in its own right, not a multiple of a smaller one. Sides 9, 40, 41: 1 681 against 81 plus 1 600, which is 1 681. Right-angled.

Near misses. Sides 4, 7, 8: 64 against 16 plus 49, which is 65. Close, but not equal, so not right-angled; the test demands exact equality, and 65 is not 64. A calculator makes the comparison exact; in an examination without one, square carefully and compare the exact values. Questions on decimals, fractions and multiples follow.

## Subtopic: Reading the Inequality: Acute or Obtuse

When the test fails, the direction of the failure says more. Compare the square of the longest side with the sum of the other two squares. If the longest side's square is smaller than the sum, the angle opposite it is less than 90 — the triangle is acute-angled. If the longest side's square is larger than the sum, the angle opposite is more than 90 — obtuse-angled. Equality is the right angle in between.

Why. Fix two sides a and b with an angle between them, and imagine opening and closing that angle. At 90 degrees the third side c satisfies c squared equals a squared plus b squared. Close the angle below 90 and the third side shortens, so c squared drops below a squared plus b squared. Open it past 90 and the third side lengthens, so c squared rises above. The third side's square tracks the angle opposite it, and the sum a squared plus b squared is the dividing line.

Sides 4, 5, 6. Longest 6; 36. Others: 16 plus 25 is 41. 36 is less than 41, so the angle opposite the 6 is acute, and since it is the largest angle, the triangle is acute-angled.

Sides 5, 6, 8. Longest 8; 64. Others: 61. 64 is greater than 61, so the angle opposite the 8 is obtuse: obtuse-angled.

Sides 4, 7, 8. 64 against 65: 64 is less, so acute-angled, but only just — the angle opposite the 8 is about 89 degrees.

Sides 3, 4, 6. 36 against 9 plus 16, which is 25. 36 is greater than 25: obtuse-angled, and clearly so — the angle opposite the 6 is about 117 degrees.

The three cases together give a complete classification by angles from the sides alone. Longest squared less than the sum: acute. Equal: right. Greater: obtuse. The Grade 8 syllabus asks for right-angled or not; the two unequal cases are a useful extension and a check on sense — a triangle with sides 3, 4, 6 drawn to scale visibly has a wide angle, and the test agrees.

Before any of this, check that the three lengths make a triangle at all: the two shorter sides must add to more than the longest. Sides 3, 4, 8 fail — 7 is less than 8 — and no triangle exists, so no classification applies. Questions on the inequality follow.

## Subtopic: Locating the Right Angle and Writing the Conclusion

A yes from the test is only half the answer. The right angle must be placed: it is opposite the longest side, which means it is at the vertex where the two shorter sides meet. In a triangle with sides 7, 24, 25, the right angle is between the sides 7 and 24. In a triangle PQR with PQ equal to 9, QR equal to 40 and PR equal to 41, the longest side is PR, so the right angle is at Q, the vertex not on PR.

Writing the full conclusion. For PQR: PR squared equals 1 681; PQ squared plus QR squared equals 81 plus 1 600, which is 1 681; these are equal, so triangle PQR is right-angled at Q, by the converse of the Theorem of Pythagoras. Four lines: the longest squared, the other two squared and added, the comparison, the conclusion with the vertex and the reason.

For a no: triangle with sides 5, 6, 8; 8 squared equals 64; 5 squared plus 6 squared equals 61; 64 is not 61, so the triangle is not right-angled. Adding, if asked, that 64 exceeds 61 so it is obtuse-angled, with the obtuse angle opposite the 8.

In a labelled diagram, name the right angle by its vertex — right-angled at Q — or by the three letters, angle PQR equals 90 degrees. Do not say the right angle is at the longest side; the longest side is opposite the right angle.

Problems sometimes hide the test. A ladder of 6,5 metres leans with its foot 2,5 metres from a wall and its top 6 metres up: is the wall vertical to the ground. Sides 2,5, 6, 6,5; 42,25 against 6,25 plus 36, which is 42,25; equal, so the angle between ground and wall is 90. A triangular garden bed has sides 8, 15, 17 metres; 289 against 64 plus 225, which is 289; the corner between the 8 and the 15 is square. A roof truss has members 3, 5 and 6 metres; 36 against 9 plus 25, which is 34; not right-angled, and 36 exceeds 34 so the angle opposite the 6 is obtuse.

A quadrilateral can be tested corner by corner if a diagonal is known. A plot with sides 30 and 40 metres meeting at a corner and a diagonal of 50 across that corner: 2 500 against 900 plus 1 600, which is 2 500, so that corner is square. The opposite corner needs the other two sides and the same diagonal.

The conclusion is always a sentence with a vertex and a reason, never just the word yes. Questions on locating and writing follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same test as stories about a builder who checks every corner of a foundation with a tape measure and three numbers, a learner who exposed a picture frame that was not square, and a soccer pitch marked out with a 3-4-5 triangle before the lines were painted — stories about proving a right angle without ever measuring an angle.

## Subtopic: The Builder's Three Numbers

A builder marks out the foundation of a rectangular room, 4 metres by 3, with pegs and string. Before any concrete is poured she must be certain that every corner is a right angle, because a foundation that is a parallelogram instead of a rectangle makes every wall, floor and roof panel after it a struggle. She owns a large square, but a square that fits a 30-centimetre corner tells her little about a 4-metre wall. She uses the tape.

From one corner peg she measures 3 metres along one string and marks it; 4 metres along the other and marks it. Then she measures between the two marks. If it reads 5 metres, the corner is square — 9 plus 16 is 25 — by the converse of the theorem. It reads 5,06. The corner is not square: 5,06 squared is about 25,6, more than 25, so the angle at the corner is a little more than 90 degrees. She moves the peg a few centimetres inward and measures again: 5,00. Square.

She repeats at every corner. Then she checks the whole rectangle with the diagonals: in a true rectangle, both diagonals are equal, and each should be the hypotenuse of a 3, 4 triangle, 5 metres. Both read 5,00. The room is a rectangle.

A learner on holiday work asks why she does not simply use 3, 4, 5 at the scale of the room. She does: the room happens to be 3 by 4, so the diagonal is the check. For a 6 by 8 room she would expect a 10-metre diagonal — 6, 8, 10 is twice 3, 4, 5. For a 5 by 7 room there is no triple, and she would calculate: 25 plus 49 is 74, and the square root of 74 is about 8,60; the diagonal must measure 8,60 metres.

She also explains the inward move. The reading was too long, so the angle was too wide: the longest side's square exceeded the sum, obtuse. A reading too short — 4,95, say — would mean the angle was too narrow, acute. The tape tells her not only whether the corner is wrong but which way.

The learner writes the method down. Mark 3 along one side and 4 along the other, in any unit. Measure across. Exactly 5: square. More: open the angle less. Less: open it more. The whole theorem and its converse, in a builder's pocket. Questions on the builder follow.

## Subtopic: The Frame That Was Not Square and the Pitch That Was

A learner buys a picture frame described as 30 by 40 centimetres and finds the glass will not sit flat. She measures the diagonal: 50,8 centimetres. If the frame were a true rectangle its corners would be right angles and the diagonal would be the hypotenuse of a 30, 40 triangle: 900 plus 1 600 is 2 500, square root 50. The diagonal is 50,8, whose square is about 2 580, more than 2 500. The corner the diagonal spans is obtuse; the frame is a parallelogram, pushed slightly out of true. She measures the other diagonal: 49,2 centimetres, square about 2 421, less than 2 500 — the other corners are acute, as they must be, since adjacent angles of a parallelogram add to 180. She takes the frame back with the two numbers written on the receipt.

Across town, a groundsman is marking out a soccer pitch, 100 metres by 64. The corner flags must be at right angles or the pitch is a parallelogram and the goals will not face each other. He has no instrument for angles over such distances, only a long tape and two assistants. From a corner peg he runs 30 metres along the goal line and 40 metres along the touchline — three times 10 and four times 10 — and has an assistant measure between the two marks: 50 metres means a right angle, by the converse, since 900 plus 1 600 is 2 500. It reads 50,3; he swings the touchline string in a little; 50,0. He does the same at the other three corners, then checks the two long diagonals against each other: both should be the square root of 100 squared plus 64 squared, which is the square root of 10 000 plus 4 096, that is the square root of 14 096, about 118,73 metres. Both tapes read 118,7. The pitch is a rectangle, and the lines are painted.

The learner and the groundsman did the same mathematics. Both measured three sides of a triangle, both squared the longest and compared it with the other two squares, and both read the result: equal means square, more means too wide, less means too narrow. The frame failed; the pitch, after adjustment, passed.

She writes to the frame shop quoting the converse of the Theorem of Pythagoras. The shop replaces the frame and, she suspects, does not read the letter. Questions on the frame and the pitch follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for testing a triangle from its sides. One: check the three lengths form a triangle — the two shorter add to more than the longest. Two: identify the longest side. Three: square it; square the other two and add. Four: compare exactly. Equal: right-angled, with the right angle opposite the longest side, at the vertex where the two shorter sides meet; reason, the converse of the Theorem of Pythagoras. Not equal: not right-angled; and if asked, longest squared smaller means acute-angled, larger means obtuse-angled. Five: write the conclusion as a sentence with the comparison, the vertex and the reason. Six: use known triples and their multiples to recognise right-angled cases quickly, but square and compare when in doubt.

A typical examination item gives three sides and asks whether the triangle is right-angled, with working. Four lines: longest squared, sum of other squares, comparison, conclusion with reason.

Another gives a labelled triangle and asks where the right angle is. Opposite the longest side; name the vertex.

Another gives a real situation — a ladder, a frame, a plot with a diagonal — and asks whether a corner is square. Extract the three lengths, test, conclude in the context.

Another gives several triangles and asks which are right-angled. Test each; multiples of triples are quick.

Another gives a non-right case and asks for acute or obtuse. Read the inequality.

Another asks you to state the converse. If the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, the triangle is right-angled.

Errors to avoid. Squaring a shorter side alone. Accepting a near miss as equal. Saying the right angle is at the longest side. Forgetting to check the triangle exists. Using the theorem's statement as the reason when the converse is what was used. Omitting the vertex in the conclusion.

Looking ahead: the next lesson finds a missing side — including a leg, not just the hypotenuse — and leaves irrational answers in surd form. Grade 9 applies the theorem and its converse in coordinate geometry and three-dimensional problems. Final questions follow.
