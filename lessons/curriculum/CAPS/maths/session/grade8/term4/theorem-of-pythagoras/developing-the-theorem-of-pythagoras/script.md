# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates the relationship between the sides of a right-angled triangle to develop the Theorem of Pythagoras. Accurately constructed right-angled triangles are measured and the squares of their side lengths compared; squares drawn on each side on grid paper are counted; both approaches show that the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the other two sides. The theorem is stated in words and as a squared plus b squared equals c squared, with c the hypotenuse opposite the right angle, and the Pythagorean triples 3, 4, 5 and 5, 12, 13 and their multiples are identified. The anchor figures are triangles with legs 3 and 4, 5 and 12, 6 and 8, and 1 and 1.

## Subtopic: Constructing and Measuring Right-Angled Triangles

Begin with a right angle. Draw two lines meeting at 90 degrees using a set square or protractor, and mark 3 centimetres along one and 4 along the other. Join the two marks to make the third side, and measure it: 5 centimetres, as nearly as a ruler can tell. The third side, opposite the right angle, is the hypotenuse, and it is always the longest side.

Now square the three lengths. 3 squared is 9; 4 squared is 16; 5 squared is 25. And 9 plus 16 is 25. The square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

Try another. Legs 5 and 12, hypotenuse measured at 13. Squares: 25, 144, 169. And 25 plus 144 is 169. Again. Legs 6 and 8, hypotenuse 10. Squares 36, 64, 100. 36 plus 64 is 100. Again. Legs 8 and 15, hypotenuse 17: 64 plus 225 is 289, which is 17 squared. Again.

Try legs that do not give a whole-number hypotenuse. Legs 1 and 1: the hypotenuse measures about 1,4. Squares 1 and 1, sum 2, and 1,4 squared is 1,96 — close to 2, with the difference being measurement error, since the exact hypotenuse is the square root of 2, about 1,414. Legs 2 and 3: hypotenuse measures about 3,6; squares 4 and 9, sum 13; 3,6 squared is 12,96 — again close to 13, exact value the square root of 13.

Each learner in a class constructs different right-angled triangles, measures, squares and adds. Every time, the two smaller squares add to the largest, within measurement error. That is the investigation's result, and it is called the Theorem of Pythagoras after the Greek school that first recorded a proof, though builders in Egypt and Babylon used the relationship a thousand years before.

Record the work in a table: leg, leg, hypotenuse, leg squared, leg squared, hypotenuse squared, sum of the leg squares. The last two columns agree. And test that it fails for triangles without a right angle: a triangle with sides 4, 5, 6 has squares 16, 25, 36, and 16 plus 25 is 41, not 36. The relationship belongs to right-angled triangles alone. Questions on constructing and measuring follow now.

## Subtopic: Squares on the Sides: The Area Picture

The theorem is a statement about areas, and grid paper shows it. Draw the 3, 4, 5 triangle on squared paper with the legs along grid lines. On the leg of 3, draw a square outward: 3 by 3, area 9 small squares. On the leg of 4, a square 4 by 4, area 16. On the hypotenuse, a tilted square with side 5; its area is harder to count directly because it is tilted, but it can be found by enclosing it in a 7 by 7 square and subtracting the four right-angled triangles at the corners, each with legs 3 and 4 and area 6: 49 minus 24 is 25. The square on the hypotenuse has area 25, and 9 plus 16 is 25. The two smaller squares, cut up, would exactly fill the largest.

The same picture for 1 and 1. Squares of area 1 and 1 on the legs. The tilted square on the hypotenuse, enclosed in a 2 by 2 square with four triangles of area one half at the corners: 4 minus 2 is 2. So the hypotenuse squared is 2, and the hypotenuse is the square root of 2 — a length that exists on the grid exactly, as the diagonal of a unit square, even though it is not a whole number or a terminating decimal.

A general argument runs the same way. Take any right-angled triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c. Arrange four copies of it inside a square of side a plus b, each with its right angle at a corner, so that the four hypotenuses form a tilted square in the middle. The big square has area the quantity a plus b squared, which is a squared plus 2ab plus b squared. The four triangles have total area 4 times a half times a times b, which is 2ab. So the middle tilted square, whose side is c, has area a squared plus 2ab plus b squared minus 2ab, which is a squared plus b squared. Its area is also c squared. Therefore a squared plus b squared equals c squared, for every right-angled triangle. That is a proof, not a measurement, and it is the one most Grade 8 learners can follow.

In words: in a right-angled triangle, the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the other two sides.

The area picture explains the word squared. It is not just a number raised to a power; it is a geometric square with that side. Questions on the area picture follow.

## Subtopic: Stating the Theorem and Naming the Sides

The Theorem of Pythagoras, stated. In a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares on the other two sides. In symbols, with the hypotenuse c and the other two sides a and b: a squared plus b squared equals c squared.

Everything depends on identifying the hypotenuse correctly. It is the side opposite the right angle — the side that does not touch the right angle — and it is the longest side. In a diagram, find the small square marking the right angle and look across the triangle to the side that does not meet it: that is c. The two sides that form the right angle are the legs, a and b, in either order.

Common wrong identifications. Choosing the side that looks longest in a diagram that is not to scale — use the right angle, not the appearance. Choosing a leg because it is drawn horizontally at the bottom like a base — the hypotenuse is often drawn sloping, but it can be drawn anywhere. In a triangle labelled with vertices, the hypotenuse is named by the two vertices that are not at the right angle: in triangle ABC with the right angle at B, the hypotenuse is AC.

The statement also says what it does not say. It applies only to right-angled triangles; the 4, 5, 6 triangle showed that. And it relates squares of sides, not the sides themselves: 3 plus 4 is 7, not 5; the hypotenuse is less than the sum of the legs, as the triangle inequality demands, and more than either leg.

Writing the theorem for a specific triangle: in triangle PQR with the right angle at Q, PR squared equals PQ squared plus QR squared. Writing it with letters for the sides: c squared equals a squared plus b squared. Either form earns the mark for stating the theorem, provided the hypotenuse is the one on its own.

The theorem's converse — if a squared plus b squared equals c squared then the triangle is right-angled — is true too, and is the next lesson. For now, the direction is: right angle given, therefore the squares add. Questions on stating the theorem follow.

## Subtopic: Pythagorean Triples and Finding a Hypotenuse

Three whole numbers that satisfy a squared plus b squared equals c squared are a Pythagorean triple. The small ones are worth knowing: 3, 4, 5; 5, 12, 13; 8, 15, 17; 7, 24, 25. Check the last: 49 plus 576 is 625, which is 25 squared.

Any multiple of a triple is a triple, because scaling every side by the same factor scales every square by the factor squared. From 3, 4, 5: 6, 8, 10 — 36 plus 64 is 100; 9, 12, 15 — 81 plus 144 is 225; 30, 40, 50; and 0,3, 0,4, 0,5. From 5, 12, 13: 10, 24, 26. Recognising a triple saves calculation: legs 9 and 12 are three times 3 and 4, so the hypotenuse is three times 5, which is 15.

Finding a hypotenuse from the legs is the theorem used directly. Legs 6 and 8: c squared equals 36 plus 64, which is 100, so c equals the square root of 100, which is 10. Legs 5 and 12: c squared equals 25 plus 144, which is 169, so c is 13. Legs 7 and 9: c squared equals 49 plus 81, which is 130, so c is the square root of 130, which is about 11,4 — not a whole number, and left as the square root of 130 or rounded as the question asks.

Write the working in three lines. Line one, the theorem for this triangle: c squared equals a squared plus b squared. Line two, substitution and arithmetic: c squared equals 36 plus 64, which is 100. Line three, the square root with units: c equals 10 centimetres. Taking the square root at the end is the step most often forgotten; c squared equals 100 is not the length.

Decimal legs work the same way. Legs 1,5 and 2: c squared equals 2,25 plus 4, which is 6,25, so c is 2,5 — the 3, 4, 5 triple halved. Legs 2 and 3: c squared equals 13, c is about 3,61.

Units: if the legs are in metres, the hypotenuse is in metres; the squares are in square metres along the way and the square root brings the units back. State the unit in the final answer. Questions on triples and hypotenuses follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same discovery as stories about Egyptian rope-stretchers with a knotted loop, a tiler who checked a corner with nothing but a tape, and a learner who built the theorem out of sticky-note squares on a classroom window — stories about a fact people trusted their buildings to long before anyone proved it.

## Subtopic: The Rope with Twelve Knots

Long before anyone wrote down a proof, surveyors in ancient Egypt needed right angles for the corners of fields and the bases of temples, and they had no set squares. What they had was a loop of rope with twelve equally spaced knots — twelve equal intervals around the loop.

Three people hold the rope. The first holds a knot. The second walks three intervals along and pulls tight. The third walks four intervals from the second and pulls tight, and the remaining five intervals run back to the first. The rope now forms a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 intervals, and the angle at the second person — between the 3 and the 4 — is a right angle, exactly, every time, in any field.

Why it works is the theorem in reverse, which the next lesson treats properly: 9 plus 16 is 25, so the triangle with sides 3, 4, 5 is right-angled. The rope-stretchers knew the fact without the reason. They laid out the corners of buildings that are still standing, with corners still square, using nothing but counting and tension.

A Grade 8 class repeats it on the sports field with a rope and twelve pieces of tape. Three learners stretch it into the 3, 4, 5 triangle and a fourth checks the corner with a large set square: square. They then try 2, 5, 5 — an isosceles triangle, no right angle, and the set square confirms it. And 4, 4, 4 — equilateral, sixty degrees. Only the 3, 4, 5 split gives the right angle, because only it satisfies the squares.

They go further. A loop of thirty intervals, split 5, 12, 13: right angle again. A loop of forty, split 8, 15, 17: again. A loop of twenty-four, split 6, 8, 10 — twice the original — again. The class has discovered that the triples are exactly the splits that produce right angles.

The rope is a theorem made of hemp. Any time three lengths satisfy a squared plus b squared equals c squared, the triangle they form has a right angle opposite the longest; any time a triangle has a right angle, its sides satisfy the equation. The Egyptians used the first; this lesson has investigated the second; and the two together are why a knotted rope could lay out a temple. Questions on the rope follow.

## Subtopic: Sticky-Note Squares on the Window

A learner is not convinced by the measuring. Measurements are always a bit off, she says, so how do we know it is exactly 25 and not 24,9. The teacher hands her a pad of square sticky notes and points at the classroom window.

She sticks notes on the glass to make a right-angled triangle with legs 3 notes and 4 notes along the edges — the triangle itself is just the outline. Then she builds the square on the 3 leg: 3 rows of 3, nine notes. The square on the 4 leg: 4 rows of 4, sixteen notes. Then the square on the hypotenuse, which is tilted; she lays notes along the slope, turns them to match, and fills in: it takes exactly twenty-five, and she can count them one by one. Nine and sixteen notes peeled off the small squares would cover the big one with none left over and none missing. No measuring, no 24,9; a count.

The teacher asks whether this proves it for every right-angled triangle. She thinks and says no: it proves it for 3, 4, 5. For 5, 12, 13 she would need 169 notes, and for legs 1 and 1 she could not do it at all, because the hypotenuse square has area 2 and you cannot make 2 out of whole notes without cutting. The counting works for triples and fails otherwise.

So the teacher shows the cut. Take the two small squares, cut them along a few lines, and rearrange the pieces to fill the big square exactly — a dissection that works for any right-angled triangle, not just whole-number ones. On the window it becomes the four-triangles picture: a big square of side a plus b, four copies of the triangle in the corners, and the tilted square of side c in the middle whose area must be the big square minus the four triangles, which is a squared plus b squared. The sticky notes made it believable; the algebra makes it certain.

The learner keeps the twenty-five notes on the window with a label: 9 plus 16. The class next door comes to look. Someone from that class asks what happens with a triangle that is not right-angled, and she builds 4, 5, 6 on the glass: sixteen plus twenty-five notes do not fit thirty-six, with five left over. The right angle is the whole point. Questions on the sticky notes follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for developing and using the theorem. One: in a right-angled triangle, identify the hypotenuse as the side opposite the right angle, not touching it, and the longest. Two: state the theorem for the triangle — hypotenuse squared equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides — in words or symbols, with the hypotenuse alone on its side. Three: to find a hypotenuse, square the two legs, add, and take the square root; write the three lines and give units. Four: recognise triples and their multiples — 3, 4, 5; 5, 12, 13; 8, 15, 17; 7, 24, 25 — to shortcut the arithmetic. Five: leave non-whole answers as square roots or round as instructed. Six: remember the theorem holds only for right-angled triangles, and that the area picture — squares on the sides — is what the squares mean.

A typical examination item gives a right-angled triangle with two legs and asks for the hypotenuse. Three lines: theorem, substitution with the sum, square root with units.

Another asks you to state the theorem for a labelled triangle. Name the hypotenuse by its two vertices and write the equation.

Another asks for the area of the square on the hypotenuse given the squares on the legs — 9 and 16 give 25 — without finding any length.

Another gives a grid picture and asks for the area of a tilted square. Enclose it and subtract the corner triangles.

Another asks whether a set of three numbers is a Pythagorean triple. Square and compare.

Another asks to explain in words what the theorem says, or to describe an investigation. Squares on the sides, areas, the sum, right-angled triangles only.

Errors to avoid. Picking the wrong hypotenuse. Adding the sides instead of the squares. Forgetting the square root. Applying the theorem to a triangle without a right angle. Dropping units. Writing a squared plus b squared equals c squared with c a leg.

Looking ahead: the next lesson turns the theorem round to test whether a triangle is right-angled from its three sides, and the one after finds a missing leg rather than the hypotenuse, leaving irrational answers in surd form. Grade 9 extends to problems in rectangles, coordinates and three dimensions. Final questions follow.
