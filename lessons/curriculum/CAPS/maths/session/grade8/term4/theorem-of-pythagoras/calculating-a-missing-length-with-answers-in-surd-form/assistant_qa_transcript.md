### subtopic_1 — Finding a Hypotenuse and Finding a Leg
**Assistant:** How do I remember whether to add or subtract?

**Tutor:** Ask one question: is the side I want the hypotenuse? The theorem says the hypotenuse squared is the sum of the other two squares, so if the hypotenuse is missing you add the two leg squares to build that sum. If a leg is missing, the hypotenuse squared is already the total, and the missing leg's square is what is left after the known leg's square is taken away, so you subtract — always the smaller square from the larger, the leg from the hypotenuse. A check that catches every mistake: the hypotenuse is the longest side. If you computed a hypotenuse and it came out shorter than a leg, you subtracted when you should have added; if you computed a leg and it came out longer than the hypotenuse, or the number under the root went negative, you added when you should have subtracted or subtracted the wrong way. Drawing the triangle and marking the right angle before writing anything makes the question answer itself, because the hypotenuse is the side you can see does not touch the right angle.

### subtopic_2 — Surds and Simplifying Them
**Assistant:** Why is a surd considered a better answer than its decimal, when the decimal is what I can actually measure?

**Tutor:** Because the surd is exact and the decimal is not, and mathematics answers with the truth and lets the user round. The square root of 2 is a definite number — the length of the diagonal of a unit square — but its decimal expansion never ends, so any decimal you write, 1,4 or 1,414 or 1,41421356, is a little wrong, and squaring it does not give back 2. If you round early and keep calculating, the small error grows; if you carry the surd, every later step stays exact and you round once at the end. Surds also keep the structure visible: 80 root two tells you the diagonal is root two times the side, which is true for every square, while 113,1 tells you about one square only. That is why the glazier's sheet had the surds first. The decimal is the right thing to hand to someone with a saw, which is why the question tells you when to round and to how many places. Give what is asked; when nothing is asked, give the exact value, and add the decimal if it helps.

### subtopic_3 — Diagonals, Heights and Dropped Perpendiculars
**Assistant:** Why does the perpendicular from the apex of an isosceles triangle hit the midpoint of the base?

**Tutor:** Because of the triangle's symmetry. The isosceles triangle has a line of symmetry through the apex: folding along it lands one equal leg on the other and one base angle on the other, so the two halves match exactly. The fold line meets the base at a point that is its own mirror image, and the two halves of the base are mirror images of each other, so they are equal — the point is the midpoint. The fold line also makes equal angles with the base on either side, and since those two angles lie on a straight line they are each 90; the fold line is perpendicular to the base. So the perpendicular from the apex and the line to the midpoint are the same line, and it splits the triangle into two congruent right-angled triangles with the leg as hypotenuse and half the base as one leg. In Grade 9 this becomes a congruence proof. For a scalene triangle there is no symmetry, the perpendicular from the apex does not meet the base at its midpoint, and the two pieces of the base must be found separately.

### subtopic_6 — The Ladder That Was Long Enough on Paper
**Assistant:** The painter's extra metre of ladder gave almost a metre of height. Would that always happen?

**Tutor:** Only when the ladder is steep. The reach is the leg h with h squared equal to L squared minus d squared, where L is the ladder and d the foot distance. When d is small compared with L, the ladder is nearly vertical, the subtraction removes very little, and h is close to L; then adding a metre to L adds nearly a metre to h — from 5 at 1,25 to 6 at 1,5 the reach went from 4,84 to 5,81, a gain of 0,97. When d is large compared with L, the ladder is shallow, the subtraction removes a great deal, and h is much less than L; an extra metre of ladder then adds much less than a metre of reach — a 5-metre ladder with its foot at 3 reaches 4, and a 6-metre ladder with its foot at 3 reaches the square root of 27, about 5,20, a gain of 1,20 — in fact more, because the longer ladder at the same foot distance is also steeper. The general lesson is that reach depends on both numbers, and the only way to know is to do the subtraction. The painter's rule of one quarter keeps d proportional to L, so the ratio is fixed and the reach is always about 0,97 of the ladder length; the table she wrote shows exactly that proportion.
