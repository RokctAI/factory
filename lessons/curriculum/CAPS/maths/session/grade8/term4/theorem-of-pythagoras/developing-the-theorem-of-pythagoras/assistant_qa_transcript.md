### subtopic_1 — Constructing and Measuring Right-Angled Triangles
**Assistant:** My measured hypotenuse squared never comes out exactly equal to the sum. Does the theorem only hold approximately?

**Tutor:** The theorem holds exactly; your measurements are approximate. A ruler reads to the nearest millimetre at best, so a hypotenuse of exactly 5 might be read as 4,9 or 5,1, and squaring magnifies the error: 4,9 squared is 24,01 and 5,1 squared is 26,01, a spread of two units around the true 25. The legs carry errors too. So the sum of the leg squares and the hypotenuse square will differ by a unit or two in a careful construction, and the difference shrinks as the construction improves — larger triangles, sharper pencils, careful right angles. The pattern across a class — every total close to equality, none far off, differences in both directions — is what tells you the exact relationship is there underneath. The proof then settles it: the four-triangles argument shows a squared plus b squared equals c squared exactly, for every right-angled triangle, with no measurement at all. In an investigation write-up, report your measured values, note the measurement error, and then state the exact theorem.

### subtopic_2 — Squares on the Sides: The Area Picture
**Assistant:** Why is the proof with four triangles in a big square valid for every right-angled triangle?

**Tutor:** Because nothing in it uses the particular lengths; it uses only that the triangle has a right angle and sides a, b and c. Take any right-angled triangle. Four copies can always be placed in the corners of a square of side a plus b with their right angles at the corners, because at each corner a leg a and a leg b lie along the two edges and meet at the right angle, and a plus b is exactly the side of the square. The four hypotenuses then enclose a quadrilateral in the middle; its sides are all c, and its angles are each 180 minus the two acute angles of the triangle, which is 90 since those acute angles add to 90, so it is a square of side c. The big square's area is the quantity a plus b squared; the four triangles have total area 2ab; the difference is the middle square's area, c squared. Expanding the quantity a plus b squared and subtracting 2ab leaves a squared plus b squared. Every step would read the same for legs 3 and 4, legs 1 and 1, or legs 2,7 and 9,1, so the conclusion holds for all of them.

### subtopic_4 — Pythagorean Triples and Finding a Hypotenuse
**Assistant:** When the hypotenuse squared is 130, should I write the square root of 130 or 11,4?

**Tutor:** Write both unless the question specifies: c equals the square root of 130, which is approximately 11,4. The square root form is exact — there is no decimal that equals the square root of 130 — and it is what mathematicians call a surd. The decimal is a rounded approximation useful for measuring or cutting. If the question says leave your answer in surd form or give the exact value, write the square root of 130 and stop. If it says correct to one decimal place, write 11,4; to two places, 11,40. If it says nothing, give the exact value and a rounded one. The third lesson of this topic works with surd answers in detail. Two cautions: do not round early — carry 130 under the root until the final line, because rounding the square to 13 times 10 or similar and then rooting introduces error — and do not simplify the square root of 130 by splitting it into the square root of 13 times the square root of 10, which is correct but no simpler; a surd simplifies only when a square factor can be taken out, as the square root of 50 becomes 5 times the square root of 2.

### subtopic_5 — The Rope with Twelve Knots
**Assistant:** If the rope-stretchers were using the theorem backwards, is that a different fact?

**Tutor:** Yes; it is the converse, and it needs its own justification, which the next lesson supplies. The theorem says: if a triangle has a right angle, then a squared plus b squared equals c squared. The converse says: if a squared plus b squared equals c squared, then the triangle has a right angle opposite c. These are different statements — one goes from the angle to the equation, the other from the equation to the angle — and in general a statement and its converse can have different truth values. Here both are true, which is why the rope works: the lengths 3, 4, 5 satisfy the equation, and the converse guarantees the right angle. The Egyptians relied on the converse without a proof; the Greeks later proved both directions. In your own work, keep the direction clear. When a diagram gives you a right angle, you may use the theorem to find a side. When a problem gives you three sides and asks whether the triangle is right-angled, you are using the converse, and you should say so. The next lesson makes that test precise, including what the comparison tells you when the squares do not match.
