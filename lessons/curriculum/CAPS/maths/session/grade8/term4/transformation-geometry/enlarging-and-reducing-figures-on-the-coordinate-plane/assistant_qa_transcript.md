### subtopic_1 — The Rule: Multiply Both Coordinates by k
**Assistant:** Why do we multiply the coordinates instead of adding something to them?

**Tutor:** Because an enlargement stretches distances from a centre, and stretching is multiplication. A point's coordinates tell you how far across and how far up it is from the origin. Enlarging from the origin by a factor k means every point ends up k times as far from the origin in exactly the same direction, so both the across distance and the up distance are multiplied by k. The point 1 semicolon 2 becomes 2 semicolon 4 under k equal to 2: still on the same ray from the origin, twice as far out. Adding a number to the coordinates does something quite different: it slides every point the same distance in the same direction, which is a translation, and the figure keeps its size. You can see the difference with two vertices. Under enlargement by 2, the points 1 semicolon 2 and 3 semicolon 2, which are 2 apart, go to 2 semicolon 4 and 6 semicolon 4, which are 4 apart. Under adding 2 to each coordinate, they go to 3 semicolon 4 and 5 semicolon 4, still 2 apart. Only multiplication changes the distance between points, which is what changing the size means.

### subtopic_2 — Investigating Sides, Angles and Distances
**Assistant:** Why does the image move away from the origin as well as getting bigger?

**Tutor:** Because the centre of the enlargement is the origin, and the origin is the only point that stays fixed. Every other point is pushed outwards along its ray from the origin to k times its distance. A vertex that was close to the origin moves a little; a vertex that was far moves a lot. The triangle with A at 1 semicolon 2 is the square root of 5 from the origin at A; after enlarging by 2, A prime is twice the square root of 5 away. So the whole figure grows and, unless it touches the origin, it also shifts away. If the figure had a vertex at the origin, as the logo does, that vertex would stay put and the figure would grow from that corner, which is why the banner logo kept its bottom-left corner fixed. Think of a torch shining on a wall: move a cut-out closer to the torch and its shadow becomes bigger and spreads further from the centre of the beam. The torch is the centre of enlargement. In Grade 9 you will choose other centres, and then the image grows away from that point instead.

### subtopic_4 — Finding and Testing the Scale Factor
**Assistant:** In the test example the image was the right shape but not an enlargement from the origin. How can that be?

**Tutor:** Because being the right shape only tells you the image is similar, while an enlargement from the origin also fixes where the image must be. The original triangle had vertices 1 semicolon 1, 3 semicolon 1 and 1 semicolon 4, with legs 2 and 3. The image 4 semicolon 4, 8 semicolon 4 and 4 semicolon 10 has legs 4 and 6, exactly twice as long, so its shape and size match an enlargement by 2. But the enlargement by 2 from the origin puts the vertices at 2 semicolon 2, 6 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 8. The given image is that triangle slid 2 units right and 2 units up. So it is an enlargement by 2 followed by a translation, and a single enlargement from the origin cannot produce it. The coordinate test catches this immediately: dividing image by original gives 4 from the first vertex but about 2,67 from the second, so there is no single k. When a question asks whether a figure is an enlargement from the origin, the division test on every coordinate is the reliable check, because shapes on a grid can look right while sitting in the wrong place.

### subtopic_6 — The Photocopier's Percentages
**Assistant:** Why is the photocopier setting for halving a page 71 percent and not 50 percent?

**Tutor:** Because the photocopier's percentage is a scale factor for lengths, while halving the page is about area. A setting of 50 percent multiplies every length by one half, so the area is multiplied by one half times one half, which is one quarter. To make the area one half, the length factor must be a number which, multiplied by itself, gives one half. That is the square root of one half, about 0,707, which the copier rounds to 71 percent. Check it on A4 paper, which is 210 by 297 millimetres: 297 times 0,71 is about 211, and 210 times 0,71 is about 149, almost exactly the A5 size of 148 by 210. The reverse setting, 141 percent, is the square root of 2, about 1,414, which doubles the area. The A paper sizes were designed with their sides in the ratio 1 to the square root of 2, precisely so that cutting a sheet in half gives a smaller sheet of the same shape. It is a neat everyday case of the rule from these lessons: the scale factor for lengths is the square root of the factor for areas.
