### subtopic_1 — Points, Images and the Three Reflections
**Assistant:** Why does reflecting in the line y equals x swap the coordinates rather than changing a sign?

**Tutor:** Because the mirror is diagonal, and the roles of across and up are exchanged by it. Take the point 3 semicolon 2: it is 3 along the x-direction and 2 along the y-direction. The line y equals x treats the two directions symmetrically — it is the set of points where across equals up — so reflecting in it turns across into up and up into across, giving 2 along x and 3 along y, which is 2 semicolon 3. You can verify with the two tests for any reflection. The midpoint of 3 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 3 is 2,5 semicolon 2,5, which lies on the line y equals x, so the mirror passes halfway between point and image. And the segment joining them goes 1 left and 1 up, which is perpendicular to a line going 1 right and 1 up. Both conditions hold, so the swap is the correct reflection. The axis reflections change a sign because those mirrors are horizontal or vertical: they flip one direction and leave the other alone. Points on the line y equals x, such as 5 semicolon 5, swap to themselves, exactly as points on an axis stay put under reflection in that axis.

### subtopic_3 — Rotations About the Origin
**Assistant:** How can I tell which of the two 90-degree rules is anticlockwise?

**Tutor:** Test it on a point you can picture. Take 1 semicolon 0, one unit along the positive x-axis — three o'clock on a clock face. Anticlockwise a quarter-turn takes it to twelve o'clock, which is 0 semicolon 1. Apply the candidate rule negative y semicolon x: negative 0 semicolon 1 is 0 semicolon 1. Correct, so that rule is anticlockwise. The other rule, y semicolon negative x, takes 1 semicolon 0 to 0 semicolon negative 1, which is six o'clock, a quarter-turn clockwise. Thirty seconds with the point 1 semicolon 0 settles it every time, and it is far more reliable than trying to remember which rule is which. The same test works for any transformation you are unsure of: apply it to a simple point whose image you can see on a sketch, and see whether the rule agrees. For rotations the sketch is an arrow from the centre to the point; turn it the stated way by the stated amount, and read where the arrowhead lands.

### subtopic_4 — Rotation About Another Point and Combining Transformations
**Assistant:** Why does the order of two transformations matter? Moving is moving.

**Tutor:** Because most transformations do not treat all positions alike, so where a point is when a transformation acts changes what happens to it. A reflection in the x-axis sends a point to the mirror position across that axis; if the point has first been translated upwards, it is further from the axis and lands further below it than it would otherwise. Reflect 3 semicolon 2 then translate 1 left and 4 up: 3 semicolon negative 2, then 2 semicolon 2. Translate first then reflect: 2 semicolon 6, then 2 semicolon negative 6. Different points. The exceptions are worth knowing: two translations commute, because adding shifts in either order gives the same total; two rotations about the same centre commute, because turning by one angle then another is turning by the sum either way. But a reflection combined with a translation, a rotation combined with a reflection, or rotations about different centres generally do not. So a sequence is always performed in the order written, and a description of a sequence must give the order. In Grade 9 and beyond, the rule that order matters is the first thing to check whenever transformations are combined.

### subtopic_6 — The Mirror in the Tile Shop and the Battleship Fleet
**Assistant:** The fleet stayed congruent under every transformation. Does anything change at all?

**Tutor:** Position, and sometimes orientation; never size or shape. Reflections, translations and rotations are rigid motions: the distance between any two points of the figure is the same after as before, and so every side length and every angle is preserved, which is what congruent means. What a translation changes is only where the figure is. What a rotation changes is where it is and which way it faces — the fleet turned through a quarter-turn points in a new direction, but a ship that was to the north-east of another is still the same distance from it. What a reflection changes is where it is and its handedness: a ship shaped like an L becomes a backwards L, a printed R reads backwards, and no amount of sliding or turning will bring the reflected figure back onto the original without flipping it over. For the battleship fleet, handedness is irrelevant, so a reflection is as good a disguise as a rotation. For a tile with a letter on it, handedness is the whole difference between a correct tile and a misprint. The next topic's enlargement is the first transformation that changes size, and it is deliberately treated separately for that reason.
