# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses proportion to describe the effect of enlargement and reduction on perimeter and area. An enlargement or reduction multiplies every length of a figure by the same scale factor, called k: greater than 1 for an enlargement and between 0 and 1 for a reduction. The angles do not change, so the image is similar to the original. Because perimeter is a length, the perimeter is multiplied by k; because area counts squares, and each unit square becomes a k by k square, the area is multiplied by k squared. The anchor figure is a rectangle 3 units by 2 units, with perimeter 10 units and area 6 square units, enlarged by factors 2 and 3 and reduced by the factor one half.

## Subtopic: Scale Factor: Enlargements and Reductions

An enlargement makes a figure bigger without changing its shape; a reduction makes it smaller without changing its shape. In both, every length of the figure is multiplied by the same number, the scale factor, written k.

If k is greater than 1, the figure is enlarged. If k is between 0 and 1, the figure is reduced. If k equals 1, nothing changes in size. The scale factor is found by dividing a length in the image by the matching length in the original: image length divided by original length.

Take a rectangle 3 units long and 2 units wide. Enlarge it by the scale factor 2: the length becomes 3 times 2, which is 6, and the width becomes 2 times 2, which is 4. The image is a rectangle 6 by 4. Enlarge the original by the factor 3: 9 by 6. Reduce it by the factor one half: 1,5 by 1.

Every length is multiplied by k — not only the sides. A diagonal, a height, the radius of a circle: all of them. The diagonal of the 3 by 2 rectangle is the square root of 13; in the image enlarged by 2 it is two times the square root of 13.

Angles do not change. The rectangle's right angles stay right angles; a triangle's 30-degree angle stays 30 degrees. That is what makes the image the same shape. Figures related in this way are similar, as in Term 3: matching angles equal and matching sides in the same ratio.

Check that a pair of figures is an enlargement by testing that every pair of matching sides gives the same ratio. A rectangle 6 by 4 compared with 3 by 2: 6 divided by 3 is 2 and 4 divided by 2 is 2, the same, so it is an enlargement by 2. A rectangle 6 by 3 compared with 3 by 2: 6 divided by 3 is 2 but 3 divided by 2 is 1,5, not the same, so it is not an enlargement; it has been stretched more in one direction than the other and has a different shape.

Note that adding the same amount to every side does not enlarge a figure. Adding 3 to each side of the 3 by 2 rectangle gives 6 by 5, which fails the ratio test. Enlargement is multiplication.

The scale factor can be written as a whole number, a fraction or a decimal: one half and 0,5 are the same reduction. A ratio such as 1 to 4 on a model means the model's lengths are one quarter of the real ones. Questions on scale factor follow now.

## Subtopic: The Effect on Perimeter

Perimeter is a length — the total distance around the figure — so it behaves like every other length: it is multiplied by the scale factor.

The 3 by 2 rectangle has perimeter 3 plus 2 plus 3 plus 2, which is 10 units. Enlarged by 2 it is 6 by 4, with perimeter 6 plus 4 plus 6 plus 4, which is 20 units. Enlarged by 3 it is 9 by 6, with perimeter 30 units. Reduced by one half it is 1,5 by 1, with perimeter 5 units. In each case the new perimeter is the old perimeter times k: 10 times 2 is 20, 10 times 3 is 30, 10 times one half is 5.

The reason is simple. Each side is multiplied by k, and the perimeter is the sum of the sides, so the sum is multiplied by k as well. Multiplying every term by k multiplies the total by k — the distributive law.

The same holds for every figure. A triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 has perimeter 12. Enlarged by 2 its sides are 6, 8 and 10 and its perimeter is 24, which is 12 times 2. A circle of radius 5 has circumference 2 times pi times 5, which is 10 pi, about 31,42 units. Enlarged by 3 its radius is 15 and its circumference is 30 pi, which is 3 times 10 pi.

Stated as a proportion: the ratio of the perimeters equals the ratio of the matching sides, which equals the scale factor. If two similar figures have matching sides in the ratio 2 to 5, their perimeters are also in the ratio 2 to 5.

This lets you find a perimeter without measuring every side. A regular hexagon of side 4 cm has perimeter 24 cm. A similar hexagon whose side is 10 cm has scale factor 10 divided by 4, which is 2,5, so its perimeter is 24 times 2,5, which is 60 cm — and indeed 6 times 10 is 60.

Working backwards: if the perimeter of an image is 45 and the original's is 15, the scale factor is 45 divided by 15, which is 3. If a figure is reduced and its perimeter falls from 40 to 10, the scale factor is 10 divided by 40, which is one quarter.

Perimeter is directly proportional to the scale factor: double the factor and the perimeter doubles. A graph of perimeter against scale factor is a straight line through the origin. Questions on perimeter follow now.

## Subtopic: The Effect on Area: The Square of the Scale Factor

Area does not scale by k. It scales by k squared, and this is the most important fact of the lesson.

The 3 by 2 rectangle has area 3 times 2, which is 6 square units. Enlarged by 2 it is 6 by 4, area 24 square units. That is not 12, which is what doubling would give; it is 4 times the original. Enlarged by 3 it is 9 by 6, area 54 square units, which is 9 times the original. Reduced by one half it is 1,5 by 1, area 1,5 square units, which is one quarter of the original. The multipliers 4, 9 and one quarter are 2 squared, 3 squared and one half squared.

Why the square. Area counts unit squares. Under an enlargement by k, each unit square becomes a square k units on each side, which has area k times k. Every one of the original squares has turned into k squared squares, so the total is multiplied by k squared. With k equal to 2, each square becomes a 2 by 2 block of four squares. Draw it once and the result is obvious: the 6 squares of the original become 6 blocks of 4.

Another way: area is a length times a length. The rectangle's area is length times width. Both are multiplied by k, so the product is multiplied by k twice — k times k.

This applies to any figure, because any area can be thought of as built from small squares. The triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 has area half of 3 times 4, which is 6; enlarged by 2, its area is half of 6 times 8, which is 24, four times the original. A circle of radius 5 has area 25 pi; enlarged by 3, its radius is 15 and its area is 225 pi, nine times as much.

Stated as a proportion: if the matching sides of two similar figures are in the ratio a to b, their areas are in the ratio a squared to b squared. Sides 2 to 5 give areas 4 to 25.

Area is not directly proportional to the scale factor. Doubling k multiplies the area by 4; tripling k multiplies it by 9. A graph of area against scale factor is a curve that grows ever more steeply, not a straight line.

For reductions the effect is just as strong in the other direction. A map reduced by the factor one tenth shows each region at one hundredth of its area on the original drawing. Questions on area follow now.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards and Comparing with Proportion

The two rules — perimeter times k, area times k squared — can be run in either direction, and they connect perimeter and area of similar figures to each other.

From an area ratio to the scale factor: take the square root. If an enlarged figure has 9 times the area of the original, the scale factor is the square root of 9, which is 3, and the perimeter is 3 times as long. If a reduced figure has one sixteenth of the area, the scale factor is the square root of one sixteenth, which is one quarter, and the perimeter is one quarter as long.

From a perimeter ratio to the area ratio: square it. Two similar triangles with perimeters 12 cm and 30 cm have scale factor 30 divided by 12, which is 2,5, so their areas are in the ratio 1 to 6,25. If the smaller has area 6 square centimetres, the larger has area 6 times 6,25, which is 37,5 square centimetres.

From one area and the scale factor to the other area. A logo occupies 12 square centimetres on a letterhead and is enlarged by the factor 4 for a banner. Its area on the banner is 12 times 16, which is 192 square centimetres — not 48.

A common trap: a question says the area doubled and asks for the scale factor. The scale factor is not 2; it is the square root of 2, about 1,41. The sides grow by about 41 percent to double the area.

Another trap: adding instead of multiplying. Increasing each side of a square of side 5 by 5 centimetres gives a square of side 10 — that happens to be an enlargement by 2, because adding 5 to 5 doubles it. But increasing the 3 by 2 rectangle's sides by 3 gives 6 by 5, which is not an enlargement at all.

A table organises the work: one row for lengths, one for perimeter, one for area, with columns for original, factor and image. Lengths and perimeter use k; area uses k squared. Fill in what is given and use the factor to complete the rest.

Proportion also allows comparison without the actual sizes. A photograph and its enlargement have matching sides in the ratio 2 to 3. Without knowing any measurements, the frame of the enlargement needs one and a half times as much moulding, and the print uses two and a quarter times as much paper. Questions on working backwards follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same proportions as stories about a gardener who doubled the sides of a vegetable patch and ran out of compost, two pizzas on a takeaway menu, and a photograph enlarged for a school reunion — stories about the difference between twice as long and twice as big.

## Subtopic: The Vegetable Patch That Ran Out of Compost

A community garden in Mamelodi has a square vegetable patch 5 metres on each side, fenced and covered every spring with compost at 2 bags per square metre. The garden committee decides to make the patch bigger and agrees to double the side to 10 metres.

The treasurer plans the budget by doubling last year's figures. Last year the fence was 4 times 5, which is 20 metres; she budgets for 40 metres. Last year the patch was 5 times 5, which is 25 square metres, and took 50 bags of compost; she budgets for 100 bags.

The fencing arrives and fits exactly: the new perimeter is 4 times 10, which is 40 metres, twice the old. The fence is a length, and doubling the side doubled it.

The compost runs out halfway. The new patch is 10 times 10, which is 100 square metres, and at 2 bags per square metre it needs 200 bags, not 100. The area went up four times, not twice. A learner who helps at the garden on Saturdays explains it with chalk on the shed wall: draw the old 5 by 5 patch, then the new 10 by 10 patch beside it, and the new one holds four copies of the old — a 2 by 2 arrangement. Doubling the side doubled the fence and quadrupled the ground.

The committee asks a follow-up question: they have money for exactly 150 bags this year. How large a square patch can they cover? 150 bags at 2 per square metre covers 75 square metres. The side is the square root of 75, about 8,66 metres. Compared with the old 5-metre patch, the scale factor is about 1,73, and the area is 3 times as large, which matches 1,73 squared being about 3.

The learner then shows the committee a quick rule for the future. Lengths — fence, edging, irrigation pipe along the border — scale with the side. Areas — compost, seed, mulch, netting over the top — scale with the square of the side. If the side is to triple next year, to 15 metres, the fence will be 60 metres, three times as long, but the compost will be 9 times 50, which is 450 bags.

The committee buys the extra 100 bags, and the treasurer adds a line to her notebook: when the side changes by a factor, the fence changes by that factor and the compost by its square. Questions on the vegetable patch follow.

## Subtopic: Two Pizzas and a Reunion Photograph

A takeaway in Cape Town sells a small pizza 20 centimetres across for R60 and a large pizza 30 centimetres across for R110. A group of learners must decide which gives more pizza for the money.

One learner compares the diameters: 30 is one and a half times 20, so he reckons a fair price for the large would be one and a half times R60, which is R90. Since R110 is more than R90, he concludes the large is poor value. Another learner stops him: the amount of pizza is an area, not a length. The scale factor from small to large is 30 divided by 20, which is 1,5, so the area is multiplied by 1,5 squared, which is 2,25. The large pizza has more than twice the pizza of the small one, for less than twice the price, since R110 divided by R60 is about 1,83.

They check with the actual areas, using the radius: 10 centimetres for the small and 15 for the large. The small has area pi times 100, about 314 square centimetres. The large has pi times 225, about 707 square centimetres. Per rand, the small gives about 314 divided by 60, which is about 5,24 square centimetres, and the large about 707 divided by 110, which is about 6,43 square centimetres. The large is the better buy, as the area argument predicted. Two small pizzas would cost R120 and give about 628 square centimetres — less pizza than one large, for more money.

The crust, however, is a length around the edge: the circumference. It scales by 1,5, not 2,25. The large pizza has 1,5 times as much crust as the small but 2,25 times as much topping, which is why bigger pizzas feel less crusty.

At the same school, the organisers of a twenty-year reunion want to enlarge an old class photograph that measures 10 centimetres by 15 centimetres to 20 by 30 centimetres for a display board. The scale factor is 2. The photo shop charges by paper area: the original is 150 square centimetres and the enlargement 600, four times as much, so the print costs four times the price of a standard print, not twice. The frame, sold by the length of moulding, needs the perimeter: 50 centimetres for the original and 100 for the enlargement, twice as much. The organisers budget correctly on the first try, because one of them remembered the pizza argument: edges by k, surfaces by k squared. Questions on pizzas and photographs follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for the effect of an enlargement or reduction. One: find the scale factor k, as image length divided by matching original length, and check that all matching sides give the same k. Two: lengths of every kind — sides, diagonals, heights, radii, perimeters, circumferences — are multiplied by k. Three: areas are multiplied by k squared. Four: angles are unchanged. Five: to go backwards from an area ratio, take the square root to find k; from a perimeter ratio, k is the ratio itself, and squaring it gives the area ratio.

A typical examination item gives a figure with its perimeter and area and asks for both after an enlargement by a stated factor. Multiply the perimeter by k and the area by k squared, and say which rule you used.

Another gives two similar figures and the area of one, and asks for the area of the other. Find k from matching sides, then multiply by k squared.

Another asks what happens to the area when every side is doubled, or halved, or multiplied by 3. The answer is a word statement: the area is multiplied by 4, divided by 4, or multiplied by 9.

Another asks for the scale factor that would double, or triple, the area. The square root of 2, or the square root of 3.

Another gives a rectangle and a second rectangle whose sides have been increased by a fixed amount, and asks whether one is an enlargement of the other. Test the ratios; adding a constant is usually not an enlargement.

Errors to avoid. Multiplying the area by k instead of k squared. Taking the scale factor as the area ratio itself instead of its square root. Dividing the original by the image when the factor should be image over original, giving the reciprocal. Treating a stretch in one direction as an enlargement. Adding a constant instead of multiplying.

A useful self-check: an enlargement must give an area that grows faster than the perimeter. With k equal to 2, the perimeter doubles and the area quadruples; if your answers show the same multiplier for both, something is wrong.

Looking ahead: the next lesson places enlargements on the coordinate plane, where multiplying both coordinates of every vertex by k enlarges a figure from the origin, and these same rules for perimeter and area apply. In the surface area and volume lessons, the pattern extends: surface areas scale by k squared and volumes by k cubed. Final questions follow.
