### subtopic_1 — One Vertex at a Time: The Table Method
**Assistant:** When I reflect a triangle in the line y equals x, one vertex did not move. Did I make a mistake?

**Tutor:** Not necessarily; check whether that vertex lies on the mirror line. A reflection leaves every point of its mirror exactly where it is, because such a point is zero distance from the mirror and so is its image. For the line y equals x, a point lies on the mirror when its two coordinates are equal, like 1 semicolon 1 or negative 3 semicolon negative 3, and swapping equal coordinates changes nothing. So in triangle ABC, with A at 1 semicolon 1, the image A prime is 1 semicolon 1, while B at 4 semicolon 1 goes to 1 semicolon 4 and C at 1 semicolon 3 goes to 3 semicolon 1. The same thing happens with the axes: a vertex on the x-axis, such as 5 semicolon 0, stays put under reflection in the x-axis, and a vertex on the y-axis stays put under reflection in the y-axis. If a vertex did not move and it is not on the mirror, then something did go wrong, and the usual cause is forgetting to apply the rule to that row of the table. A fixed vertex is in fact a helpful check, because it tells you that the image must touch the original at that corner.

### subtopic_3 — Rotating a Triangle About the Origin
**Assistant:** Is rotating a triangle 180 degrees the same as reflecting it?

**Tutor:** No, even though the image can look upside down and back to front. A rotation of 180 degrees about the origin uses the rule negative x semicolon negative y, negating both coordinates, while each Grade 8 reflection changes only one coordinate's sign or swaps the two. The clearest way to tell them apart on a triangle is orientation. Walk round triangle ABC from A to B to C: the route goes anticlockwise. Under the half-turn the vertices go to negative 1 semicolon negative 1, negative 4 semicolon negative 1 and negative 1 semicolon negative 3, and walking A prime, B prime, C prime still goes anticlockwise. Under a reflection in the x-axis the route becomes clockwise. So the half-turn keeps orientation and a reflection reverses it. There is one connection worth knowing, discovered in the mural story: reflecting in the y-axis and then in the x-axis produces the same image as a 180-degree rotation, because negating x and then negating y negates both. Two reflections made a rotation, which is why the orientation flipped twice and ended up unchanged. A single reflection, though, can never equal a rotation.

### subtopic_4 — What Stays the Same: Congruence and Orientation
**Assistant:** Why do we bother checking the area of the image if it is always the same?

**Tutor:** Because the check catches mistakes that the eye misses. Reflections, translations and rotations are rigid, so the image must have exactly the same side lengths, angles and area. If you compute the image vertices and the area comes out different, at least one vertex is wrong, and you know before plotting the wrong triangle. For a right-angled triangle with sides along the grid lines the check is quick: triangle ABC has legs 3 and 2, so its area is half of 3 times 2, which is 3 square units, and every image must have legs 3 and 2 as well. For a slanting side, use the theorem of Pythagoras: BC runs 3 across and 2 up, so it is the square root of 13, and in the rotated image B prime to C prime runs 2 across and 3 down, still the square root of 13. In an examination, one line stating that the area is unchanged because the transformation is rigid earns credit when asked about the image's area, and it saves recalculating. The habit becomes more important in the next lessons, where enlargements do change lengths and areas, and knowing exactly which transformations preserve them prevents confusion.

### subtopic_6 — The Stencil That Went on Backwards
**Assistant:** How does walking round the corners tell me which way the triangle goes?

**Tutor:** Pick the vertices in their alphabetical order and trace the route on the grid: from the first vertex to the second, then to the third, then back to the first. Imagine standing in the middle of the triangle and watching the route; it goes either the same way as the hands of a clock or the opposite way. For the stencil, D at 1 semicolon 2 to E at 5 semicolon 2 goes right along the bottom, then E to F at 1 semicolon 5 goes up and left, then F back to D goes down. Along the bottom to the right, up the slant, down the left side: that is anticlockwise. For the painted marker, with D at negative 1 semicolon 2, E at negative 5 semicolon 2 and F at negative 1 semicolon 5, the route goes left along the bottom, up and right, then down: clockwise. The key is to use the same order of labels for the original and the image, D then E then F for both. If the directions agree, the transformation kept orientation and was a rotation or translation; if they disagree, it was a reflection. A quick sketch is enough; you do not need any calculation, which makes this the fastest test there is.
