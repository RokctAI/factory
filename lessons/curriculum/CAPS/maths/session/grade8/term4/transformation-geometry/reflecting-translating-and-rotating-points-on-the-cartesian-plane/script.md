# Part 1 — Expert

This session transforms points on the Cartesian plane. A point and its image are named with prime notation, and each transformation is given as a coordinate rule: reflection in the x-axis changes the sign of y, reflection in the y-axis changes the sign of x, and reflection in the line y equals x swaps the coordinates; a translation adds fixed amounts to x and to y, carrying points within and across quadrants; and rotation about the origin through 90 degrees anticlockwise, 180 degrees or 90 degrees clockwise follows its own sign-and-swap rule, with rotation about another centre handled by counting from that centre. The anchor point is A at 3 semicolon 2, with further points in every quadrant.

## Subtopic: Points, Images and the Three Reflections

A transformation takes a point to a new position called its image. The point A has image A prime, written with a small dash after the letter; a second transformation would give A double prime. The rule of a transformation says how the coordinates change: x semicolon y maps to the image's coordinates.

Reflection in the x-axis. The x-axis is the mirror. A point and its image are the same distance from the axis, on opposite sides, directly above and below each other. So the x-coordinate stays and the y-coordinate changes sign. Rule: x semicolon y maps to x semicolon negative y. The point A at 3 semicolon 2 has image A prime at 3 semicolon negative 2. The point negative 4 semicolon negative 1 maps to negative 4 semicolon 1. A point on the x-axis, such as 5 semicolon 0, maps to itself.

Reflection in the y-axis. The y-axis is the mirror. The y-coordinate stays and the x-coordinate changes sign. Rule: x semicolon y maps to negative x semicolon y. A at 3 semicolon 2 maps to negative 3 semicolon 2. The point negative 4 semicolon negative 1 maps to 4 semicolon negative 1. Points on the y-axis stay put.

Reflection in the line y equals x. The mirror is the diagonal line through the origin where every point has equal coordinates. Reflecting across it swaps the two coordinates. Rule: x semicolon y maps to y semicolon x. A at 3 semicolon 2 maps to 2 semicolon 3. The point negative 4 semicolon 1 maps to 1 semicolon negative 4. A point on the line, such as 5 semicolon 5, maps to itself. To see why the swap is right, plot 3 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 3 and draw the line y equals x: the two points sit symmetrically either side of it, and the segment joining them is perpendicular to the line.

Every reflection keeps the distance from the mirror. Check an image by measuring: A at 3 semicolon 2 is 2 units above the x-axis, so A prime must be 2 units below, at 3 semicolon negative 2. For the line y equals x, the check is that the midpoint of the point and its image lies on the line: the midpoint of 3 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 3 is 2,5 semicolon 2,5, which is on the line.

Reflections reverse orientation: a figure and its image are mirror images, like a left hand and a right hand. For a single point that does not show; for triangles in a later lesson, it does. Questions on reflections follow now.

## Subtopic: Translations Within and Across Quadrants

A translation slides every point the same distance in the same direction: so many units left or right, so many up or down. Nothing turns and nothing flips; the image is the same way up as the original, just moved.

The rule adds fixed numbers to the coordinates. A translation of 4 units right and 3 units up: x semicolon y maps to x plus 4 semicolon y plus 3. A translation of 2 units left and 5 units down: x semicolon y maps to x minus 2 semicolon y minus 5. Right and up are positive; left and down are negative.

The point A at 3 semicolon 2 translated 4 right and 3 up: 3 plus 4 is 7, 2 plus 3 is 5, image 7 semicolon 5. Translated 2 left and 5 down: 3 minus 2 is 1, 2 minus 5 is negative 3, image 1 semicolon negative 3. The second image is in a different quadrant from A — the fourth, below the x-axis — so the translation carried the point across an axis. Translations cross quadrants whenever the shift is bigger than the point's distance from the axis.

A point in the third quadrant, negative 6 semicolon negative 2, translated 8 right and 2 up: negative 6 plus 8 is 2, negative 2 plus 2 is 0, image 2 semicolon 0 — on the x-axis, having crossed from the third quadrant to the boundary of the first and fourth. Translated 1 right and 1 down instead: negative 5 semicolon negative 3, still in the third quadrant.

Reading a translation from a point and its image: subtract the original from the image, coordinate by coordinate. From 3 semicolon 2 to 7 semicolon 5: 7 minus 3 is 4, 5 minus 2 is 3, so 4 right and 3 up. From 3 semicolon 2 to negative 1 semicolon 2: negative 1 minus 3 is negative 4, 2 minus 2 is 0, so 4 left and no vertical move.

A translation can be described in words — 4 units right and 3 units up — or as a rule — x semicolon y maps to x plus 4 semicolon y plus 3. Both are accepted; the rule is more precise and should be given when asked for a rule.

Check an image by plotting both points and counting squares: from A to A prime should be exactly the stated number of squares across and up. Questions on translations follow.

## Subtopic: Rotations About the Origin

A rotation turns a point about a fixed centre through a given angle in a given direction. In Grade 8 the centre is usually the origin and the angles are 90 degrees, 180 degrees and 270 degrees; anticlockwise is the standard direction, and clockwise is stated when meant. A rotation of 90 degrees clockwise is the same as 270 degrees anticlockwise.

The point A at 3 semicolon 2. Picture the segment from the origin to A: 3 across, 2 up. Rotate it a quarter-turn anticlockwise about the origin. The segment now points up and to the left: 2 across to the left, 3 up. The image is negative 2 semicolon 3. Rule for 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin: x semicolon y maps to negative y semicolon x — swap the coordinates and change the sign of the new first one.

Rotate A a half-turn, 180 degrees. The segment points the opposite way: 3 to the left, 2 down. Image negative 3 semicolon negative 2. Rule: x semicolon y maps to negative x semicolon negative y — both signs change. Direction does not matter for a half-turn.

Rotate A 90 degrees clockwise, which is 270 anticlockwise. The segment points down and to the right: 2 across, 3 down. Image 2 semicolon negative 3. Rule: x semicolon y maps to y semicolon negative x — swap, and change the sign of the new second one.

Check with distances: every image is the same distance from the origin as A, since rotation preserves distance from the centre. A is the square root of 13 from the origin; so are negative 2 semicolon 3, negative 3 semicolon negative 2 and 2 semicolon negative 3. And the four points A, its 90-degree image, its 180-degree image and its 270-degree image sit at the corners of a square centred on the origin.

Try a point in another quadrant. The point negative 1 semicolon 4, rotated 90 anticlockwise: negative y semicolon x is negative 4 semicolon negative 1. Rotated 180: 1 semicolon negative 4. Rotated 90 clockwise: y semicolon negative x is 4 semicolon 1.

The rules are easy to confuse, so check with a sketch: draw the point, draw the arrow from the origin, turn the arrow the right way by the right amount, and read off where it points. The 90-degree rules both swap the coordinates; anticlockwise negates the new x, clockwise negates the new y. The 180 rule negates both and swaps nothing. Questions on rotations follow.

## Subtopic: Rotation About Another Point and Combining Transformations

Rotation can be about any centre, not only the origin. The syllabus says rotation about a given point, so the method must work for a centre such as 1 semicolon 1. The idea: measure the point from the centre instead of from the origin, rotate those offsets with the usual rule, and add the centre back.

Rotate A at 3 semicolon 2 through 90 degrees anticlockwise about the centre C at 1 semicolon 1. Offsets of A from C: 3 minus 1 is 2 across, 2 minus 1 is 1 up — A is 2 right and 1 up from C. Rotate the offset 2 semicolon 1 by 90 anticlockwise using negative y semicolon x: negative 1 semicolon 2. Add the centre back: 1 plus negative 1 is 0, 1 plus 2 is 3. Image 0 semicolon 3. Check: A is the square root of 5 from C, and 0 semicolon 3 is 1 left and 2 up from C, also the square root of 5.

Rotate A 180 degrees about C at 1 semicolon 1. Offset 2 semicolon 1 becomes negative 2 semicolon negative 1; add the centre: negative 1 semicolon 0. The centre is the midpoint of A and its image, as it must be for a half-turn: the midpoint of 3 semicolon 2 and negative 1 semicolon 0 is 1 semicolon 1.

On squared paper the same thing is done by counting: from the centre, count across and up to the point; turn those counts by the rule; count from the centre to place the image. The centre itself never moves.

Transformations can be done one after another. Reflect A at 3 semicolon 2 in the x-axis to get 3 semicolon negative 2, then translate 1 left and 4 up to get 2 semicolon 2. The order matters: translating first gives 2 semicolon 6, and reflecting that in the x-axis gives 2 semicolon negative 6, a different point. Describe a sequence in the order it is performed, and name the images A prime and A double prime.

Two reflections in the axes, one after the other, give a rotation: reflect 3 semicolon 2 in the x-axis to 3 semicolon negative 2, then in the y-axis to negative 3 semicolon negative 2 — the 180-degree rotation about the origin. Two translations give one translation: 4 right then 2 left is 2 right.

Every transformation in this lesson is a rigid motion: distances between points and the sizes of angles do not change, so a figure and its image are congruent. Enlargement, which changes size, is a later lesson. Questions on other centres and sequences follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same transformations as stories about a dance routine called in coordinates across a hall floor, a mirror on the wall of a tiling shop, and a game of battleships where a learner moved her whole fleet by one rule — stories about moving everything the same way and knowing exactly where it lands.

## Subtopic: The Dance Called in Coordinates

A dance teacher rehearses a routine in a hall whose floor is marked out as a grid, with the centre of the floor as the origin, one tile to a unit, the stage direction as positive y and the right-hand wall as positive x. Instead of shouting left and right, which dancers hear differently depending on which way they face, she calls transformations.

A dancer stands at 3 semicolon 2. Reflect in the x-axis: the dancer walks to 3 semicolon negative 2, the same distance behind the centre line as she was in front. Reflect in the y-axis: to negative 3 semicolon 2. Reflect in the line y equals x — the teacher has taped the diagonal on the floor — and the dancer goes to 2 semicolon 3, one tile over and one tile forward, across the tape.

Translate 4 right and 3 up: every dancer adds 4 to x and 3 to y. The one at 3 semicolon 2 goes to 7 semicolon 5; the one at negative 6 semicolon negative 2 goes to negative 2 semicolon 1, crossing the centre line on the way. The formation keeps its shape because every dancer moves the same amount; the teacher calls it a slide.

Rotate 90 degrees anticlockwise about the centre: each dancer looks at the centre of the floor, imagines the line from the centre to herself, and swings a quarter-turn to the left. The dancer at 3 semicolon 2 goes to negative 2 semicolon 3. The dancer at 0 semicolon 4, straight ahead of centre, goes to negative 4 semicolon 0, by the left wall. The dancer standing on the centre does not move at all.

Rotate 180: everyone crosses to the diagonally opposite tile — 3 semicolon 2 to negative 3 semicolon negative 2 — and the formation appears upside down from the stage.

Then she rotates about a dancer instead of the centre. The dancer at 1 semicolon 1 stands still and the others turn a quarter anticlockwise around her. The dancer at 3 semicolon 2 is 2 right and 1 up from the pivot; after the turn she is 1 left and 2 up from the pivot, at 0 semicolon 3. The teacher checks: both positions are the same distance from the pivot, so the dancer has moved along a circle around her, which is what rotation means.

The routine's final sequence is a reflection in the x-axis followed by a translation of 1 left and 4 up. The dancer at 3 semicolon 2 ends at 2 semicolon 2. A dancer who does the translation first ends at 2 semicolon negative 6, in the wrong corner, and is told that order is part of the instruction. Questions on the dance follow.

## Subtopic: The Mirror in the Tile Shop and the Battleship Fleet

A tile shop has a display wall with a floor-to-ceiling mirror along one edge, and the owner lays sample tiles on a gridded mat in front of it so customers see a pattern twice the size. A learner helping in the shop works out where every reflected tile appears. With the mirror along the y-axis of the mat, a tile at 3 semicolon 2 appears in the mirror at negative 3 semicolon 2 — same height, same distance from the mirror, opposite side. A tile touching the mirror at 0 semicolon 2 appears to touch its own reflection. The owner asks for the mirror to be moved to the diagonal of the mat for a new display; the learner tapes the line y equals x and the tile at 3 semicolon 2 now appears at 2 semicolon 3, while a tile at 4 semicolon 4, lying on the line, is its own image.

The learner also notices what the mirror does that the other transformations do not: a tile printed with a letter R shows a backwards R in the mirror. Reflections reverse orientation. A translated or rotated R is still an R, just moved or turned.

At home, her younger brother is losing at battleships, a game played on a grid. She teaches him to move his whole fleet by a rule between games so his opponent's memory is useless. Translate every ship 3 right and 2 down: the ship at 2 semicolon 7 goes to 5 semicolon 5, the one at negative 4 semicolon 3 to negative 1 semicolon 1, and the fleet keeps its shape and spacing because every ship moves identically. Rotate the fleet 90 clockwise about the origin: the ship at 2 semicolon 7 goes to 7 semicolon negative 2 by the rule y semicolon negative x, and a ship at negative 4 semicolon 3 goes to 3 semicolon 4. He checks each ship's distance from the origin before and after and finds them the same — rotation turns, it does not stretch.

He wants to rotate about his flagship at 1 semicolon 1 instead. For the ship at 2 semicolon 7: offset 1 right and 6 up; rotate 90 clockwise: 6 right and 1 down; add the flagship: 7 semicolon 0. The flagship stays where it is, which he says is the point of being the flagship.

He tries a reflection in the x-axis and asks whether his fleet is still the same fleet. His sister says: congruent, yes — same ships, same gaps — but mirror-imaged, so a ship that pointed north-east now points south-east. For battleships that does not matter; for a pattern of letters on a tile, it is everything. Questions on the mirror and the fleet follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for transforming points. One: write the point's coordinates and name the image with a prime. Two: apply the rule. Reflection in the x-axis: change the sign of y. Reflection in the y-axis: change the sign of x. Reflection in the line y equals x: swap the coordinates. Translation: add the horizontal shift to x and the vertical shift to y, with right and up positive. Rotation about the origin: 90 anticlockwise, negative y semicolon x; 180, negative x semicolon negative y; 90 clockwise, y semicolon negative x. Rotation about another centre: subtract the centre, rotate, add the centre back. Three: for a sequence, do the transformations in the stated order, naming A prime and A double prime. Four: check by plotting — equal distances from a mirror line, the stated squares for a slide, equal distance from the centre for a rotation — and by sense: a half-turn's centre is the midpoint of point and image.

A typical examination item gives a point and asks for its image under a named transformation. Apply the rule, write the coordinates with a semicolon.

Another gives a point and asks for its image under a sequence. In order, naming each image.

Another gives a translation in words and asks for the rule, or the rule and asks for words.

Another asks for the image under rotation about a given centre not at the origin. Offsets, rotate, add back.

Another asks you to plot a point and its images under several transformations on one grid. Label each with its prime and the transformation.

Errors to avoid. Changing the wrong sign in a reflection. Swapping when reflecting in an axis. Mixing up the two 90-degree rules. Doing a sequence in the wrong order. Forgetting to add the centre back. Writing coordinates with a comma instead of a semicolon. Confusing the point with its image in a translation subtraction.

Looking ahead: the next lesson reverses the question — given a point and its image, name the transformation — and the one after transforms whole triangles. Grade 9 extends to enlargements with any centre and to the general line of reflection. Final questions follow.
