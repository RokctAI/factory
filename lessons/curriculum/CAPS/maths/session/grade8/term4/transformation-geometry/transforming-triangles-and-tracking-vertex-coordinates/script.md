# Part 1 — Expert

This session transforms triangles on the coordinate plane and tracks the coordinates of their vertices. A triangle is transformed by applying the same rule to each vertex: reflection in the x-axis, the y-axis or the line y equals x; translation by a stated shift; or rotation of 90 or 180 degrees about the origin. The image vertices carry primes — A becomes A prime — so that each corner can be matched with its original. Every one of these transformations produces a congruent image with the same side lengths, angles and area, and reflections reverse the order of the vertices around the triangle while translations and rotations keep it. The anchor triangle has vertices A at 1 semicolon 1, B at 4 semicolon 1 and C at 1 semicolon 3, with a right angle at A, AB equal to 3 units and AC equal to 2 units.

## Subtopic: One Vertex at a Time: The Table Method

A triangle is three points joined by sides, so transforming a triangle means transforming three points and joining them again. The rule does not change from point to point: whatever happens to one vertex happens to all three.

Take triangle ABC with A at 1 semicolon 1, B at 4 semicolon 1 and C at 1 semicolon 3. Side AB runs 3 units along the line y equals 1; side AC runs 2 units up the line x equals 1; the right angle is at A.

Reflect ABC in the x-axis. The rule is x semicolon y maps to x semicolon negative y. A at 1 semicolon 1 becomes A prime at 1 semicolon negative 1. B at 4 semicolon 1 becomes B prime at 4 semicolon negative 1. C at 1 semicolon 3 becomes C prime at 1 semicolon negative 3. Join A prime, B prime and C prime: the image is the same right-angled triangle hanging below the x-axis, with the right angle still at A prime.

Organise the work in a table with three columns: the vertex, the original coordinates, and the image coordinates. Write the rule at the top of the image column, so that every entry is produced by the same instruction. The table makes errors visible: if one row does not follow the rule, it stands out against the other two.

The labels matter. A prime is the image of A, B prime the image of B, C prime the image of C. Keeping the letters matched lets you compare each corner with its original and lets a marker follow the work. A triangle drawn in the right place but with the labels muddled has lost the information about which corner went where.

Now reflect the same triangle in the y-axis, rule x semicolon y maps to negative x semicolon y. A prime is negative 1 semicolon 1, B prime is negative 4 semicolon 1, C prime is negative 1 semicolon 3. The image sits in the second quadrant, a mirror copy across the y-axis.

Reflect in the line y equals x, rule x semicolon y maps to y semicolon x. A at 1 semicolon 1 lies on the mirror and stays where it is: A prime is 1 semicolon 1. B prime is 1 semicolon 4 and C prime is 3 semicolon 1. The side AB, which ran across, now runs up; the side AC, which ran up, now runs across. A vertex on the mirror line is its own image, which is a useful check.

After plotting, read the image back from the grid and confirm that each vertex matches its table entry. Questions on the table method follow now.

## Subtopic: Translating a Triangle Within and Across Quadrants

A translation slides every vertex by the same amount: the same number of units across and the same number up or down. The rule has the form x semicolon y maps to x plus a semicolon y plus b, where a is the horizontal shift, positive to the right, and b is the vertical shift, positive upward.

Translate triangle ABC 5 units left and 2 units up. The rule is x semicolon y maps to x minus 5 semicolon y plus 2. A at 1 semicolon 1 becomes A prime at negative 4 semicolon 3. B at 4 semicolon 1 becomes B prime at negative 1 semicolon 3. C at 1 semicolon 3 becomes C prime at negative 4 semicolon 5. The whole triangle has crossed from the first quadrant into the second. AB prime still runs 3 units across, now along the line y equals 3; A prime C prime still runs 2 units up, along the line x equals negative 4.

A translation can carry a triangle partly across an axis. Translate ABC 2 units left and 3 units down, rule x semicolon y maps to x minus 2 semicolon y minus 3. A prime is negative 1 semicolon negative 2, B prime is 2 semicolon negative 2, C prime is negative 1 semicolon 0. The image straddles the y-axis, with A prime in the third quadrant, B prime in the fourth and C prime on the x-axis itself. Nothing special happens at the axes during a translation; each coordinate simply has a number added or subtracted, and the signs fall where they fall.

The quickest check on a translation: work out image minus original for each vertex. Every vertex must give the same pair. For the first translation, A gives negative 5 semicolon 2, B gives negative 5 semicolon 2, C gives negative 5 semicolon 2. If one vertex gave a different shift, the image is not a translation of the triangle and one row of the table is wrong.

A translation never turns or flips the shape. A side that was horizontal stays horizontal; a side that sloped up to the right still slopes up to the right at the same steepness. If the image looks tilted compared with the original, a vertex has been miscalculated.

Signs are the usual source of error. Left is a minus in the x-coordinate, down is a minus in the y-coordinate; when the original coordinate is negative, subtracting makes it more negative. Translating negative 4 semicolon 3 by 3 left and 4 down gives negative 7 semicolon negative 1, not negative 1 semicolon negative 1. Questions on translating triangles follow now.

## Subtopic: Rotating a Triangle About the Origin

A rotation about the origin turns the whole triangle around the point 0 semicolon 0, and each vertex follows the same rule. For 90 degrees anticlockwise the rule is x semicolon y maps to negative y semicolon x. For 90 degrees clockwise it is x semicolon y maps to y semicolon negative x. For 180 degrees, in either direction, it is x semicolon y maps to negative x semicolon negative y.

Rotate triangle ABC 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin. A at 1 semicolon 1 becomes negative 1 semicolon 1. B at 4 semicolon 1 becomes negative 1 semicolon 4. C at 1 semicolon 3 becomes negative 3 semicolon 1. The image is in the second quadrant. The side AB, which ran 3 units to the right from A, now runs 3 units up from A prime; the side AC, which ran 2 units up, now runs 2 units to the left. Each side has turned a quarter-turn anticlockwise, which is exactly what a rotation should do.

Rotate ABC 90 degrees clockwise. A prime is 1 semicolon negative 1, B prime is 1 semicolon negative 4, C prime is 3 semicolon negative 1. The image is in the fourth quadrant; AB now runs down and AC now runs to the right.

Rotate ABC through 180 degrees. A prime is negative 1 semicolon negative 1, B prime is negative 4 semicolon negative 1, C prime is negative 1 semicolon negative 3. The image is in the third quadrant, upside down and back to front, but this is not a reflection: it is a half-turn, and it can be checked by noting that the origin is the midpoint of each vertex and its image.

Three checks for a rotation about the origin. First, every vertex stays the same distance from the origin: B at 4 semicolon 1 is the square root of 17 from the origin, and so is negative 1 semicolon 4, because 1 plus 16 is 17 both times. Second, every side turns through the same angle as the whole figure. Third, the image is the same shape and size as the original, and its corners still go round in the same order, which is the subject of the next section.

The most common error is mixing up the clockwise and anticlockwise rules, or negating the wrong coordinate after the swap. Do one vertex, then picture it: does the arrow from the origin to the vertex turn the right way? Then apply the same rule to the other two vertices without re-thinking it each time. Questions on rotating triangles follow now.

## Subtopic: What Stays the Same: Congruence and Orientation

Reflections, translations and rotations are called rigid transformations because the triangle moves without stretching. The image is congruent to the original: the same three side lengths, the same three angles and the same area.

Check with triangle ABC. AB is 3 units, AC is 2 units, and the area is half of 3 times 2, which is 3 square units. After the reflection in the x-axis, A prime B prime is from 1 semicolon negative 1 to 4 semicolon negative 1, still 3 units; A prime C prime is from 1 semicolon negative 1 to 1 semicolon negative 3, still 2 units; the area is still 3 square units. The third side BC is the square root of 13 in the original, because it runs 3 across and 2 up, and it is the square root of 13 in every image. If an image side comes out a different length, a vertex is wrong.

One property does change under some of these transformations: orientation, the direction in which the vertices go round. In triangle ABC, walk from A to B to C and back to A. From A go right to B, then up and left to C, then down to A. That route goes round anticlockwise.

Now walk round the reflection in the x-axis: from A prime at 1 semicolon negative 1 right to B prime at 4 semicolon negative 1, then down and left to C prime at 1 semicolon negative 3, then up to A prime. That route goes round clockwise. The reflection has reversed the orientation, just as a mirror turns a right hand into a left hand.

Walk round the 90-degree anticlockwise rotation: A prime at negative 1 semicolon 1, up to B prime at negative 1 semicolon 4, then down and left to C prime at negative 3 semicolon 1, then right to A prime. That route is anticlockwise, the same as the original. Rotations keep orientation; so do translations, which do not turn the shape at all.

This gives a test that a single point could never provide. If a triangle and its image have opposite orientations, the transformation was a reflection. If they have the same orientation, it was a rotation or a translation; and if every side kept its direction, it was a translation.

Identifying the transformation of a triangle therefore combines two clues: the coordinate pattern from the previous lesson, applied to each vertex, and the orientation check on the whole figure. Questions on congruence and orientation follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same transformations as stories about a mural designed on a grid for a school wall, a sign-writer whose stencil went on the wrong way round, and a learner who could tell a flip from a turn by walking round the corners — stories about moving a shape and knowing exactly where every corner ends up.

## Subtopic: The Mural on the School Wall

A Grade 8 class in Polokwane is designing a mural for the outside wall of their school hall. The art teacher has divided the wall into a grid of squares, half a metre on each side, with the origin at the centre of the wall. The design is built from one triangular motif repeated four times, and the learners must give the painters the exact grid position of every corner.

The motif is triangle PQR with P at 2 semicolon 1, Q at 6 semicolon 1 and R at 2 semicolon 4. PQ runs 4 units across, PR runs 3 units up, and QR is the slanting side. A learner checks it with the theorem of Pythagoras: 16 plus 9 is 25, so QR is 5 units. On the wall that is 2 and a half metres, and the area is half of 4 times 3, which is 6 square units.

The first copy is a reflection in the y-axis, so the design is symmetric about the centre line of the wall. The learner writes the table: P prime at negative 2 semicolon 1, Q prime at negative 6 semicolon 1, R prime at negative 2 semicolon 4. The painters will see the mirror image on the left.

The second copy is a reflection in the x-axis, so the lower half mirrors the upper. P double prime at 2 semicolon negative 1, Q double prime at 6 semicolon negative 1, R double prime at 2 semicolon negative 4.

The third copy, the teacher says, must fill the bottom-left corner. One learner suggests reflecting the first copy in the x-axis; another suggests rotating the original 180 degrees about the origin. They work out both. Reflecting P prime negative 2 semicolon 1 in the x-axis gives negative 2 semicolon negative 1, Q prime gives negative 6 semicolon negative 1, R prime gives negative 2 semicolon negative 4. Rotating the original 180 degrees gives P at negative 2 semicolon negative 1, Q at negative 6 semicolon negative 1, R at negative 2 semicolon negative 4. The same three points. Two reflections in perpendicular axes through the origin make a half-turn, and the class has discovered it from the table.

Before the painting starts, a learner checks every copy: four tables, each side 3, 4 and 5 units, each area 6 square units. The reflected copies go round clockwise where the original goes anticlockwise; the bottom-left copy, a rotation of the original, goes round anticlockwise again. The painters receive one sheet listing twelve corners, and the mural goes up in a single day without one triangle in the wrong place. Questions on the mural follow.

## Subtopic: The Stencil That Went on Backwards

A sign-writer in Durban cuts a triangular arrow stencil to paint direction markers on the floor of a warehouse. The stencil is a right-angled triangle, and on the job sheet it is drawn on a grid with D at 1 semicolon 2, E at 5 semicolon 2 and F at 1 semicolon 5, so that DE runs 4 units, DF runs 3 units, and the arrow points along DE. The job sheet asks for two more markers: one turned a quarter-turn anticlockwise about the origin of the floor plan, and one translated 6 units left.

For the quarter-turn, the sign-writer applies negative y semicolon x to each corner: D prime at negative 2 semicolon 1, E prime at negative 2 semicolon 5, F prime at negative 5 semicolon 1. The arrow now points up the floor.

For the translation, x semicolon y maps to x minus 6 semicolon y: D prime at negative 5 semicolon 2, E prime at negative 1 semicolon 2, F prime at negative 5 semicolon 5.

He paints both. The next morning the warehouse manager phones: the second marker is wrong. It has the right size and position, but the arrow's short side is on the wrong end — the right-angle corner is at the tip, not the tail.

The sign-writer looks at what he did. To reach the second position quickly he flipped the stencil over instead of sliding it. He records the painted corners from the floor: the right angle at negative 1 semicolon 2, the far end of the long side at negative 5 semicolon 2, and the third corner at negative 1 semicolon 5. Compare with the original D at 1 semicolon 2, E at 5 semicolon 2, F at 1 semicolon 5: every x negated, every y the same. A reflection in the y-axis, not a translation.

The orientation confirms it. Walk D, E, F on the original: right, then up and left, then down — anticlockwise. Walk the painted marker in the same order of corners: from negative 1 semicolon 2 left to negative 5 semicolon 2, then up and right to negative 1 semicolon 5, then down — clockwise. Reversed orientation: a reflection. A translation could never have done that, because sliding keeps orientation.

He repaints the marker by sliding the stencil, checking that every corner moved by negative 6 semicolon 0. On every job since, he marks the face side of each stencil, because a flipped stencil is a reflection and a mirror image of an arrow is not the same arrow. Questions on the stencil follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for transforming a triangle. One: write the rule in coordinate form — x semicolon negative y for reflection in the x-axis, negative x semicolon y for the y-axis, y semicolon x for the line y equals x, x plus a semicolon y plus b for a translation, negative y semicolon x for 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin, y semicolon negative x for 90 degrees clockwise, negative x semicolon negative y for 180 degrees. Two: draw a table of vertex, original and image, with the rule heading the image column. Three: apply the rule to each vertex in turn. Four: label the image with primes, A prime for the image of A. Five: plot and join, and read the image back from the grid. Six: check — side lengths and area unchanged, translation shifts all equal, rotation distances from the origin unchanged, orientation reversed only for a reflection.

The method for identifying the transformation of a triangle. Compare each vertex with its image and read the pattern, as for single points; the pattern must be the same for all three vertices. Confirm with orientation. Give the complete description.

A typical examination item gives a triangle on a grid and asks for its image under a stated transformation, with the coordinates of the image vertices. Table, plot, label, check.

Another gives a triangle and its image and asks for the transformation. Pattern on all three vertices, orientation, full description.

Another applies two transformations in turn, for example a reflection in the x-axis followed by a translation 3 units right. Apply the first to get the primes, then the second to get the double primes. Triangle ABC reflected in the x-axis gives A prime at 1 semicolon negative 1; then 3 units right gives A double prime at 4 semicolon negative 1.

Another asks for the area or a side length of the image. It equals the original; say so, and state why: the transformation is rigid.

Errors to avoid. Applying the rule to two vertices and guessing the third. Mixing up the rotation rules. Losing the labels so that A prime is not the image of A. Translating with the wrong sign. Calling a flipped image a rotation without checking orientation. Forgetting that a vertex on a mirror line stays put.

Looking ahead: the next lessons enlarge and reduce triangles, which keeps the shape but not the size, so lengths and areas change by a scale factor. Final questions follow.
