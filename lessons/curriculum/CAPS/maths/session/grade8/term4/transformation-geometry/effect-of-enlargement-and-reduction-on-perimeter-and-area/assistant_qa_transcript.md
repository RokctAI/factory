### subtopic_2 — The Effect on Perimeter
**Assistant:** If the perimeter is multiplied by k, why is the area not multiplied by k as well?

**Tutor:** Because perimeter and area measure different kinds of thing. Perimeter is a length, a single distance around the edge, and every piece of that distance is stretched by the factor k, so the total is multiplied by k. Area counts squares, and a square has two directions, a length and a width. An enlargement stretches both directions by k at the same time, so each unit square becomes a square k by k, which holds k times k unit squares. Take the 3 by 2 rectangle enlarged by 2. The edge goes from 10 units to 20 units, twice as long. But the inside goes from 6 unit squares to 24, because each of the original 6 squares has become a 2 by 2 block of 4 squares. You can see it directly by drawing the enlarged rectangle on squared paper and outlining the original squares inside it. A useful way to remember it is to look at the units: perimeter is in centimetres, one length; area is in square centimetres, length times length, so the factor appears twice. When you meet volume, in cubic centimetres, the factor appears three times, and volumes scale by k cubed.

### subtopic_3 — The Effect on Area: The Square of the Scale Factor
**Assistant:** Does the k squared rule work for shapes that are not rectangles, like circles?

**Tutor:** Yes, for every shape, as long as the enlargement multiplies all lengths by the same factor. For a circle, the radius is a length, so it is multiplied by k, and the area formula pi r squared then gives pi times k r squared, which is k squared times pi r squared, the old area times k squared. A circle of radius 5 has area 25 pi; enlarged by 3, its radius is 15 and its area is 225 pi, which is 9 times 25 pi. For a triangle, the base and the perpendicular height are both lengths, so half of base times height is multiplied by k times k. The triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5 has area 6, and enlarged by 2 it has area 24. For an irregular shape with no formula at all, imagine covering it with a fine grid of tiny squares; the enlargement turns each tiny square into a square k times as wide and k times as tall, so the total area is multiplied by k squared, whatever the outline. The only condition is that the enlargement is uniform. If a shape is stretched by 2 horizontally and 3 vertically, it is no longer similar to the original, and its area is multiplied by 6, not by a square.

### subtopic_4 — Working Backwards and Comparing with Proportion
**Assistant:** If the area doubles, why is the scale factor not 2?

**Tutor:** Because a scale factor of 2 would multiply the area by 2 squared, which is 4, giving four times the area, not twice. To find the factor for a given area ratio, you reverse the squaring by taking the square root. Twice the area needs k squared to equal 2, so k is the square root of 2, which is about 1,41. In practical terms, each side has to grow by only about 41 percent for the area to double. Check it: a square of side 10 has area 100; a square of side 14,14 has area about 199,9, very close to 200. The same reasoning gives the square root of 3, about 1,73, for three times the area, and the square root of 9, which is exactly 3, for nine times the area. This is why the vegetable patch with money for 150 bags, three times the old 50, could have a side of about 8,66 metres, not 15. Whenever a question gives a ratio of areas and asks about lengths, the square root is the step that connects them, and forgetting it is the most common error in the topic.

### subtopic_6 — Two Pizzas and a Reunion Photograph
**Assistant:** The first learner compared the pizza diameters and got the wrong answer. What exactly went wrong?

**Tutor:** He compared the wrong quantity. The diameter is a length, and the question was about the amount of pizza, which is an area. Comparing lengths showed that the large pizza is one and a half times as wide, and since the price ratio of about 1,83 is more than 1,5, the large pizza looked like poor value. But the amount of pizza scales with the square of the width: 1,5 squared is 2,25, so the large pizza has two and a quarter times as much. Against that, a price ratio of 1,83 is good value. The general lesson is to ask what the price is really paying for. For pizza, carpet, paint, paper or grass seed, the cost depends on area, so compare areas, which means squaring the length ratio. For fencing, crust, ribbon or skirting board, the cost depends on length, so compare lengths directly. The calculation with actual areas, 314 square centimetres for R60 against about 707 for R110, confirms it: about 5,24 square centimetres per rand for the small and about 6,43 for the large. The area argument reached the same conclusion in one line, without needing pi at all.
