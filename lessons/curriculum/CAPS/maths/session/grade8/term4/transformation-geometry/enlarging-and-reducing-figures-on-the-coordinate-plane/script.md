# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates the coordinates of the vertices of figures enlarged or reduced by a given scale factor. When the centre of enlargement is the origin, each vertex x semicolon y has image k x semicolon k y: both coordinates multiplied by the scale factor k. Each image vertex lies on the line from the origin through the original vertex, k times as far from the origin. Sides of the image are parallel to the matching sides of the original and k times as long, the angles are unchanged, and the area is multiplied by k squared. A scale factor greater than 1 enlarges the figure away from the origin; a factor between 0 and 1 reduces it towards the origin. The anchor triangle has vertices A at 1 semicolon 2, B at 3 semicolon 2 and C at 1 semicolon 5.

## Subtopic: The Rule: Multiply Both Coordinates by k

An enlargement needs two things: a scale factor and a centre. The centre is the fixed point from which everything is stretched. In Grade 8 the centre is the origin, and then the rule is short: x semicolon y maps to k x semicolon k y. Multiply both coordinates of each vertex by the scale factor.

Take triangle ABC with A at 1 semicolon 2, B at 3 semicolon 2 and C at 1 semicolon 5. Enlarge it from the origin by the factor 2. A prime is 2 times 1 semicolon 2 times 2, which is 2 semicolon 4. B prime is 6 semicolon 4. C prime is 2 semicolon 10. Plot the image: it is the same shape, twice the size, and further from the origin.

Enlarge ABC by the factor 3 instead. A prime is 3 semicolon 6, B prime is 9 semicolon 6, C prime is 3 semicolon 15.

Use the same table as for the other transformations: vertex, original, image, with the rule k x semicolon k y heading the image column. Every vertex gets the same factor, and both of its coordinates are multiplied — not one coordinate only, and not one added to the other.

Why does multiplying the coordinates work? The point A at 1 semicolon 2 is reached from the origin by going 1 across and 2 up. Enlarging from the origin by 2 means going twice as far in the same direction: 2 across and 4 up. That is exactly 2 semicolon 4. The direction from the origin is unchanged; only the distance is multiplied.

So the line from the origin through A passes through A prime as well, and so on for each vertex. Drawing these three lines — called rays — from the origin through the original vertices is a way to construct an enlargement without coordinates: measure along each ray to k times the distance.

The rule works with negative coordinates too, because multiplying by a positive k keeps the sign. The point negative 3 semicolon 1 enlarged by 2 is negative 6 semicolon 2, still in the second quadrant, twice as far out along the same ray.

Notice the difference from a translation. A translation adds the same numbers to each coordinate and slides the figure without changing its size. An enlargement multiplies each coordinate and changes the size. If the image of 1 semicolon 2 is 3 semicolon 4, the change could be adding 2 to each coordinate, a translation; for an enlargement from the origin, 3 semicolon 4 would require 1 times k equal to 3 and 2 times k equal to 4, which cannot both hold. Questions on the rule follow now.

## Subtopic: Investigating Sides, Angles and Distances

Once the image coordinates are known, the effect on the figure can be investigated by measuring.

Side lengths. In triangle ABC, AB runs from 1 semicolon 2 to 3 semicolon 2, so it is 2 units long. AC runs from 1 semicolon 2 to 1 semicolon 5, 3 units long. BC runs 2 across and 3 up, so by the theorem of Pythagoras it is the square root of 13, about 3,61 units. In the image enlarged by 2, A prime B prime runs from 2 semicolon 4 to 6 semicolon 4, 4 units; A prime C prime runs from 2 semicolon 4 to 2 semicolon 10, 6 units; B prime C prime runs 4 across and 6 up, the square root of 52, which is 2 times the square root of 13, about 7,21. Every side has doubled.

Directions. AB is horizontal and so is A prime B prime. AC is vertical and so is A prime C prime. BC goes 2 left for every 3 up; B prime C prime goes 4 left for every 6 up, the same slope. Each side of the image is parallel to the matching side of the original. That is why the angles are unchanged: the right angle at A is a right angle at A prime, and the other two angles are matched as well.

Distances from the origin. A is the square root of 1 plus 4, which is the square root of 5, from the origin. A prime at 2 semicolon 4 is the square root of 4 plus 16, the square root of 20, which is 2 times the square root of 5. Twice as far, as the rule promised.

Area. ABC has a right angle at A with legs 2 and 3, so its area is half of 2 times 3, which is 3 square units. A prime B prime C prime has legs 4 and 6, area half of 4 times 6, which is 12 square units. Four times as large: 2 squared, as the previous lesson predicts.

For the factor 3 the same investigation gives sides 6, 9 and 3 times the square root of 13, and area half of 6 times 9, which is 27 square units, nine times the original.

The pattern holds for every figure enlarged from the origin: lengths times k, directions unchanged, angles unchanged, distances from the origin times k, and area times k squared. The image is similar to the original and placed so that the origin, each vertex and its image lie in a straight line.

One more observation: the origin itself does not move, because 0 times k is 0. A vertex at the origin stays at the origin; the centre of enlargement is the one fixed point. Questions on the investigation follow now.

## Subtopic: Reductions: Scale Factors Between 0 and 1

A reduction uses the same rule with a scale factor between 0 and 1. The image is smaller and closer to the origin.

Take triangle PQR with P at 4 semicolon 6, Q at 8 semicolon 2 and R at 2 semicolon 2. Reduce it from the origin by the factor one half. P prime is one half of 4 semicolon one half of 6, which is 2 semicolon 3. Q prime is 4 semicolon 1. R prime is 1 semicolon 1. Every vertex has moved halfway towards the origin along its ray.

Check the sides. QR runs from 8 semicolon 2 to 2 semicolon 2, 6 units; Q prime R prime runs from 4 semicolon 1 to 1 semicolon 1, 3 units. Half. The area of PQR: base QR is 6 and the height from P down to the line y equals 2 is 4, so the area is half of 6 times 4, which is 12 square units. The image has base 3 and height 2, area 3 square units — one quarter, which is one half squared.

Reductions often produce fractions or decimals. Triangle ABC from the previous sections, with A at 1 semicolon 2, B at 3 semicolon 2 and C at 1 semicolon 5, reduced by one half, gives 0,5 semicolon 1, 1,5 semicolon 1 and 0,5 semicolon 2,5. That is correct; coordinates need not be whole numbers. Plot them carefully, using half-squares on the grid.

A factor such as one third or 0,25 works in the same way. The point 6 semicolon 9 reduced by one third is 2 semicolon 3. The point 8 semicolon negative 4 reduced by 0,25 is 2 semicolon negative 1, still in the fourth quadrant.

Reducing and enlarging undo each other. Reducing by one half and then enlarging by 2 returns every vertex to its starting place, because one half times 2 is 1. In general, the enlargement by k is undone by the reduction by 1 divided by k. If a question gives an image and the scale factor, and asks for the original, divide each coordinate by k.

For example, A prime is 9 semicolon 6 after an enlargement by 3. The original A is 9 divided by 3 semicolon 6 divided by 3, which is 3 semicolon 2.

A scale factor of 1 leaves everything where it is. A factor of 0 would shrink every point onto the origin, which is no longer a figure, so the scale factors used are positive and not zero. Questions on reductions follow now.

## Subtopic: Finding and Testing the Scale Factor

The investigation can be reversed: given a figure and its image, find the scale factor and decide whether the image really is an enlargement from the origin.

To find k, divide an image coordinate by the matching original coordinate. If A at 2 semicolon 3 has image 8 semicolon 12, then 8 divided by 2 is 4 and 12 divided by 3 is 4, so k is 4. Both coordinates must give the same factor, and every vertex must give the same factor. If any division gives a different number, the image is not an enlargement from the origin with that factor.

Test a claimed enlargement. Triangle with vertices 1 semicolon 1, 3 semicolon 1 and 1 semicolon 4 has an image with vertices 3 semicolon 3, 9 semicolon 3 and 3 semicolon 12. Divisions: 3 over 1, 3 over 1, 9 over 3, 3 over 1, 3 over 1, 12 over 4. All equal 3. An enlargement from the origin by 3.

Another image: 4 semicolon 4, 8 semicolon 4 and 4 semicolon 10. Divisions: 4, 4, then 8 over 3 is about 2,67, which differs. Not an enlargement from the origin. In fact this image is similar to the original — sides 4 and 6 against 2 and 3 — so it is an enlargement by 2, but its position is not the one the origin rule gives: the correct image would be 2 semicolon 2, 6 semicolon 2 and 2 semicolon 8, so this one has also been translated 2 units right and 2 up. The coordinates reveal what a glance at the shape cannot.

Zero coordinates need care: a vertex on an axis, such as 5 semicolon 0, gives 0 divided by 0 for its second coordinate, which says nothing. Use the other coordinate and the other vertices.

The scale factor from coordinates and the scale factor from lengths must agree. If the image of a 2-unit side is 6 units, k is 3, and every image coordinate must be 3 times the original. Use one to check the other.

Connecting to the previous lesson: once k is known from the coordinates, the perimeter of the image is k times the original and its area k squared times. A rectangle with vertices at the origin, 4 semicolon 0, 4 semicolon 3 and 0 semicolon 3 has perimeter 14 and area 12. Enlarged by 2,5, its vertices are at the origin, 10 semicolon 0, 10 semicolon 7,5 and 0 semicolon 7,5, its perimeter 35 and its area 75, which is 12 times 6,25. Questions on finding and testing the scale factor follow now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rule as stories about a school logo drawn on a grid and blown up for a banner, a photocopier set to 50 percent and then to 71, and a learner who noticed that A5 paper is A4 paper reduced by the square root of one half — stories about multiplying every coordinate by the same number.

## Subtopic: The Logo on the Banner

The learners' council at a school in Bloemfontein designs a logo for the school's open day: a simple house shape representing a school building. It is drawn on squared paper with the bottom-left corner at the origin and one square per centimetre. The vertices are at the origin, 4 semicolon 0, 4 semicolon 3, 2 semicolon 5 and 0 semicolon 3 — a rectangle 4 by 3 with a triangular roof on top.

The logo must go on a banner, enlarged by the factor 4 from the same corner. A learner fills in the table, multiplying both coordinates of each vertex by 4: the origin stays at the origin; 4 semicolon 0 becomes 16 semicolon 0; 4 semicolon 3 becomes 16 semicolon 12; the roof peak 2 semicolon 5 becomes 8 semicolon 20; and 0 semicolon 3 becomes 0 semicolon 12. On the banner the logo will be 16 centimetres wide and 20 centimetres tall.

Another learner wants to know how much paint the banner logo needs, given that the paint tin covers a certain area. The original logo's area: the rectangle is 4 times 3, which is 12 square centimetres, and the roof triangle has base 4 and height 2, area 4 square centimetres, so 16 in total. The enlarged logo: rectangle 16 times 12, which is 192, plus roof half of 16 times 8, which is 64, total 256 square centimetres. And 256 is 16 times 16 — the original area times 4 squared. Sixteen times the paint, not four times.

The roof slope is checked too. On the original, the roof rises 2 for every 2 across; on the banner it rises 8 for every 8 across. Same slope, as the parallel-sides property says, so the roof angle is unchanged and the building does not look squashed.

Then the council asks for a small version for the corner of the programme, reduced by the factor one half from the original drawing. The learner halves every coordinate: the origin, 2 semicolon 0, 2 semicolon 1,5, 1 semicolon 2,5 and 0 semicolon 1,5. Its area is 16 times one quarter, which is 4 square centimetres.

Finally, the council finds that the banner frame is only 60 centimetres tall and the logo could be bigger. What is the largest factor from the original that keeps the peak, originally at height 5, within 60 centimetres? 60 divided by 5 is 12. With k equal to 12 the logo is 48 centimetres wide and 60 tall, and its area is 16 times 144, which is 2 304 square centimetres. They settle on 12, because the coordinates told them exactly where every corner would land. Questions on the logo follow.

## Subtopic: The Photocopier's Percentages

A teacher in Pietermaritzburg photocopies a worksheet diagram — a right-angled triangle — and the copies come out wrong in size. A learner helping in the staff room investigates using coordinates measured in millimetres from the bottom-left corner of the page, because the copier enlarges and reduces from that corner.

On the original, the triangle has vertices at 40 semicolon 20, 160 semicolon 20 and 40 semicolon 110. The horizontal leg is 120 millimetres, the vertical leg 90 millimetres, and the hypotenuse, by the theorem of Pythagoras, 150 millimetres.

The copier was set to 50 percent, a scale factor of one half. The learner predicts the copy's vertices by halving each coordinate: 20 semicolon 10, 80 semicolon 10 and 20 semicolon 55. She measures the copy and finds exactly that: legs of 60 and 45 millimetres and a hypotenuse of 75. The triangle moved towards the corner of the page as well as shrinking, as a reduction from that corner should. The area fell from half of 120 times 90, which is 5 400 square millimetres, to half of 60 times 45, which is 1 350 — one quarter, not one half. That is why the diagram looked so much smaller than the teacher expected from the word half.

The teacher actually wanted to fit two copies of the worksheet, side by side, on one sheet. A sheet of A4 paper is 210 by 297 millimetres. Half of it, folded across the long side, is A5, 148 by 210 millimetres, which has half the area. The copier's preset for A4 to A5 is 71 percent. The learner works out why: halving the area needs k squared to be one half, so k is the square root of one half, which is about 0,71. And the reverse preset, A5 to A4, is 141 percent, because the square root of 2 is about 1,41.

She checks it against the paper sizes: 297 times 0,71 is about 210,9, close to 210, and 210 times 0,71 is about 149, close to 148. The A series of paper is built so that halving the area keeps the shape, and that is only possible because the sides are in the ratio 1 to the square root of 2.

With the copier at 71 percent, the triangle's vertices go to about 28,4 semicolon 14,2, then 113,6 semicolon 14,2 and 28,4 semicolon 78,1, and its area to about half the original. Two worksheets fit on one page, and the diagram is still large enough to read. Questions on the photocopier follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for enlarging or reducing a figure from the origin. One: write the rule x semicolon y maps to k x semicolon k y, with the given scale factor. Two: make a table and multiply both coordinates of every vertex by k. Three: label the image with primes, plot it and join the vertices. Four: check that each image vertex lies on the line from the origin through its original, and that each side of the image is parallel to the matching side and k times as long. Five: if asked, give the perimeter of the image as k times the original and the area as k squared times.

The method for finding the scale factor. Divide each image coordinate by the matching original coordinate; every division must give the same k. Skip any division by zero and rely on the other coordinates. If the factors differ, the image is not an enlargement from the origin; say so.

A typical examination item gives a triangle on a grid and asks for its image enlarged by 2, or reduced by one half, from the origin. Table, plot, label, check.

Another gives a figure and its image and asks for the scale factor. Divide, check all vertices, state k, and say whether it is an enlargement or a reduction.

Another asks how the area or perimeter of the image compares with the original. Perimeter times k, area times k squared, with a sentence of reasoning.

Another gives an image and the factor and asks for the original. Divide each coordinate by k.

Another asks the learner to investigate, by measuring, what happens to the lengths and the angles. State the findings: lengths multiplied by k, angles unchanged, sides parallel.

Errors to avoid. Multiplying only one coordinate. Adding k instead of multiplying. Using a different factor for different vertices. Forgetting that the origin is the centre, so that the image moves as well as grows. Squaring k for perimeter, or forgetting to square it for area. Reading a reduction factor as the number to divide by when it is the number to multiply by: a factor of one half means multiply by one half.

Looking ahead: Grade 9 enlarges from centres other than the origin and uses enlargement to prove that triangles are similar. The coordinate rule here is the simplest case and the foundation for all of that. Final questions follow.
