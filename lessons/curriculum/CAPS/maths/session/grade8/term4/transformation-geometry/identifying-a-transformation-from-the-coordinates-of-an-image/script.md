# Part 1 — Expert

This session identifies the transformation of a point given the coordinates of its image. Comparing the image with the original reveals the rule: an unchanged x with y negated is reflection in the x-axis; x negated with y unchanged is reflection in the y-axis; swapped coordinates is reflection in the line y equals x; both coordinates negated is rotation of 180 degrees about the origin; a swap with one sign changed is a 90-degree rotation about the origin, the sign fixing the direction; and a constant difference in each coordinate is a translation described by those differences. Checks on distance from the axes and from the origin confirm the identification, and cases in which a single point does not distinguish between transformations are resolved with a second point. The anchor pairs begin from the point 3 semicolon 2 and from points in every quadrant.

## Subtopic: Reading the Clues: Signs and Swaps

Given a point and its image, write them side by side and compare coordinate by coordinate. Four questions sort out most cases. Did x stay the same? Did y stay the same? Did a coordinate change sign? Did the two coordinates swap?

Point 3 semicolon 2, image 3 semicolon negative 2. The x stayed; the y changed sign. Reflection in the x-axis. Check: both points are 2 units from the x-axis, on opposite sides, and directly above and below each other.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image negative 3 semicolon 2. The y stayed; the x changed sign. Reflection in the y-axis.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image 2 semicolon 3. The coordinates swapped, no signs changed. Reflection in the line y equals x. Check: the midpoint 2,5 semicolon 2,5 lies on the line y equals x.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image negative 3 semicolon negative 2. Both coordinates changed sign, no swap. Rotation of 180 degrees about the origin. Check: the origin is the midpoint of the point and its image — the midpoint of 3 semicolon 2 and negative 3 semicolon negative 2 is 0 semicolon 0.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image negative 2 semicolon 3. Swapped, and the new first coordinate changed sign. Rotation of 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin — the rule negative y semicolon x.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image 2 semicolon negative 3. Swapped, and the new second coordinate changed sign. Rotation of 90 degrees clockwise about the origin — the rule y semicolon negative x.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image 7 semicolon 5. Nothing swapped or negated; both coordinates increased. Subtract: 7 minus 3 is 4, 5 minus 2 is 3. Translation of 4 units right and 3 units up, rule x semicolon y maps to x plus 4 semicolon y plus 3.

The habit: write the two coordinate pairs one above the other, then say what happened to x and what happened to y. Same, negated, swapped in, or shifted by a constant. The pattern names the transformation.

Points with negative coordinates follow the same reading. Point negative 4 semicolon 1, image negative 4 semicolon negative 1: x same, y negated, reflection in the x-axis. Image 4 semicolon 1: reflection in the y-axis. Image 1 semicolon negative 4: swapped, reflection in y equals x. Image negative 1 semicolon negative 4: swapped with the new first negated — rotation 90 anticlockwise, since negative y semicolon x gives negative 1 semicolon negative 4. Image 4 semicolon negative 1: both negated, rotation 180. Questions on reading clues follow now.

## Subtopic: Describing the Transformation Precisely

Naming the type is not enough; the description must be complete. A reflection needs its mirror line. A translation needs its direction and distance, or its rule. A rotation needs its centre, its angle and its direction.

Reflection: say in the x-axis, in the y-axis, or in the line y equals x. Reflection alone is incomplete.

Translation: say 4 units right and 3 units up, or give the rule x semicolon y maps to x plus 4 semicolon y plus 3. Both are accepted; the rule is unambiguous. For a shift with negative parts, say 2 units left and 5 units down, or x semicolon y maps to x minus 2 semicolon y minus 5. Do not write a translation as a single number; it has two parts.

Rotation: say about the origin, through 90 degrees, anticlockwise. The centre is essential, because a rotation about another point gives a different image. For a half-turn the direction is optional, since 180 clockwise and 180 anticlockwise agree, but stating about the origin is still required. A 90-degree clockwise rotation may also be written as 270 degrees anticlockwise; either is correct, and one must be given.

Marks are commonly split: one for the type, one or two for the details. A reflection in the x-axis earns both; reflection earns one; the point moved earns none.

Translations are read by subtraction, image minus original, and the signs give the direction. From 1 semicolon negative 3 to negative 2 semicolon 4: negative 2 minus 1 is negative 3; 4 minus negative 3 is 7. Translation 3 left and 7 up. From negative 5 semicolon 0 to negative 5 semicolon negative 6: 0 across, negative 6 up — that is, 6 down. A translation can be purely vertical or purely horizontal.

Rotation direction is read from the swap pattern: with the original x semicolon y, an image of negative y semicolon x is anticlockwise and y semicolon negative x is clockwise. If unsure, test on a sketch: draw the arrow from the origin to the point and the arrow to the image and see which way the turn went.

Write the final answer as a full sentence or a labelled phrase: the transformation is a rotation of 90 degrees clockwise about the origin. Questions on describing follow.

## Subtopic: Checks: Distances from Axes, the Origin and the Mirror

Each kind of transformation leaves something unchanged, and the unchanged quantity is a check on the identification.

A reflection keeps the distance from the mirror line. If the image is a reflection in the x-axis, the point and the image are the same distance from the x-axis — same size of y-coordinate — and have the same x. If a candidate image has a different x, it is not a reflection in the x-axis, whatever the signs suggest. For the line y equals x, the check is that the midpoint of the point and the image lies on the line and that the segment joining them runs at right angles to it, going one way across for each the other way up.

A rotation about the origin keeps the distance from the origin. The point 3 semicolon 2 is the square root of 13 from the origin; any rotation of it about the origin has an image also the square root of 13 away. The image negative 2 semicolon 3: 4 plus 9 is 13, yes. The image 2 semicolon negative 3: yes. The image negative 3 semicolon negative 2: yes. The image 7 semicolon 5: 49 plus 25 is 74, no — so 7 semicolon 5 cannot be a rotation about the origin, and it was indeed a translation. Reflections in the axes and in the line y equals x also keep the distance from the origin, since those mirrors pass through the origin, so this test rules out translations but does not separate reflections from rotations; the sign-and-swap pattern does that.

A translation keeps nothing fixed about position — every point moves — but it keeps the differences: the shift is the same for every point. If two points move by different shifts, the transformation is not a translation.

A rotation about a centre other than the origin keeps the distance from that centre. If the image of 3 semicolon 2 is 0 semicolon 3 and the suggested transformation is rotation about 1 semicolon 1, check: 3 semicolon 2 is 2 across and 1 up from the centre, distance the square root of 5; 0 semicolon 3 is 1 across and 2 up from the centre, distance the square root of 5. Consistent. And the offsets 2 semicolon 1 and negative 1 semicolon 2 are related by the 90-anticlockwise rule, so the rotation is 90 anticlockwise about 1 semicolon 1.

Use the checks in order. First, is the distance from the origin the same? If not, translation, found by subtraction. If so, look at the sign-and-swap pattern to choose between reflections and rotations, and confirm with the mirror-distance or midpoint check. Questions on checks follow.

## Subtopic: When One Point Is Not Enough

A single point and its image sometimes fit more than one transformation, and the honest answer names the possibilities or asks for a second point.

Point 2 semicolon 2, image 2 semicolon 2. The point has not moved. Reflection in the line y equals x fixes every point on that line, so it is a candidate; so is a rotation of 360 degrees, which is doing nothing; so is a translation of zero. The point lies on the mirror, so reflection in y equals x is the usual intended answer, but the point alone cannot prove it; a second point, such as 3 semicolon 1 mapping to 1 semicolon 3, would.

Point 4 semicolon 0, image negative 4 semicolon 0. Reflection in the y-axis fits — x negated, y unchanged. Rotation of 180 about the origin fits too — both negated, and y is zero so negating it changes nothing. One point on the x-axis cannot separate them; a point off the axis can. If 3 semicolon 2 goes to negative 3 semicolon 2, it was the reflection; if to negative 3 semicolon negative 2, the rotation.

Point 3 semicolon 3, image 3 semicolon 3 again — a point on the line y equals x maps to itself under reflection in that line, as above. Point 3 semicolon 3, image negative 3 semicolon negative 3: rotation 180 fits; so does reflection in the line y equals negative x, which is beyond Grade 8 but shows that negated-both is not always a rotation.

Point 3 semicolon 2, image negative 2 semicolon 3. Rotation 90 anticlockwise about the origin fits. Could a reflection fit? A reflection keeps the distance from its mirror; the mirror would have to be the perpendicular bisector of the segment from 3 semicolon 2 to negative 2 semicolon 3, which is a line through the origin — again not one of the three Grade 8 mirrors. Within the syllabus's set of transformations, the rotation is the only answer.

Translations are never ambiguous: the shift is image minus original, and a translation moves every point, so a translation never fixes a point.

The general principle: a transformation is determined by what it does to the whole plane, and one point shows only one piece of that. In an examination, the question usually supplies enough to make the answer unique, or asks which transformation could have produced the image, where could signals that a plausible, complete answer is wanted. When two answers genuinely fit, give both and say what second point would decide. Questions on ambiguity follow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same detective work as stories about a GPS log that showed where a delivery scooter ended but not how, a learner who caught a classmate's homework with a rule that could not have produced the answer, and a photograph of a chessboard taken from an unknown corner — stories about naming the move from where things landed.

## Subtopic: The Scooter's GPS Log

A delivery company tracks its scooters on a map gridded with the depot at the origin, one block to a unit. One morning the log for a scooter shows only two entries: start at 3 semicolon 2, end at 2 semicolon negative 3. The dispatcher wants to know what happened — whether the rider took a legal shortcut or cut through the park — and she asks the learner on work experience to read the move.

The learner writes the pairs. 3 semicolon 2 above 2 semicolon negative 3. The coordinates swapped, and the new second one changed sign: the pattern y semicolon negative x. That is a rotation of 90 degrees clockwise about the depot. He checks the distance: 9 plus 4 is 13 both times, so the scooter ended the same distance from the depot as it started, which fits a rotation and rules out a straight translation. The dispatcher nods: the ring road around the depot is exactly a quarter-circle from that start to that end, and the rider used it.

A second scooter: start negative 1 semicolon 4, end negative 1 semicolon negative 4. The x is the same, the y changed sign. Reflection in the x-axis — the river, which runs along the x-axis on this map. The rider crossed the bridge directly opposite and ended at the mirror-image address. Distance from the depot: 1 plus 16 is 17 both times, consistent with a reflection in a line through the depot.

A third: start 5 semicolon negative 2, end 1 semicolon 6. Nothing swapped or negated cleanly. Distances: 25 plus 4 is 29; 1 plus 36 is 37. Different, so not a rotation about the depot or a reflection through it. Subtract: 1 minus 5 is negative 4, 6 minus negative 2 is 8. Translation 4 blocks west and 8 north. The rider simply drove.

A fourth: start 6 semicolon 0, end negative 6 semicolon 0. The learner pauses. Reflection in the y-axis fits; rotation 180 about the depot fits too. The log does not say which, because the start was on the x-axis. He asks for the mid-trip position; the log shows 0 semicolon 6 halfway. That is on the ring road, a quarter-turn round: the rotation, not the reflection. One extra point decided it.

The dispatcher adopts the learner's method for the whole fleet: compare the pairs, check the distance to the depot, read the pattern, and if the start lies on an axis, ask for a mid-point. By the end of the week the log software prints the transformation name next to each trip. Questions on the GPS log follow.

## Subtopic: The Homework That Could Not Have Happened

A learner is marking a classmate's transformation homework in a peer-review exercise. The classmate was asked to transform five points by stated rules and then, in a second question, to identify transformations from given images. The marker finds that reading the second question backwards exposes mistakes in the first.

Question one asked for the image of 4 semicolon negative 3 under reflection in the y-axis. The classmate wrote 4 semicolon 3. The marker checks: reflection in the y-axis negates x, so the image should be negative 4 semicolon negative 3. The classmate negated y instead — a reflection in the x-axis. The marker writes: the image you gave is the x-axis reflection; the y-axis reflection is negative 4 semicolon negative 3.

Question one also asked for the image of negative 2 semicolon 5 under rotation of 90 degrees anticlockwise about the origin. The classmate wrote 5 semicolon 2. The marker reads it as an identification problem. The original has x equal to negative 2 and y equal to 5. The anticlockwise rule, negative y semicolon x, gives negative 5 semicolon negative 2. The clockwise rule, y semicolon negative x, gives 5 semicolon 2 — exactly the classmate's answer. Wrong direction. The marker notes: your image is the clockwise rotation; anticlockwise gives negative 5 semicolon negative 2.

Question two gave the point 1 semicolon 6 and image 6 semicolon 1 and asked for the transformation. The classmate wrote rotation 90 degrees. The marker checks the distance from the origin: 1 plus 36 is 37 both times, so a rotation is possible; but the pattern is a plain swap with no sign change, which is reflection in the line y equals x, not a rotation. A rotation of 90 would give negative 6 semicolon 1 or 6 semicolon negative 1. The marker writes: reflection in the line y equals x, with the midpoint 3,5 semicolon 3,5 on the line as the check.

Question two also gave 2 semicolon 2 mapping to negative 2 semicolon negative 2. The classmate wrote reflection in the y-axis. The marker checks: reflection in the y-axis would give negative 2 semicolon 2, with y unchanged. Both coordinates changed sign, so rotation 180 about the origin. The marker adds that the classmate's answer could not have happened: no reflection in the y-axis changes y.

The classmate reads the comments and sees the method: every answer can be tested by reading it backwards. The image you wrote names a transformation; if it is not the one asked for, the error shows itself. Questions on the homework follow.

## Subtopic: Method Summary and Examination Technique

The method for identifying a transformation. One: write the point and its image one above the other. Two: compare the distances from the origin — the sum of the squares of the coordinates. Different: translation; find the shift by subtracting original from image and describe it as units left or right and up or down, or as a rule. Same: reflection or rotation; go on. Three: read the pattern. x same and y negated: reflection in the x-axis. y same and x negated: reflection in the y-axis. Swapped, no sign change: reflection in the line y equals x. Both negated, no swap: rotation 180 about the origin. Swapped with the new first negated: rotation 90 anticlockwise about the origin. Swapped with the new second negated: rotation 90 clockwise about the origin. Four: confirm with a check — distance from the mirror, midpoint on the mirror, or distance from the centre. Five: give the complete description: line for a reflection; shift for a translation; centre, angle and direction for a rotation. Six: if the point lies on an axis or on the mirror and two transformations fit, say so and name what second point would decide.

A typical examination item gives one point and its image and asks for the transformation. Pattern, check, full description.

Another gives several pairs and asks for each transformation. Work each independently; different pairs may be different transformations.

Another gives a point, its image and a claimed transformation, and asks whether the claim is correct. Apply the claimed rule to the point and compare.

Another gives a point and its image under a translation and asks for the rule. Subtract.

Another gives a rotation about a centre not at the origin. Compare offsets from the centre.

Errors to avoid. Calling a swap a rotation when no sign changed. Giving the wrong direction for a 90-degree rotation. Omitting the mirror line, the centre or the direction. Reading a translation as original minus image. Assuming a point on an axis fixes the transformation uniquely. Confusing which coordinate was negated.

Looking ahead: the next lesson transforms whole triangles and tracks all three vertices, where the identification methods here apply vertex by vertex and orientation becomes visible. Grade 9 adds reflections in any line and general rotations. Final questions follow.
