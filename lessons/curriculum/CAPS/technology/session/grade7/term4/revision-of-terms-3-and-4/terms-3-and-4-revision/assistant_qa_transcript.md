### subtopic_1 — Term Three: Electricity, Magnetism and Mechanisms
**Assistant:** The examination covers the whole year. How should I divide my revision time between the four terms?

**Tutor:** Follow the weighting the paper follows, which is roughly the time the syllabus gave each topic, and then tilt toward the things that can be tested with a pencil rather than the things that were marked in the practical tasks. Terms one and two, the Jaws of Life and structures, and terms three and four, the crane and emergency situations, each took half the year, so plan about half your time on each half, which means this lesson's two terms deserve about as much again as the first two. Within that, spend most on the content that appears as structured questions with diagrams and short calculations, because that is where the marks sit: mechanical advantage with levers, cranks, pulleys and hydraulics, which spans terms one and three; electric circuits and the electromagnet, drawn and explained; structures, how frames are stiffened and why towers stand, from term two, which returns in the crane and the shelter; and the graphical skills, oblique and isometric drawing, line types, scale, dimensions and flow charts, which the paper almost always tests with a small drawing or an ordering task. Then give solid time to the term four content that is examined as short written answers: the ranked needs of displaced people with reasons and the three figures, the five questions for camp food and the processing methods, fibres and their burning behaviour, the health and safety requirements of a shelter and the properties of indigenous materials, because these are quick marks if you know them and quick losses if you do not. Give the least time to the fine detail of making, how to glue a mast, how to wind a coil neatly, because that was assessed in the PAT; the paper will ask you to describe a method or explain a choice rather than perform it. Across everything, revise the design process itself, because a question can present any product, a crane, a meal, a shelter, a radio, and ask for a brief, specifications and constraints, two ideas, an evaluation or a flow chart, and the answer has the same shape each time. Revise by doing, not reading: draw the circuit, calculate the advantage, sketch the box in oblique, rank the needs, write a brief for something in your kitchen. The paper asks you to do those things, so practise doing them.

### subtopic_2 — Term Four: Needs, Food and Textiles
**Assistant:** A question gives me a product I have never studied, like a solar lamp for a camp, and asks for a design brief with specifications and constraints. How do I start?

**Tutor:** You start the same way every brief in the year started, with three questions and two columns, and the unfamiliar product matters far less than the familiar method. First, read the scenario for the need: who is the user, what problem do they have, where and when. For a solar lamp in a camp, the user is a displaced family in a shelter with no electricity, the problem is light at night without the fire risk of a candle or the cost of batteries, the place is a camp with sun by day and the time is a month or more. Second, write the brief in one or two sentences from those answers: design a solar-charged lamp for a family in an emergency shelter that gives safe light for several hours each night, charges in a day of sun, and can be hung from the shelter's lamp hook. What, for whom, why, and nothing about how. Third, write specifications, each with a number or a test, for what the product must do: light for at least four hours per night; charge fully in six hours of sunlight; bright enough to read by at one metre; hangs from a hook and stands on a floor; survives a drop of one metre; works for a month without maintenance; no exposed hot parts. Four to six is enough; order them by importance to the user. Fourth, write constraints, in a separate column, for the limits on the designer: cost under a stated figure so a thousand can be bought; available or made in South Africa; delivered within two weeks; uses only safe low-voltage parts; recyclable where possible. Check each line by asking whether it is about the product or about the programme. If the question continues into sketches, give two that differ in how they work, a lamp with the panel on its body against a lamp with a separate panel on the shelter roof and a cable, label them and tick them against your specifications; if it continues into evaluation, name observable criteria from your specifications. You have done exactly this for a crane, a camp's food, a shelter textile and a shelter; the examiner is testing whether you can do it for anything, and the lamp is just a new noun. The marks are for the structure of the answer and for specifications that can be measured, so a brief with a number in every line beats a brief with a brilliant idea and no numbers.

### subtopic_3 — Term Four: Shelters, and the Process Across Both Terms
**Assistant:** What are the most common mistakes in the term three and four part of the paper, so I can avoid them?

**Tutor:** Markers see the same handful every year, and most of them are a word or a habit rather than a gap in knowledge, which makes them the cheapest marks to save. First, metal for ferrous metal: any sentence about magnets, sorting or electromagnets that says the magnet picks up metal loses the mark, because the whole point of the investigation was that only iron and steel respond. Second, a fixed pulley said to halve the effort, or a crank called a first-class lever; a fixed pulley changes direction only and its advantage is one, and the crank's load sits between pivot and effort so it is second class. Third, circuit diagrams drawn freehand, with curved wires, the switch drawn closed, a bulb that is a plain circle and no labels; the marks are for a ruler, square corners, correct symbols, an open switch and labels, and they are lost in seconds. Fourth, specifications without numbers or tests, must be strong, must be waterproof, must be nutritious; every specification needs a how-much and a how-tested, and markers are told to withhold the mark otherwise. Fifth, specifications and constraints mixed in one list, or constraints written as if they were things the product does; two columns, and the question is whether the line is about the product or about the maker. Sixth, in the needs ranking, food placed first because hunger is obvious, when shelter and clean water harm sooner; and the related error of treating everyone displaced as a healthy adult, forgetting babies, the elderly, the sick and language. Seventh, oblique drawings with receding edges at 30 or 60 degrees, or dimensions that give the paper size instead of the real size, or no scale in the title block. Eighth, flow charts with steps written as nouns, a decision diamond with one exit, or no test before the end. Ninth, in the impact of technology, seeing technology only as the cure and forgetting that the stove and the blocked drain are technology too, so an answer that names both sides and weighs them scores where a one-sided answer does not. Tenth, in the textiles work, saying polyester is a good fire fabric because it is strong, or calling a repellent-treated fabric waterproof without mentioning pressure. Read your answers back for these ten before you hand in, and you will recover marks that knowledge alone would not.
