# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the revision of terms three and four for the end-of-year examination. The anchor is a classroom in Mthatha where the two terms' design folders, the crane with its electromagnet and the shelter sketches, lie on the front bench as the lesson walks the class through each topic in turn with its key facts, its examinable skill and a sample question. The governing idea is that the second half of the year has two threads, the electromagnet crane in term three and emergency situations in term four, that each topic in them has a small set of definitions, facts and skills the paper tests, and that the design process of investigate, design, make, evaluate and communicate runs through both and is itself examinable. Each topic revised here is examinable in the end-of-year paper, which also covers terms one and two.

## Subtopic: Term Three: Electricity, Magnetism and Mechanisms

Magnetism: a magnet has a north and a south pole, like poles repel and unlike attract, a suspended magnet points north so a compass works, the field is drawn as lines from north to south, and only ferrous metals, iron and steel with nickel and cobalt, are attracted, while copper, brass, aluminium, lead and zinc are not, which is why a magnet sorts scrap. Domains line up to make a magnet and are scattered by hammering or heating. Say ferrous, not metal.

Circuits: cell, conductor, switch and output in a closed loop; symbols drawn with a ruler, cell as long thin and short thick line, switch as a gap with a slant drawn open, bulb as a circle with a cross; series shares current and parallel gets full voltage; metals conduct, plastics insulate. Electromagnets: current in a wire makes a field, a coil concentrates it, an iron core multiplies it, more turns and more current strengthen it, it can be switched off, adjusted and reversed, and it is used in bells, relays, motors and scrap cranes. Mechanisms: mechanical advantage is load over effort, traded for distance and multiplied along a chain; the crank is a second-class lever with pivot at the axle, load at the drum and effort at the handle, advantage handle over drum; the pulley is a wheel and axle for guiding rope, a fixed one changes direction and a movable one halves effort, with advantage equal to the ropes holding the load.

Skills: oblique drawing with the front face true, receding edges at 45 degrees and half length in cabinet oblique, four line types, a stated scale and real dimensions once each with a title block; the flow chart with oval, rectangle, diamond and arrows, steps as instructions in dependency order with tests before assembly; a design brief that says what, for whom and why, with measurable specifications and separate constraints; two sketches that differ, a rubric of observable criteria and an evaluation with reasons. Sample questions: draw the circuit for an electromagnet with an indicator bulb; explain three ways to strengthen an electromagnet; calculate the mechanical advantage of a 90 millimetre handle on a 15 millimetre drum; put six jumbled making steps into a flow chart with a decision.

The questions for this section are with you now: magnetism and ferrous metals, circuits and electromagnets, cranks, pulleys and advantage, and the term's skills.

## Subtopic: Term Four: Needs, Food and Textiles

Refugees and displaced people: a refugee has been forced across a border by war, persecution or disaster, a displaced person is forced from home inside their own country; those who arrive are a mix of babies, the elderly, the sick and the healthy, many languages, some alone; the first problems in order are shelter because cold kills in hours, clean water because dirty water brings disease in a day, sanitation because it protects the water, then food, with medicine, safety and information alongside; needs come before wants and the ranking is reasoned and checked against the mix. Figures: 15 to 20 litres of water per person per day, treated by boiling, chlorine or filter and chlorine and stored covered, a tap within 500 metres; about 2 100 kilocalories per person per day balanced across staple, protein, fat and vegetables, adjusted for babies, the sick, pregnant women and culture; 3.5 square metres of covered space per person.

Emergency food: judged by five questions, keeps without a fridge, nourishes, travels light, cooks with little fuel and water, accepted; processing by drying, milling, tinning, fermenting, salting and fortifying makes food pass, with South African maize meal and flour fortified by law; the camp list is fortified maize meal and rice, beans and tinned pilchards, oil, tinned tomatoes and hardy vegetables; the brief quantifies types and quantities for one hundred people with constraints on cost, fuel, water and storage; a meal is written for ten and scaled, cooked to a sequence of manufacture with hygiene and safety as steps and tests of texture, taste and temperature, and evaluated by others for flavour and texture with nutrition reasoned from the plate.

Protective textiles: start with the hazards of the job, flame, radiant heat, steam, cold water, waves, darkness, and list the properties they demand; cotton soaks and burns to ash, wool chars and insulates when damp, polyester and nylon are strong but melt and drip, aramids char without melting; weaving gives strength, knitting stretch, PVC coating waterproofs without breathing, a laminated membrane breathes, retroreflective tape sends light back; garments are built in layers, outer against weather, middle to insulate, inner for comfort, as in the fire fighter's shell, moisture barrier and liner and the NSRI volunteer's base layer, fleece, coated outer and life jacket. Sample questions: rank the first needs of displaced people with reasons; explain why milk powder suits a camp and fresh milk does not; name three properties a fire fighter's jacket needs and a fibre for each.

The questions for this section are with you now: refugees and the ranked needs with figures, emergency food and its processing, and protective textiles by hazard, fibre and layer.

## Subtopic: Term Four: Shelters, and the Process Across Both Terms

Shelters: shack fires, floods, earthquakes and strife each destroy homes in their own way, with Imizamo Yethu 2017, KwaZulu-Natal April 2022, Orkney 2014 and the violence of 2008 as examples, and technology is both cause, the paraffin stove, the blocked drain, and cure, the tent, the phone alert, with impacts on people and the environment to weigh. The task is a sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter for a family of six for a month: 21 square metres, 1.8 metres headroom, a 60 kilometre per hour wind, waterproof roof, walls and floor, two adults in under an hour with a mallet, carried by two, lasting a month. Health and safety: two metre fire spacing, a stove place and a slow-burning cover, high and low hooded vents against carbon monoxide and damp, a lamp hook, drainage, water within 500 metres, toilets 50 metres away downhill and one per twenty, a door that fastens inside, opaque walls, lit paths and a cleared, high, planned site.

Materials: indigenous houses use grass, saplings, mud, dung, stone and rushes that are readily available, appropriate and environmentally friendly, in domes, cones, thatch, daga and lashings that are sound engineering, with thatch's flammability and slow building as honest limits; the shelter borrows the bent-rod dome, overlaps laid bottom up, eaves, tie-downs, a raised floor, a courtyard layout and the builders themselves. Textiles for the shelter: waterproofed by repellents, wax, oil, silicone, which bead and breathe but leak under pressure, or coatings, acrylic, polyurethane, PVC, which block water but not vapour; tested by drip, spray and pressure; seams must be sealed; in flame cotton burns to ash, synthetics melt and drip, PVC chars with toxic smoke, wool and flame-retardant-treated fabric char and go out. The textile brief writes ten measurable specifications, water, flame, mass, strength, UV, opacity, ventilation, seams, fixings, handling, with constraints of cost and availability, and chooses a flame-retardant coated ripstop fabric for the cover with tarpaulin for the floor, justified by tests. The sketches offer two structures, dome, ridge or box, labelled with door, vents, stove place, tie-downs, pitch and packed form, checked in a table and tested as straw models.

The process runs through both terms and is examinable on its own. Investigate: the scenario, the case studies, the practical tests, the needs ranked. Design: the brief with what, for whom and why, measurable specifications and separate constraints, two ideas that differ, a reasoned choice, a working drawing or specification. Make: a sequence of manufacture as a flow chart with tests, hygiene and safety as steps, made to the drawing, recorded. Evaluate: against the specifications with a rubric of observable criteria, by others, with results, causes and improvements. Communicate: sketches, drawings, charts, tables and a presentation with evidence. A question can ask for any stage of this on any product, a crane, a meal, a shelter, and the answer has the same shape every time.

The error museum, four exhibits for both terms. One: metal for ferrous metal. Two: a specification with no number or test, "must be strong", "must be waterproof". Three: specifications and constraints mixed in one list. Four: food first on the needs list or a sealed shelter with no vents, which are the same error, forgetting what harms soonest.

The questions for this section are with you now: shelter scenarios, task and safety, materials and textiles, and the design process as an examinable whole.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the two folders open on the bench — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Term Three Shelf

Magnets: two poles, like repel, only ferrous metals stick, domains line up. Circuits: cell, switch, wire, bulb in a closed loop, drawn with a ruler and the right symbols. Electromagnet: coil plus iron core, switched off, stronger with turns, current and core.

Crank: a second-class lever, handle over drum. Pulley: a wheel and axle for rope; fixed turns the pull, movable halves it; count the ropes. Advantage is load over effort, paid in distance, multiplied along a chain.

Skills: oblique at 45 degrees and half depth with real sizes; flow charts with ovals, boxes, diamonds; a brief with numbers; a rubric you can see.

Keep the crane in mind and try the questions for this part: magnets, circuits, electromagnets, mechanisms and skills.

## Subtopic: Term Four Shelf: People, Food, Cloth

Refugees cross a border; the displaced stay inside their country. First needs: shelter, clean water, toilets, food, in that order, with reasons. 20 litres, 2 100 kilocalories, 3.5 square metres, each.

Food: five questions; processing by drying, milling, tinning, fermenting, fortifying; maize meal, beans, pilchards, oil, tinned tomatoes; a brief with quantities for one hundred; a recipe for ten scaled up, cooked to a chart with hygiene steps, judged on flavour, texture and nutrition.

Textiles: hazard first, property second. Cotton burns, wool chars, polyester melts, aramids do not. Weave, knit, coat, laminate, reflect. Layers: outer, middle, inner.

Hold the bucket, the bag and the fire jacket in mind and try this part's questions: needs, food and textiles.

## Subtopic: Term Four Shelf: Shelter, and the Whole Process

Fire, flood, quake, strife; technology causes and cures. A sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter for six for a month, with fire spacing, vents, a stove place, drainage, toilets downhill, a door that fastens. Borrow the dome, the overlaps, the eaves and the builders from indigenous houses, not the thatch. Waterproof by repellent or coating, seal the seams, test by drip, spray and pressure; cotton burns, plastics drip, treated cloth chars and stops. Ten-line textile brief; two sketches checked and modelled.

The process: investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate, the same shape for a crane, a meal or a shelter.

Four errors to avoid: metal for ferrous, specifications without numbers, specifications mixed with constraints, forgetting what harms soonest.

Pocket summary of the two terms. Term three: ferrous metals, closed circuits, switchable electromagnets, second-class cranks, wheel-and-axle pulleys, advantage as load over effort, oblique drawing and flow charts. Term four: ranked needs with figures, processed camp food planned and cooked to a chart, textiles chosen by hazard and layered, shelters specified with safety, borrowed from indigenous building, covered in a tested textile and sketched in two structures. Through both, the design process.

Hold the two folders, and take the final questions of the year: shelters, the process, the errors and the summary.
