# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens term four's theme of emergency situations and the investigation of refugees' needs. The anchor is a Technology classroom in Musina, where a class studies photographs and a short account of families arriving at a temporary camp after a flood, and builds from them a list of causes, a profile of who is present and a ranked list of first problems. The governing idea is that people become refugees or displaced persons when war, persecution, natural disaster or political strife makes it unsafe or impossible to stay at home, that those who arrive are a mix of ages, health, skills and languages, that the first problems are shelter from weather, clean water, food, sanitation, medical care, safety and finding family, and that an investigation lists these problems and ranks them by urgency so that designers know what to solve first. Describing the causes, the people and their first needs is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Makes People Leave Home

A refugee is a person who has been forced to leave their home and cross a border to find safety, because staying would mean danger, persecution or death. A person forced from home but still inside their own country is called an internally displaced person, and in South Africa we see both. The causes fall into groups. War and armed conflict drive millions from countries such as the Democratic Republic of Congo, South Sudan and Somalia, and some of those families arrive in South Africa. Persecution, being hunted for your religion, ethnic group, political views or who you are, drives others. Political strife, including xenophobic violence inside South Africa in 2008 and 2015, has displaced thousands of foreign nationals from townships into church halls and police stations overnight.

Nature drives people too. Floods, such as the KwaZulu-Natal floods of April 2022 that destroyed homes and roads and left thousands in community halls, and the Mozambique floods of 2000 and 2019 that sent families across the border. Drought, which empties villages when the crops and cattle die. Earthquakes, which flatten towns in minutes. Fires, especially shack fires in dense informal settlements, where one paraffin stove can take hundreds of homes before dawn, as in Imizamo Yethu in 2017 and in many Cape Town winters. Each cause has its own speed: a flood or fire gives minutes, a drought gives months, a war may give a day or a year.

Whatever the cause, the people who arrive are not a crowd; they are a mix. Babies and small children, who need milk, warmth and protection. Pregnant women and new mothers. Elderly people, who may be frail or ill. People injured in the disaster or the journey. People with chronic illnesses, such as diabetes or HIV, whose medicines were left behind. Healthy adults, who can work and help. People who speak Zulu, Shona, French, Portuguese, Somali, Arabic, and who may not understand each other or the officials. Families that arrived whole, and people who arrived alone, not knowing where their children are. An investigation begins by recognising this mix, because a shelter, a meal or a toilet that works for a healthy adult may fail a baby or a grandmother.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a refugee, the causes grouped by kind and the mix of people who arrive.

## Subtopic: The First Problems

Imagine the first twenty-four hours in a field beside a river, after a flood, with three hundred people and nothing. List what goes wrong, in the order it hurts. Weather first: by nightfall it is cold, it may rain again, and the youngest and oldest are at risk of hypothermia within hours. So shelter, anything that keeps off rain and wind, is the first physical problem. Water second: people are thirsty within hours, and the river is brown with mud and sewage, so drinking it brings diarrhoea within a day or two, which kills small children faster than hunger. Clean water, or a way to make water safe, is the second problem.

Sanitation third, though people are embarrassed to say so: three hundred people with no toilets foul the ground and the water within a day, and disease follows. Food fourth: a healthy adult can go a day without eating, a baby cannot, and after two days everyone is weak and tempers are short. Medical care runs alongside all of these: injuries from the flood, people without their chronic medicines, mothers in labour. Safety: in the dark, in a crowd of strangers, children and women are at risk, and belongings disappear. Information and family: people need to know where they are, what is happening, where to register, and above all where their missing relatives are.

Notice how many of these are technology problems. Shelter is a structure and a textile. Clean water is a filter, a chemical or a boiling method. A toilet is a pit, a structure and a process. Food is processing, storage and cooking with little fuel. Communication is a list, a board, a radio or a phone charger. Everything this term, from the food you will plan and cook, to the textiles you will test, to the shelter you will design, answers one of the problems on this list. That is what investigation is for: to find the real problems before anyone starts designing solutions to the wrong ones.

The questions for this section are with you now: the problems in order of urgency, why water matters more than food in the first days, and which problems are technology problems.

## Subtopic: Needs, Wants and Ranking

An investigation must separate needs from wants. A need is something without which health or life is in danger: shelter, water, food, sanitation, medical care, safety. A want is something that would be good to have but whose absence does not harm: a mattress rather than a mat, a hot meal rather than a cold one, privacy, a phone charger. In a disaster, resources are short, so needs come first, and designers who meet wants before needs waste what little there is. The ranking is not permanent. On day one a phone charger is a want; by day three, when it is the only way to find a missing child or tell relatives you are alive, it has become a need. Good investigators revisit the list.

Rank with a reason. Write the list on the board and beside each item write why it sits where it does: shelter first because cold kills in hours; water second because thirst and disease follow in a day; sanitation third because it protects the water; food fourth because hunger takes days to harm. Then test the ranking against the mix of people: for a baby, milk moves up; for a diabetic, medicine moves up; for a mother with a lost child, information moves up. A ranked, reasoned list is the product of this week's investigation and the brief for every design in the term.

South Africa's own disaster response follows the same list. After the 2022 floods, community halls and churches opened for shelter, water tankers arrived, mobile toilets were brought in, food parcels and cooked meals came from the Gift of the Givers and local organisations, clinics set up tents, and the municipality posted lists of the missing. Each is an item on your list, delivered by people who had investigated what was needed before. When the system fails, it is usually because the order was wrong or because the mix of people was not considered, such as food that babies could not eat or a hall with no toilet for a hundred people.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling everyone who has lost a home a refugee; a person inside their own country is displaced, not a refugee. Two: putting food first because hunger is the most obvious; cold and dirty water harm sooner. Three: planning for a crowd of healthy adults and forgetting babies, the old, the sick and people who speak other languages. Four: confusing wants with needs, such as planning mattresses before toilets.

The questions for this section are with you now: needs against wants, ranking with reasons and the South African response.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a photograph of a family arriving at a camp and a list on the board — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Why People Leave

A refugee has been forced from home across a border to stay safe. Someone forced from home inside their own country is displaced. Causes: war, persecution, political violence including xenophobic attacks, floods, drought, earthquakes and shack fires.

Some causes give minutes, like a fire or a flood. Some give months, like a drought.

Those who arrive are a mix: babies, pregnant women, old people, the injured, people without their medicines, healthy adults, many languages, and some who have lost their families. Plan for the mix.

Keep the family in the photograph in mind and try the questions for this part: the definition, the causes and the mix.

## Subtopic: The First Day

Night falls and it is cold: shelter first. Thirst within hours and dirty river water that brings diarrhoea: clean water second. No toilets for three hundred people: sanitation third. Hunger that takes days to harm: food fourth. Medicine, safety and finding family run alongside.

Nearly every one is a technology problem: a structure, a textile, a filter, a stove, a notice board.

This term's food, textiles and shelter work all answer this list.

Hold the cold first night in mind and try this part's questions: the order, why water beats food and the technology in each.

## Subtopic: Needs Before Wants

A need keeps you alive and healthy: shelter, water, food, toilets, medicine, safety. A want is nice: a mattress, a hot meal, a charger. Needs first, and the list can change: by day three the charger that finds a lost child is a need.

Rank with a reason beside each item, then check it against babies, the old, the sick and the lost.

South Africa's flood response follows the same list: halls, tankers, toilets, food parcels, clinic tents, lists of the missing.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Refugees are forced across a border by war, persecution or disaster; the displaced are forced from home inside their country. Those who arrive are a mix of ages, health and languages. First problems in order: shelter, clean water, sanitation, food, with medicine, safety and information alongside. Separate needs from wants, rank with reasons and plan for the mix.

Hold the ranked list, and take the final questions of the lesson: needs and wants, ranking and the summary.
