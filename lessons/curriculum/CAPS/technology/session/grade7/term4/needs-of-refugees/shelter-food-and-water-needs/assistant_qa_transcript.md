### subtopic_1 — Water: How Much and For What
**Assistant:** If water is so short, why does the standard give 6 or 7 litres a day just for washing? Could people not skip washing until things are better?

**Tutor:** They could, and in the first day or two they often have to, but the standard includes washing water because skipping it is what turns a camp into an epidemic. Think about where disease comes from in a camp of one hundred people with pit toilets. Every visit to the toilet leaves traces of faeces on hands, and those hands then touch food, pots, children's faces and the water scoop. Diarrhoeal diseases, cholera, typhoid and hepatitis all travel on that route, and the single most effective barrier is washing hands with soap after the toilet and before handling food. That takes perhaps a litre per person per day if done properly, and the standard allows for it several times a day. Body washing matters for a different reason: skin infections, scabies and lice spread fast among people sleeping close together in the same clothes, and washing, especially of children, keeps them at bay. Washing cooking pots in clean water stops yesterday's food poisoning tomorrow's meal. So the 6 or 7 litres is not a luxury; it is medicine, and aid workers will tell you that when water is cut to the drinking minimum the clinic queue grows within three days. This is also why the ranking from last lesson puts sanitation third and ties it to water: toilets, hand washing points beside them with soap, and enough water for both are one system. For a designer the lesson is in the specification: if you write 300 litres of drinking water for the camp and stop, you have designed an outbreak; write 1 500 to 2 000 litres and say what each part is for, and the water point, the soap and the washing area follow. In your own food work in weeks two and three, remember that the person cooking must be able to wash their hands and the pots, and put that water in your plan.

### subtopic_2 — Food: Enough, Balanced and Possible
**Assistant:** Why can we not just give everyone the same food? It would be much simpler than planning for babies and sick people separately.

**Tutor:** Simpler to organise, and dangerous for exactly the people least able to cope, which is why the humanitarian standards insist on the adjustments. A single ration designed for a working adult, say 400 grams of maize meal with beans and oil, is too much bulk and too little nutrition for a two-year-old, who has a small stomach, needs to eat five or six times a day, and needs more protein and fat per mouthful than an adult to keep growing; on an adult ration a toddler fills up on pap and slowly becomes malnourished, which is what aid workers see in badly run camps within a few weeks. A baby under six months cannot eat the ration at all, and the alternative that seems obvious, powdered formula, is lethal where water is unsafe and bottles cannot be sterilised, so the plan has to feed and rest the mother so she can breastfeed, which is a different kind of provision altogether. A grandmother with few teeth cannot chew hard beans and dry pap and quietly eats less. Someone with diarrhoea loses water and salt faster than any food can replace and needs oral rehydration solution first. A diabetic who eats a huge plate of pap once a day will swing from high to low blood sugar. And a Muslim family given tinned pork, or a family who has never eaten maize, will not eat, and uneaten food is wasted food. None of this requires a different kitchen for every person. It requires the planner to know the mix, which is why last lesson's investigation came first, and to add a few items and a few rules: a soft porridge pot for small children and the elderly, oil and groundnuts to enrich it, milk powder for children over six months mixed only with boiled water, rehydration salts at the clinic, a protein that everyone can eat, and meals at regular times. The design brief in week two asks you for types and quantities of food for one hundred refugees, and the mark is in showing that you know they are not one hundred identical adults.

### subtopic_3 — Shelter: Space, Weather and Time
**Assistant:** Where do the numbers like 3.5 square metres and 15 litres come from, and do they ever change?

**Tutor:** They come from the Sphere standards, a handbook that humanitarian organisations around the world, including the Red Cross and the United Nations agencies, agreed on so that help in an emergency anywhere reaches a minimum level, and they were worked out from decades of camp experience and health research. The 15 litres of water per person per day is the amount below which disease rises sharply, measured in real camps; 20 is the target. The 2 100 kilocalories is the average energy need of a mixed population of all ages at light activity in a moderate climate. The 3.5 square metres of covered living space is the minimum below which respiratory infections and the spread of disease between families rise, and it is the figure for warm climates; in cold climates where people must also cook and store fuel inside, it rises to 4.5 square metres or more. So yes, they change, in two ways. They are adjusted for the place and the people: more water in a hot climate or where there is cholera, more energy for people doing heavy work or in cold weather, more space where it is cold, different staples where people eat rice rather than maize. And they are revised over time as evidence improves; the handbook is updated every few years, and the current edition emphasises asking the people themselves what they need rather than only applying figures. For your purposes this term, use the figures as the floor of a specification and then write the adjustment beside it with the reason: 20 litres per person, rising for a breastfeeding mother; 3.5 square metres, rising to 4.5 for a Highveld winter because the family will cook and shelter inside. That is exactly how a professional writes a brief, and it shows an examiner that you understand the figure is a tool, not a rule.
