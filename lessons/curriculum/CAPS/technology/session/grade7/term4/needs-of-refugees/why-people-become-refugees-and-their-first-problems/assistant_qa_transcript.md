### subtopic_1 — What Makes People Leave Home
**Assistant:** Why is clean water ranked above food? Surely people need to eat.

**Tutor:** They do, and in an emergency the question is not what people need but what harms them first, because that decides what must arrive first. A healthy adult can go several days without food and feel weak but recover fully; children and the frail have less reserve but still a day or two. Water is different in two ways. First, dehydration harms within hours in heat and within a day anywhere, and the body cannot postpone it. Second, and this is the point that disaster workers stress, the danger is not only too little water but the wrong water. Three hundred people beside a flooded river will drink from it, because thirst gives them no choice, and flood water carries sewage, dead animals and the contents of every pit latrine upstream. Within a day or two, diarrhoea spreads through the camp, and diarrhoea kills small children by dehydration faster than any shortage of food could. Cholera, which follows floods in Mozambique and has appeared in South Africa, is exactly this. So clean water, or a way of making water safe by boiling, chlorine tablets or a filter, prevents the camp's first epidemic, and that is why it sits second only to shelter from cold. Food takes its place fourth not because it does not matter but because its harm is slower, which gives organisers a day to bring it. There is a connection to this term's food work: the meals you plan will be for people who may already have had diarrhoea, so they must be easy to digest and must not need unsafe water to prepare. The ranking is a tool for the first days; after a week, with water and shelter in place, food and sanitation move to the top of a longer list, and after a month, schooling, work and privacy join it.

### subtopic_2 — The First Problems
**Assistant:** Are the people displaced by shack fires in South Africa really refugees? They have not left the country.

**Tutor:** Strictly, no, and the distinction matters for how they are helped, which is why the syllabus asks you to know it. The word refugee has a legal meaning: a person who has crossed an international border because of a well-founded fear of persecution, war or violence, and who is protected by international agreements and by South Africa's Refugees Act. Someone whose shack burned down in Khayelitsha has lost everything and may be sleeping in a community hall tonight, but they are in their own country, with their own identity document, their own municipality and their own relatives nearby, and the law calls them a displaced person or a disaster victim. The practical difference is in who is responsible and what help is available. A South African displaced by fire or flood is the responsibility of the municipality and the national disaster management system, can be registered quickly, can access grants and clinics, and usually has family in the area. A refugee from Congo who was displaced by xenophobic violence in the same township may have no identity document that officials recognise, may speak no local language, cannot go home, and depends on Home Affairs, the United Nations refugee agency and charities. Both groups arrive at the same hall with the same first problems, cold, thirst, no toilet, no food, which is why this term treats them together under emergency situations, and why the technology, a shelter, a water filter, a cooked meal, serves both. But when you write the brief for a shelter or a meal, say who it is for, because a shelter for families who will return to rebuild in a month differs from one for people who may be in it for years. In the examination, use the right word: refugee for those who have crossed a border, displaced person or disaster victim for those inside their own country, and emergency situation for both.

### subtopic_3 — Needs, Wants and Ranking
**Assistant:** What does it mean to say that shelter is a technology problem? It is just a tent.

**Tutor:** A tent is one answer to the problem, and the fact that it looks simple hides how much technology sits inside it, which is the point of studying it. Start with what shelter must do on the first night: keep rain and wind off, keep in body heat, stand up in a wind, be put up in minutes by tired people in the dark, and do all that for a family of six, often a baby and a grandmother among them. Then look at the tent as a designer. It is a structure, a frame of poles or an inflatable arch or a tensioned cable, which must be stable and strong, and that is term two's structures again. It is a textile, canvas or woven plastic, which must be waterproof, tear-resistant, light and, ideally, slow to burn, and you will test exactly those properties in week six. It is a system of joints, pegs, guy ropes and zips that must work with cold fingers. It is a product that must pack flat, be carried on a truck and cost little, because ten thousand are needed at once. And it comes with health and safety issues: ventilation so a paraffin stove inside does not poison the family, spacing between tents so a fire does not spread, and a floor that keeps out water and cold. Every one of those is a design decision with a specification and a constraint behind it, and every one can be done badly: tents that flood, that tear in the first wind, that take an hour to erect, that burn. The same applies to indigenous shelters, which you will investigate in week six: a Zulu beehive hut or a Xhosa rondavel solves the same problems with local materials and centuries of refinement, and often solves them better than an imported tent. So when the list says shelter, a designer hears structure, textile, joints, erection, transport, cost, health and safety, and that is the brief you will write in week seven.
