# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the investigation of refugees' needs by quantifying shelter, food and water. The anchor is a classroom in Thohoyandou where a class is given the scenario of a camp of one hundred people after a flood and works out, with a 20 litre bucket, a 500 gram bag of maize meal and a 3 by 4 metre tarpaulin as props, what one person and then one hundred need each day. The governing idea is that humanitarian standards give minimum figures, about 15 to 20 litres of water per person per day for drinking, cooking and washing, about 2 100 kilocalories of food per person per day from a balanced mix of staple, protein, fat and vegetables, and about 3.5 square metres of covered shelter per person with protection from rain, wind, cold and sun, that these figures are adjusted for babies, the sick, the elderly and the climate, and that writing them as specifications is the bridge from investigation to design. Stating refugees' needs for shelter, food and water is examinable.

## Subtopic: Water: How Much and For What

Hold up the 20 litre bucket. That is roughly what one person needs each day in an emergency camp, according to the standards that aid organisations use: a minimum of 15 litres, better 20. It is not all for drinking. Drinking takes 2.5 to 3 litres in a warm climate, more for a breastfeeding mother or someone with diarrhoea. Cooking takes 3 to 6 litres, because maize meal, rice and beans must be boiled and pots washed. Personal washing and hand washing take 6 to 7 litres, and hand washing after the toilet and before eating is what stops disease spreading. Washing clothes and cleaning take the rest. For one hundred people that is 1 500 to 2 000 litres a day, a water tanker every one or two days, or a borehole with a pump, or a river with a treatment point.

Quantity is half the specification; quality is the other half. Drinking and cooking water must be safe: free of the bacteria and parasites that flood water carries. There are three ways to make it so, each a technology. Boiling for one minute kills everything but uses fuel. Chlorine, as tablets or household bleach at a measured dose, kills most organisms and protects the water in storage, and it is what municipalities use. Filtering through cloth, sand or a ceramic filter removes dirt and many parasites but not all bacteria, so it is usually combined with chlorine. Water must also be stored in covered containers so it is not contaminated after treatment, and the tap or scoop must not be dipped in by dirty hands.

Distance matters. If the water point is more than a few hundred metres away, people, usually women and children, spend hours carrying buckets, and they fetch less than they need. The standard is a water point within 500 metres of every shelter and no more than 250 people per tap. So the water specification for the camp of one hundred reads: at least 1 500 litres a day, of which 300 litres drinking quality, treated by chlorine with a measured dose, stored covered, at a tap within 500 metres of every shelter.

The questions for this section are with you now: the daily litres and what they are for, the three ways to make water safe and the distance rule.

## Subtopic: Food: Enough, Balanced and Possible

Hold up the 500 gram bag of maize meal. Cooked as pap, that is about three days of staple for one adult, and a staple is where most of the energy comes from. The standard for an emergency ration is about 2 100 kilocalories per person per day, enough for a moderately active adult, and it must be balanced: a staple such as maize meal, rice, bread or samp for energy; a protein such as beans, lentils, tinned fish, eggs or milk powder for growth and repair; a fat such as cooking oil or peanut butter, which gives a lot of energy in a small weight and makes food taste better; and vegetables or fruit, fresh or tinned, for vitamins and minerals. Salt and, where possible, sugar and tea complete the list. Water to cook with is part of the food plan, which is why the two needs sit together.

Now adjust for the mix. Babies under six months need breast milk, which means feeding and resting the mother, not powdered formula mixed with unsafe water, which kills. Small children need smaller, more frequent meals, soft food and extra protein and fat. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need more of everything. Elderly people may need soft food and smaller portions. People with diarrhoea need rehydration solution, salt and sugar in clean water, before anything else. People with diabetes need regular meals and less sugar. Religious and cultural rules matter too: a camp with Muslim families cannot serve pork, and a camp in Limpopo will eat pap gladly while one of Congolese families may prefer rice or cassava.

Food must also be possible in camp conditions, which is where the design challenge of weeks two and three comes from. It must be cooked with little fuel and little water, in big pots, by people who are tired, on open fires or gas burners or paraffin stoves, so a meal that needs an oven or an hour of simmering is out. It must keep without a fridge, so dried, tinned and fresh-today foods are in and raw meat for tomorrow is out. It must be safe to prepare where hand washing is limited. The food specification for one hundred people for one day therefore reads: about 210 000 kilocalories in total, roughly 40 kilograms of staple, 10 kilograms of beans or equivalent protein, 3 litres of oil, 10 kilograms of vegetables, salt, with milk, soft food and rehydration solution for the babies, small children and the sick, cookable in two large pots on open fires with under 400 litres of water.

The questions for this section are with you now: the daily energy figure and the four food groups, adjustments for the mix and what camp conditions rule out.

## Subtopic: Shelter: Space, Weather and Time

Unfold the 3 by 4 metre tarpaulin. That is 12 square metres, and the standard says each person needs at least 3.5 square metres of covered space to sleep, store belongings and move, so this tarpaulin covers three people, four if they are small. A family of six needs about 21 square metres, roughly 4 by 5 metres, which is a large tent or two tarpaulins over a frame. Space is the first shelter number, and overcrowding is not just uncomfortable: it spreads coughs, measles and tuberculosis, and it means someone sleeps against the wet wall.

Shelter must protect from the weather of the place and the season. In a South African winter in the Cape or the Highveld that means rain and wind and nights near or below freezing, so the shelter must be waterproof above and below, because ground water soaks through a floorless tent, windproof, and insulating enough that six bodies keep the space warm. In Limpopo in summer it means heat and sun, so shade and ventilation matter more than warmth, and a dark plastic sheet in the sun makes an oven. The same tarpaulin may be the right roof in both places if it is pitched, raised, floored and ventilated differently, which is why the design brief must say where and when.

Shelter must be quick to erect by the people themselves with few tools, because it is needed on the first night and the helpers are few, and it must stand for the time it is needed, a month at least, through several storms, without the frame collapsing or the cover tearing at the fixings. It must be transportable: flat-packed, light enough for two people to carry, cheap enough that a thousand can be bought. It must be safe: a paraffin stove inside needs ventilation and a fire gap of at least two metres between shelters, the cover should be slow to burn, and there must be a door that closes for privacy and security. Putting those together gives the shelter specification for a family of six: at least 21 square metres covered, waterproof roof and floor, windproof walls, stands in a 60 kilometre per hour wind, erected by two adults in under an hour with no tools beyond a hammer, packs into a bundle two people can carry, lasts at least a month, has a closable door and ventilation, and sits two metres from its neighbour. That is the brief you will design to in week seven.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting only drinking water, 3 litres, and forgetting cooking and washing, which brings disease. Two: planning powdered baby formula in a camp without safe water. Three: a shelter sized for a family of six at 2 square metres each, which is a hospital ward for coughs. Four: a shelter designed for the Highveld winter sent to a Limpopo summer, with no ventilation and a black roof.

The questions for this section are with you now: the space per person, protection from the local weather, erection, transport, duration and safety, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a bucket, a bag of maize meal and a tarpaulin — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Water by the Bucket

One bucket, 20 litres, per person per day. About 3 litres to drink, 3 to 6 to cook, 6 to 7 to wash hands and bodies, the rest for clothes and cleaning. One hundred people: a tanker every day or two.

Make it safe: boil for a minute, add measured chlorine, or filter and then chlorinate. Store it covered. Do not dip dirty hands in.

A tap within 500 metres of every shelter, no more than 250 people per tap.

Keep the bucket in mind and try the questions for this part: litres and uses, the three treatments and the tap rule.

## Subtopic: Food That Can Be Cooked There

About 2 100 kilocalories a day each, balanced: a staple like maize meal, a protein like beans or tinned fish, a fat like oil, and vegetables. Salt. Water to cook with.

Adjust for the mix: breast milk for babies, soft frequent meals for small children, more for pregnant women, rehydration drink for anyone with diarrhoea, no pork for Muslim families.

It must be cookable with little fuel and water, in big pots, and must keep without a fridge. No ovens, no raw meat for tomorrow.

Hold the bag of maize meal in mind and try this part's questions: the figure, the four groups and camp conditions.

## Subtopic: Shelter for Six for a Month

3.5 square metres each, 21 for a family of six, about 4 by 5 metres. Crowding spreads coughs and measles.

Keep out the local weather: rain, wind and cold on the Highveld; sun and heat in Limpopo. Waterproof roof and floor. Ventilation. Fire gap of two metres and a stove kept away from the cover. A door that closes.

Two adults put it up in under an hour with a hammer. Two people carry it. It lasts a month of storms.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Water: 15 to 20 litres each per day, treated and stored covered, a tap within 500 metres. Food: 2 100 kilocalories each, balanced across staple, protein, fat and vegetables, adjusted for babies, the sick and culture, cookable in camp conditions. Shelter: 3.5 square metres each, weatherproof for the place and season, quick to erect, transportable, lasting a month, safe from fire.

Hold the tarpaulin, and take the final questions of the lesson: space, weather, erection and the summary.
