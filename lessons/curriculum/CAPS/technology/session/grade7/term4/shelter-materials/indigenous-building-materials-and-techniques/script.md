# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates the materials and techniques of indigenous housing in rural South Africa as a source of ideas for the emergency shelter. The anchor is a classroom in Eshowe with photographs and a scale model of a Zulu beehive hut, a Xhosa rondavel, a Basotho mokhoro, a Venda homestead and a Nama matjieshuis, alongside samples of thatching grass, a bundle of saplings, a reed mat, a brick of mud and dung and a length of bark rope. The governing idea is that indigenous builders solved shelter with materials that are readily available, appropriate to the climate and the task, and environmentally friendly, that their techniques, bent sapling frames, woven and thatched covers, mud and dung plaster, stone footings, lashed joints, are sound structural and textile engineering, and that an emergency shelter can borrow both materials and principles, especially where local materials and local hands are faster and cheaper than imported tents. Describing indigenous shelter materials and techniques is examinable.

## Subtopic: Houses Built from the Landscape

The Zulu beehive hut, the iQhugwane, is a dome. Saplings are cut, their thick ends set in a circle of holes in the ground, and their thin ends bent over and lashed to each other and to horizontal hoops of saplings, so the frame is a basket of arches, a dome, which is one of the strongest shapes a structure can take because every push is carried round the curve to the ground. Over the frame goes thatch, long grass laid in overlapping layers from the bottom up and tied on, so water runs from one layer onto the next and off; in the Zulu version woven grass mats are laid over the frame and a net of grass rope tied over all to hold it in wind. The door is low, so wind and rain do not drive in and heat does not escape, and the floor is earth beaten hard and finished with a mixture of mud and cow dung, which dries to a smooth, hard, dust-free surface.

The rondavel of the Xhosa and the Sotho mokhoro is a cylinder with a cone. The walls are a ring of posts with saplings woven between them, wattle, and plastered inside and out with daga, a mud and dung mixture, which sets hard, keeps out wind and insects and, being thick, holds the night's cool and the day's warmth so the inside is comfortable in both; or the walls are of stone or of sun-dried mud bricks. The roof is a cone of poles tied at the apex and resting on the wall ring, thatched thickly, with the thatch overhanging the wall so rain falls clear of the plaster. The cone sheds water in every direction and offers little face to the wind, and the circular wall has no corners to crack. Basotho builders decorate the walls with coloured earth patterns, litema, which is also a renewable protective coat.

The Venda and Tsonga homestead groups round huts and granaries round a courtyard, with low walls and reed screens giving shade and privacy; the Venda used stone walls on the hills, dry-built, no mortar. The Nama matjieshuis of the Northern Cape is the closest thing to an emergency shelter the country ever made: a frame of bent saplings lashed into a dome, covered with mats woven from rushes, which let the breeze through in the heat and swell shut in the rain, and the whole thing could be taken down, rolled, loaded onto an ox and re-erected the same day. Every one of these buildings came from a radius of a few kilometres: grass from the veld, saplings from the river, mud from the pit, dung from the cattle, stone from the hillside, rushes from the pan.

The questions for this section are with you now: the beehive dome and its thatch, the rondavel's wattle and daga and cone roof, and the mobile matjieshuis.

## Subtopic: Why the Materials Work

The syllabus gives three words for these materials: readily available, appropriate and environmentally friendly, and each is a design principle. Readily available means no transport, no cost beyond labour, no waiting: when a hut was needed the materials were within walking distance and the community knew how to work them, so a hut could be built in days by the family and neighbours. An emergency shelter that arrives three weeks late from overseas fails this test; one that can be built from poles and sheeting bought in the nearest town, or from the reeds beside the river, passes it.

Appropriate means suited to the climate and the purpose. Thatch is a thick layer of air trapped between stems, so it insulates against the hot sun in summer and the cold at night, far better than a sheet of iron, which is an oven by day and a radiator of cold by night, and it is quiet in rain and hail. Thick daga walls store heat and release it slowly, smoothing the day's swings. A dome or cone sheds wind and water and needs no heavy beams. A low door keeps weather and heat where they belong. Mats that breathe in heat and swell shut in rain are a textile designed for the Kalahari's extremes. Each choice answers the local hazard, which is exactly the method you used for protective clothing.

Environmentally friendly means the materials grow back, return to the earth and harm nothing. Grass regrows every season; saplings are coppiced and regrow; mud and dung come from the ground and the cattle and go back to them when the hut is abandoned, leaving no waste; stone is simply moved. No factory, no fuel, no plastic, no transport emissions, and at the end of its life the hut is compost, where a plastic-sheet shelter is a thousand years of litter. There are costs to be honest about: thatch burns easily, and a hut with an open fire inside has a smoke problem that indigenous builders managed with a high roof and a door that drew the air; thatch harbours insects and needs renewing every few years; daga needs a yearly recoat; and cutting too many saplings or too much grass damages the veld, so these systems worked because communities managed their resources.

Compare with the shelter requirements from last lesson, line by line. Sturdy: a lashed dome stands in strong wind; appropriate. Waterproof: layered thatch and overhanging eaves; appropriate, but a thatched roof takes days, not an hour. Easy to erect: the matjieshuis yes, the rondavel no. Transportable: the matjieshuis yes, the rest are built in place. Fire-safe: thatch no, and that is the material's great weakness for a camp. Ventilated: the low door and high roof, yes. Durable: years, more than a month. The investigation is not asking you to build a hut; it is asking what to take from the hut.

The questions for this section are with you now: readily available, appropriate and environmentally friendly as design principles, the honest costs and the line-by-line comparison.

## Subtopic: What the Emergency Shelter Can Borrow

Borrow the frame. The bent-sapling dome is light, strong, needs no beams and no joints beyond a lashing, and can be made from saplings, bamboo, or the cheapest modern equivalents, plastic conduit or thin steel rod, pushed into the ground and bent over; it is the same geometry as the geodesic domes and hoop tents that aid agencies and campers use, and it goes up in minutes. Borrow the lashing: a frame tied with cord or cable ties can be untied and moved, where a nailed or welded frame cannot, and a tied joint flexes in wind rather than snapping. Borrow the ring of hoops that keeps the dome from spreading, which is term two's triangulation in curved form.

Borrow the cover principles even where the material changes. Overlapping layers laid from the bottom up so water runs off: a tarpaulin fixed with its lower edge overlapping the one below does the same. Eaves that overhang the wall so rain falls clear. A low or screened door facing away from the wind. A breathing wall that lets air through and a roof that sheds water. A cover tied over with a net or ropes so the wind cannot lift it, which is how a Zulu thatch survives a storm and how a tarpaulin survives one too. The reed mat is worth a second look: woven mats of reeds or rushes are still made in KwaZulu-Natal and the Northern Cape, they are light, they shade, they can line a plastic shelter to make it opaque and insulated, and they can be made locally by the displaced people themselves, giving work.

Borrow the floor. A beaten earth floor sealed with mud and dung is hard, dust-free and insulating, and a raised earth platform under a shelter keeps the floor above runoff; at the least, a shelter should stand on a mound rather than in a hollow, which indigenous builders always did. Borrow the layout: huts round a courtyard give shared sheltered space, privacy and a place for the cooking fire away from the sleeping huts, which answers two of last lesson's safety requirements at once. And borrow the biggest idea of all: involve the people who will live in the shelter in building it, because they already know how, and a camp built with local materials and local hands is faster, cheaper, more acceptable and better maintained than one unpacked from a container.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: dismissing indigenous housing as primitive; the dome, the thatch and the thermal wall are sophisticated engineering that modern designers copy. Two: romanticising it, forgetting that thatch burns and that a hut takes days to build. Three: proposing to thatch the emergency shelter, which fails the one-hour and the fire requirements. Four: borrowing the look of a hut without the principle, a cone-shaped plastic tent with no overhang, no ventilation and no tie-down.

The questions for this section are with you now: the dome frame and lashing, the cover principles and the reed mat, the floor and layout, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a model hut on the desk and a bundle of grass and sticks beside it — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Built from What Is There

Zulu beehive hut: saplings bent and lashed into a dome, covered in layered thatch and grass mats, tied down with a grass net, low door, floor of beaten earth and dung.

Rondavel: a ring of posts woven with saplings and plastered with mud and dung, or stone or mud brick, under a thatched cone that overhangs the wall and sheds rain every way.

Nama matjieshuis: a sapling dome covered in rush mats that breathe in heat and swell shut in rain, taken down and moved in a day. Everything from within a few kilometres: grass, sticks, mud, dung, stone, rushes.

Keep the model hut in mind and try the questions for this part: the dome, the rondavel and the matjieshuis.

## Subtopic: Why They Work

Readily available: within walking distance, no cost but work, built in days by the family. Appropriate: thatch insulates against sun and cold, thick mud walls store heat, domes and cones shed wind and water, low doors keep weather out. Environmentally friendly: it all grows back and goes back to the earth, no factory, no plastic, no waste.

Honest costs: thatch burns, harbours insects and needs renewing; mud needs a yearly coat; too much cutting harms the veld.

Against the shelter list: sturdy yes, waterproof yes but slow, easy to erect only the matjieshuis, fire-safe no.

Hold the three words in mind and try this part's questions: available, appropriate, friendly, and the costs.

## Subtopic: What to Borrow

The dome frame of bent rods pushed into the ground and lashed: light, strong, up in minutes, the same shape as a hoop tent. Hoops to stop it spreading. Tied joints that flex and can be undone.

Overlapping layers from the bottom up, eaves that overhang, a low door away from the wind, a net or ropes over the cover. Reed mats to line and shade a plastic shelter, made locally by the people themselves.

A raised floor on a mound. Huts round a courtyard with the fire outside. Above all, let the people build it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Indigenous houses use grass, saplings, mud, dung, stone and rushes that are readily available, appropriate to the climate and environmentally friendly, with domes, cones, thatch, plaster and lashings that are real engineering. Thatch burns and building is slow, so do not copy the hut; borrow its frame, its cover principles, its floor, its layout and its builders.

Hold the bent-rod dome, and take the final questions of the lesson: what to borrow and the summary.
