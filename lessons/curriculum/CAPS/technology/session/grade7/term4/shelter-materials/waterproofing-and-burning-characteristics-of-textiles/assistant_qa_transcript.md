### subtopic_1 — How a Textile Is Waterproofed
**Assistant:** If wax makes canvas waterproof and is cheap, why does anyone bother with expensive coatings?

**Tutor:** Because wax solves the rain problem and leaves four others, and whether those matter depends on what the fabric is for, which is the whole point of testing against a brief. Wax works by making each fibre repel water, so drops bead and roll off, and as long as the water is only lying on the surface the fabric stays dry underneath. The first problem is pressure: your thumb test showed it. Where water is pushed against the cloth, by a pool on a sagging roof, by a guy rope pulling a fold tight, by someone leaning on a wet wall or kneeling on the floor, the pressure forces drops through the gaps in the weave, and a camp shelter has all of those. The second is wear: wax rubs off where the cloth folds and chafes, which on a shelter is every seam, corner and pole contact, and it softens in heat and cracks in cold, so it must be reapplied every season, which nobody in a camp will do. The third is weight: waxed canvas is heavy, two or three times the weight of coated polyester for the same cover, and a shelter that two people must carry cannot afford it. The fourth is fire: your flame test showed waxed canvas burning faster and hotter than plain, because wax is a fuel, which is a serious mark against it for a shelter with a stove inside. Coatings answer those four at a price. An acrylic or PVC film is a continuous barrier, so pressure does not push water through; it does not rub off in the way wax does, though it can crack; on a polyester base it is light and strong; and PVC in particular resists ignition. The price is money, loss of breathability, which ventilation must replace, toxic smoke from PVC if it does burn, and a product that must come from a factory rather than a tin of wax and a brush. Wax has its place: a jacket, a bag, a tent that will be looked after by its owner. For a shelter that must be light, pressure-tight, maintenance-free and as fire-safe as possible, coated fabric wins, and your table should show the four reasons rather than just the verdict.

### subtopic_2 — Testing Waterproofing
**Assistant:** PVC scored well on fire in the test, so why did you call it poisonous?

**Tutor:** Because fire safety has two parts, how easily a thing burns and what it does to you while it burns, and PVC scores well on the first and badly on the second. PVC, polyvinyl chloride, contains chlorine, and that is both its strength and its danger. The chlorine makes it hard to ignite and tends to put the flame out when the source is removed, which is why PVC-coated fabric charred and stopped in the demonstration, and why PVC is used for electrical cable insulation and for fire-resistant tarpaulins. But when PVC does burn or even heat strongly, it releases hydrogen chloride gas, which turns to hydrochloric acid in the eyes, nose and lungs, along with dense black smoke and other toxic products; in a small sealed space these can injure or kill before the fire reaches anyone, and they make it much harder to find the exit. Fire fighters treat PVC smoke as a serious hazard and wear breathing apparatus for it. So in a shelter the judgement is this: a PVC-coated roof is less likely to catch from a stove flare or a candle than tarpaulin or cotton, which is a real advantage, but if a fire does take hold, perhaps from bedding or a paraffin spill inside, the roof above the family becomes a source of poison gas, and the ventilation that we insisted on last week matters even more. The same reasoning applies to the other plastics: polyester and nylon give off less toxic smoke than PVC but melt and drip burning droplets onto skin, and polypropylene burns away quickly with dripping; cotton burns fast but cleanly; wool and flame-retardant-treated cotton char and smoulder with less smoke. This is why the choice table has separate columns for ignition, for melting and dripping and for smoke, and why the verdict is a compromise rather than a winner. Your results table should record for PVC: hard to ignite, self-extinguishing, chars, dense acrid toxic smoke, and your reasoning for the shelter should mention both halves. In the examination, if asked to describe PVC's burning characteristics, that sentence is the full answer.

### subtopic_3 — Burning Characteristics and the Choice
**Assistant:** Could we make our own flame-retardant treatment for the canvas, and would it stop the fabric burning completely?

**Tutor:** You can make one from household chemicals, it works well enough to see in the flame test, and no, it does not make the fabric fireproof; it makes it harder to ignite and stops the flame spreading, which is what retardant means. The classic home recipe, used for theatre scenery and school projects for a century, is a solution of borax and boric acid in water, roughly seven parts borax to three parts boric acid by weight, dissolved in hot water at about 10 percent strength, into which the cotton is soaked and then dried without rinsing. Both salts work in two ways when the treated cloth is heated: they melt into a glassy layer that coats the fibres and keeps oxygen away, and they release water and gases that dilute the flammable vapours the cotton gives off, so the cloth chars black where the flame touches but does not catch alight and the char does not spread. Commercial treatments for tents and workwear use related phosphorus and nitrogen compounds that bond more permanently to the fibre, because the borax treatment is washed out by rain, which is its weakness for a shelter: a treated canvas roof would lose its protection in the first storm unless it also had a waterproof coating over it, which is exactly the two-layer logic of a real shelter fabric, flame-retardant base plus waterproof coat. There are limits. Flame retardants work on fabrics that char, mainly cotton and other cellulose fibres and wool; they do little for polyester, nylon or polypropylene, which melt and drip whatever you soak them in, so there is no home treatment that makes a hardware shop tarpaulin fire-safe. And no retardant survives a large sustained fire: a paraffin pool burning under a treated canvas will eventually burn through it. What the treatment buys is the critical first seconds, so that a candle brushing the wall or a spark from a stove makes a black spot instead of a burning roof, and the family has time to act. If your class tries it, treat half a cotton square and leave half untreated, dry it, and let the teacher hold the boundary in the candle: the line between burning and charring is the clearest demonstration of what a flame retardant does. Record it, and in your brief specify a flame-retardant-treated fabric with a waterproof coating over the treatment, which is what you have just discovered a shelter needs.
