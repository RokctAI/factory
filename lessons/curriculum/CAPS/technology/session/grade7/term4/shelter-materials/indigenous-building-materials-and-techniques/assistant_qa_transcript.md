### subtopic_1 — Houses Built from the Landscape
**Assistant:** Why is a dome so strong when it is made of thin sticks that I could snap over my knee?

**Tutor:** Because the dome puts every stick into the one kind of loading that thin sticks are good at, and avoids the kind they are bad at, which is a lesson from term two's structures applied to a curve. A straight stick is weak in bending: hold it at both ends and push the middle and it bows and snaps, because the material on the outside of the bend is stretched and the inside squashed, and the stick is too thin to resist. The same stick is remarkably strong in pure compression along its length or in tension along its length; try to shorten it or stretch it end to end and you will not manage. Now bend a sapling into an arch with both ends in the ground and push down on the top. The push does not bend the arch further; it is carried round the curve as compression along the stick to the two feet, where the ground pushes back. That is why an arch of thin material can carry a load that a straight beam of the same material cannot. A dome is a set of arches crossing each other and tied together by horizontal hoops, and the hoops do the second job: when a load tries to flatten the dome and push its feet outward, the hoops go into tension round the circle and hold the feet in, so the arches stay arches. Every member is in compression along its length or in tension round the ring, and none is asked to bend. The lashings at the crossings share the load, so that a push on one point spreads over many sticks and a gust on one side is resisted by the whole basket. This is why the Zulu beehive hut stands in storms, why the hoop tent and the geodesic dome are the lightest shelters for their strength, and why engineers roof stadiums with domes of thin steel. For your model shelter, the practical rule is: if you use thin rods, bend them into arches with their feet fixed and tie them to hoops, and never ask a straight thin rod to carry a load across its middle.

### subtopic_2 — Why the Materials Work
**Assistant:** If thatch is such good insulation and so environmentally friendly, why does the lesson say not to use it on the emergency shelter?

**Tutor:** Because the emergency shelter has two requirements that thatch cannot meet, and a designer must be honest about a material's limits even when it is admirable. The first is time. A thatched roof is laid bundle by bundle, each tied to the frame, in overlapping courses from the eaves up, and a skilled thatcher with helpers takes days to roof a single rondavel. The shelter must go up in an hour, by two untrained adults, tonight, in the rain, a thousand times over. Thatch cannot be made into a kit, and the grass must be cut, dried and bundled in season, which may be months away. The second is fire, and this is the decisive one. Dry thatch ignites from a spark and burns fiercely, and a camp of thatched shelters two metres apart with paraffin stoves inside is a disaster already written; traditional homesteads managed the risk with wide spacing, cooking fires in a separate hut or outside, and a community that watched its fires, none of which a camp of strangers in a hurry has. There is a third, smaller reason: thatch needs a frame strong enough to carry its weight, especially when wet, which pushes the structure beyond a light, portable kit. So the lesson does not say forget thatch; it says take what thatch teaches. The insulation comes from trapped air in a thick layer, so the shelter can trap air with a double skin or a lining, a reed mat inside a plastic sheet is one version, and a quilted or bubble insulation another. The waterproofing comes from overlapping layers laid bottom up so water runs off, which a tarpaulin or shingled sheets can copy. The shedding roof shape and the overhanging eaves come straight across. And where a camp is going to last months in a rural area with grass and thatchers to hand, a thatched community building, a clinic or a school, built after the first weeks when there is time, can be the best and cheapest answer, as several rural projects have shown. The design judgement is to match the material to the phase: sheet and frame for the first night, local materials for the first months, and that is a sentence worth writing in your brief.

### subtopic_3 — What the Emergency Shelter Can Borrow
**Assistant:** Could the displaced people really build their own shelters from local materials? Would it not be faster to bring tents?

**Tutor:** Both have their place, and the best disaster responses use both, in order. In the first hours and days, when people are wet and cold and nothing has been organised, a stock of tents or shelter kits, frame, sheet, pegs, rope, held in a warehouse in the province and trucked to the site, is unbeatable: it is tonight's roof, and no amount of local material helps a family who have no tools, no light and no energy left. South Africa's disaster management keeps such stocks, and the lesson's requirement list, up in an hour by two adults, is written for exactly that kit. But tents are expensive, limited in number, and wear out in months of sun and wind, and when a disaster is large the stock runs out on the first night, as it did in parts of KwaZulu-Natal in 2022. That is when local materials and local hands take over, and the indigenous tradition shows how much is possible. In rural areas the people displaced are often the very people who know how to cut saplings, bend a frame, weave a mat and lay a floor, and the materials are on the hillside; what they lack is time, tools, a safe site and perhaps sheeting for a quick waterproof roof. A response that supplies those, plus a design that combines a sapling dome with a plastic or canvas cover, gets more families under cover faster than waiting for the next container, puts money into local hands for mats and poles rather than into a distant factory, and produces shelters that families will maintain and improve because they built them. Aid agencies call this approach owner-driven or community-built shelter, and after the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami and several African floods it housed more people than the imported tents did. The honest limits are that it needs materials that are actually available, which in a city after a shack fire they may not be, that it needs the fire and ventilation rules enforced in the design, and that it takes a little longer than unrolling a tent. So your brief in week seven can say: first night, kit shelters; first month, kits supplemented by locally built frames and mats; and that is a design that has learned from both the warehouse and the hillside.
