# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the practical investigation of waterproofing chemicals and burning characteristics for the shelter textile. The anchor is a Technology room in Stellenbosch with a tray of fabric squares, cotton canvas untreated, canvas waxed, canvas treated with a silicone spray, acrylic-coated canvas, PVC-coated polyester, woven polypropylene tarpaulin and a plastic sheet, a drip test rig made from a funnel and a clamp, a spray bottle, a candle, long tongs, a metal tray of sand and a results table. The governing idea is that textiles are waterproofed either by filling the fibres and gaps with a water-repellent substance such as wax, oil or silicone, which repels drops while allowing some breathing, or by coating the fabric with a continuous film such as acrylic, polyurethane or PVC, which blocks water entirely, that the treatment's effectiveness is tested by drip and spray with time and penetration recorded, that fabrics differ sharply in flame, cotton burning to ash, synthetics melting and dripping, wool charring and self-extinguishing, with flame-retardant treatments slowing ignition, and that a shelter textile must balance waterproofing, fire behaviour, weight, cost and durability. Investigating waterproofing and burning characteristics is examinable.

## Subtopic: How a Textile Is Waterproofed

Woven canvas, the traditional tent fabric, is cotton or a cotton blend woven tightly, and on its own it is not waterproof. Water wets the cotton fibres, which swell, and the first rain soaks through, although a tightly woven canvas swells so much that the gaps close and it becomes nearly waterproof while wet, which is how old canvas tents worked, heavily and slowly. To make canvas waterproof from the start, it is treated with a chemical, and there are two families of treatment.

The first family repels water from the fibres. Wax, usually paraffin wax, sometimes with beeswax, is melted and worked into the cloth, so each fibre is coated in a substance water cannot wet; drops bead and roll off, the weave still has tiny gaps so the fabric can breathe, and the treatment is cheap and can be renewed by rubbing more wax in. Oils and oil-based dressings, such as the linseed oil on oilskin, do the same and were the sailor's waterproofing for centuries. Modern fluorocarbon and silicone water-repellent sprays and dips put an extremely thin repellent layer on every fibre, so the fabric looks and feels untreated but beads water; they are light and breathable but wear off with abrasion and washing and must be reapplied. All of these are water repellent rather than strictly waterproof: they stop rain, but water pressed hard against them, a pool on a sagging roof or a knee on wet ground, will eventually come through.

The second family coats the fabric with a continuous film that water cannot pass at all. Acrylic coatings are painted or sprayed on canvas and dry to a flexible waterproof skin; polyurethane coatings are used on nylon and polyester tent flys; PVC, polyvinyl chloride, is laminated or spread onto polyester to make the heavy truck-tarpaulin material that is completely waterproof, tough and long lasting, and the woven polypropylene tarpaulin sold in every hardware shop is plastic tape woven and then laminated with a thin plastic film on both sides. Coated fabrics do not breathe, so condensation forms inside, which is why a coated shelter must have ventilation, and the coating can crack with age and ultraviolet light. Seams are the weak point of every waterproof fabric: each needle hole leaks, so seams are taped, welded or sealed with a seam sealant, or the fabric is used in one piece.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plain canvas leaks, the repellent family of wax, oil and silicone, the coating family and seams.

## Subtopic: Testing Waterproofing

Test each sample the same way so that the results can be compared. The drip test: clamp the fabric square taut over a jar, set the funnel 100 millimetres above it, pour 50 millilitres of water into the funnel so it drips steadily onto one spot, and time how long before the first drop appears in the jar, up to five minutes, then measure what has collected. The spray test: hold the square at 45 degrees over a tray, spray it ten times from 200 millimetres with the bottle, and score what happens: water beads and runs off completely, beads but some wetting of the surface, soaks in but does not pass, or passes through. The pressure test: lay the wet square on a paper towel and press with a thumb for ten seconds; a dry towel means it resists pressure, a wet patch means it does not.

Record the results in a table of sample against test. Expect this pattern. Untreated canvas: first drip within a minute, soaks in on the spray, wet towel. Waxed canvas: no drip in five minutes, beads on spray with slight surface wetting, towel damp under firm pressure. Silicone-sprayed canvas: no drip, perfect beading, towel damp under pressure, and if a square is rubbed hard with sandpaper first, beading fails where it was rubbed. Acrylic-coated canvas: no drip, no wetting, dry towel. PVC-coated polyester and polypropylene tarpaulin: no drip, no wetting, dry towel, and a cut edge shows the layers. Plastic sheet: dry towel but tears at the clamp. Add a column for weight, by weighing each square, and for feel and flexibility, because a shelter fabric that is waterproof but too heavy to carry or too stiff to fold fails other requirements.

Then test a seam. Sew two pieces of the tarpaulin together with a needle and thread and run the drip test on the seam line: it leaks at every hole. Cover the seam with tape or a smear of silicone sealant and test again. This small experiment explains why every tent and every shelter kit comes with taped seams or seam sealant, and it is a design detail you must show on your shelter drawing next week.

The questions for this section are with you now: the drip, spray and pressure tests, the expected results by sample, and the seam test.

## Subtopic: Burning Characteristics and the Choice

The flame test is a teacher's demonstration, at arm's length over a tray of sand, with long tongs, a candle, a jug of water and the windows open, and no learner handles a burning sample. Each square is held edge-first in the candle flame for three seconds, withdrawn, and watched. Record four things: does it catch alight; does it keep burning when taken out of the flame or go out by itself; does it melt and drip; what is left, ash, a hard bead, a char. Cotton canvas, untreated: catches at once, burns steadily with a yellow flame when withdrawn, no melting, leaves soft grey ash; waxed canvas burns faster and hotter, because wax is fuel. Polyester and nylon: shrink from the flame, melt into a bead that drips and keeps burning briefly, leave a hard black bead that would stick to skin; PVC-coated polyester: hard to ignite, chars and gives off dense, acrid, toxic smoke, goes out when withdrawn, which is why PVC is both a safer and a more poisonous material in fire. Polypropylene tarpaulin: melts quickly, drips burning droplets, burns away. Wool: singes, chars slowly, smells of burnt hair, goes out when withdrawn. Canvas treated with a flame-retardant chemical, such as borax and boric acid solution or a commercial treatment: chars where the flame touches but does not catch or spread, and goes out.

Those results are the burning characteristics the syllabus names: ease of ignition, whether the fabric sustains a flame, whether it melts and drips, the smoke, and the residue. They matter for the shelter because the families inside will have stoves and candles, because a dripping, melting roof causes the worst burns, and because smoke in a sealed shelter kills before flame does. The textile designer cannot have everything. The most waterproof, cheapest and lightest option, polypropylene tarpaulin, has the worst fire behaviour; the fire-retardant canvas is heavier, dearer and needs its waterproofing renewed; PVC resists fire but smokes poisonously and weighs most. Real shelter fabrics are compromises: a flame-retardant-treated, waterproof-coated polyester or canvas, which costs more but drips less and resists ignition, or a cheap tarpaulin used with strict fire spacing, an outdoor cooking area and a liner of slower-burning material inside.

Make the choice as a table. Down the side, the samples. Across the top, waterproof score, pressure resistance, breathability, ignition, melting and dripping, smoke, weight, cost, durability, and a row for the verdict. Weight the columns by the brief: waterproofing and fire are safety and come first, then weight and cost because thousands are needed, then breathability, which ventilation can replace. Choose, and write the reason, because the brief in week seven asks you to specify a textile for the shelter and the examiner wants to see that the specification came from tests and not from a guess. Most teams land on a flame-retardant-treated coated canvas or polyester for the roof and walls and a cheaper tarpaulin for the groundsheet, where fire is less of a risk and pressure resistance matters more.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling waxed canvas waterproof after the spray test alone, without the pressure test that shows it leaks under a knee. Two: sewing a waterproof shelter with plain seams and expecting it to stay dry. Three: choosing polypropylene tarpaulin for everything because it scored best on water, ignoring that it drips fire. Four: learners holding samples in the flame themselves; the burning test is a demonstration, with tongs, sand and water, by the teacher.

The questions for this section are with you now: the flame demonstration and each fabric's behaviour, what the characteristics mean for a shelter, and the choice table.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a funnel dripping on fabric squares and a candle in the teacher's hand — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Keeping Water Out

Plain canvas soaks through, though it swells nearly shut when wet. Two ways to fix it. Repel: wax, oil or silicone coats each fibre so drops bead and roll off; it breathes, it is cheap, it wears off, and water under pressure gets through. Coat: acrylic, polyurethane or PVC lays a continuous film; nothing gets through, nothing breathes, so you need vents, and the coating cracks with age.

The hardware shop tarpaulin is woven plastic tape laminated both sides.

Every needle hole leaks. Tape, weld or seal the seams.

Keep the beading drops in mind and try the questions for this part: why canvas leaks, repelling, coating and seams.

## Subtopic: Test It

Drip test: funnel 100 millimetres above the clamped square, 50 millilitres, time to the first drop, up to five minutes. Spray test: ten sprays at 45 degrees, score beading to soaking through. Pressure test: wet square on a paper towel, thumb for ten seconds.

Plain canvas fails fast. Waxed and silicone canvas bead but dampen under pressure. Coated canvas, PVC and tarpaulin stay dry. Weigh each square too.

Sew a seam in tarpaulin and drip on it: it leaks at every hole. Tape it and test again.

Hold the thumb on the wet square in mind and try this part's questions: the three tests, the pattern of results and the seam.

## Subtopic: Fire, and the Choice

Teacher only, tongs, sand, water, three seconds in a candle. Cotton catches and burns to ash; waxed cotton burns hotter. Polyester and nylon melt and drip a hot bead. PVC resists but smokes poison. Polypropylene tarpaulin melts and drips burning drops. Wool chars and goes out. Flame-retardant canvas chars and goes out.

Record: catches, keeps burning, melts and drips, smoke, what is left. A melting roof is the worst burn; smoke kills first.

Make the choice table: water, pressure, breathing, ignition, dripping, smoke, weight, cost, durability. Safety first, then weight and cost. Most teams choose flame-retardant coated canvas or polyester for the cover and cheap tarpaulin for the floor, and write why.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Textiles are waterproofed by repellents like wax, oil and silicone, which bead and breathe but leak under pressure, or by coatings like acrylic, polyurethane and PVC, which block water but not vapour; seams must be sealed. Test by drip, spray and pressure. In flame, cotton burns to ash, synthetics melt and drip, PVC chars and smokes, wool and treated fabrics char and go out. Choose the shelter textile from a table with safety first.

Hold the choice table, and take the final questions of the lesson: the flame results, what they mean and the summary.
