### subtopic_1 — Cooking to the Chart
**Assistant:** If the tasters say the relish is delicious, why do we still have to work out the nutrition separately? Surely good food is nourishing.

**Tutor:** Because taste and nourishment are different properties of the same plate and one tells you almost nothing about the other, which is a lesson the whole food industry depends on. Sugar, salt and fat make food taste good, and a plate of chips with sauce would score excellent for flavour from most Grade 7 tasters while carrying almost no protein, no vitamins and far too much salt. The opposite is also true: a plain lentil and spinach stew can be nutritionally close to perfect and score adequate for flavour because nobody seasoned it. In a camp this matters more than at home, because the people eating have no other food to balance the plate; if the one hot meal a day is tasty but short of protein, children slowly become malnourished while cleaning their plates. So nutritional value is assessed by reasoning, not tasting, and the reasoning is the one you practised in the design brief: name the food groups on the plate, staple, protein, fat and vegetable, estimate the portion weights, apply the rough energy figures, cereals about 350 kilocalories per 100 grams dry, tinned fish about 150, oil 900, vegetables 20 to 40, and compare with the target of about a third of 2 100 for a main meal. Then check the special needs: is there a soft, enriched portion for the under-fives and the elderly, is the meal fortified, is there anything the sick or pregnant need. A dish can therefore score excellent for flavour, good for texture and adequate for nutrition, and that is a useful result, because it tells the cook exactly what to change, a spoon more oil or a side of cabbage, without touching the recipe that tasters loved. Keeping the three criteria separate on the sheet is what makes the evaluation a tool rather than a compliment, and in the examination, if asked how to evaluate a meal for nutritional value, the answer is by its food groups and quantities against the daily requirement, never by how it tastes.

### subtopic_2 — Evaluating Flavour, Texture and Nutrition
**Assistant:** Our pap came out lumpy. Does that count against the whole dish, and how do we stop it next time?

**Tutor:** It counts against texture for the pap, and it should be recorded exactly like that, as a specific result with a cause and a fix, which is what turns a lumpy pot into a good evaluation. Lumps in pap form when dry meal hits boiling water in a mass, so the outside of each clump cooks instantly into a skin while the inside stays dry, and no amount of stirring afterwards breaks them. Cooks prevent it in one of two ways, and your team should pick one and write it into the chart. The first is to make a slurry: mix the meal with a little cold water in a bowl to a smooth paste, then pour the paste into the boiling water while stirring, so every grain is already wet and separated before it meets the heat. The second is the traditional method: lower the heat so the water is just simmering, sprinkle the meal in slowly through the fingers with the other hand stirring constantly, and only once it is all in and smooth, cover and turn the heat low to cook through. Either way the stirring spoon stays in motion during the adding and the meal never goes in faster than the stirring can absorb. Two other points help: use the quantity the chart says, because too much meal for the water thickens too fast, and stir from the bottom with a wooden spoon, scraping, so nothing catches, then cover and leave it for twenty minutes without lifting the lid every minute, which lets steam escape and dries the top. For the soft-food portion, take some pap out early, while it is still thin, and stir in the milk powder and oil, rather than trying to thin stiff pap later, which makes lumps of its own. In the evaluation, write: pap texture adequate, lumpy, because meal was added in one go to boiling water; improvement: make a cold slurry first and add while stirring; soft portion taken aside before the pap thickens. That paragraph shows the examiner the whole cycle of make, evaluate, diagnose, improve, and it is worth more than smooth pap you could not explain.

### subtopic_3 — Writing the Evaluation and Improvements
**Assistant:** Why can we not keep the leftover relish for tomorrow? It seems wasteful to throw good food away.

**Tutor:** It is wasteful, and it is also the rule, and the two facts together are why camp kitchens cook the right quantity in the first place. Cooked food left between about 5 and 60 degrees is in what food safety people call the danger zone, where bacteria that survived cooking or landed afterwards from hands, flies and the air double in number every twenty minutes or so. A pot of fish and tomato relish at room temperature in a warm hall is at the ideal temperature for them, and by the next morning it can carry enough bacteria and the toxins they make to cause vomiting and diarrhoea in everyone who eats it, even if it is reheated, because some toxins survive heat. At home we break the chain with a fridge, which holds food below 5 degrees and slows the bacteria almost to a stop, and we reheat leftovers thoroughly. The brief for this task says there is no fridge, so there is no way to break the chain, and the safe rule becomes the simple one that every aid kitchen follows: hot food is served within two hours of cooking and anything left after that is thrown away. The waste is real, so the answer is not to accept it but to plan it out, and that is a design improvement for your evaluation. Cook the number of portions on the list, 150 grams of relish per person measured with the ladle, and keep a tally of plates served against people fed so that the second pot is started only when needed. Keep some ingredients unopened rather than cooking everything at once, because a sealed tin keeps for years and an open pot keeps for two hours. If leftovers do appear, serve second helpings at once to the hungry, especially growing teenagers and breastfeeding mothers, which is not waste at all. And if a camp is going to last, a fridge or a cold box with ice becomes a priority on the needs list, right behind water and toilets, precisely because it turns tonight's waste into tomorrow's lunch. In the written evaluation, note how much was left over, what you did with it, and what quantity you would cook next time.
