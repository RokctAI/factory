# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the making and evaluating lesson of the emergency food task: preparing one item of the planned meal and evaluating it for flavour, texture and nutritional value. The anchor is a school kitchen in Polokwane with four teams at four burners, each with its flow chart, ingredients for ten portions, a pot, a board, a knife, a tin opener, a hand washing station, clean tasting spoons and an evaluation sheet with the three criteria and a four-level scale. The governing idea is that the item is made by following the sequence of manufacture with hygiene and safety steps observed, that readiness is tested by texture, taste and temperature, that the finished food is evaluated against criteria set in advance by tasters who did not cook it, that nutritional value is judged from the food groups and quantities on the plate rather than by taste, and that the written evaluation records results, problems, fixes and improvements. Making and evaluating a food item are marked and examinable skills.

## Subtopic: Cooking to the Chart

Set up before lighting anything. The flow chart is propped where all four can see it. The hand washing station, bucket, jug, soap and towel if there is no tap, is at the end of the bench. Ingredients are laid out in the order they are used, left to right; equipment likewise; the raw board and the ready board are separate and labelled with tape. Everyone washes hands and the hygiene lead ticks the first box. Then the pap cook sets the water to boil, because the chart says that column starts first, and the relish cook begins chopping onions on the raw board while the water heats.

Follow the boxes and tick them. Heat the oil on medium, add the onions, fry, and at five minutes apply the first diamond: press an onion against the pot; soft and translucent, continue; still firm, two more minutes and test again. Open the tomato tin now, not earlier, lid lifted by its edge, add and let it simmer two minutes. Open the pilchards, slide them in with their sauce, break the fish gently into chunks with the spoon rather than mashing. Salt and a pinch of curry powder. Simmer, small bubbles, ten minutes, stirring now and then with the handle turned away from the walkway. Meanwhile the pap cook has added the meal to the boiling water, stirring to a smooth paste, covered it and turned the heat low for twenty minutes, and will take a portion aside to thin with milk powder and oil for the soft-food plates.

Second diamond at ten minutes: taste with a clean spoon, once, spoon into the dirty bowl. Flat, add a pinch of salt or spice, stir, a minute, taste again with a new spoon. Seasoned, continue. Third diamond: steaming hot throughout and simmered its ten minutes, yes, serve. The hygiene lead washes hands again, portions the pap at about 250 grams and the relish at about 150 grams per plate using the ladle as a measure, and keeps the soft portions separate. The safety lead lifts the pots with a dry cloth, moves them off the heat and turns off the burners. Note the time on the chart; the plan said thirty minutes for the relish and forty for the pap, and the actual times go beside them.

The questions for this section are with you now: setting up, following the boxes with the three diamonds, and portioning and timing.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Flavour, Texture and Nutrition

Evaluation uses the criteria agreed last week, before the cooking, so that the test is fair and not a matter of liking your own food. Tasters are from another team, two or three per dish, with a clean spoon each, water to clear the palate, and the sheet with its three criteria and four levels: excellent, good, adequate, poor, with a description for each level so that two tasters agree. The cooking team does not comment while the tasters taste; they listen and record.

Flavour: is the dish seasoned so that the salt is not tasted as salt, the spice is a warmth, the tomato and onion have cooked into the fish, and a person would want a second helping? Excellent: well seasoned, rich, appetising. Good: pleasant, slightly under or over seasoned. Adequate: edible but bland or harsh. Poor: unpleasant or unsafe. Texture: are the onions soft, the fish in chunks not mush, the sauce thick enough to sit on pap, the pap smooth and stiff for adults, and the soft-food portion smooth, thin and lump-free for a child or a grandmother? Each gets its own line, because adults and the soft-food group have different needs. Nutritional value is not tasted; it is reasoned. Does the plate carry a staple, a protein, a fat and a vegetable? Yes: pap, pilchards, oil, tomato and onion, with cabbage if the side was made. Does the portion carry roughly a third of the day's energy? Pap 250 grams at about 350 kilocalories per 100 grams dry weight, which is about 120 grams of meal, gives roughly 420; relish about 150 grams gives about 200; total about 620 to 650, a little under the 700 target, so the evaluation notes it and suggests a spoon of oil or a slice of bread. Is it fortified meal, and does the soft portion carry extra energy and protein from the milk powder and oil?

Tasters score each criterion, write one sentence of evidence beside the score, "onions soft, fish in chunks, sauce coats the spoon", and sign. The cooks record the scores, add their own observations of the process, actual times against planned, any step that went wrong, any safety or hygiene slip, and the fixes made on the spot, and then compare the dish with the specification from the brief: a nutritious, tasty meal preparable in camp conditions, 150 grams of relish per portion, served hot within two hours.

The questions for this section are with you now: fair tasting, the four-level descriptions for flavour and texture, and reasoning nutritional value from the plate.

## Subtopic: Writing the Evaluation and Improvements

The written evaluation is the last product of the task and it follows a fixed shape. One: what was made, for whom and against what brief, in two sentences. Two: results, the tasters' scores for flavour, texture and nutrition with their evidence, the actual times, the portions achieved, whether the specification was met. Three: what went well, with reasons: "the relish was ready in twenty-eight minutes because the onions were chopped while the water boiled." Four: what went wrong, with causes: "the pap was lumpy because the meal was added too fast to the boiling water and not stirred to a paste first." Five: fixes made on the day and improvements for next time, each tied to a cause: add the meal through the fingers while stirring; add a spoon of oil to the relish to lift the energy to 700; taste at eight minutes rather than ten so there is time to adjust. Six: what the exercise taught about camp cooking: that fuel and time are the real limits, that hygiene has to be built into the sequence, that the soft-food group needs its own portion, that a tin opener is the most important tool in the kitchen.

Be honest and specific, because that is what earns marks and what makes the next meal better. "It was nice" is not an evaluation; "flavour scored good, slightly under seasoned according to two of three tasters, because we added salt only once at the end" is. A dish that scored adequate with a clear diagnosis and a convincing improvement is worth more in the PAT than a dish that scored excellent with no analysis, and in a real camp kitchen the cook who writes down why the porridge was lumpy is the one whose porridge is smooth tomorrow.

Finish the session as the chart says: pots and spoons washed in hot soapy water, boards scrubbed, surfaces wiped, leftover food covered and either eaten within two hours or discarded, since a camp has no fridge and neither does the plan. Tick the last box. Put the chart with its actual times, the signed tasting sheets and the written evaluation into the design folder, which now holds the whole task from investigation to evaluation.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: the cooks tasting with the stirring spoon and putting it back in the pot. Two: judging nutritional value by whether it tastes healthy; nutrition is reasoned from the food groups and quantities on the plate. Three: an evaluation that says "it was nice" and "we would do the same again". Four: leftover relish left on the bench to be eaten next day, in a plan that has no fridge.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six-part written evaluation, honesty and specificity, and the clean-up and folder.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pot on the burner, a clean spoon and a scoring sheet — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Cook It

Chart up, hand washing station ready, ingredients in order, raw board and ready board labelled. Everyone washes hands. Pap water on first; onions chopped while it heats.

Follow the boxes. Fry, test soft. Tomato in, simmer two. Fish in gently, season, simmer ten with the handle turned in. Pap stirred smooth, covered, low heat, a portion thinned with milk powder and oil for the soft plates.

Taste with a clean spoon, once. Adjust. Steaming hot? Serve: 250 grams pap, 150 grams relish, soft portions kept apart. Dry cloth on the pots, burners off, actual times written beside the planned ones.

Keep the clean tasting spoon in mind and try the questions for this part: setup, the boxes and diamonds, and portions.

## Subtopic: Taste It Fairly

Tasters from another team, clean spoons, water, a sheet with three criteria and four levels agreed last week. Cooks stay quiet and write.

Flavour: seasoned, warm spice, would you want more? Texture: soft onions, fish in chunks, sauce that sits on pap; pap stiff for adults, smooth and thin for the soft group. Nutrition is reasoned, not tasted: staple, protein, fat, vegetable on the plate, about 620 to 650 kilocalories, a little under 700, so add a spoon of oil.

Score, write one sentence of evidence, sign.

Hold the four-level sheet in mind and try this part's questions: fair tasting, flavour and texture levels, and nutrition reasoning.

## Subtopic: Write It Up

Six parts: what and for whom; results with scores, times and portions; what went well and why; what went wrong and why; fixes and improvements tied to causes; what it taught about camp cooking.

Be specific. "Under seasoned because salt was added only once at the end" earns marks; "it was nice" does not. A diagnosed adequate beats an unexplained excellent.

Wash up hot and soapy, cover or discard leftovers within two hours, tick the last box, file the chart, the tasting sheets and the evaluation.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Cook to the chart with hygiene and safety as steps and three readiness tests. Have others taste against agreed criteria for flavour and texture, and reason nutrition from the plate. Write a six-part evaluation that is honest, specific and tied to causes, and finish with the clean-up and the folder.

Hold the signed tasting sheet, and take the final questions of the lesson: the six parts, honesty and the summary.
