# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the making-skills planning lesson for the emergency food task: writing the sequence of manufacture for one item of the planned meal. The anchor is a school kitchen in Port Elizabeth where a team has its meal plan for ten portions, a blank sheet, a ruler and the flow chart shapes from term three, and forty minutes to plan a cooking session of thirty minutes. The governing idea is that preparing food is a manufacturing process with inputs, steps and an output, that the steps are written as instructions in dependency order with times, that hygiene and safety steps, hand washing, clean surfaces, safe knife use, handling of hot pots, are steps in the sequence and not afterthoughts, that decision points test readiness by taste, texture and temperature, and that the chart is rehearsed and roles assigned before cooking begins. Writing a sequence of manufacture is examinable and the cooked item is evaluated in the next session.

## Subtopic: Cooking as Manufacturing

A kitchen is a small factory. Raw materials come in, maize meal, tins, onions, water; processes happen to them, washing, opening, chopping, heating, stirring, seasoning; and a product goes out, a plate of food. Technology treats food preparation as a manufacturing process exactly like making the crane, and plans it the same way, with a sequence of manufacture that lists every step in order with its time, its tools and its person. The difference is that food spoils, burns and poisons if the order or the timing is wrong, so the sequence matters even more.

Choose the item. The relish for ten is a good one: two tins of pilchards in tomato sauce, one tin of tomatoes, two onions, a tablespoon of oil, salt and a pinch of curry powder, cooked in one pot on one burner in twenty minutes. The soft enriched porridge is another: 400 grams of maize meal, 2 litres of water, 50 grams of milk powder, a tablespoon of oil and a pinch of salt, fifteen minutes. Pick one and list its raw materials as inputs, its equipment as resources, pot, knife, board, tin opener, wooden spoon, ladle, bowls, and its output as a number of portions at a stated texture and temperature.

Then list the steps on scrap paper as instructions beginning with a verb, before drawing anything. For the relish: wash hands with soap; wipe the surface; collect ingredients and equipment; peel and chop the onions; heat the oil in the pot on medium; fry the onions until soft, about five minutes; open the tomato tin and add; open the pilchard tins, break the fish gently and add with the sauce; add salt and curry powder; simmer, stirring, for ten minutes; taste and adjust; check it is steaming hot; serve 150 grams per portion with pap; wash up. That is fourteen steps, and almost all of them have a time and a tool.

The questions for this section are with you now: inputs, processes and output in a kitchen, the chosen item and the step list.

## Subtopic: Order, Hygiene and Safety

Order the steps by dependency and by time, as with the crane. Hands are washed before anything is touched, and again after handling raw onions and before serving. The surface is wiped before food is put on it. Onions are chopped before the oil is hot, because a hot pan waits for no one. Tins are opened just before they are added, not an hour before, so the open food does not sit in the warm kitchen collecting flies. Water for pap is set to boil first because boiling takes longest; in a two-item sequence the pap and the relish run in parallel columns, pap started first, and meet at serving.

Hygiene steps are steps, drawn in their own rectangles, because in a camp they are the difference between a meal and an outbreak. Wash hands with soap and clean water for twenty seconds before cooking, after the toilet, after touching raw food, before serving. Use clean water for cooking and for washing the vegetables. Keep raw food and ready food on separate boards and in separate bowls. Cover food that is waiting. Serve hot food hot, above 60 degrees, steaming, and within two hours of cooking; food left warm for hours is where bacteria multiply. Clean the pot and spoons with hot water and soap after use, not just a rinse.

Safety steps are steps too. Knives: cut on a board, blade away from the body, fingers curled under on the holding hand, knife put down flat, not left in the washing water. Tins: open with the opener fully round, lift the lid by its edge, never run a finger round the rim. Heat: the pot handle turned away from the walkway, a dry cloth to lift it, never a wet one, which conducts heat; lids lifted away from the face so steam goes the other way; the pot never filled past two thirds so it does not boil over; children and crowding kept away from the burner. Burns are the commonest kitchen injury and in a camp there may be no clinic for them. Write a safety step beside each hot or sharp step on the chart, and name who is responsible.

The questions for this section are with you now: dependency order, the hygiene steps that must be drawn and the safety rules for knives, tins and heat.

## Subtopic: Drawing, Timing and Rehearsing the Chart

Draw the chart with the term-three shapes. Oval: start. Rectangles for every step, each with a verb, a time and the person's initials. Diamond for each test: "onions soft?" with no looping back to "fry two more minutes"; "tastes seasoned?" with no going to "add salt or spice" and back; "steaming hot and simmered ten minutes?" with no returning to the simmer. Oval: end, at serving. For two items cooked at once, two columns, pap on the left starting with "boil 4 litres of water", relish on the right, joined by arrows into a single "serve" rectangle. Ruler, square corners, no crossing arrows.

Add the times up each column. Relish: hands and surface two minutes, chop four, fry five, open and add three, simmer ten, taste and adjust two, serve three, about thirty minutes. Pap: boil water eight, add meal and stir five, cook covered twenty, stir and rest five, about forty, so the pap column starts ten minutes before the relish column, and the chart shows that by placing its first box higher. If the period is forty-five minutes, washing up must fit in the last five, which the chart can show as a final shared box. A chart that adds to sixty minutes in a forty-five minute period is a plan to fail, and now is when you shorten it: chop while the water boils, use the pilchards' sauce instead of a separate tomato tin, serve as soon as hot.

Assign roles and rehearse. Four people: one on pap, one on relish, one on hygiene and serving, who manages the hand washing, boards and bowls and portions out, and one on safety and washing up, who handles the hot pots with the dry cloth and keeps the burner area clear. Walk through the chart with each person pointing at their own boxes, saying what they do and with what, and checking that no two people need the knife or the burner at the same moment. Where a conflict shows, move a box. Where a step has no tool listed, add it. The rehearsed, timed, role-assigned chart goes into the design folder and comes to the kitchen next period as the recipe card.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: hygiene left off the chart because "we will obviously wash our hands", which in a rushed kitchen means nobody does. Two: tins opened at the start and left in the warm for half an hour. Three: a chart that totals sixty minutes for a forty-five minute period. Four: no test before serving, so the relish goes out underseasoned or the porridge lumpy, and the evaluation next week marks it down.

The questions for this section are with you now: the shapes and the three tests, adding up the times and assigning roles.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a recipe card, four shapes and a ruler — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Kitchen Is a Factory

Inputs: maize meal, tins, onions, water. Processes: wash, open, chop, heat, stir, season. Output: plates of food. Plan it like the crane, with a sequence of manufacture.

Pick one item: the relish for ten, or the soft porridge. List inputs, equipment and the output as portions at a stated texture and temperature.

List the steps on scrap paper with a verb each: wash hands, wipe surface, chop onions, heat oil, fry, open and add tins, season, simmer, taste, check hot, serve, wash up.

Keep the relish pot in mind and try the questions for this part: inputs and outputs, the item and the step list.

## Subtopic: Order, Clean, Safe

Hands first. Chop before the oil is hot. Open tins just before they go in. Water for pap on first because it takes longest.

Hygiene is a step with its own box: soap and clean water for twenty seconds, before cooking, after raw food, before serving. Separate boards. Cover waiting food. Serve steaming hot within two hours. Hot soapy wash-up.

Safety is a step too: knife on a board, blade away, fingers curled; tin lid lifted by the edge; pot handle turned in, dry cloth, lid lifted away from the face, never more than two thirds full, nobody crowding the burner.

Hold the dry cloth in mind and try this part's questions: order, hygiene boxes and safety boxes.

## Subtopic: Draw It, Time It, Rehearse It

Oval start, rectangles with verb, time and initials, diamonds for "onions soft?", "seasoned?", "steaming hot?" with no arrows looping back, oval end at serving. Two columns if pap and relish cook together, pap starting earlier.

Add up the times. Relish about thirty minutes, pap about forty. If it does not fit the period, shorten: chop while water boils, use the tin's own sauce.

Four roles: pap, relish, hygiene and serving, safety and washing up. Walk through pointing at your own boxes. Move any clash.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Cooking is manufacturing: inputs, steps, output. List steps with verbs, order them by dependency and time, draw hygiene and safety as steps, test by taste, texture and temperature before serving, time the columns against the period, assign roles and rehearse.

Hold the timed two-column chart, and take the final questions of the lesson: shapes, times, roles and the summary.
