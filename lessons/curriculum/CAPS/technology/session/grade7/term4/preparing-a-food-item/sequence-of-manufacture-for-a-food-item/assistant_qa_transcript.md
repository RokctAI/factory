### subtopic_1 — Cooking as Manufacturing
**Assistant:** Why does the chart put "wash hands" as a step with a box? Everybody knows to wash their hands.

**Tutor:** Everybody knows, and in kitchens around the world the inspectors still find that it does not happen, which is why professional kitchens and factories write it as a step with a time and a place rather than relying on knowing. Three things go wrong with knowing. The first is pressure: when the water is boiling, the onions are catching and ten hungry people are waiting, the mind narrows to the pot, and the hands go from the raw onion board to the serving spoon without a thought; a box on the chart at that exact point, after raw food, before serving, is an interruption that the pressure cannot delete. The second is the camp itself: in a school kitchen there is a tap and soap beside you, but in a hall or a tent the water is in a bucket across the room and the soap is wherever it was put down, so hand washing has to be planned as a station, with a bucket, a jug to pour, soap and a towel, placed near the cooking, and that planning only happens if the step is on the chart. The third is accountability: when a box has initials beside it, one person knows it is theirs, and the hygiene and serving role in your team exists precisely so that one pair of eyes is on hands, boards and bowls while the cooks are on the pots. The consequences are not small. Diarrhoeal outbreaks in camps are traced again and again to food handlers' hands, and in a camp with limited water and no clinic a bout of food poisoning among the elderly or the under-fives is dangerous. So the chart treats hygiene as manufacturing treats quality control: not a thing good people naturally do but a step in the process, with a time, a place, materials and a name. When you draw it, draw it at least three times, at the start, after raw food, before serving, and put the hand washing station on the kitchen layout beside the chart.

### subtopic_2 — Order, Hygiene and Safety
**Assistant:** How do we know when the relish is ready? Different people in our group think it should cook for different times.

**Tutor:** You stop arguing about minutes and agree on tests, which is what the diamonds on the chart are for, and the tests are the ones every cook uses whether they know it or not. For the relish there are three readiness tests, in order. First, texture of the onions: they should be soft and translucent before the tins go in, because raw onion in a short-cooked sauce stays sharp and crunchy, and the test is to press a piece against the pot's side with the spoon and see it give. That takes about five minutes on medium heat but could be three or eight depending on the burner and the chop, which is exactly why a time alone is not enough. Second, after the tomato and fish are in, the sauce should simmer, small bubbles breaking the surface steadily, not a rolling boil that breaks up the fish and spits, for long enough that the flavours join and the sauce thickens slightly so it coats the spoon; around ten minutes, and the test is the spoon, not the clock. Third, taste, with a clean spoon used once and not returned to the pot: it should be seasoned enough that the salt is not noticeable as salt and the spice is a warmth rather than a bite, and if it is flat, a pinch more salt or curry powder, stir, wait a minute, taste again with a new spoon. Alongside these is the safety test, which is not negotiable: the food is steaming hot throughout, which for a sauce with tinned ingredients that are already cooked means heated to a simmer and held there, and it is served within two hours. Put those three diamonds on the chart with their no arrows looping back, soft? seasoned? steaming? and the argument about minutes disappears, because minutes become estimates for planning and the diamonds become the decision. That is also the answer an examiner wants to a question about quality control in food preparation: readiness is tested by texture, taste and temperature at defined points in the sequence, not assumed from time.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing, Timing and Rehearsing the Chart
**Assistant:** Could we make the flow chart shorter by combining steps like "open tomato tin" and "open pilchard tins" into one box?

**Tutor:** You can combine steps when they happen at the same moment, with the same tool, by the same person, and nothing needs to happen between them, and the two tins almost pass that test, so it is a fair question. Both are opened with the same tin opener by the same person, and both go into the pot at about the same point. But look at what sits between them in a good sequence: the tomato goes in first and simmers for a minute or two before the fish, because the fish is already cooked and falls apart if it is stirred through a long simmer, so there is a short step between the two tins, and if you combine the boxes the person doing it opens both at once and either adds them together, breaking the fish, or leaves the open pilchard tin standing, which is a hygiene issue. Keeping them as two boxes, "open and add tomato tin, simmer two minutes" and then "open pilchard tins, add gently with sauce", costs one rectangle and keeps the order visible. The general rule from term three applies: a step is one box if one person can do it without stopping, with one set of tools, and nobody else depends on something happening halfway through. Steps split when there is a wait, a change of tool, a change of person or a test between them. Steps combine when they are a single uninterrupted action; "peel and chop the onions" is one box, not two, because nobody peels and then goes away. Watch the other direction too: a chart with forty tiny boxes is as useless as one with four huge ones, because nobody can follow it in a hot kitchen. For a relish, twelve to sixteen boxes plus three diamonds is about right. The test of a good chart is that a team mate who did not write it can cook the dish from it without asking a question, and a cook who was handed "open tins" would have asked which first.
