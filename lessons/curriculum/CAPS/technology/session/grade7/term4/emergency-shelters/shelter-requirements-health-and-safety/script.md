# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the emergency shelter design task and its health and safety requirements. The anchor is a classroom in Pietermaritzburg where a class, having studied the scenarios, works through a model site plan of a shelter camp on a sports field after a flood, with shelter footprints, paths, water points, toilets and a fire gap drawn to scale, and derives from it the requirements for one family shelter and for its placement. The governing idea is that the shelter task is to design and make a model of a sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter for a family of six for a month, that its requirements cover space, weather protection, structural stability, erection, transport and durability, and that health and safety requirements, fire prevention and spacing, ventilation against smoke and damp, safe cooking, drainage, water and sanitation placement, security, privacy and site hazards, are specifications of equal standing because a shelter that keeps the rain out and poisons or burns its family has failed. Stating the shelter task and its health and safety issues is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Task and Its Core Requirements

The task, as the syllabus words it: design and make a model of a sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter for a family of six that will house them for a month. Each adjective is a requirement. Sturdy: it must stand up to wind of at least 60 kilometres per hour, which is a strong Highveld or Cape storm, without collapsing or lifting, and carry the weight of rain on its roof and of people leaning on its walls; it must survive a month of use, children climbing on it and daily opening and closing. Waterproof: no rain through the roof or walls, no water coming up through the floor or running in under the walls, which means a raised or sealed floor and a roof pitched so water runs off rather than pooling. Easy to erect: two adults, under an hour, no tools beyond a hammer or mallet, instructions in pictures.

The family of six sets the size: at least 3.5 square metres each, 21 square metres, about 4 by 5 metres, with headroom to stand in the middle, at least 1.8 metres, and a door. A month sets durability and also comfort: a family will cook, wash, store food and belongings, and sleep for thirty nights, so they need somewhere to put things off the floor, a way to hang a light, and some separation inside between sleeping and the rest. Transport and cost follow from the scenario: thousands are needed at once, so the shelter must pack flat into a bundle two people can carry, fit many to a truck, and cost little, which pushes toward simple frames and sheet materials rather than rigid panels.

Write these as the first block of a requirement list: space 21 square metres and 1.8 metres headroom; wind 60 kilometres per hour; waterproof roof, walls and floor; erected by two adults in under an hour with a mallet; packed bundle under 40 kilograms; lasts a month; has a door. Each will become a specification in the brief in week seven and a test for the model in the evaluation, where the model will be sprayed with water, blown with a fan or hair dryer, loaded and timed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the task sentence and its adjectives, what the family and the month require, and the first block of the list.

## Subtopic: Fire, Smoke and Air

Health and safety requirements begin with fire, because the families in the shelters will cook and heat with the same paraffin stoves, gas rings and candles that caused the fires in the first scenario, and a camp of plastic-sheet shelters a metre apart is a settlement waiting to burn again. Three requirements follow. Spacing: at least two metres between shelters, more between rows, so radiant heat does not carry fire from one to the next, and a wider fire break every few rows so an engine or a bucket chain can reach the middle of the camp. Cooking: outside the shelter if the weather allows, in a sheltered cooking area, and if inside, on a stable raised surface away from the walls and the door, never on the floor beside bedding; the shelter design should give a place for this. Materials: a cover that is flame retardant or at least slow to burn and does not drip burning plastic, which is a textile property you will test in week six, and a frame that does not feed the fire.

Smoke and air come next. A paraffin stove or open fire inside a closed shelter produces carbon monoxide, which has no smell and kills people in their sleep, and smoke that harms lungs, especially children's. A sealed waterproof shelter is therefore also an airtight one unless ventilation is designed in: vents high up, where warm air and smoke collect, and low down, where fresh air enters, protected from rain by hoods or overlaps, and closable in a storm. Ventilation also controls damp: six people breathing in a sealed tent for a night deposit litres of water on the inside of the cover, which drips on bedding, grows mould and chills the family. So the requirement reads: ventilation openings at high and low level, rain-protected, closable, totalling at least a stated area, with a stove position marked that is away from the cover.

Light and heat belong here too. A candle is a fire; the requirement is a hook for a solar or battery lamp, which the camp should provide, and a design that lets in daylight through a translucent panel so candles are not needed by day. Against cold, the shelter must hold body heat: an insulating layer, a floor that stops heat going into the ground, and a door that closes fully. Against heat in summer, shade and ventilation, a light-coloured or reflective roof, and an air gap between the roof and the ceiling if there is one. The design has to say which climate it is for, and in South Africa a shelter for the Western Cape in winter and one for Limpopo in summer will differ.

The questions for this section are with you now: fire spacing, cooking and materials, ventilation against carbon monoxide and damp, and light and temperature.

## Subtopic: Water, Sanitation, Security and the Site

Water and sanitation are shelter requirements because they are decided by where the shelter stands. Drainage: the shelter must be on ground that sheds water, with a small trench or raised edge around it so rain running across the site goes round and not in, and the camp laid out so shelters are not in the lowest, wettest part of the field. Water point: within 500 metres of every shelter, as the standard says, and the paths to it clear and lit. Toilets: at least 50 metres from shelters so smell and flies do not reach them, but not so far that people, especially women and children at night, will not use them, and downhill from the water point so they cannot contaminate it; one toilet for every twenty people. Waste: a rubbish point for each group of shelters, emptied, because waste attracts rats and flies. Hand washing beside the toilets and beside the cooking area, with soap.

Security and privacy are health and safety for people who have just lost everything and are living among strangers. A door that closes and can be fastened from inside. Walls that are not see-through at night when lit from within. Shelters grouped so that families from one street or village are together, and the layout lit at night along paths and at toilets. Women and children at particular risk, so toilets and washing areas for women are placed where they are visible from shelters and lit. Space inside for the family's belongings so they are not left outside. These do not cost much in design terms, a door flap with ties, an opaque cover, a layout decision, and they make the difference between a camp that is safe and one that is not.

The site itself has hazards. After a fire the ground is covered in nails, glass and sharp iron, so the shelter needs a floor and the site needs clearing. After a flood the ground is mud and may flood again; shelters go on the highest, firmest part. After an earthquake, away from damaged buildings and walls that may fall in an aftershock. Everywhere: away from power lines, rivers and roads, with vehicle access for water tankers and ambulances, and with a plan drawn on paper, because a camp that grows without a plan ends up with toilets uphill of the water and no way in for the fire engine. A health and safety requirement list, then: fire spacing two metres; stove position and flame-retardant cover; high and low rain-protected ventilation; lamp hook and daylight panel; insulated floor; drainage trench; water within 500 metres; toilets 50 metres away, downhill, one per twenty; hand washing points; fastenable door and opaque walls; lit paths; cleared, high, firm, safe site with vehicle access.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a perfectly waterproof shelter with no ventilation, which fills with carbon monoxide the first cold night a stove is lit inside. Two: shelters pitched a metre apart to fit more on the field, recreating the fire that displaced the families. Three: toilets placed uphill of the water point for convenience. Four: a see-through plastic shelter with no fastening on the door, in a camp of strangers.

The questions for this section are with you now: drainage, water and toilet placement, security and privacy, site hazards and the full list.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a plan of the camp on the board and one family's shelter marked on it — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Task

Design and make a model of a sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter for a family of six for a month.

Sturdy: stands in a 60 kilometre per hour wind and a month of use. Waterproof: roof, walls and floor, with a pitched roof and a raised or sealed floor. Easy to erect: two adults, under an hour, a mallet, picture instructions.

Six people: 21 square metres, 1.8 metres headroom, a door, somewhere to put things. A month: durable and liveable. Thousands at once: flat-packed, two people carry it, cheap.

Keep the task sentence in mind and try the questions for this part: the three adjectives and what six people and a month require.

## Subtopic: Fire and Breathing

Families will cook inside with the same stoves that started the fires. So: two metres between shelters and fire breaks between rows; a stable raised stove place away from walls and bedding, or cook outside; a cover that does not drip burning plastic.

A sealed shelter with a stove inside fills with carbon monoxide, which has no smell, and with smoke and damp. So: vents high and low, hooded against rain, closable in a storm.

A lamp hook instead of candles, a daylight panel, an insulated floor and a door that closes fully for cold; shade, a light roof and air gaps for heat.

Hold the hooded vent in mind and try this part's questions: spacing, the stove, ventilation and light.

## Subtopic: Water, Toilets, Safety, Site

A trench or raised edge keeps running water out. Water point within 500 metres. Toilets 50 metres away, downhill of the water, one per twenty people, with hand washing and soap. A rubbish point that is emptied.

A door that fastens from inside, walls you cannot see through at night, families grouped, paths and toilets lit, women's areas visible and lit, space for belongings.

Clear the nails and glass, choose high firm ground, stay away from damaged walls, power lines and rivers, leave room for a tanker and an ambulance, and draw the plan.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The task is a sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter for six for a month: 21 square metres, 60 kilometre per hour wind, waterproof roof, walls and floor, up in an hour by two adults, carried by two, lasting a month. Health and safety are specifications too: fire spacing and stove place, ventilation against carbon monoxide and damp, lamp and daylight, drainage, water and toilet placement, a fastening door and opaque walls, a cleared and planned site.

Hold the camp plan, and take the final questions of the lesson: water and toilets, security, the site and the summary.
