### subtopic_1 — The Task and Its Core Requirements
**Assistant:** Why is ventilation such a big deal? If the family is cold, surely they want the shelter as sealed as possible.

**Tutor:** They do want it sealed, and that instinct kills people every winter, which is why the requirement is written as a hard specification rather than left to the occupants. The danger is carbon monoxide. Any flame, a paraffin stove, a gas ring, a charcoal brazier or imbawula, a candle, burns the oxygen in the air and gives off carbon dioxide, and when the oxygen runs low in a closed space the burning becomes incomplete and produces carbon monoxide instead. Carbon monoxide has no colour, no smell and no taste; it binds to the blood far more strongly than oxygen does, so the body suffocates while breathing normally; the first symptoms, headache, drowsiness and confusion, feel like tiredness, so people lie down and go to sleep and do not wake. Deaths from braziers and stoves in sealed rooms and shacks are reported in South Africa every cold season, often whole families. The second danger is slower: six people breathe out about a litre of water each overnight, and a stove adds more, so a sealed waterproof shelter has its inside surfaces streaming with condensation by morning, bedding is damp, mould grows within days, and damp cold bedding is worse for the body than a draught. So the design has to provide ventilation that the family will not block, which means it cannot be a hole they can stuff with a blanket. The standard solution is a high vent under the roof peak, where hot air, smoke and carbon monoxide gather, and a low vent near the floor on the opposite side, so that a gentle current passes through, both protected by a hood or an overlap of fabric so rain cannot enter, and with a flap that can be partly closed in a storm but not completely. The area is set by the shelter's size; for 21 square metres, two openings of about 0.1 square metre each at the top and bottom are a typical minimum. Pair this with a stove place that is raised, stable, away from the cover and under the high vent, and the family keeps warm and keeps breathing. In your model, show the vents and the stove place and label them, and in the evaluation, test that the vents are hooded by spraying water at them.

### subtopic_2 — Fire, Smoke and Air
**Assistant:** Two metres between shelters wastes a lot of space on a crowded field. Could we not put them closer and just ban stoves inside?

**Tutor:** You could write the rule, and you could not enforce it, and the gap is cheaper than the fire. Start with enforcement: a camp of a thousand shelters has perhaps a dozen staff at night, families with small children and elderly people need warmth and cooked food, it is raining, and the stove is inside within a week whatever the notice says, because the alternative is a cold child. Aid agencies learned long ago to design for what people will do, not for what they are told to do, which is why the stove place and the ventilation are inside the brief rather than a prohibition. Now the space arithmetic, which is less bad than it looks. A 4 by 5 metre shelter with a two metre gap on all sides occupies 6 by 7 metres, 42 square metres of field for 20 of shelter, so roughly half the field is gaps; on a full-size sports field of about 7 000 square metres that is about 160 family shelters, or close to a thousand people, which is a reasonable camp for one site. Pitching them a metre apart would raise that to perhaps 230 shelters, but at the cost of the whole camp being one fire, and a camp that burns displaces everyone a second time with nothing. The gaps are not wasted either: they are the paths families use, the space where cooking happens outside when weather allows, the room for a water bucket and a line of washing, and the drainage route for rain. The standard goes further and asks for a wider fire break of about 30 metres between blocks of shelters every so often, so that a fire in one block can be stopped and vehicles can reach the middle. If the field is genuinely too small, the right answer is a second site, not a tighter layout, and that is a decision for the camp plan you draw before anyone pitches a shelter. There is one more consideration from last lesson: the families in the shelters may have just lost everything to a fire that spread exactly because their homes were a metre apart. Rebuilding that layout in plastic would be a failure of investigation as well as of design.

### subtopic_3 — Water, Sanitation, Security and the Site
**Assistant:** Why does the lesson treat privacy and a lockable door as health and safety? That sounds more like comfort.

**Tutor:** Because in a camp of strangers after a disaster, the lack of them leads directly to harm, and the people harmed are mostly women and children. Consider what a displaced family has: nothing but what is in the shelter, which is now their house, their safe, their wardrobe and their bedroom. Without a door that fastens, belongings are taken while they fetch water; without opaque walls, the shelter lit by a lamp at night shows every movement inside to the whole row; without separation inside, a mother has no way to change, nurse a baby or wash a child out of sight. Humanitarian studies of camps report consistently that gender-based violence and theft rise where shelters cannot be secured, where toilets are far and dark, and where strangers are mixed without lighting. These are injuries as real as a burn. Privacy also carries health in a plainer sense: a sick family member can be kept a little apart, a baby can sleep, and the mental strain of weeks with no moment unobserved, which doctors in camps treat as a real condition, is reduced. The design cost of meeting these needs is small, which is why they belong in the brief rather than in a wish list. A door flap that overlaps and ties or toggles from the inside. A cover fabric that is opaque, or a liner inside a translucent one. A sheet or curtain line inside to divide sleeping from living. A layout rule that puts families from the same street together and lights the paths and toilets. And, from the site plan, women's washing areas and toilets placed where they are visible and near, not hidden at the edge. Comfort is a word for things that would be nice; these are things without which people are hurt, which is the definition of a need from week one, and that is why they stand in the health and safety list beside fire and water. When you sketch your shelter in week seven, draw the door fastening and the internal divider and label them, because the examiner is looking for exactly that evidence of having understood the people as well as the weather.
