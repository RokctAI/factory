### subtopic_1 — Fire and Flood
**Assistant:** Why do shack fires spread so fast when brick houses in the suburbs almost never burn down a whole street?

**Tutor:** Because almost every factor that slows a fire in a suburb is reversed in an informal settlement, and listing them is a lesson in how technology and planning decide safety. Materials first: a brick house with a tiled or iron roof on a timber frame has walls that do not burn and a roof that resists for a long time, while a shack is timber poles, hardboard, cardboard, plastic sheeting and iron, where the iron does not burn but everything holding it up does, and the plastic burns with dripping, spreading flame. Spacing second: suburban houses sit five to ten metres apart with walls, gardens and driveways between them, so radiant heat from one burning house rarely ignites the next, while shacks stand a metre apart or share walls, and radiant heat alone at that distance will light the neighbour's wall before any flame touches it; the wind then carries burning fragments across the gaps. Ignition sources third: a suburban house has an electrical supply with a circuit breaker and a stove on a fixed base, while a shack is lit by candles and heated by a paraffin stove on the floor beside bedding, with wiring often an illegal connection through bare joins; the night-time knock, the child's blanket, the drunk fall are each a start. Detection and response fourth: a suburban fire wakes the household through smoke alarms and solid walls give minutes to get out and call a fire engine that can drive to the door and find a hydrant; a shack fire wakes people when the heat reaches them, the lanes are too narrow for an engine, and there is no hydrant, so the first water arrives when the fire is already a block wide. Finally, a settlement has no fire breaks, the open strips that planners leave to stop a fire crossing, because every metre of land is a home. None of this is about the people; it is about the technology and the planning around them, and that is why the cures are technological too: fire-retardant building boards, wider lanes and fire breaks laid out with the community, networked smoke alarms that wake the whole block, safer stoves with wide bases, and a shelter for the displaced that does not repeat the mistakes, with a two-metre gap between units and a stove kept outside.

### subtopic_2 — Earthquake and Strife
**Assistant:** If technology causes these disasters, would people not be safer without it?

**Tutor:** It is the right question to ask and the wrong conclusion to draw, and working out why is the heart of the impact of technology topic. Take the paraffin stove. It causes shack fires, so remove it: the family now has no way to cook or to warm a room in a Cape winter, and the alternatives are an open fire indoors, which is worse, or no hot food and children sick from cold. The problem was never the stove in itself; it was a stove designed to a low price with a narrow base that tips, used on the floor beside bedding because there was no table, in a room with no ventilation. The technological answer is a better stove and a safer setting, not no stove, and that is what organisations have worked on: stoves with wide bases and flame guards, cooking shelves, and solar lamps so the candle is not needed. The same holds for each scenario. Floods are made worse by hard surfaces and blocked drains, but they are made survivable by weather radar, phone alerts, bridges built to standard and houses built off the flood plain, all of which are technology. Mining sets off tremors, but mining also pays the wages of the town that was shaken, and the engineering answer is monitoring and building codes rather than closing the mines. Even the shack itself is technology that people chose because the alternative was no home, and the question is how to make it a safer one. So the way to think about the impact of technology, and the way to answer an examination question on it, is in three steps: name the technology and what it is for; name its positive impacts and who benefits; name its negative impacts and who or what is harmed, people, society, the environment; and then say how the design could be changed to keep the benefit and reduce the harm. A stove that does not tip, a settlement with fire breaks, a drain that is cleared, a shelter that is spaced and ventilated. Technology without thought causes disasters; technology with investigation and design is how communities survive them.

### subtopic_3 — The Impact of Technology
**Assistant:** Why does the shelter have to be easy for the people themselves to put up? Will there not be aid workers to do it?

**Tutor:** There will be far fewer than you imagine, far later than you would hope, and the people are already there, which is why the humanitarian world treats self-erection as a basic requirement rather than a nice feature. Consider the numbers in a real event. The 2022 KwaZulu-Natal floods displaced tens of thousands of people across hundreds of sites on the same night, with roads and bridges down; the disaster teams, fire services and volunteers in the province numbered a few thousand, most of them searching for the missing, clearing roads and running water and food. If every shelter needed a trained team of three and an hour, a thousand shelters would take three thousand worker-hours, which is a week's work for a hundred people who are not available. Meanwhile the families are sitting in the rain tonight. The only system that scales to the size of a disaster is one in which the shelter arrives as a kit, with a picture instruction sheet that needs no language, and two adults who have never seen it before put it up in under an hour with at most a hammer. This is the same logic as the flow chart in term three: design the process so that the people who are already present can do it, in parallel, without waiting for a scarce resource. There are three further reasons. People who build their own shelter choose where it faces, how it is pegged and where the door goes, and they maintain it, which a shelter put up for them rarely gets. A shelter they can erect is one they can take down, move and re-erect when the site changes, which happens often. And in the first hours, doing something practical for your own family is itself a help against the shock of the night. So when you write the specification in week seven, the erection requirement is as hard a specification as waterproofing: two adults, under an hour, one tool, picture instructions, and you will test your own design against it with a stopwatch.
