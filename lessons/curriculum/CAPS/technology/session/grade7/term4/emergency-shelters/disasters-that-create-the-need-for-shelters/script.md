# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the emergency shelter task through its scenarios and the impact of technology on them. The anchor is a classroom in Cape Town in winter, with news photographs of a shack fire aftermath in Khayelitsha, the 2022 KwaZulu-NATAL floods, the 2014 Orkney earthquake and families displaced by violence sheltering in a community hall, studied one at a time for cause, speed, scale and what people need that night. The governing idea is that shack fires, floods, earthquakes and political strife each destroy housing in a particular way and on a particular timescale, that technology is both a cause and a cure, the stove and the dense settlement that spread fire, the drainage and the building standards that fail, and the shelter, the water tanker and the early-warning message that help, that the people displaced need a sturdy, waterproof, easy-to-erect shelter within hours, and that understanding the scenario tells the designer what the shelter must survive. Describing these scenarios and the impact of technology is examinable.

## Subtopic: Fire and Flood

Shack fires are the most frequent disaster in South African cities. In an informal settlement the homes are built of corrugated iron, timber, cardboard and plastic sheeting, a metre or less apart, and heated and lit with paraffin stoves, candles and illegal electrical connections. A knocked-over stove or a candle against a curtain lights a shack in a minute, the wind carries flames across the gap to the next, and the fire moves faster than people can wake. In 2017 a fire in Imizamo Yethu in Hout Bay destroyed over two thousand homes and displaced about ten thousand people in a night; smaller fires take fifty or a hundred homes most winter weeks across the country. What is left is a field of ash, iron sheets and the stumps of poles. People escape with what they are wearing, often barefoot, often having lost identity documents, school uniforms and the week's food. The need is immediate: a dry, warm place to sleep that same night, in winter, often in rain, for hundreds at once, on land that is covered in sharp debris.

Floods come from heavy rain on ground that cannot absorb it, rivers that overflow and storm drains that block or were never built. In April 2022 more than 300 millimetres of rain fell on parts of KwaZulu-Natal in a day; rivers through Durban's informal settlements rose metres in hours, washed shacks off slopes, collapsed roads and bridges and killed more than four hundred people, with thousands losing homes. Floods also hit formal houses: in Ladysmith, in the Vaal, in Mozambique's Limpopo valley in 2000 where whole towns were under water for weeks. Flood victims may have hours of warning or none; they lose homes to water rather than fire, so belongings are soaked and spoiled rather than burnt, roads are cut so help is slow to arrive, water supplies are contaminated, and the ground where the shelter must stand is mud. The need is for a shelter that is raised or floored against wet ground, that stands on soft soil in wind, and that lasts weeks, because flood-damaged homes take months to repair and rebuilding on the same flood plain may be forbidden.

The questions for this section are with you now: how shack fires start and spread, what the 2022 floods destroyed and what each disaster leaves people needing.

## Subtopic: Earthquake and Strife

Earthquakes are rare in South Africa but not absent. In August 2014 a magnitude 5.5 earthquake centred near Orkney in the North West shook the gold fields, killed one person, damaged hundreds of houses in Khuma and surrounding townships and was felt from Durban to Botswana; many of the tremors in mining areas are set off by deep mining itself. In an earthquake, buildings crack or fall, and the danger continues afterwards from aftershocks and unstable walls, so people cannot go back inside even to houses that look intact until engineers have checked them. The displaced sleep outside, in cold, often for days, next to their damaged homes, with their possessions inside and unreachable. Overseas, earthquakes in Türkiye and Syria in 2023 and in Haiti in 2010 displaced millions. The need is for shelters that go up quickly in open spaces such as sports fields, that are light so no one is hurt if an aftershock knocks one down, and that can stand for weeks while houses are inspected or rebuilt.

Political strife displaces people by making a place unsafe rather than by destroying it. In May 2008 xenophobic violence spread through townships from Alexandra to Cape Town, more than sixty people were killed and tens of thousands of foreign nationals fled with what they could carry to police stations, churches and community halls, where some stayed for months; the violence returned in 2015 and in smaller waves since. War and civil conflict in the Democratic Republic of Congo, Somalia, Zimbabwe at various times, and Mozambique's Cabo Delgado have sent refugees across Southern Africa, some to camps in neighbouring countries, some to South African cities. Strife has its own pattern: people flee suddenly and far, they are afraid to return, they may be targeted again in the shelter, they may not speak the local language, and they often cannot prove who they are. The need is for shelter that offers safety and privacy as well as weather protection, that can be set up in or beside existing buildings, and that may be needed for a long time.

Across all four, the shelter brief is remarkably similar. Within hours. For families, so with privacy and a door. Sturdy against wind, waterproof against rain, with a floor against wet or sharp ground. Easy to erect by the people themselves with few tools. Light enough to transport in large numbers and to move again. Lasting at least a month. Safe from fire, because the stoves that caused one disaster will be used again inside the shelter. The differences are in detail: a raised floor for floods, lightness for earthquakes, separation and lockable doors for strife, fire spacing above all for a settlement that has already burned once.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Orkney earthquake and its aftermath, how strife displaces people, and the brief that all four scenarios share.

## Subtopic: The Impact of Technology

Technology sits on both sides of every one of these disasters, and the syllabus asks you to see both. On the causing side: the paraffin stove, cheap and portable, is the main source of shack fires, and the illegal electrical connection the second; the dense layout of a settlement, with no fire breaks, is itself a technology decision made by poverty and by the absence of planning. Floods are made worse by hard surfaces that stop rain soaking in, by storm drains blocked with plastic waste, by houses built on flood plains and steep slopes because there was nowhere else, and by a changing climate that brings heavier rain. Mining sets off tremors. Social media spreads the rumours that light xenophobic violence. Each is a technology or a technological choice with an unintended impact on people and on the environment.

On the curing side: the shelter itself, from a plastic sheet to a tent to a prefabricated unit, is technology. The early warning system that sent flood alerts to phones in 2022 saved lives where it reached people. The fire station's pumps and the water tanker. The water purification tablets and the mobile toilet. The registration database that helps a family prove who they are and reunite. The temporary classrooms so children keep learning. The safer paraffin stove with a wide base that does not tip, and the solar lamp that replaces the candle. A community fire alarm system in some Cape Town settlements uses networked smoke detectors to wake neighbours before a fire spreads, a technology designed exactly for this scenario.

Technology also has impacts on the environment and on society that a designer must weigh. A thousand plastic-sheet shelters that are abandoned after a month become a thousand sheets of plastic waste. Tents imported from overseas cost foreign money and arrive late; shelters made locally from local materials arrive sooner and give local work, which is one reason the next lesson looks at how rural South Africans have always built. A shelter that is too comfortable and permanent can become a settlement nobody intended, as happened in some post-2008 camps. A shelter that is too flimsy fails in the first storm and the family is displaced twice. Positive and negative impacts both belong in your investigation and both will appear in the examination: name a technology, say who it helps and how, and say what harm it may do and to whom.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating the disasters as the same; a flood needs a raised floor and an earthquake needs lightness, and the designer who ignores the scenario builds the wrong shelter. Two: seeing technology only as the cure, forgetting that the stove and the blocked drain are technology too. Three: forgetting that people will cook inside the shelter, so fire safety is designed out of a brief for fire victims. Four: assuming everyone displaced has documents, language and family nearby.

The questions for this section are with you now: technology as a cause of each disaster, technology as a cure, and the social and environmental impacts to weigh.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with four photographs on the board and one question under each: what must the shelter do here — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Fire and Water

Shacks stand a metre apart, built of iron, wood and plastic, lit by candles and heated by paraffin stoves. One tips over, the wind carries the fire, and thousands of homes go in a night, as in Imizamo Yethu in 2017. People escape with what they wear. They need a dry, warm place to sleep tonight, on ground covered in sharp debris.

Floods come from heavy rain, overflowing rivers and blocked drains. In April 2022 KwaZulu-Natal lost over four hundred people and thousands of homes in a day of rain. Belongings are soaked, roads are cut, water is dirty, and the ground is mud.

A flood shelter needs a floor and must last weeks on soft ground.

Keep the field of ash and the muddy slope in mind and try the questions for this part: how fires spread, what floods destroy and what each needs.

## Subtopic: Shaking and Violence

In 2014 an earthquake near Orkney damaged hundreds of houses. After a quake people cannot go back inside until the walls are checked, so they sleep outside for days. Shelters must go up fast on open ground and be light enough to do no harm if they fall.

In 2008 xenophobic violence drove tens of thousands into police stations and halls. People flee suddenly, cannot go back, may be targeted again and may have no documents. Shelters must give safety and privacy and may be needed for months.

All four scenarios share one brief: within hours, for families, sturdy, waterproof, floored, easy to erect, light, lasting a month, safe from fire. The scenario sets the details.

Hold the family sleeping outside a cracked house in mind and try this part's questions: earthquake, strife and the shared brief.

## Subtopic: Technology Both Ways

Technology causes: the paraffin stove, the illegal wire, shacks a metre apart, blocked drains, houses on flood plains, mining tremors, rumours on phones.

Technology cures: the shelter, the flood alert on a phone, the water tanker, purification tablets, the mobile toilet, the registration database, the safer stove, the solar lamp, networked smoke alarms that wake the neighbours.

Weigh the impacts: plastic waste from abandoned shelters, imported tents that arrive late, camps that become permanent, flimsy shelters that fail. Name who is helped and who is harmed.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Shack fires, floods, earthquakes and strife each take homes in their own way and speed. All leave people needing a sturdy, waterproof, floored, quick, light, lasting, fire-safe shelter, with details set by the scenario. Technology causes and cures, and its impacts on people and the environment must both be weighed.

Hold the stove and the shelter side by side, and take the final questions of the lesson: causes, cures, impacts and the summary.
