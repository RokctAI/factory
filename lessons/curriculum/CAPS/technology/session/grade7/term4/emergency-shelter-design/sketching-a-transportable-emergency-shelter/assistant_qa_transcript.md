### subtopic_1 — Two Structures, Not One
**Assistant:** How do we make a curved dome cover out of flat fabric? Fabric comes in flat rolls.

**Tutor:** You cannot bend a flat sheet into a dome without wrinkles, which is why every dome tent and every globe is made from panels, and the designer's choice is how many and what shape, with a trade-off between fit and seams. The simplest method is gores, the shape of orange segments: for a dome of 5.2 metres diameter you might cut six or eight panels, each a curved-sided triangle wide at the base and narrowing to the top, and join them along their curved edges to make a cover that follows the rods closely. More gores fit the curve better but add seams, and every seam must be welded or taped and is a place to leak and to tear, so most relief domes use six to eight. The pattern for a gore is found by laying a strip of paper or cloth over the straw model or a half-scale frame, marking the rod lines, cutting and flattening it; tent makers do exactly this on a full-size frame, which is why making a dome cover takes a factory rather than a camp. The second method is the one agencies often choose precisely because it avoids all of that: a dome or hoop frame with a flat rectangular cover thrown over it, as a tarpaulin over hoops, which wrinkles at the corners and pools a little at the low points but needs no cutting, uses the standard four by six metre sheet, and can be replaced by any tarpaulin from any shop; the wrinkles are managed by pulling the sheet tight over the hoops and folding the corners under like a bed sheet. A third method is a hybrid: a flat cover over a ridge or tunnel of parallel hoops, which is a half-cylinder rather than a dome, so a flat sheet fits it perfectly with no cutting at all, and that tunnel shape, a row of hoops with a straight ridge, is the one many aid tents actually use. So the answer to put on your sketch is one of three: gored cover, factory-made, best fit, most seams; flat sheet over hoops, no cutting, wrinkles and some pooling, replaceable anywhere; or a tunnel of hoops under a flat sheet, no cutting and a good fit, with flat ends that must be guyed. Choose, draw it, and write the reason, because the question of how the cover is cut is exactly the kind of making problem the design stage is supposed to catch before the model.

### subtopic_2 — Annotating for the Lists
**Assistant:** Why must the roof be pitched at 30 degrees? Would a shallower roof not give more headroom and catch less wind?

**Tutor:** It would do both, and it would also fill with water and collapse, which is the failure that has flattened more relief tents than wind ever has. Picture a roof of fabric, not iron: fabric sags between its supports, and however tight you pull it on the first day it stretches, the pegs loosen and the poles settle. On a shallow roof, the sag quickly becomes a hollow below the level of the edges, rain collects in it instead of running off, the weight of the water deepens the hollow, more water collects, and in one night a few millimetres of rain can leave a hundred litres, a hundred kilograms, hanging in the roof, which tears the fabric at the eyelets or bends the frame and brings the shelter down on the family. Snow on a tent does the same, faster. A pitch of 30 degrees or more means that even when the fabric sags, every point on the roof is still higher than the edge below it, so water runs off as fast as it falls and the roof carries only its own weight. Steeper is safer still, which is why the A-frame and the dome, which is steep near its edges, shed water so well, and why the beehive hut and the rondavel roof are steeper than 45 degrees. The headroom you lose is real, and the design answer is not a flatter roof but higher walls under a pitched one, which is what the ridge-with-raised-walls hybrid and the box frame do, at the cost of more frame. The wind point is also real but smaller than you would think: a pitched roof does catch wind, which is what the guy ropes and tie-downs are for, but a shallow roof pooling water is a certain failure where a pitched roof in wind is a managed risk. Your straw-and-tissue model shows all this in a minute: pour a teaspoon of water on a flat tissue roof and watch it pool and sag; pour it on a pitched one and watch it run to the edge. Put 30 degrees on the sketch as a dimension, and in the evaluation of your model, the water spray test will confirm it.

### subtopic_3 — Checking, Choosing and Modelling
**Assistant:** Our dome idea scored best on wind and speed but the stove place is a problem because the wall curves in. Should we abandon the dome?

**Tutor:** Not yet, because the stove place is a problem to be designed around rather than a flaw in the structure, and the check table is meant to generate exactly this kind of design work, not just a verdict. Look at why the curved wall is a problem: a stove on the floor near the edge of a dome is close to fabric that leans in over it, so heat and any flare go straight onto the cover. Three design responses, each of which you can sketch and credit. First, move the stove to the centre of the dome, where the roof is highest, directly under the peak vent, which is where the smoke wants to go anyway, and mark a one-metre square of floor there with a paving slab or a sheet of iron, and a hanging guard; this is how the beehive hut managed its fire, in the middle under the high point, and it keeps the family's sleeping places round the edges where the roof is low but the stove is far. Second, raise the dome on a short vertical wall, a ring of straight rod or pole sections of 600 to 800 millimetres before the arcs begin, which gives usable headroom at the edges, moves the fabric away from a stove near the wall, and is the shape of many real relief domes and of the Nama matjieshuis's steeper sides; it costs a little more rod and a slightly bigger cover. Third, take the cooking outside altogether, under a small separate awning or in the camp's shared kitchen, and mark the shelter as sleeping only, which is the safest answer of all and is what the health and safety list prefers where the climate allows. Any of these turns the question mark into a tick, and the choice between them depends on the scenario you wrote at the top of the brief: a Cape winter pushes toward option one or two, a Limpopo summer toward option three. So the dome stays in the running, your sketch gains a second version with the chosen fix drawn and labelled, and the check table gains a row of reasoning that the examiner will read as design thinking. Abandon an idea when a cross cannot be designed away, such as a box frame that cannot be made in under an hour; keep it when the cross is an invitation to improve.
