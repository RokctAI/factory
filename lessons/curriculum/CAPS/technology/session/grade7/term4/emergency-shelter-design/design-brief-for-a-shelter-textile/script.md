# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the design brief and specification for a textile suitable for an emergency shelter, drawing on the investigations of protective clothing, indigenous materials and the waterproofing and burning tests. The anchor is a classroom in Bloemfontein where each team has its results tables, the shelter requirement list and a sample of its preferred fabric, and writes a brief that a textile supplier could quote against. The governing idea is that a textile brief states the purpose, the user and the conditions, that its specifications quantify waterproofing by a named test, flame behaviour by a named test, mass per square metre, tear and abrasion strength, ultraviolet resistance, opacity, colour, breathability or ventilation allowance and seam sealing, that constraints cover cost per square metre, availability in South Africa, delivery time and quantity, and that the brief is traceable to the tests, so that each number has an experiment behind it. Writing a specification for a shelter textile is examinable.

## Subtopic: From Tests to a Brief

A brief for a textile is written for a different reader from a brief for a crane. The crane brief was for your own team; the textile brief is written as if for a supplier or a factory, who will not see your tests and must be told exactly what to make or sell, and for an inspector, who must be able to check the delivered roll against the words. So it begins, as always, with what, for whom and why, in one or two sentences: "A waterproof, flame-retardant, lightweight, hard-wearing fabric for the roof and walls of a family emergency shelter, 21 square metres covered, to be erected by displaced families in South African conditions of rain, wind, sun and cold, and to protect them for at least a month while cooking and heating take place inside with paraffin or gas." Every phrase traces back: waterproof and flame-retardant from the tests, lightweight from the transport requirement, hard-wearing from the month and the wind, South African conditions from the scenarios, cooking inside from the health and safety list.

Then look at the results tables and ask of each property: what did we measure, what did the best acceptable sample achieve, and how do we write that as a requirement someone else could test? Waterproofing: the drip test with 50 millilitres from 100 millimetres for five minutes gave no penetration on the coated samples, and the thumb pressure test left the towel dry; so the specification says: no water penetration in the five-minute drip test and dry under the ten-second thumb pressure test on a wet sample. In industry the equivalent is a hydrostatic head figure, the height of a column of water the fabric holds before leaking, and a shelter fabric needs at least 1 500 millimetres, so write both, your test and the industry test, if you know it.

Flame: the candle test showed what we want and do not want. The specification says: must not sustain a flame after three seconds in a candle flame, must not melt or drip, must self-extinguish within two seconds of removal, char allowed; and it names the industry version, a recognised flame-retardant standard for tent fabrics, as the supplier's proof. The method is the same for every line: our test, our result, the requirement, and where possible the recognised standard.

The questions for this section are with you now: who the textile brief is written for, the brief sentence and its traceability, and turning the water and flame tests into requirements.

## Subtopic: The Full Specification

Write the specifications as a numbered list in order of importance, each measurable. One, waterproofing, as above. Two, flame behaviour, as above. Three, mass: the shelter's cover of about 30 square metres including overlaps must be carried by two people with the frame, so the fabric may weigh at most 350 grams per square metre, which puts the cover under 11 kilograms; coated polyester and lighter coated canvas meet this, heavy PVC truck tarpaulin at 600 to 900 grams does not, and that is a decision made by a number. Four, strength: it must not tear further when a 20 millimetre cut is pulled by hand, and must resist abrasion where it rubs on the frame; in industry these are tear strength and abrasion tests with numbers, and the brief can ask for a ripstop weave, where thicker threads at intervals stop a tear running, which you can see in many tent fabrics. Five, ultraviolet resistance: it must keep its strength and waterproofing after a month of South African sun, so a UV-stabilised coating or fibre is required, because untreated polypropylene tarpaulin goes brittle in a season.

Six, opacity and colour: opaque when lit from inside at night, for privacy, and light in colour on the roof to reflect sun, with a stated colour so that a camp is visible to helicopters and not camouflaged; many agencies use white or light grey roofs with a coloured band. Seven, breathability or its substitute: the fabric may be non-breathing provided the shelter design has the ventilation openings specified last week, and the brief says so, because the textile and the structure are designed together. Eight, seams: all seams to be heat-welded or taped, no plain sewn seams, tested by the drip test on a seam sample. Nine, fixings: the fabric must take eyelets or webbing loops at 500 millimetre spacing without tearing, reinforced with a second layer at each point, because that is where wind tears a cover. Ten, handling: it must fold without cracking at minus 5 degrees and not stick to itself at 40 degrees, since the kit will be stored and used in both.

Now the constraints, in their own column, because they are about the programme rather than the fabric. Cost: no more than a stated rand figure per square metre, which you set from a hardware shop price list, perhaps R60 to R90 for coated fabric, so that a cover costs under R3 000 and a thousand shelters are affordable. Availability: produced or stocked in South Africa, deliverable within two weeks, in rolls or pre-cut covers, in quantities of a thousand. Compatibility: must work with the frame design, with the eyelet spacing matching the frame's hoops. Environment: the agency may ask that the fabric be recyclable or reusable, given the waste left by abandoned shelters. Check each line against the question from term three: is this about the fabric, or about the programme? Fabric lines are specifications; programme lines are constraints.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ten specifications and the numbers behind them, and the constraints of cost, availability, compatibility and environment.

## Subtopic: Choosing, Justifying and Presenting

Put your tested samples against the specification and see which survives. Untreated canvas fails waterproofing and flame. Waxed canvas fails pressure and flame and is heavy. Silicone-treated canvas fails pressure and wear. Plain polypropylene tarpaulin passes water and mass and cost, fails flame badly and UV. PVC-coated polyester passes water, flame ignition and strength, fails mass if it is truck grade and raises the smoke concern. Flame-retardant-treated, acrylic- or polyurethane-coated canvas or polyester passes water, flame, mass at about 300 grams, strength with ripstop, UV if stabilised, and costs more. So the brief points at a flame-retardant-treated, coated, UV-stabilised, ripstop polyester or light canvas of about 300 grams per square metre for the roof and walls, with a heavier, cheaper coated tarpaulin for the groundsheet, where flame matters less and pressure resistance matters more. Write that recommendation under the specification with the reasons, sample by sample, because the examiner is looking for a choice justified by tests.

Be honest about the compromise. The chosen fabric costs perhaps twice the price of plain tarpaulin, and a programme with a fixed budget might choose tarpaulin and manage fire by spacing and outdoor cooking instead; say so, and say which you recommend and why, because that is a design judgement and both answers can earn marks if they are reasoned. State what still has to be tested on the real product that your classroom could not: hydrostatic head, tear strength in newtons, the recognised flame standard, UV ageing. A brief that admits what it does not know is a better brief.

Lay the page out as before: the brief sentence at the top; the numbered specifications in one column with the test and the number for each; the constraints in a second column; the recommendation with its justification; and the list of further tests. Attach the results tables and a labelled sample of the preferred fabric. Have a team mate read it as the supplier: can they quote from it without a question? And as the inspector: can they test a delivered roll against every line? Each question they cannot answer is a missing number. This page is the textile half of the shelter design; the structure half is the sketching next lesson.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: "must be waterproof" with no test and no number, which any supplier will claim and no inspector can check. Two: specifying heavy truck-grade PVC because it scored best on water and flame, and producing a cover two people cannot lift. Three: a fabric specified in isolation from the shelter, with no mention of ventilation or eyelet spacing. Four: choosing by one column, such as cost, instead of by the whole table weighted with safety first.

The questions for this section are with you now: eliminating samples against the specification, the honest compromise and further tests, and the page and its two readers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the results tables on the desk and one page to fill — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Tests Become Words

This brief is for a supplier and an inspector, not your team, so every line must be checkable. Brief: a waterproof, flame-retardant, light, hard-wearing fabric for the roof and walls of a family shelter, 21 square metres, South African weather, a month, cooking inside.

Water: no penetration in the five-minute drip test, dry under the thumb test; industry says a hydrostatic head of at least 1 500 millimetres. Flame: three seconds in a candle, no sustained flame, no melting or dripping, out within two seconds.

Our test, our result, the requirement, the recognised standard: that is the pattern for every property.

Keep the supplier and the inspector in mind and try the questions for this part: the readers, the brief sentence and the two safety lines.

## Subtopic: The Ten Lines and the Limits

One water. Two flame. Three mass, at most 350 grams per square metre so two people carry the cover. Four strength, no tear running from a 20 millimetre cut, ripstop. Five UV resistance for a month of sun. Six opaque at night, light on top, stated colour. Seven non-breathing allowed only with the shelter's vents. Eight welded or taped seams, drip-tested. Nine eyelets every 500 millimetres, reinforced. Ten folds at minus 5, does not stick at 40.

Constraints: price per square metre from the price list, made or stocked in South Africa, delivered in two weeks, a thousand at a time, fits the frame, reusable if possible.

Fabric line or programme line? Specification or constraint.

Hold the ten lines in mind and try this part's questions: the numbers behind them and the constraints.

## Subtopic: Choose and Say Why

Run the samples through. Plain and waxed canvas fail water or flame. Tarpaulin passes water and price, fails flame and sun. Truck PVC too heavy. Flame-retardant coated ripstop polyester or light canvas at about 300 grams passes, at a price. Tarpaulin for the floor.

Be honest: the good fabric costs twice as much, and a tight budget might take tarpaulin and manage fire by spacing. Say which you recommend and why. List what the classroom could not test.

One page: brief, numbered specifications with tests, constraints, recommendation, further tests, samples attached. Let a team mate read it as the supplier and as the inspector.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A textile brief is written so a supplier can quote and an inspector can check. Ten measurable specifications, water, flame, mass, strength, UV, opacity, ventilation, seams, fixings, handling, each from a test, and constraints of cost, availability, fit and environment. Choose the fabric by the whole table with safety first, justify it, and admit what still needs testing.

Hold the sample pinned to the page, and take the final questions of the lesson: the choice, the honesty and the summary.
