# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the design stage of the emergency shelter task: sketching design ideas for a transportable shelter for a family of six and choosing one. The anchor is a classroom in Nelspruit where each team has the shelter requirement list, the health and safety list, the textile brief, squared paper, pencils and a handful of straws, string and a tissue for quick form models, and produces two or more annotated sketches and a reasoned choice. The governing idea is that a shelter is a structure and a textile designed together, that two genuinely different structural ideas should be sketched, such as a bent-rod dome and a ridge or A-frame, each annotated with frame material, cover, floor, door, vents, stove place, tie-downs, dimensions and packed form, that each idea is checked against the specifications with ticks and crosses and borrowed features credited, and that the chosen idea is the one that fails fewest specifications with reasons recorded. Sketching and choosing shelter design ideas is examinable and the sketch leads to the model.

## Subtopic: Two Structures, Not One

Begin as in term three: more than one idea, differing in how the structure works, not in colour. For a transportable shelter there are three classic families, and you have met the engineering behind each. The dome, from the hoop tent and the beehive hut: bent rods pushed into the ground or into a base ring and tied to each other and to hoops, so every member is in compression along its length or tension round a ring and none bends; light, strong in wind from any side, no beams, quick, but needs rods that bend without breaking and a cover cut to the curve. The ridge or A-frame, from the classic tent: two A-shaped ends joined by a ridge pole, the cover thrown over and pegged out, triangulated by its own shape; simple, cover is flat sheets, poles can be straight timber or steel, good headroom along the ridge, but it catches wind on its ends and needs guy ropes. The frame or box, from the gazebo and the prefabricated unit: a rectangular frame of poles with a pitched roof, braced with diagonals as the tower crane was; the most room and the most familiar shape, but the heaviest and slowest, and every rectangle must be triangulated or it folds.

Fold the squared paper in half and sketch idea A on the left and idea B on the right, in 3D if you can manage it, in a side and end view if not. Freehand, labelled, with rough dimensions proving the 21 square metres and 1.8 metre headroom: a dome of 5.2 metres diameter gives 21 square metres and about 2 metres at the centre; a ridge tent 4 by 5.3 metres with a 2 metre ridge gives the same floor but low walls; a box 4 by 5.3 with 1.8 metre walls and a pitched roof gives the most usable space. Write the frame material and section, 16 millimetre fibreglass or conduit rods for the dome, 32 millimetre steel tube or 50 millimetre timber poles for the ridge or box, and the joints, sleeves, lashings, pins, because the joint is where a shelter fails in wind.

Then draw the cover on, in the specified textile, showing how it fixes: eyelets at 500 millimetre spacing over hooks or ties on the frame, a hem that pegs to the ground or tucks under the groundsheet, overlaps laid so water runs off, eaves overhanging the wall if there is one. Draw the groundsheet, the cheaper tarpaulin, turned up at the edges to make a tray against runoff. These are the structure and the textile designed together, which is the whole lesson of the term.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three structural families and their engineering, the two sketches with dimensions and materials, and how the cover and floor are drawn on.

## Subtopic: Annotating for the Lists

Now go down the requirement list and the health and safety list and put every item on the sketch as a labelled feature, because an unlabelled sketch meets nothing. The door: an overlapping flap on the side away from the prevailing wind, with ties or toggles inside and out, drawn and labelled, and its position stated. The vents: a hooded opening at the peak or ridge and a low opening on the opposite wall, each labelled with its size, about 0.1 square metre, and its rain hood. The stove place: a marked rectangle of floor near the high vent, away from the walls, with a note that a sheet of flat iron or a paving slab goes under it; or an outdoor cooking area in the camp layout. The lamp hook at the peak. A daylight panel, a strip of translucent fabric high on one wall, if the textile allows. Storage: a hanging pocket or a low shelf on poles along one wall.

Wind: the tie-downs, guy ropes from the frame to pegs at 45 degrees, and the hem pegged every 500 millimetres; for the dome, a note that the rod feet are pushed 300 millimetres into the ground or fixed to a weighted ring; for the ridge, the guy ropes from each end and from the ridge. Rain: the pitch of the roof, at least 30 degrees for a ridge or box so water runs off and does not pool, which is the error that collapses relief tents; the overhang of the eaves; the turned-up groundsheet. Fire: the two metre spacing, which belongs on a small camp plan beside the sketch, not on the shelter itself, and the stove place. Privacy: the opaque cover, the door that fastens inside, an internal curtain line from a cord across the frame.

Transport and erection: draw the packed form. The dome packs as a bundle of rods, perhaps 1.2 metres long with jointed sections, a rolled cover and a bag of pegs and ties, and you estimate its mass, rods about 8 kilograms, cover 10, groundsheet 4, pegs and ropes 2, under 25 kilograms; the ridge tent packs similarly with longer poles; the box frame is longer and heavier. Write the erection steps in six numbered lines beside the sketch, with the time you believe each takes, and check that they add to under an hour and need only a mallet. That list becomes the picture instruction sheet in the kit and the flow chart for building your model.

Borrow and credit. The bent-rod dome from the hoop tent and the beehive hut. The ridge from the canvas tent. The triangulated frame from the crane and the pylon. The overlapping cover laid bottom up and the net over the roof from Zulu thatch. The low door from the beehive hut. The courtyard layout from the Venda homestead. The turned-up groundsheet from the matjieshuis mat that swells shut. Write each source beside the feature; the rubric gives credit for design ideas traceable to the investigation.

The questions for this section are with you now: labelling door, vents, stove place and storage, wind and rain features, the packed form and erection steps, and crediting sources.

## Subtopic: Checking, Choosing and Modelling

Make the check table under the sketches: each specification and safety requirement down the side, idea A and idea B across, a tick, a cross or a question mark in each cell with a word of reason. Dome: 21 square metres, tick; headroom at the edges, question, low walls; wind, tick, strong from any side; waterproof, tick if the cover is cut to the curve, question if it is flat sheets with many seams; erection, tick, rods and a cover; transport, tick; door and vents, tick; stove place, question, the curved wall is close to the cooking spot; cost, question, bent rods cost more than straight poles. Ridge: space, tick; headroom only along the ridge, cross for a family of six; wind on the ends, question, needs guys; waterproof, tick, flat sheets and few seams; erection, tick; transport, tick; stove place, tick, more floor away from the walls; cost, tick. Box: space and headroom, tick; wind, tick if triangulated; waterproof, tick; erection, cross, more than an hour; transport, cross, heavy and long; cost, cross.

Choose the idea with the fewest crosses on the most important lines, and write the reasons as sentences. Most teams land on the dome or on a ridge tent with raised side walls, a hybrid that borrows the dome's wind strength and the ridge's flat cover; a hybrid is allowed as long as its parents are named and its own sketch is drawn. Record what the chosen idea must still solve, which becomes the first question for the model: how the dome's cover is cut from flat fabric, how the ridge's ends are guyed in a small space, where the stove goes.

Test the idea in three dimensions before committing, with the straws, string and tissue on the desk: a dome of five straws bent and tied to a ring, a ridge tent of six straws and a thread, a box of twelve, each with a tissue cover. Push each from the side and the top, blow on it, pour a teaspoon of water on the tissue roof and watch where it runs. The forms tell you in a minute what the sketch cannot: the untriangulated box folds, the ridge tent leans until it is guyed, the dome springs back. Note what you saw beside the check table, and let it change the choice if it should. Then the chosen sketch is redrawn clean, with all labels and dimensions, the camp plan showing spacing and the water and toilet positions, the erection steps and the packed form, and filed as the design product the model will be built from and marked against.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: two sketches that are the same tent in two sizes; the structures must differ. Two: a pretty shelter with no door position, no vents and no stove place drawn, so the health and safety list is unmet on paper. Three: a flat roof, which pools water and collapses in the first storm. Four: a box frame of rectangles with no diagonals, which the straw model folds in your hand.

The questions for this section are with you now: the check table and its verdicts, choosing with reasons and hybrids, the straw models and the clean redraw.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a page folded in half, a dome on the left and a tent on the right — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Shapes

Dome: bent rods tied to hoops, strong from any side, quick, cover cut to the curve. Ridge or A-frame: two A ends and a ridge pole, flat sheets, guy ropes, headroom only in the middle. Box: poles and a pitched roof, most room, heaviest, every rectangle needs a diagonal.

Fold the page. Sketch two that differ in structure, with sizes: 21 square metres, 1.8 metres headroom. Name the rods or poles and the joints.

Draw the cover on with its eyelets, overlaps and pegged hem, and the tarpaulin floor turned up at the edges.

Keep the dome and the ridge side by side in mind and try the questions for this part: the three families, the sizes and the cover.

## Subtopic: Label Everything

Door away from the wind with inside ties. High hooded vent and low vent, 0.1 square metre each. Stove place near the high vent, away from the walls, on a slab. Lamp hook. Daylight strip. Storage pocket.

Guy ropes at 45 degrees, hem pegged every half metre, rod feet 300 millimetres in, roof pitched at least 30 degrees, eaves overhanging, groundsheet turned up. Spacing on a small camp plan. Opaque cover, door that fastens inside, curtain line.

Draw the packed bundle and estimate its mass under 25 kilograms. Write six erection steps with times under an hour and a mallet. Credit each borrowed idea: hoop tent, beehive hut, crane, thatch, matjieshuis.

Hold the hooded vent at the peak in mind and try this part's questions: the features, wind and rain, packing and credit.

## Subtopic: Check, Choose, Model

Table: requirements down, ideas across, tick, cross or question with a reason. Dome: strong and quick, low at the edges, curved cover. Ridge: flat sheets, low walls, needs guys. Box: roomy, heavy, slow, must be triangulated.

Choose the fewest crosses on the lines that matter most and write why. A hybrid is fine if you name its parents and draw it.

Straws, string and tissue: build each in a minute, push it, blow on it, pour a teaspoon of water on the roof. Let what you see change the choice. Redraw the winner clean with every label, the camp plan, the steps and the bundle.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sketch two shelters that differ in structure, dome, ridge or box, with sizes, materials and joints, cover and floor drawn on. Label door, vents, stove place, lamp, storage, tie-downs, pitch, overhang and privacy, draw the packed form and the erection steps, credit the sources. Check against the lists in a table, test with straw models, choose with reasons and redraw clean.

Hold the straw dome springing back, and take the final questions of the lesson: the table, the models and the summary.
