### subtopic_1 — From Tests to a Brief
**Assistant:** Why do we need to give a mass per square metre? Surely lighter is just better.

**Tutor:** Lighter is better right up to the point where the fabric tears, leaks or wears through, and the mass figure is the way a designer marks that point so that a supplier does not go past it in either direction. Mass per square metre, grams per square metre or gsm, is the standard way textiles are specified because it captures, in one number, how much material there is: a heavier fabric has thicker threads or a thicker coating or both, which generally means stronger, more waterproof, more UV-resistant and more expensive, and a lighter one means the opposite. Your brief sets a maximum because of the transport requirement: a cover of about 30 square metres at 350 grams is 10.5 kilograms, which with a 15 kilogram frame makes a 25 kilogram bundle two people can carry; at the 650 grams of truck tarpaulin the cover alone is nearly 20 kilograms and the bundle is a two-person struggle up a muddy slope. But the brief should also set a minimum, or at least name a sensible range, because a supplier asked only for under 350 grams might quote a 120 gram nylon fly sheet that passes the drip test on day one and tears at the first eyelet in the first wind, and whose coating is gone by week three in the sun. Tent makers use roughly 200 to 300 grams for good fly sheets and 300 to 450 for heavy-duty relief tents, so a range of 280 to 350 grams for the shelter cover is defensible and you can say why: enough material to carry a ripstop weave, a flame-retardant treatment and a UV-stabilised coating, little enough to carry. The number also lets you check a delivered roll with a kitchen scale and a ruler, cut a 100 by 100 millimetre square and multiply by a hundred, which is the inspector's test. That is the general lesson about specifications: a number is not only a target, it is a boundary that stops the wrong trade-off being made by someone who was not in the room when you did the tests.

### subtopic_2 — The Full Specification
**Assistant:** Our team cannot agree whether to recommend the expensive flame-retardant fabric or the cheap tarpaulin with fire spacing. Which is right?

**Tutor:** Both can be right, and the examiner will accept either if it is justified, because this is a real trade-off that aid agencies argue about too; what will not be accepted is a choice made without showing the reasoning. Lay the two options out against the brief. Option A, flame-retardant coated fabric at roughly R80 a square metre: passes every specification, cover costs about R2 400, so a thousand shelters cost R2.4 million in fabric; a candle or a stove flare against the wall makes a char mark, not a fire; the fabric lasts the month and well beyond. Option B, polypropylene tarpaulin at roughly R30: passes water, mass and cost, fails flame and UV; cover costs about R900, so a thousand shelters cost R900 000 and the saving buys another 1 600 covers; but a flame against the wall drips burning plastic, the fabric is brittle by the end of a summer, and the whole camp's fire safety now rests on the two-metre spacing, outdoor cooking and lamps instead of candles being enforced for a month. Now bring in the scenario. For a camp after a shack fire, where the displaced have just lost everything to exactly this kind of spread and will be cooking with paraffin, most designers choose A, because the people cannot be asked to carry the risk twice and the fire rules will be hard to enforce among traumatised strangers. For a flood camp in a rural area with a short stay, space to spread out and an organised community kitchen, B with strict spacing may be the better use of money, because it shelters more families sooner. There is also a middle path worth writing down: tarpaulin roofs with a flame-retardant inner liner, or tarpaulin for the first week while the better fabric is procured. So your brief should state the recommendation, the scenario it assumes, the reason, and the alternative with its condition: "we recommend A for fire-displaced urban families because fire rules are hard to enforce; B is acceptable for short rural flood camps if spacing, outdoor cooking and lamp provision are guaranteed." That sentence shows judgement, which is the top mark in design, rather than a preference, which is not.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing, Justifying and Presenting
**Assistant:** What is a hydrostatic head, and why does the lesson say to write it when we never measured it?

**Tutor:** A hydrostatic head is the standard industry measure of how waterproof a fabric is, and you write it because the supplier and the inspector will use it even though your classroom could not, and a good brief speaks the reader's language where it can. The test clamps a piece of fabric under a column of water and raises the water pressure until drops appear on the underside; the pressure at which that happens is expressed as the height of the water column, in millimetres. A fabric that leaks at a column of 1 000 millimetres has a hydrostatic head of 1 000; one that holds 3 000 millimetres is more waterproof. Your drip and thumb tests are rough versions of the same idea: the drip test applies almost no pressure, just the weight of falling drops, so passing it means a head of a few hundred millimetres at most, and the thumb pressure test applies something like 1 000 to 2 000 millimetres of water pressure for a moment, which is why it separates the repellent fabrics from the coated ones. For a tent or shelter the relevant pressures are rain driven by wind, which is modest, water pooling on a sagging roof, which can be several hundred millimetres, and someone kneeling or lying on a wet floor, which is the highest; standards for relief tents typically ask for a head of 1 500 millimetres or more for the fly and higher for the groundsheet. So the brief writes both: our test, which the supplier can see is a sensible proxy, and the standard figure, which the supplier can prove with a test certificate from the factory. Writing the standard figure also protects you: a supplier who says a fabric is waterproof has said nothing; one who certifies 2 000 millimetres hydrostatic head has made a claim an inspector can check. The same logic applies to the flame line, where you name a recognised flame-retardancy standard as proof, and to tear strength, where industry quotes newtons. You are not pretending to have measured these; you are saying, honestly, that your classroom tests pointed to the requirement and that the product must be certified to the industry test before it is bought. That honesty is part of what makes the brief professional, and an examiner reading "we could not measure hydrostatic head and require the supplier to certify at least 1 500 millimetres" sees a designer who knows the limits of their own evidence.
