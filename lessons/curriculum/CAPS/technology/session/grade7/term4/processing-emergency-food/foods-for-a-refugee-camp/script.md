# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the investigation that precedes the emergency food design brief. The anchor is a Consumer Studies kitchen in Bloemfontein, lent to the Technology class, where a table is laid with maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, lentils, tinned pilchards, tinned tomatoes, cooking oil, peanut butter, milk powder, soya mince, dried fruit, salt, fresh cabbage, onions, potatoes, a loaf of bread and a litre of fresh milk, and each is judged by the same five questions. The governing idea is that food for a camp must be nutritious, energy dense, long keeping without refrigeration, light and compact to transport, cheap in bulk, quick to cook with little fuel and water, and acceptable to the people who will eat it, that processing such as drying, milling, tinning, fermenting and fortifying turns fresh foods into foods that meet those tests, and that the result is a short list of reliable staples, proteins, fats and vegetables. Investigating foods for a refugee camp is examinable.

## Subtopic: Five Questions for Every Food

Pick up each food on the table and ask five questions. Does it keep: for how long, at room temperature, in a tent? Is it nutritious: which food group is it, and how much energy per kilogram? Does it travel: how heavy and bulky is it for the nourishment it carries, and does it survive being dropped off a truck? Can it be cooked here: how much fuel, water and time, in a big pot, by tired people? Will people eat it: is it familiar, acceptable to their religion and culture, and suitable for babies and the old? A food that fails any one question is a poor camp food however good it tastes.

Run the fresh milk through the five. It keeps a day without a fridge, then sours and makes people ill; it is heavy, mostly water; it needs no cooking; it is nutritious and everyone drinks it; but the first answer disqualifies it. Now milk powder: keeps a year in a sealed bag, light, a tenth of the weight for the same milk, mixed with boiled water, nutritious, and accepted everywhere. Same food, processed, and now it passes. Run the loaf of bread: keeps three days, bulky, no cooking, good energy, loved by all; useful for the first days from a town bakery, not for storage. Run the maize meal: keeps months in a dry sack, light and dense, cooked in twenty minutes as pap with water and a fire, the staple of most of South Africa. It passes every question, which is why maize meal is the first thing on every South African food parcel.

Write the results in a table: food, keeps for, group, travels, cooking, acceptability, verdict. By the end of the lesson the table divides itself into three kinds of food: foods that pass and form the camp's core, foods that pass only for the first day or two, and foods that fail and must be processed or left out.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five questions, the milk and bread examples and the three kinds of food.

## Subtopic: Processing Makes Food Travel

Processing is any treatment that changes a raw food into a more useful form, and for emergency food the useful form is one that keeps and travels. Drying removes the water that bacteria and moulds need: dried beans, lentils, rice, maize kernels, dried fruit, biltong, milk powder and soup powder all keep for months or years, and they weigh a fraction of the fresh food. Milling grinds grain into meal or flour so it cooks quickly: maize meal cooks in twenty minutes where whole kernels take hours and far more fuel; samp, which is crushed maize, sits in between. Tinning seals cooked food in a can heated to kill every organism, so pilchards, beans, tomatoes and corned meat keep for years without a fridge and need no cooking, only a tin opener; the cost is weight, because the can and the water inside travel too.

Fermenting uses friendly bacteria or yeasts to preserve and flavour: amasi, sour milk, keeps longer than fresh and is easier to digest; mageu, fermented maize drink, is a nourishing energy drink that keeps a few days; bread itself is fermented dough, baked. Salting and smoking preserve fish and meat, as with dried salted snoek and biltong. Fortifying adds nutrients during processing: South African maize meal and wheat flour are fortified by law with vitamin A, iron, zinc and B vitamins, which matters enormously in a camp where vegetables are short, and a camp planner should insist on fortified meal. Finally, packaging is a kind of processing: sealed, labelled, measured bags protect food from damp, rats and dirt and make it quick to count and distribute.

Each process has a cost. Drying and milling lose some vitamins and fibre. Tinning adds weight and sometimes salt and sugar. Processing itself needs energy and factories, which is why processed emergency food comes from towns and is trucked in rather than made at the camp. A good planner mixes processed foods for keeping with fresh foods bought locally for taste and vitamins: onions, cabbage and potatoes keep a week or two in a cool shaded place, and a nearby farmer or market can supply them daily, which also puts money into the local economy rather than importing everything.

The questions for this section are with you now: drying, milling, tinning, fermenting, salting and fortifying, and the cost of each.

## Subtopic: The Camp Food List

Put the survivors of the five questions into the four groups from last lesson. Staples for energy: maize meal, fortified, first; rice; samp; wheat flour or bread for the first days; potatoes as a fresh staple that keeps. Proteins: dried beans and lentils, which are cheap, light and keep for a year but need long soaking and cooking, so they take fuel; tinned pilchards and tinned beans, which need no cooking at all; soya mince, light and quick; eggs if a local supply exists; milk powder for children; peanut butter, which is protein and fat together. Fats: sunflower oil, dense in energy and essential for cooking pap and vegetables; peanut butter; margarine, which keeps better than butter. Vegetables and fruit: tinned tomatoes, which keep for years and carry vitamin C; onions and cabbage, which keep for weeks and go into every pot; pumpkin and butternut, which keep for months whole; dried fruit; oranges in season, which are cheap in South Africa and need no cooking. Salt, sugar, tea and spices make food acceptable and lift morale, which is also nutrition.

Now test the list against the mix of people. Babies over six months: soft porridge from maize meal with milk powder, mashed butternut. Toddlers and the elderly: softer, smaller, oftener, enriched with oil and peanut butter. The sick: oral rehydration from salt, sugar and boiled water, then soft food. Pregnant and breastfeeding women: extra protein, beans, pilchards, milk powder. Muslim families: no pork, so tinned pilchards and beans rather than tinned pork. Families from other countries: rice as an alternative staple. The list does not change much; the proportions and the cooking do.

Test it against the camp kitchen too. Beans soaked overnight cook in an hour on a fire, which is a lot of fuel for one hundred people, so plan a bean day and a pilchard day rather than beans daily. Pap plus a tinned fish and tomato relish is twenty minutes and one pot per fifty people. Soup from lentils, onions, cabbage and oil is a single pot that feeds everyone soft food. These are the meals you will specify in the design brief and cook in week three, and the lesson's investigation is what makes them realistic rather than wishful.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: planning fresh milk, meat and bread for a week in a camp without a fridge. Two: choosing only staples, pap every day, so children fill up and slowly starve of protein and vitamins. Three: a bean stew every day in a camp short of firewood. Four: forgetting fortification, so a camp fed for a month on unfortified meal shows the first signs of vitamin A and iron shortage.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four-group list, adjusting for the mix and the kitchen, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the table of foods and five questions asked of each — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Ask Five Questions

For each food: Does it keep without a fridge? Is it nutritious? Does it travel light? Can it be cooked in a big pot with little fuel and water? Will these people eat it?

Fresh milk fails at the first question; milk powder passes all five. Bread is fine for two days only. Maize meal passes everything, which is why it is first in every food parcel.

Sort the table into: core foods, first-day foods, and foods that fail unless processed.

Keep the fresh milk and the milk powder side by side in mind and try the questions for this part: the five questions and the three kinds.

## Subtopic: How Processing Helps

Drying takes out the water that mould needs: beans, rice, dried fruit, milk powder. Milling makes grain cook fast: maize meal in twenty minutes. Tinning seals cooked food for years: pilchards, tomatoes, beans, no cooking needed, but heavy.

Fermenting: amasi, mageu, bread. Salting and smoking: biltong, dried fish. Fortifying: South African maize meal and flour carry added vitamin A, iron and zinc by law. Good packaging keeps out damp and rats.

Every process costs something: vitamins, weight, energy. Mix processed foods for keeping with fresh onions, cabbage and potatoes bought nearby.

Hold the sealed tin in mind and try this part's questions: the processes, fortification and the costs.

## Subtopic: The List

Staples: fortified maize meal, rice, samp, potatoes. Proteins: beans, lentils, tinned pilchards, soya mince, milk powder, peanut butter. Fats: sunflower oil, peanut butter. Vegetables: tinned tomatoes, onions, cabbage, butternut, dried fruit, oranges. Salt, sugar, tea.

Soft porridge for babies and grandmothers, enriched with oil. Rehydration drink for the sick. Pilchards not pork for Muslim families. Rice for those who do not eat pap.

Beans take fuel, so one bean day; pap and pilchard relish is twenty minutes; lentil soup is one pot for everyone.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Five questions: keeps, nourishes, travels, cooks easily, accepted. Processing by drying, milling, tinning, fermenting and fortifying makes food pass. The camp list is fortified maize meal and rice, beans and pilchards, oil, tinned tomatoes and hardy fresh vegetables, adjusted for the mix and the fuel.

Hold the camp food list, and take the final questions of the lesson: the list, the adjustments and the summary.
