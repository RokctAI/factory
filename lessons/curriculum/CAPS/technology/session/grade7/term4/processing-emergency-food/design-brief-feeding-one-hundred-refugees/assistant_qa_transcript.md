### subtopic_1 — The Brief and Its Specifications
**Assistant:** Why write the recipe for ten and then multiply, instead of writing it for one hundred straight away?

**Tutor:** Because a recipe for ten is one you can actually test, and a recipe for one hundred is one you can only hope about. Next week you will cook a portion of this meal in the school kitchen or at home, and ten portions is roughly what one normal pot and one family's worth of ingredients will make, so the ten-person recipe is the version that gets tasted, timed and corrected. If the relish is bland at ten, you add an onion and a spoon of curry powder and write it down; if the pap is too stiff for the children, you note the extra litre of water. Those corrections then scale with everything else. Writing for one hundred first means working with 12 kilograms of maize meal and 20 tins of fish on paper, numbers nobody can taste, and any error in the seasoning or the water is multiplied by ten before anyone notices it. There is a second reason, which is bulk packaging. Maize meal comes in 12.5 kilogram sacks, pilchards in trays of 12 or 24 tins, oil in 2 or 5 litre bottles, and once you have the ten-person quantities multiplied out you round each to the pack size and see what is left over: a 12.5 kilogram sack covers the 12 kilograms with half a kilogram spare for the children's porridge; two trays of 12 tins give 24 for 20 needed, with four for the breastfeeding mothers' extra portion. That rounding is a real design decision that only shows up when you scale. And a third reason, which cooks know: some things do not scale in a straight line. Water for pap does, more or less, but cooking time grows with the size of the pot, salt and spice are often less per portion in a big batch, and oil for frying onions depends on the pan's surface rather than the number of onions. So the method is: recipe for ten, test it, multiply by ten, round to pack sizes, and then on the day taste the big pot before serving and adjust. That is how every school feeding scheme and army kitchen in the country works, and it is what the examiner means by scaling a recipe.

### subtopic_2 — Planning One Nutritious, Tasty Meal
**Assistant:** Our team wants to cook a chicken curry because it is more nutritious and everyone loves it. Why is it a poor choice for the brief?

**Tutor:** It is a good meal and a poor answer to this brief, and the gap between those two things is the whole lesson. Run it through the constraints, which is what the brief is for. Storage: raw chicken must be kept below 5 degrees and cooked within a day or two, and the hall has no fridge, so chicken for one hundred people has to arrive on the morning it is cooked and be cooked at once, which depends on a delivery that a flooded road may stop; if the truck is late the chicken is unsafe by evening and you have no meal and a food-poisoning risk. Pilchards and beans sit in the corner for a month and are safe whenever the cooks are ready. Cost: chicken pieces at around R70 a kilogram, with about 150 grams needed per person, is R1 050 for the protein alone, against R400 for the pilchards, and it pushes the day well past R20 a head. Fuel and time: a curry for one hundred must be browned in batches and simmered for an hour or more until the chicken is safely cooked through, which ties up both burners and the whole cooking window; undercooked chicken in a camp is a serious risk. Safety in preparation: raw chicken on boards and hands in a kitchen with limited water is the classic route for salmonella, and the cooks are volunteers. Acceptability: good, almost everyone eats chicken, so it passes that question. Nutrition: no better than pilchards, which carry protein, fat, calcium from the soft bones and vitamin D, and it lacks the vegetables unless you add them. So chicken curry fails on storage, cost, fuel, time and preparation safety and wins only on popularity. That does not make it wrong forever: once the camp has a fridge, a reliable road and a bigger budget, a chicken day is a real morale boost and a good planner schedules one. But for one hundred people on the first days in a hall with gas burners and R20 a head, the brief points at pilchards, beans and lentils, and a team that chooses them and says why has understood what a brief is for. Write the chicken curry under improvements for later, with the condition that makes it possible: a refrigerator and a confirmed daily delivery.

### subtopic_3 — Costing, Checking and Presenting the Plan
**Assistant:** How do we know the meal gives about 700 kilocalories a plate? We cannot measure energy in a kitchen.

**Tutor:** You estimate it the way dietitians do, from standard figures for each ingredient, and a few of those figures are worth remembering because they let you check any meal plan in a minute. Dry maize meal is about 350 kilocalories per 100 grams, so the 120 grams of meal per person in your pap, 1.2 kilograms for ten, gives about 420 kilocalories; the water adds nothing. Pilchards in tomato sauce are about 150 kilocalories per 100 grams, and two 400 gram tins for ten people is 80 grams each, about 120 kilocalories. Tinned tomatoes and onion add perhaps 30. Oil is the dense one at about 900 kilocalories per 100 grams, so the two tablespoons across ten people, about 3 grams each, add 25, and this is why a little oil stirred into a child's porridge is such an easy way to add energy. Cabbage is about 25 per 100 grams, so 100 grams on the plate adds 25. Total around 620 to 650 kilocalories, a little under the 700 target, and the fix is to add a spoon of oil to the relish or a slice of bread, or to accept it and make the breakfast porridge slightly larger. The useful figures to carry in your head: cereals and pulses dry around 350 per 100 grams; tinned fish around 150; oil 900; sugar 400; most vegetables 20 to 40; bread about 250. With those you can also check the whole day: 400 grams of staple is 1 400, 60 grams of pulses is 210, 25 grams of oil is 225, 15 grams of sugar is 60, vegetables about 50, which comes to roughly 1 950 to 2 000, close to the 2 100 standard, and you can see at once that the quickest way to lift the day is the oil, not another kilogram of pap. Nobody expects you to weigh a plate in the examination, but you may be asked to explain why oil is in every emergency ration or to say roughly how much energy a plate of pap and relish provides, and this is the reasoning that answers both.
