### subtopic_1 — Five Questions for Every Food
**Assistant:** Tinned food passes every test except weight. How much does the weight really matter?

**Tutor:** More than you would think when the numbers are for a hundred people for a month, and less than you would think when the alternative is cooking fuel, so the planner has to weigh one against the other, literally. Take protein for the camp. A day's protein from dried beans is about 100 grams per person dry, 10 kilograms for the camp, in one sack. The same protein from tinned pilchards is about one 400 gram tin per three people, roughly 35 tins, which is 14 kilograms of fish and about 4 kilograms of tin and brine, so around 18 kilograms, nearly double, and in bulky, rigid cans rather than a soft sack. For a month that is a quarter of a tonne of extra load for the truck, which matters when roads are flooded and every trip is contested. But now count fuel. Beans need soaking overnight and an hour or more on the fire for a hundred people, perhaps two large bundles of wood or several kilograms of gas per day, and in many camps firewood is the scarcest thing of all, so scarce that women walk for hours and strip the countryside bare to find it. Pilchards need no cooking: open, mix with tinned tomato and onion, serve with pap that took twenty minutes. So the tin's weight is traded against the sack's fuel, and most camp planners use both, a bean day when fuel allows and a tinned-fish day when it does not, with lentils, which cook in half the time of beans, as a middle option. There is a third thing the tin gives for its weight: safety. A sealed tin cannot be contaminated by dirty hands or flood water, and in the first days when there is no clean water and no kitchen, a tin opener and a tin of beans is a safe meal that a child can eat cold. That is why the first truck into a flood zone carries tins and bread, and the sacks of maize meal and beans follow when the kitchen is running.

### subtopic_2 — Processing Makes Food Travel
**Assistant:** Why does the lesson insist on fortified maize meal? Is ordinary maize meal not the same food?

**Tutor:** It is the same grain, but not the same nutrition, and in a camp the difference shows within weeks. Maize is a good source of energy and some protein, but it is low in several nutrients the body needs every day, and the milling that makes it cook quickly removes the germ and bran where much of the remaining vitamins and minerals sit. A person who eats a varied diet with vegetables, meat, fruit and milk makes up the gap easily. A camp diet is pap twice a day with a little relish, and the gap is not made up. The first to suffer are children: vitamin A shortage weakens the immune system and in severe cases damages eyesight, iron shortage causes anaemia and slows learning, and zinc shortage stunts growth and makes diarrhoea more dangerous. South Africa learned this the hard way in the 1990s, when surveys found that a large share of children were short of vitamin A and iron, and since 2003 the law has required maize meal and wheat flour sold in the country to have vitamin A, iron, zinc, folic acid and several B vitamins added at the mill. Fortified meal looks, cooks and tastes the same as unfortified; the only visible difference is the fortification logo on the bag. For a camp planner this makes fortified meal the easiest nutritional decision of the whole plan: at no extra cost and no extra cooking, every bowl of pap carries a part of the day's vitamins, which matters most exactly when fresh vegetables are scarce. The lesson insists on it because it is the kind of detail that is invisible until it is missed, and because it illustrates a bigger idea in this term: processing is not only about keeping food, it can also be about improving it. When you write the design brief for the camp's food next lesson, specify fortified maize meal and fortified flour, and in the examination, if asked how processing can improve a food's nutritional value, fortification is the answer.

### subtopic_3 — The Camp Food List
**Assistant:** Should the camp grow its own vegetables instead of trucking them in?

**Tutor:** If the camp will last more than a few weeks, yes, and many do, but it is a solution for the second month, not the first week, and the reasons tell you a lot about emergency food planning. A vegetable garden needs land, water, seed, tools, time and someone to tend it, and it produces nothing for six to twelve weeks even for fast crops like spinach, lettuce and radish, longer for cabbage, pumpkin and beans. In the first days the camp has none of those things to spare: the land is a muddy field, water is being rationed at 20 litres a head, and every adult is busy with shelter and children. So the first month's vegetables must come from outside, and the lesson's advice is to buy them from nearby farmers and markets rather than trucking them from far away, which gets fresher food, puts money into the local economy and reduces the resentment that can grow between a camp and its neighbours. By the second month, if the camp is still there, a garden becomes one of the best investments possible. It gives fresh vitamins that tinned food lacks, it gives people work and purpose, which is a real need once survival is secured, it can use grey water from washing if the garden is laid out below the wash points, and it teaches skills that go home with the family. Camps in Africa that have lasted years have substantial gardens and even small markets. For your design brief, which is for one hundred people in the first month, the garden is a recommendation for the future, worth a sentence under improvements: after the first month, establish a vegetable garden using grey water, starting with spinach and pumpkin. For the examination, the broader point is the one to remember: emergency food planning has phases, the first days of tins and bread, the first weeks of trucked staples and local fresh food, and the longer term of gardens and local supply, and a good plan says which phase it is for.
