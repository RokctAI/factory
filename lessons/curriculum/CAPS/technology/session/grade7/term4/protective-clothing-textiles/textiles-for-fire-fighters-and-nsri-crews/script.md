# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the investigation of textiles used for protective clothing in dangerous occupations, which prepares the shelter textile work later in the term. The anchor is a classroom in Durban with a fire fighter's turnout jacket borrowed from the local station, an NSRI foul-weather jacket and life jacket, a welder's leather apron, a miner's reflective overall and a tray of fabric swatches: cotton, wool, polyester, nylon, PVC-coated nylon, aramid and a reflective tape. The governing idea is that protective clothing is designed from the hazards of the job, that textile properties such as heat and flame resistance, water resistance, insulation, buoyancy, abrasion resistance, visibility and breathability are chosen to counter those hazards, that natural fibres such as cotton and wool and synthetic fibres such as polyester, nylon and aramids each bring different properties, that weaving, knitting, coating and laminating turn fibres into fabrics with new properties, and that layering combines fabrics so the whole garment protects while remaining wearable. Investigating protective textiles is examinable.

## Subtopic: Hazards First, Then Properties

Protective clothing begins with the danger, not the fabric. List what each job faces. A fire fighter faces flame, radiant heat that cooks skin from a distance, hot water and steam, falling debris, sharp edges, and the heat of their own exertion inside a sealed suit. An NSRI sea rescue volunteer faces cold water that drains body heat twenty-five times faster than air, waves that can wash a person overboard, darkness, wind and salt spray, and the need to swim and climb. A welder faces sparks and molten metal spatter, ultraviolet light and heat. A miner faces rock falls, abrasion, dust, darkness and machinery. A traffic officer faces being unseen by drivers at night and in rain.

Each hazard asks for a property. Flame asks for a fabric that does not catch fire or melt onto skin: flame resistance. Radiant heat asks for insulation and a reflective outer. Water asks for a waterproof layer, but exertion asks for the sweat to escape, so breathability must be balanced with waterproofing. Cold water asks for insulation that works when wet and for buoyancy to keep the head up. Sharp edges and rock ask for abrasion and tear resistance. Darkness asks for high visibility: fluorescent colours by day, retroreflective tape by night. Movement asks for stretch and low weight. And every job asks that the garment be wearable for hours, so comfort, weight and fit are specifications too, not luxuries.

Write the hazards and properties as two columns for each job. Fire fighter: flame, heat, water, debris, sweat; needs flame resistance, insulation, water resistance, strength, breathability. NSRI: cold water, waves, dark, salt; needs insulation when wet, buoyancy, waterproofing, visibility, corrosion-resistant fittings. This table is the specification a textile designer works to, and it is the method you will use in week seven to specify a textile for an emergency shelter, where the hazards are rain, wind, sun and fire.

The questions for this section are with you now: the hazards of each job, the property each hazard demands and the two-column table.

## Subtopic: Fibres, Fabrics and Their Properties

A textile starts as a fibre. Natural fibres come from plants and animals: cotton from the cotton boll, soft, absorbent, cool, but weak when wet, slow to dry and quick to burn; wool from sheep, warm, insulating even when damp because its crimped fibres trap air, naturally slow to burn and self-extinguishing, but heavy when wet and slow to dry; linen and silk for other uses. Synthetic fibres are made from oil-based chemicals: polyester, strong, quick-drying, resistant to stretching and shrinking, but it melts in flame and sticks to skin; nylon, very strong and abrasion-resistant, light, quick-drying, also melts; acrylic, wool-like but flammable; and the high-performance aramids, such as the fibre sold as Nomex, which do not melt or drip, char rather than burn, and keep their strength at high temperature, and the fibre sold as Kevlar, five times stronger than steel by weight, used for cut resistance and in bulletproof vests.

Fibres become fabrics by weaving, interlacing threads at right angles to make a strong, stable, less stretchy cloth, as in canvas, denim and a fire jacket's outer shell; by knitting, looping a yarn so the fabric stretches and traps air, as in jerseys, thermal underwear and the lining of gloves; and by bonding fibres into a felt or non-woven, as in wool felt and the wadding inside a jacket. The fabric can then be finished: coated with PVC or polyurethane to make it waterproof and windproof, as in an NSRI jacket or a rain cape; laminated to a thin membrane with pores too small for water drops but large enough for water vapour, so it is waterproof yet breathable; treated with a flame-retardant chemical, as cotton for welders' overalls often is; or printed with fluorescent dye and stitched with retroreflective tape, which sends light straight back toward its source so a driver's headlights light the wearer up.

Test the swatches. Drop water on each: cotton soaks it up, polyester beads it briefly, PVC-coated nylon sheds it completely. Tear each along a cut: nylon resists, cotton tears easily, the aramid barely moves. Hold each over a candle with tongs in the teacher's demonstration: cotton flares, polyester melts into a bead that would stick to skin, wool chars slowly and goes out when removed, the aramid chars but neither burns nor melts. Shine a torch on the reflective tape from across the room and it glows back. Record each result in a table of fabric against property, because this is a practical investigation and the table is its product.

The questions for this section are with you now: natural against synthetic fibres, weaving, knitting, coating and laminating, and the swatch tests.

## Subtopic: Layering: How the Garment Is Built

No single fabric does everything, so protective garments are built in layers, each doing one job. Take the fire fighter's turnout jacket apart in your mind. The outer shell is a tightly woven aramid blend: flame resistant, does not melt, resists tearing on debris, often with a reflective trim. Behind it is a moisture barrier, a membrane laminated to a light fabric, which keeps hot water and steam from reaching the body while letting some sweat vapour out. Innermost is a thermal liner, a quilted or non-woven layer of aramid wadding that traps air and slows the heat getting through; air is one of the best insulators, which is why the liner is thick and light rather than dense. Three layers, three properties, one jacket, and the whole thing weighs several kilograms, which is why fire fighters train for fitness and why heat stress inside the suit is itself a hazard the design must manage with ventilation openings and breathable membranes.

Now the NSRI volunteer. Against the skin, a thermal base layer of polyester or merino wool that wicks sweat away and insulates even when damp. Over it, a fleece mid-layer, knitted polyester brushed to trap air. Outside, a foul-weather jacket and trousers of PVC-coated or laminated nylon, seams taped or welded so water cannot wick through the stitching, with a high collar, hood, and cuffs that seal. On top of everything a life jacket, a buoyant foam or an inflatable bladder inside a tough nylon cover, in fluorescent orange or yellow with retroreflective patches, a whistle and a light, keeping an unconscious person face up. Crews in the coldest conditions wear a drysuit, a waterproof suit sealed at the neck and wrists so the thermal layers stay dry, or a wetsuit of neoprene, a foamed rubber that traps a thin layer of water and warms it against the body.

The same layering idea runs through every protective outfit and through the shelter you will design: an outer layer against the weather, a middle layer that insulates, an inner layer that manages moisture and comfort, and fastenings and seams that do not leak. South African contexts shape the choices: the NSRI's volunteers operate from Hout Bay to Richards Bay in water that ranges from the icy Atlantic to the warm Indian Ocean, so gear is chosen by station; Working on Fire crews fighting veld fires in Mpumalanga wear lighter flame-resistant cotton-blend overalls rather than structural turnout gear, because their hazard is radiant heat and exertion in the open rather than flame in a building; and the reflective overalls on a mine or a road crew are required by law.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: choosing polyester for a fire fighter's shell because it is strong; it melts onto skin. Two: a single thick waterproof layer with no breathability, so the wearer is soaked in sweat and chilled. Three: a cotton jersey for cold-water rescue, which holds water and freezes the wearer. Four: visibility clothing in fluorescent colour but no retroreflective tape, bright by day and invisible at night.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three layers of the fire jacket, the NSRI layers and life jacket, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a fire jacket, a sea rescue jacket and a tray of fabric squares — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Start with the Danger

A fire fighter faces flame, radiant heat, steam, debris and their own sweat. An NSRI volunteer faces cold sea, waves, dark, salt and the need to swim. A welder faces sparks and spatter. A miner faces rock and dust. A traffic officer faces not being seen.

Each danger asks for a property: flame resistance, insulation, waterproofing with breathability, buoyancy, tear resistance, visibility, and always comfort for hours.

Two columns per job: hazards and properties. That table is the specification.

Keep the two columns in mind and try the questions for this part: hazards and the properties they demand.

## Subtopic: Fibres and Fabrics

Natural: cotton, soft and absorbent but weak wet and quick to burn; wool, warm even damp and slow to burn. Synthetic: polyester and nylon, strong and quick-drying but they melt; aramids, which char without melting and stay strong in heat, or are stronger than steel.

Weave for strength, knit for stretch and warmth, felt for padding. Coat with PVC to keep water out; laminate a membrane to keep water out and let sweat vapour through; treat with flame retardant; add fluorescent colour and retroreflective tape.

Test the swatches: a drop of water, a tear, a flame in the teacher's tongs, a torch across the room. Write the table.

Hold the melting polyester bead in mind and try this part's questions: fibres, fabric making, finishes and the tests.

## Subtopic: Build It in Layers

Fire jacket: aramid shell that does not burn or melt, a membrane that stops steam and lets sweat out, a thick light liner that traps air. Three layers, one jacket, several kilograms.

NSRI: wicking base layer, fleece, coated nylon outer with taped seams, a bright life jacket with reflective patches, a whistle and a light, sometimes a drysuit or neoprene wetsuit.

Every protective outfit, and your shelter later, has an outer against the weather, a middle that insulates, an inner for comfort, and seams that do not leak.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Start with the hazards of the job and list the properties they demand. Choose fibres, natural or synthetic, and fabrics, woven, knitted, coated or laminated, for those properties, and test them. Build the garment in layers so each layer does one job, and make it visible, comfortable and wearable for hours.

Hold the three-layer jacket, and take the final questions of the lesson: the layers, the NSRI kit and the summary.
