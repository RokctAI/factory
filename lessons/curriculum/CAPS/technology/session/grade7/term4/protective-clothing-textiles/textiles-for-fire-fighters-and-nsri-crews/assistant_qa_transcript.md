### subtopic_1 — Hazards First, Then Properties
**Assistant:** Why does the lesson say polyester is dangerous near fire when so much sportswear and workwear is made of it?

**Tutor:** Because polyester is an excellent fibre for almost everything except flame, and the exception is serious enough that fire services ban it even as underwear. Polyester is a thermoplastic: like the plastic it chemically is, it softens and melts when heated, at around 250 degrees, well below the temperature of a flame. When a polyester garment is exposed to fire it does not just burn; it melts into hot liquid droplets that stick to the skin and keep burning there, and the melted fabric cannot be pulled off without taking skin with it. Burns units see this pattern regularly from house fires and from paraffin stove accidents in informal settlements, where a polyester tracksuit or nylon jacket has made an injury far worse than a cotton one would have. Cotton burns too, quickly, but it burns to a dry ash that falls away, which is why welders' overalls are flame-retardant-treated cotton rather than anything synthetic, and why fire fighters wear cotton or aramid against the skin and never polyester. Wool is better still, charring slowly and going out when the flame is removed, which is why old-fashioned wool blankets are still used to smother flames. Aramids such as Nomex are the specialist answer, charring without melting and keeping their strength at several hundred degrees, which is why they form the shell of turnout gear and the overalls of racing drivers. None of this makes polyester a bad fibre; for a sports shirt, a school tracksuit or an NSRI base layer it is ideal, because it is strong, light, cheap, dries fast and wicks sweat, and the hazard there is cold and wet, not flame. The lesson's method is the answer to your question: start with the hazard, and choose the fibre whose weaknesses are not the one you face. For a shelter textile later in the term the same reasoning will apply, and you will have to decide whether a plastic sheet that melts is acceptable near a paraffin stove.

### subtopic_2 — Fibres, Fabrics and Their Properties
**Assistant:** How can a fabric be waterproof and breathable at the same time? Those sound like opposites.

**Tutor:** They sound like opposites because we picture water as one thing, but liquid water and water vapour behave very differently, and the breathable membrane is designed around that difference. A drop of rain is enormous on the scale of a fabric: millions of water molecules held together by surface tension into a sphere perhaps a millimetre across. Sweat vapour, by contrast, is single water molecules floating as gas, each unimaginably smaller than the drop. A breathable membrane is a very thin film, often a fluoropolymer or a polyurethane, with billions of pores per square centimetre, each pore far too small for a liquid drop to squeeze through, which needs the drop to deform against its own surface tension, but far larger than a single vapour molecule, which simply diffuses through from the warm, humid inside of the jacket to the cooler, drier outside. So rain stays out and sweat gets out, as long as the outside is less humid than the inside, which in cold rain it usually is, and as long as the pores are not clogged with dirt, salt or body oils, which is why such jackets are washed with care. The membrane is too fragile to be worn alone, so it is laminated to a woven nylon or polyester face fabric for strength, and the face fabric is given a water-repellent finish so drops bead and roll off instead of soaking in and blocking the pores from outside. The seams are where this fails: every needle hole is a leak, so the seams are taped on the inside with a heat-welded strip. The alternative, a PVC coating, is a solid film with no pores: completely waterproof, cheap, tough and the right answer for an NSRI deck jacket in heavy spray, but it lets no vapour out at all, so the wearer sweats into it and chills when they stop moving. That is the trade a designer makes: coated for maximum waterproofing and low cost, laminated membrane for comfort during exertion. For a shelter, where nobody is sweating inside the fabric, the cheap coated sheet is usually right, and ventilation is provided by openings instead.

### subtopic_3 — Layering: How the Garment Is Built
**Assistant:** Does the NSRI life jacket count as a textile? It is mostly foam.

**Tutor:** It is a textile product built around a buoyant core, and understanding how the two parts share the work is exactly the kind of thinking the lesson is after. The buoyancy comes from either closed-cell foam, a plastic foam whose bubbles are sealed so it cannot soak up water, or an inflatable bladder of coated nylon that fills from a gas cartridge when it hits water or when a cord is pulled. Neither would survive five minutes on a rescue boat on its own: foam tears, scuffs and crumbles under ultraviolet light, and a bare bladder would be punctured on the first cleat. The textile cover is what makes a life jacket a piece of equipment. It is a tough woven nylon or polyester, abrasion-resistant so it survives being dragged over a boat's side, coated or treated so it sheds water and does not become heavy, dyed fluorescent orange or yellow so a head in the water can be seen from a helicopter, and stitched with retroreflective tape so a searchlight finds it at night. The cover also carries the webbing straps and buckles, which are textiles too, woven polyester webbing strong enough to lift a person out of the water by, with a crotch strap so the jacket cannot ride up over the head. It carries the whistle, the light and sometimes a spray hood of clear coated fabric that stops waves breaking over the face. And the design of the cover decides whether the jacket turns an unconscious wearer face up, which is the difference between a buoyancy aid for a canoeist and a life jacket for sea rescue. So when you investigate it, treat it the way you treated the fire jacket: the hazard is cold water, waves and darkness, the properties needed are buoyancy, visibility, strength, water resistance and the right posture in the water, and the solution is a buoyant core inside a textile cover, each material doing what the other cannot. The same pairing appears in a shelter, where a frame gives the shape and a textile keeps the weather out, and in the examination you may be asked to name the properties a life jacket's cover needs: strong, abrasion-resistant, water-shedding, highly visible, with reflective tape.
