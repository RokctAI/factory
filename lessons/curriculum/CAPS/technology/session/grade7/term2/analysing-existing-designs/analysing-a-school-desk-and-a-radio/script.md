# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the analysis of existing designs through two case studies: a school desk, and an FM radio or cell phone, each examined for its features and then described in a design brief with specifications. The anchor is a Grade 7 classroom in Mitchells Plain with its rows of combined desk-and-chair units, a battery radio on the teacher's table and a learner's basic cell phone, each passed around and questioned feature by feature. The governing idea is that every product is the answer to a brief, that by listing a product's features and asking why each exists a learner can reconstruct the brief, the specifications and the constraints its designer worked to, and that this analysis is a way of learning design by studying good and bad examples. Listing features, explaining their purpose in terms of users, function, ergonomics, safety and cost, and writing a design brief with specifications for an existing product are examinable.

## Subtopic: Examining the School Desk

Start by looking rather than assuming. The desk is a combined unit: a steel tube frame, a laminated chipboard top sloping slightly towards the learner, a shelf under the top, a chipboard seat and backrest fixed to the same frame, plastic feet on the tube ends. List every feature as a fact before explaining any of it.

Now ask why each feature exists. The steel tube frame is a frame structure, light, strong and cheap to weld in a factory, and the tubes are bent rather than jointed where possible, which saves welding. The top slopes because a slightly tilted surface is easier to write on and read from, an ergonomic choice. The laminate on the top resists ink, spills and scratching and wipes clean, a durability and hygiene choice. The shelf stores books so the top stays clear. The seat and backrest are fixed to the frame so the unit cannot be separated or stolen piece by piece and so that it stands as one stable structure. The plastic feet stop the tubes scratching the floor and reduce noise when the desk is moved. The rounded corners and the absence of sharp edges are safety choices for a room full of children.

Then ask who it is for and what it costs. The user is a learner between roughly eleven and fourteen, so the seat height and top height suit that range but not a six year old or a tall adult, and a fixed seat cannot be adjusted, which is a weakness. The buyer is a provincial education department purchasing thousands at a time, so cost, stackability for transport and resistance to rough use matter more than beauty. Several learners can say at once what is wrong with the desk: the fixed distance between seat and top is uncomfortable for a tall learner, the shelf traps rubbish, the frame rusts where the paint chips. These criticisms are evaluation, and they are as valuable as praise.

Now write the brief the designer must have worked from. Schools need robust, low-cost seating and writing surfaces for Senior Phase learners. Design a combined desk and chair for learners aged eleven to fourteen that provides a sloping writing surface and book storage, is stable, safe and easy to clean, can be mass produced and transported in bulk, and will survive at least ten years of daily school use. Specifications follow: top height about 700 millimetres, seat height about 420, top size at least 600 by 450, no sharp edges, laminated top, steel frame, stackable, unit cost below a set amount. Constraints: materials available in bulk, factory welding, delivery by truck, government budget.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing the desk's features, explaining each, identifying weaknesses and writing its brief.

## Subtopic: Examining an FM Radio and a Cell Phone

Apply the same method to a small battery radio. Features: a plastic shell case, a telescopic aerial, a tuning dial with a frequency scale, a volume knob, a band switch, a speaker grille, a battery compartment with a sliding lid, a carrying strap, a headphone socket. Why each: the shell case is light, cheap to mould and protects the electronics; the telescopic aerial can be extended for a better signal and collapsed for carrying; the dial and scale let the user find a station; the band switch selects FM or AM; the grille protects the speaker while letting sound out; the battery compartment allows use where there is no electricity, which in much of South Africa is the whole point; the strap allows carrying; the headphone socket allows private listening. Users: anyone, including people with no mains power and little money, so simplicity, low cost and battery life matter most.

Now a basic cell phone. Features: a plastic or metal shell, a screen, a keypad or touchscreen, a microphone and speaker, a camera, a charging socket, a SIM card slot, a torch. Why each: the shell protects and gives grip; the screen displays information and must be readable in sunlight; the keypad or touchscreen allows input and must suit a range of hand sizes; the camera has become expected; the charging socket is standardised so chargers are shared; the SIM slot lets the user change networks; the torch answers a real need in homes without electricity. Users: almost everyone, so the designer balances cost against features, and a basic phone deliberately leaves out expensive features to reach people with little money.

Both products show design considerations in action. Function: receiving and playing a signal. Ergonomics: knobs and buttons sized for fingers, weight for a hand or a pocket. Safety: no exposed electrical parts, batteries enclosed. Aesthetics: a shape and colour people want to own. Cost: moulded plastic and standard parts. Impact: batteries and electronics become waste, and a phone's short life makes this worse than the radio's.

Write the brief for the radio: People without reliable electricity need affordable access to news, music and information. Design a portable battery-powered FM and AM radio for general use that is simple to operate, robust, light enough to carry, and runs for at least fifty hours on a set of cells, at a low retail price. Specifications and constraints follow in the same way as for the desk.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of a radio and a phone, their purposes and a brief for each.

## Subtopic: Why Analyse Existing Designs

Analysing an existing product is part of the investigate step of the design process. A designer about to make something new first studies what already exists, to learn what works, what fails and what users have come to expect. Studying the school desk teaches what makes seating robust; studying the radio teaches what makes a product usable without instructions. In the Practical Assessment Task you will investigate existing cell phone towers before designing your own, and this session gives you the method.

The method has four moves. List features as observations, without judging. Explain each feature's purpose in terms of the design considerations: users, purpose, function, cost, safety, ergonomics, aesthetics, impact. Evaluate: name the strengths and the weaknesses, with reasons. Reconstruct the brief, specifications and constraints the designer must have had. Done carefully, the method also reveals the compromises: the fixed seat saves money but hurts tall learners, the basic phone drops the camera quality to drop the price.

Analysis also builds the habit of noticing. Once you have asked why a desk top slopes, you start asking why a bottle has a waist, why a kettle's handle is where it is and why a taxi's sliding door is on the left. Every one of those is a design decision made against a brief you can reconstruct. This habit is the raw material of good design, because designers borrow and improve on what they see.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: describing what a product looks like instead of listing its features and their purposes. Two: praising a product without naming a single weakness; evaluation needs both. Three: writing a brief that describes the finished product in detail rather than the need and the requirements. Four: forgetting the buyer; a school desk is designed for the learner who uses it and for the department that pays, and the two have different priorities.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four moves of analysis, why it belongs to investigation and the habit it builds.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, starting with the desk you are sitting at — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Taking the Desk Apart With Your Eyes

Look at your desk. Steel tube frame. Chipboard top with a hard shiny surface, sloping a little towards you. A shelf underneath. A seat and a backrest fixed to the same frame. Plastic caps on the feet. List it all first, without saying whether it is good.

Now ask why. Steel tubes are light, strong and cheap to weld. The slope makes writing easier. The shiny laminate wipes clean and resists ink. The shelf keeps the top clear. Seat and desk are joined so nothing gets separated or stolen and the whole thing stands firm. Plastic feet protect the floor and cut the noise. Rounded corners keep children safe.

Who is it for? Learners of eleven to fourteen, bought by a government department in thousands. So it must be cheap, tough and stackable. What is wrong with it? A tall learner is squashed, the shelf collects rubbish, the frame rusts where the paint chips. Saying what is wrong is part of the job.

Now write the brief it came from: schools need tough, cheap seating and writing surfaces for Senior Phase learners; design a combined desk and chair that is stable, safe, easy to clean, mass-produced and lasts ten years. Then specifications: top about 700 millimetres high, seat about 420, no sharp edges, stackable, cost under a set amount.

Keep your own desk in mind and try the questions for this part: its features, their reasons and its weaknesses.

## Subtopic: A Radio and a Phone

Do the same with a small battery radio. Plastic case: light, cheap, protects the insides. Telescopic aerial: pull it out for signal, push it in to carry. Tuning dial: find the station. Volume knob. Speaker grille: lets sound out, keeps fingers off the speaker. Battery compartment: works where there is no electricity. Strap: carry it. Headphone socket: listen alone. It is for everyone, especially people with no mains power and little money, so it must be simple, cheap and run for ages on a set of batteries.

Now a basic cell phone. Shell: protects and grips. Screen: must be readable in sunlight. Keypad or touchscreen: input for all hand sizes. Camera: expected now. Standard charging socket: chargers can be shared. SIM slot: change networks. Torch: useful when the lights go out. A basic phone leaves out expensive features on purpose so poorer people can afford it.

Brief for the radio: people without reliable electricity need cheap access to news and music; design a portable battery radio, simple, tough, light, fifty hours on a set of cells, low price.

Hold the radio and the phone in mind and try this part's questions: features, purposes and the brief.

## Subtopic: Why Bother Analysing Things

Looking closely at what already exists is the investigate step. Before designing something new, a designer studies the old to learn what works and what fails. Before you design a tower, you will study real towers the same way.

Four moves. List the features without judging. Explain each one using the design considerations: users, function, cost, safety, ergonomics, looks, impact. Evaluate: strengths and weaknesses, with reasons. Write the brief the designer must have had.

Once you start, you cannot stop. Why does a bottle have a waist? Why is the kettle handle there? Why is the taxi door on the left? Every one is a design choice you can work out. That habit of noticing is where good design comes from.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Analyse an existing product by listing its features, explaining their purpose, evaluating strengths and weaknesses and writing the brief and specifications it came from. The desk shows cost, robustness and ergonomics for a department buying in bulk; the radio and phone show simplicity, battery power and affordability for users without mains power. Analysis is investigation and trains the eye.

Hold the four moves, and take the final questions of the lesson: the method and why it matters.
