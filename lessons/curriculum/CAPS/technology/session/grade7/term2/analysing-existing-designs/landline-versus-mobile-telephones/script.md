# Part 1 — Expert

This session evaluates the advantages and disadvantages of landline and mobile telephone systems. The anchor is two households in Thohoyandou: a grandmother's house with a cream-coloured landline telephone on a doily that has worked through every power cut since 1988, and her grandson's prepaid smartphone that connects through the tower on the hill but dies when the battery is flat. The governing idea is that a landline system carries speech as signals along a physical wire from a fixed handset to an exchange, while a mobile system carries it as radio waves between a portable handset and a network of towers, and that each system has strengths the other lacks, so that evaluating them means comparing cost, reliability, mobility, coverage, safety and impact rather than declaring a winner. Describing how each system works in outline and evaluating them against each other with reasons are examinable.

## Subtopic: How a Landline System Works

A landline telephone is connected by a pair of copper wires to a local telephone exchange, a building full of switching equipment that may be several kilometres away. When the grandmother lifts her handset, a small electric current flows along the wires and the exchange hears the line open. When she dials, the handset sends tones down the wire that tell the exchange which number she wants. The exchange connects her line to the line of the person she is calling, either directly or through other exchanges linked by cables, including undersea fibre optic cables for international calls.

Speech travels as an electrical signal. The microphone in the handset turns the pressure waves of her voice into a varying current, the wire carries it, and the earpiece at the other end turns it back into sound. The exchange supplies the small electric current that powers the handset, which is why a basic landline keeps working when the mains electricity fails: the exchange has its own batteries and generators. The whole system is a network of physical wires, every house needing its own pair strung on poles or buried in trenches to the exchange.

That wire is the landline's greatest strength and its greatest weakness. The connection is private, steady and does not depend on weather or on how many people are using it nearby. But a wire must be laid to every single house, which is slow and costly, and copper wire is valuable, so cable theft is common and leaves whole areas without service for weeks. A landline cannot leave the house, and in rural areas with scattered homesteads the cost of wiring each one is so high that many places never received a line at all.

South Africa's landline network, built by the national operator over most of a century, reached a peak of about five million lines in the late 1990s and then shrank steadily as mobile phones arrived. Many of those lines were in cities and towns; large parts of the former homelands were never wired.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a landline carries a call, why it works in a power cut and the cost of wiring every house.

## Subtopic: How a Mobile System Works

A mobile phone contains a small radio transmitter and receiver. When the grandson makes a call, his handset converts his voice to a digital signal and sends it as radio waves to the nearest tower, called a base station, which may be a few hundred metres away in a city or ten kilometres away in the countryside. The tower passes the call through cables or microwave dishes to the network's switching centre, which connects it to the other person, whether on a mobile, a landline or in another country. If the grandson walks or drives while talking, the network hands the call from one tower to the next without a break; this is what mobile means.

The area served by each tower is called a cell, which gives the system its name. Cells are arranged so that each tower covers a patch of ground and neighbouring towers use different radio frequencies, so that the same frequencies can be reused a few cells away. This is how a limited range of radio frequencies serves tens of millions of users. Where many people gather, cells are small and towers close together; in open country, cells are large and towers far apart.

No wire reaches the handset, so a mobile system reaches a new area the moment a tower is built and powered, and one tower serves thousands of homes. That is why mobile networks spread across Africa in two decades to places landlines had not reached in a century. The handset's weaknesses follow from the same fact: it depends on a battery that must be charged, its signal fails behind hills and in valleys and inside some buildings, calls can drop when a cell is full, and the tower itself needs electricity and is vulnerable to theft of its batteries and to load shedding.

South Africa had more than a hundred million active SIM cards by the 2020s, far more than its population, because many people use more than one network, and mobile phones carry not just voice but the internet, banking, messaging and government services for the majority of the country.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a mobile call reaches a tower and beyond, what a cell is and why mobile spread so fast.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Two Systems

Evaluate the two systems against the same headings. Cost to build: a landline requires a wire to every house, expensive and slow; a mobile tower serves thousands at once, so mobile is far cheaper per user, which is the main reason it won. Cost to use: landline calls were cheaper for long conversations and had a monthly rental; mobile prepaid lets people spend small amounts as they can afford, which suits irregular incomes. Reliability: a landline works through power cuts and does not drop calls, but is lost for weeks when copper is stolen; a mobile depends on a charged battery and a powered tower and can drop calls, but recovers quickly when the tower is restored.

Mobility and convenience: a landline is fixed to a place, so a call reaches a house, not a person; a mobile travels with its owner and can be used anywhere with signal, which transformed emergencies, business and family life. Coverage: landlines never reached scattered rural homes; mobile coverage reaches almost the whole population, though valleys and remote areas still lack signal. Services: a landline carries voice and, with equipment, slow data; a mobile carries voice, text, internet, banking, cameras and more.

Safety and health: landlines cannot be lost or stolen in the street and pose no concerns about radio waves; mobiles are stolen and lost, distract drivers, raise health worries that the evidence does not support but that people feel, and connect children to risks online. Impact on society and environment: landlines created jobs laying cable and left copper in the ground; mobiles created new kinds of work and new divides between those who can and cannot afford data, and produce vast quantities of e-waste from short-lived handsets and batteries, while towers consume power and sit in landscapes.

The evaluation does not produce a winner. For a rural clinic that must always be reachable, a landline backed up by a mobile is ideal. For a trader moving between markets, only a mobile will do. For the grandmother, the landline on the doily is a lifeline when the lights go out; for her grandson, the phone in his pocket is his bank, his school and his friends. A technology is evaluated against the needs of particular users, and both systems have earned their place. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the mobile is simply better; it is better for some needs and worse for others. Two: thinking a mobile phone works without any wires; the towers are linked by cables and dishes and need electricity. Three: forgetting that the landline works in a power cut; that is its special strength. Four: evaluating only cost; reliability, coverage, safety and impact all count.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing the two systems heading by heading and reaching a balanced judgement.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a grandmother's landline and a grandson's smartphone in the same house — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Phone on the Wire

The grandmother's landline is joined by two copper wires to a building called an exchange, a few kilometres away. Lift the handset and a little current flows, and the exchange knows you are there. Dial, and tones go down the wire to say which number. The exchange joins your wire to theirs, through more exchanges and cables, even under the sea for other countries.

Your voice becomes a wobbling electric current in the wire, and the earpiece at the other end turns it back into sound. The exchange supplies the power, and it has its own batteries, so the landline keeps working when the lights go out.

The wire is the landline's strength and its weakness. The call is private and steady, and does not care about weather or crowds. But every house needs its own wire, slow and costly to lay, and thieves steal the copper, cutting off whole areas. The phone cannot leave the house. Many rural homes never got a wire at all.

## Subtopic: The Phone in the Pocket

The grandson's phone is a little radio. It turns his voice into a digital signal and sends it as radio waves to the nearest tower, the base station. The tower passes the call along cables or dishes to the network's switching centre, which connects him to anyone. Walk or drive while talking and the call is handed from tower to tower without a break. That is what mobile means.

Each tower covers a patch of ground called a cell. Neighbouring cells use different frequencies, and the same frequencies are reused a few cells away, so a few frequencies serve millions. Towns have many small cells; the countryside has a few huge ones.

No wire reaches the phone, so one tower serves thousands of homes the day it is switched on. That is why mobiles covered Africa in twenty years when wires had not managed it in a hundred. The costs: the battery must be charged, hills and valleys block the signal, busy cells drop calls, and the tower needs electricity and gets its batteries stolen.

## Subtopic: Which Is Better? Wrong Question

Compare them heading by heading. Building cost: wire to every house versus one tower for thousands; mobile wins. Using cost: landline cheap for long calls but a monthly fee; prepaid mobile lets you spend a little at a time. Reliability: landline works in a power cut but is lost for weeks when copper is stolen; mobile needs a charged battery and a powered tower but comes back fast.

Convenience: a landline reaches a house; a mobile reaches a person, anywhere with signal. Coverage: landlines never reached scattered farms; mobile reaches almost everyone. Services: landline is voice; mobile is voice, messages, internet, banking, camera.

Safety and impact: landlines are not stolen in the street and raise no health worries; mobiles get stolen, distract drivers, worry some people and expose children to online risks. Mobiles also pile up e-waste and the towers use power. Landlines left copper in the ground and created cable-laying jobs.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A landline carries speech as electricity along a wire to an exchange; it is steady, private and works in a power cut, but needs a wire to every house and cannot move. A mobile carries speech as radio waves to a tower in a cell; it is portable, cheap to spread and carries many services, but needs a battery, signal and power at the tower. Each suits different needs, so evaluate against the user, not for a winner.

Hold the grandmother and the grandson, and take the final questions of the lesson: a balanced comparison.
