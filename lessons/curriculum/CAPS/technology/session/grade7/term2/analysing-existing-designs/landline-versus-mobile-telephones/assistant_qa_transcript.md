### subtopic_1 — How a Landline System Works
**Assistant:** Why did South Africa skip landlines and go straight to mobile, when richer countries built landlines first?

**Tutor:** Because mobile technology arrived at exactly the moment South Africa was trying to extend telephones to the majority of its people, and it offered a way to do in years what wires would have taken decades. Under apartheid the landline network served white suburbs and city centres well, but most townships and almost all of the former homelands had few or no lines. In 1994 the new government set targets for connecting millions of households, and the national operator began laying copper. At the same time, the first mobile networks were licensed, and it quickly became clear that one tower on a hill could serve a whole district for the cost of wiring a few streets. Prepaid airtime, pioneered in South Africa, removed the need for a bank account and a monthly contract, which matched the way most people managed money. Within a decade, mobile subscriptions overtook landlines, and the copper roll-out slowed and then reversed as theft and maintenance costs rose. The same pattern happened across Africa and much of Asia, and economists call it leapfrogging: a country jumps over a stage of technology that richer countries went through because a newer technology is cheaper to spread. The lesson for a designer is that the best technology for a place depends on what is already there; where wires exist, keeping them makes sense, but where they do not, building towers serves more people sooner. The cost is that leapfrogging leaves some needs unmet, such as a reliable connection during power cuts, which the old wire provided.

### subtopic_2 — How a Mobile System Works
**Assistant:** If a mobile phone is a radio, why do I need a SIM card and airtime? A radio just works.

**Tutor:** Because a broadcast radio only listens, while a phone both listens and talks, and a two-way network has to know who is speaking, where they are and whether they have paid. A radio receiver picks up a station that is transmitting to everyone at once; nobody needs to identify you because the signal flows one way. A mobile phone must transmit, which means the network has to allocate it a slice of radio frequency and time in the cell it is in, route its calls to the right place, hand it from tower to tower as it moves and connect it to the person it is calling. To do all that, the network needs to identify the handset, and the SIM card is that identity: a small chip holding your number and the secret keys that prove the phone is yours and that it belongs to a particular network. Airtime pays for the network's costs, which, as this term shows, are considerable: towers, steel, foundations, electricity, batteries, cables, switching centres, staff and the licence fees paid to government for the right to use radio frequencies, which are a scarce public resource. The exchange that serves a landline has equivalent costs, paid through line rental and call charges. So a radio is free to listen to because a broadcaster pays once to reach everyone; a phone costs because the network does individual work for every call. Understanding this also explains why a tower serves a limited number of users at once and why calls drop when a cell is full.

### subtopic_3 — Weighing the Two Systems
**Assistant:** Should we keep landlines at all, or let them disappear now that almost everyone has a mobile?

**Tutor:** Most countries are letting the old copper network shrink, but they are not letting the idea of a fixed connection disappear, and the reasons show how evaluation leads to design decisions. The copper landline's strengths were a reliable, private connection that worked without a battery and did not depend on radio conditions. Its weaknesses were the cost of a wire to every house, theft, and its inability to carry modern data. The replacement that is spreading is fibre optic cable, a glass fibre carrying light, which keeps the fixed connection's reliability and has no copper to steal, while carrying vastly more data than either copper or a mobile tower can. Fibre is being laid to homes, businesses, schools and, crucially, to the mobile towers themselves, so the two systems are becoming one network: fibre in the ground, radio for the last few hundred metres to the handset. For places where fibre is not economic, fixed wireless, a receiver on the roof pointed at a tower, gives a stable connection without a battery in your pocket. What has been lost is the exchange-powered handset that worked when all else failed, and some countries now require fibre boxes to carry a backup battery for emergency calls, which is a design response to a need the evaluation revealed. So the right answer is not to keep the copper but to keep what the copper did well, and that is exactly how a designer should read a comparison like the one in this lesson.
