### subtopic_1 — Examining the School Desk
**Assistant:** How can I write the brief for a product when I was not there and do not know what the designer was thinking?

**Tutor:** You reconstruct it from the evidence the product itself provides, in the same way a detective reconstructs events from a room, and you accept that your version is a reasoned guess rather than a copy of the original document. Every feature you list is a clue to a requirement. A laminated top tells you the brief demanded a surface that resists ink and wipes clean. A welded seat tells you theft and stability were concerns. Steel tube and chipboard tell you cost mattered more than appearance. Rounded corners tell you safety was specified. The things the product does not have are clues too: no height adjustment tells you the designer traded comfort for cost and simplicity, which points to a buyer purchasing in bulk on a tight budget. From these clues you write the brief in the normal form, need, product, user, purpose, and then the specifications as measurable requirements and the constraints as limits. Where the evidence is ambiguous, say so: the slope of the top might be for writing comfort or to let dust slide off, and a good analysis notes both possibilities. Professional designers do this all the time under the name reverse engineering, taking a competitor's product apart to learn what requirements it was built to. In an exam, markers are looking for a brief that is consistent with the features you listed and that uses the proper structure, not for mind-reading. If your brief explains every feature you observed, you have done it right.

### subtopic_2 — Examining an FM Radio and a Cell Phone
**Assistant:** Is it fair to criticise the school desk when it was designed to be cheap? Surely the designer knew about the weaknesses.

**Tutor:** It is fair and it is necessary, provided the criticism is tied to the brief rather than to wishes the brief never contained, and the designer almost certainly did know. Every design is a set of compromises, and the designer of the desk chose to accept a fixed seat height, a rust-prone painted frame and a rubbish-collecting shelf in exchange for a unit that costs a fraction of an adjustable one and can be stacked forty to a truck. Those were probably the right choices for the buyer's budget. Evaluation does not mean pretending the compromises are not there; it means naming them, explaining what was gained and lost, and asking whether a better compromise was available. For example, a galvanised rather than painted frame would resist rust for a modest extra cost, and a shelf with an open front would not trap rubbish and would use less material. Those are constructive criticisms that stay within the spirit of the brief. A criticism that the desk is not upholstered or adjustable with gas struts is unfair, because it ignores the cost constraint. The habit the curriculum is teaching is to evaluate against the requirements, and to separate a failure to meet the brief from a deliberate trade-off within it. That is also how you want your own cardboard tower to be judged: against what you promised, not against what a steel one could do.

### subtopic_3 — Why Analyse Existing Designs
**Assistant:** Why do a radio and a phone share so many features if one is forty years old and the other is new?

**Tutor:** Because both answer the same underlying need, receiving a signal and turning it into something a person can hear or see, and because successful features from one generation of technology get carried into the next. A battery compartment, a speaker, a volume control and a case that protects the electronics are common to both because the user's situation is the same: a person who wants information and entertainment, often away from mains power, holding a device in one hand. The phone added a transmitter, so it needed a microphone and a way to enter numbers, and once screens became cheap it added a display and then a camera. But look at what persisted. The telescopic aerial of the radio became an internal antenna in the phone, hidden but doing the same job. The tuning dial became a contact list. The headphone socket survived almost unchanged for decades until wireless earphones began to replace it. Designers call this path dependence: each new product inherits expectations from the one before, so users do not have to learn everything again. This is also why analysing an old product is useful for designing a new one; the needs persist even as the technology changes. When you look at a cell phone tower, you will find the same continuity: it inherits the triangulated frame of the pylon and the windmill, because the need to hold something high against the wind did not change when the thing being held became an antenna.
