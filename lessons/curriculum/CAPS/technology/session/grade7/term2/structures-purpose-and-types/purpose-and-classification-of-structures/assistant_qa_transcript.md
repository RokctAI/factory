### subtopic_1 — What a Structure Is
**Assistant:** Is a road a structure? It does not stand up or hold anything in the air.

**Tutor:** A road is a structure, and a surprisingly complicated one, which shows that holding something in the air is not the test. The test is whether something is arranged to keep its shape and carry a load, and a road carries enormous loads: thousands of vehicles a day, each pressing down with many tonnes through four small patches of tyre. If you simply poured tar onto soil, the first truck would press ruts into it and the first rain would wash it away. So a road is built in layers, like a flat sandwich lying on the ground: compacted soil at the bottom, then a thick layer of crushed stone that spreads each wheel load over a wide area, then a finer base layer, then the asphalt surface that resists wear and sheds water. Each layer supports the one above and protects the one below, so the road supports and protects in exactly the sense we have used. Where the road crosses a river it becomes a bridge and spans; where it runs along a hillside it needs retaining walls that contain the soil. Engineers describe pavements as structures and design them with the same calculations of load and strength that they use for buildings. The same is true of a sports field, a runway or even a well-made gravel path. Structures do not have to be tall; they have to work.

### subtopic_2 — Four Purposes: Contain, Protect, Support, Span
**Assistant:** Why does the curriculum bother classifying structures as natural or man-made? It seems obvious which is which.

**Tutor:** It is usually obvious, and the point of the classification is not the sorting itself but what it makes you notice. When you deliberately look at natural structures as structures, you start asking engineering questions about them: why is a bone hollow, why does a tree trunk get wider at the base, why does a spider web survive a storm that breaks a washing line. The answers turn out to be the same answers engineers use for masts, pylons and bridges, which is why the curriculum returns to natural examples throughout the term when it discusses triangulation, tubing and stability. Classifying also makes you think about materials and processes. A natural structure grows, so it adapts its shape to the loads it actually experiences, with more material where stress is high and less where it is low, and it uses local, renewable materials. A man-made structure is designed before it exists, so the designer must predict the loads and often adds extra material for safety. Comparing the two is where the idea of appropriate and environmentally friendly design comes from, which matters a great deal in later topics such as indigenous building methods and emergency shelters. Finally, there is a small trap the classification exposes: people confuse the material with the maker, and call a wooden chair natural. Getting that straight, who made it rather than what it is made of, is a habit of precise thinking the whole subject depends on.

### subtopic_3 — Natural and Man-Made Structures
**Assistant:** Which of the four purposes is a cell phone tower really for? It seems to just stand there.

**Tutor:** Its main purpose is to support, and understanding that explains almost everything about its shape. A cell phone antenna works by line of sight; radio signals from a handset travel more or less straight and are blocked by hills, buildings and trees, so the antenna must be high enough to see over obstacles and reach handsets kilometres away. The whole tower exists to hold a few antennas, weighing perhaps as much as a motorbike, at a height of twenty to fifty metres and to keep them steady, because a swaying antenna points its beam in the wrong direction and drops calls. So the load the tower supports is small, but it is high up and exposed to wind, and the real structural challenge is not the weight of the antennas at all but the sideways push of wind on the antennas and on the tower itself. That is why towers are tall thin frames of triangulated steel, wide at the base and narrow at the top, often held by guy wires: they are shaped to support a small load at a great height against a large sideways force. A tower also protects, in a modest way, by carrying cables inside its legs and keeping equipment cabinets off the ground, and a tower disguised as a tree or a minaret is partly a protecting structure for the view. But if you remember one word for a cell phone tower, it is support, with wind as the load that matters.
