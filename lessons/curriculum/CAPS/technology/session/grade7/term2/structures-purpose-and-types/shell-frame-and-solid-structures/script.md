# Part 1 — Expert

This session classifies structures by their construction into shell, frame and solid structures. The anchor is three objects on a classroom desk in Durban, a raw egg, a bent wire coat hanger and a face brick, and three large structures in the news that match them: the curved roof of the Moses Mabhida Stadium, the steel lattice of an Eskom pylon and the massive concrete wall of the Gariep Dam. The governing idea is that a shell gets its strength from a thin, curved, continuous surface, a frame from a network of slender members joined at nodes, and a solid from mass alone, and that each type suits different loads, materials and purposes. Identifying the type of a structure, explaining where its strength comes from and giving reasons for choosing a type are examinable, and the cell phone tower studied this term is a frame structure, so this classification sets up everything that follows.

## Subtopic: Shell Structures

A shell structure is a thin, usually curved, continuous skin that carries its load across its whole surface. The egg is the classic example: its shell is less than half a millimetre thick, yet a hen can sit on a dozen without breaking one, because the curved surface spreads any push over the whole shell instead of letting it concentrate at one point. Squeeze an egg evenly between your palms along its length and it is remarkably hard to break; tap it on the edge of a bowl and it cracks at once, because the sharp edge concentrates the force on a tiny area.

Man-made shells follow the same rule. A car body is pressed from thin steel sheet into curved panels that are far stiffer than flat sheet would be. A plastic bottle, a bucket, a motorcycle helmet, a canoe and an aeroplane fuselage are shells. Large shells include domes and curved roofs: the Moses Mabhida Stadium in Durban has a roof of fabric stretched in a curve beneath a great arch, and the dome of a mosque or the vault of a church spreads the roof load down to the walls through its curve.

Shells are light for their strength and use little material, which makes them cheap and quick to make from sheet or by moulding. Their weakness is point loads and holes: a dent in a car panel or a crack in an egg spreads quickly, because the skin relies on being continuous. Shells also work best when the load presses evenly on the outside, as with a dome or a submarine hull, and are weaker when pulled or loaded from inside unless shaped for it.

Natural shells are everywhere: eggs, nuts, seed pods, tortoise shells, snail shells, the skull. Each is a thin curved skin protecting something inside, and most are made of material laid down a little at a time, which is nature's version of moulding.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a shell, where its strength comes from, its strengths and weaknesses and examples.

## Subtopic: Frame Structures

A frame structure is a skeleton of members, bars, rods, tubes or beams, joined together at points called nodes or joints, with the spaces between the members left open or filled with a covering that does not carry the main load. The wire coat hanger is a frame: a triangle of thin wire that holds a jacket's weight through its shape. Bend one of the joints and the frame collapses, because frames depend on their geometry.

Man-made frames include the roof trusses of a house, the steel skeleton of a tall building before its glass goes on, a bicycle frame, scaffolding, a tent frame, a chair and the Eskom pylon striding across the veld. The cell phone tower is a frame: three or four steel legs joined by horizontal and diagonal members, wide at the base and narrowing towards the top, carrying antennas at the summit. Frames are the structures that make tall, light, open shapes possible, and they are usually built up from standard pieces bolted or welded together on site.

The strength of a frame comes from the arrangement of its members, above all from triangles. A rectangle of four members pinned at the corners folds into a parallelogram under a sideways push; add one diagonal, splitting it into two triangles, and it becomes rigid, because a triangle's shape cannot change without changing the length of a side. This is triangulation, which we investigate practically later in the term. Members in a frame are either in tension, being stretched, or in compression, being squashed, and a good design uses thin ties where members are only stretched and thicker struts where they are squashed and might buckle.

Natural frames include the skeleton of an animal, with bones as members and joints as nodes, the branching of a tree, and the stems and veins of a leaf. The skeleton leaves most of the body's volume for soft organs, exactly as a building frame leaves space for rooms.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a frame, nodes and members, the role of triangles and tension versus compression.

## Subtopic: Solid Structures and Choosing a Type

A solid structure, sometimes called a mass structure, gets its strength from the amount and weight of material in it. The brick is solid: it has no skin and no skeleton, it is simply thick clay that resists crushing. Solid structures include dam walls, retaining walls, brick and stone walls, the pyramids of Egypt, a concrete bridge pier, a stone cathedral and a sandcastle. The Gariep Dam on the Orange River holds back South Africa's largest reservoir with a curved concrete wall nearly ninety metres high and thick enough that its sheer weight stops the water pushing it over.

Solids are strong in compression, resist being pushed over and shrug off knocks and weather, which is why walls and foundations are solid. They are heavy, use a great deal of material and are poor at spanning, because a long solid beam is so heavy that it breaks under its own weight; this is why a brick wall needs a steel or concrete lintel over a window rather than more bricks. Natural solids include mountains, boulders, cliffs and coral reefs.

Many real structures combine types. A house has solid foundations and walls, a frame roof and a shell water tank. A car has a shell body on a frame chassis. A cell phone tower is a frame on a solid concrete base, with shell antenna covers. Designers choose a type for each part by asking what loads it carries and what matters most: lightness and enclosure suggest a shell, height and openness suggest a frame, resisting crushing and overturning suggests a solid.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling anything hollow a shell; a hollow frame is still a frame, and a shell is a continuous skin. Two: thinking a frame is weak because it is mostly air; its triangles make it rigid and its openness saves weight. Three: thinking solid means strong in every way; a solid is poor at spanning. Four: forcing a structure into one type; a house is several types working together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a solid, its strengths and weaknesses, combined structures and choosing a type.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an egg, a coat hanger and a brick — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Shells: Thin Skin, Curved Shape

Pick up the egg. The shell is thinner than a fingernail, yet squeeze it lengthways between your palms and it hardly gives, because the curve spreads your push over the whole shell. Tap it on the edge of a bowl and it cracks, because all the force lands on one tiny spot.

That is a shell structure: a thin skin that is strong because of its curved shape. Car bodies, plastic bottles, helmets, canoes, domes and the curved roof of the Moses Mabhida Stadium are all shells.

Shells are light and cheap and keep weather out. Their weak point is a dent or a hole, because the skin only works while it is whole. Nature's shells: eggs, nuts, snail shells, tortoise shells, your skull.

Keep the egg in mind and try the questions for this part: what a shell is and why its curve matters.

## Subtopic: Frames: A Skeleton of Bars

Pick up the wire coat hanger. It is a triangle of thin wire, mostly empty space, yet it holds a heavy coat. That is a frame: bars joined at corners, with the gaps left open. Bend a corner and it collapses, because a frame is only as good as its shape.

Roof trusses, bicycle frames, scaffolding, pylons and cell phone towers are frames. So is your skeleton: bones are the bars, joints are the corners, and the open spaces are filled with you.

Frames are strong because of triangles. A square of bars folds sideways into a diamond. Add one diagonal bar and it cannot move. Some bars in a frame are pulled, which is tension; some are squashed, which is compression. Pulled bars can be thin; squashed bars must be thicker so they do not buckle.

Hold the coat hanger in mind and try this part's questions: what a frame is, why triangles matter and pulled versus squashed bars.

## Subtopic: Solids: Strong Because They Are Thick

Pick up the brick. No skin, no skeleton, just thick heavy clay. That is a solid structure: strong because of the amount of material. Walls, dam walls, the pyramids and the Gariep Dam are solids. So are mountains and boulders.

Solids are great at being crushed and at not being pushed over. They are bad at reaching across gaps, because a long solid beam breaks under its own weight. That is why a brick wall needs a steel or concrete bar over a window.

Most real things mix the types. A house: solid walls, frame roof, shell water tank. A cell phone tower: a frame standing on a solid concrete base. Choose the type by the job. Light and enclosed, shell. Tall and open, frame. Heavy and unmovable, solid.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Shell: thin curved skin, strength from shape, light, weak at holes. Frame: bars joined at nodes, strength from triangles, tall and open. Solid: thick mass, strength from material, resists crushing, poor at spanning. Most structures combine them.

Hold the brick, and take the final questions of the lesson: solids, mixed structures and choosing a type.
