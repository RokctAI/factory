### subtopic_1 — Shell Structures
**Assistant:** If frames are mostly empty space, why are they used for the tallest structures instead of solid ones, which seem stronger?

**Tutor:** Because for a tall structure the enemy is its own weight, and a frame carries a given load with a fraction of the material a solid would need. Imagine building a fifty metre tower as a solid brick column. Every brick near the bottom must carry the weight of all the bricks above it, so the column must get enormously wide at the base, and most of that material is doing nothing but holding up more material. A frame puts steel only along the lines where force actually flows, down the legs and across the diagonals, and leaves air everywhere else. The members are triangulated so the shape cannot distort, and the whole thing weighs perhaps one twentieth of the solid column while reaching the same height. That saved weight also means a smaller foundation and cheaper transport, since the frame arrives as a pile of standard pieces on a truck rather than as thousands of tonnes of masonry. Wind adds another reason: a solid tower presents a large flat face for the wind to push on, whereas an open lattice lets most of the wind through. The Eiffel Tower, built in 1889 as a frame of wrought iron, weighs less than the air in a cylinder of the same height and base, and the engineers who build cell phone towers use exactly the same thinking. Solids win where you need mass to resist crushing or overturning, as in a dam or a foundation; frames win wherever you need height and openness for the least material.

### subtopic_2 — Frame Structures
**Assistant:** Why is a dam wall curved if it is a solid structure and gets its strength from weight?

**Tutor:** Because a clever shape lets a solid do its job with far less material, and the curve borrows an idea from shells and arches. There are two main kinds of concrete dam. A gravity dam is the pure solid: a straight, enormously thick wall whose weight alone keeps the water from pushing it over or sliding it along the riverbed. An arch dam, like the Gariep Dam, curves upstream into the reservoir, so that the water pressure pushing on the curved face is carried sideways along the arch into the rock walls of the gorge on either side, much as the weight on a stone arch bridge is carried to its abutments. The rock does part of the work that mass would otherwise have to do, so the wall can be thinner, which saves millions of tonnes of concrete. Gariep is actually a combination, called an arch-gravity dam, thick enough to rely partly on its weight and curved enough to rely partly on the gorge. This shows that the three types are tools of thought rather than rigid boxes: the dam is classified as a solid because mass is where most of its strength comes from, but its designers used the geometry of a shell to make that mass go further. When you look at any large structure, it is worth asking not just what type it is but which other types it quietly borrows from.

### subtopic_3 — Solid Structures and Choosing a Type
**Assistant:** Is a cardboard box a shell, a frame or a solid? It has thin sides like a shell but it is not curved.

**Tutor:** A cardboard box is usually classed as a shell structure, because its strength comes from thin continuous panels rather than from a skeleton of members or from mass, and shells do not have to be curved, although curves make them stronger. A flat panel of corrugated card is itself a small structure: two flat skins held apart by a wavy core, which makes it far stiffer than a single flat sheet, in the same way a folded or ribbed panel is stiffer than a plain one. Fold six such panels into a closed box and the edges and corners act like stiffening ribs, so the box holds a surprising load when it is closed and taped. Open the lid and leave it flapping, and the box twists easily, which shows that, like an egg, it relies on being a complete closed skin. Many flat-sided shells behave this way: a steel filing cabinet, a shipping container, a cereal packet and the body of a bus. Engineers sometimes call them folded-plate or box structures to distinguish them from curved shells, but in the Grade 7 classification they sit with shells because the skin, not a frame or mass, carries the load. If you wanted to make the box a frame, you would build a skeleton of card strips and glue a loose cover over it; if you filled it solid with sand it would become a solid. The box is a good test case because it forces you to ask the right question, which is not what it looks like but where its strength comes from.
