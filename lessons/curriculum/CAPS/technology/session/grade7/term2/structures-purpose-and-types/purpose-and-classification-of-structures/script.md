# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces structures: what they are, the four purposes they serve and the division between natural and man-made structures. The anchor is a single street in Orlando, Soweto, viewed from a corner: a brick house, a plastic water tank on a stand, a footbridge over a stormwater canal, a jacaranda tree, a weaver bird's nest hanging from it, a spider web on the fence and a cell phone mast on the hill behind. The governing idea is that a structure is an arrangement of parts that holds a shape and carries loads, that every structure exists to contain, protect, support or span, often more than one at once, and that nature builds structures as skilfully as people do. Defining a structure, naming its purposes with examples and classifying structures as natural or man-made are examinable, and the whole term's work on cell phone towers rests on this foundation.

## Subtopic: What a Structure Is

A structure is something made of parts arranged and joined so that it holds its shape and carries a load without collapsing. The load may be its own weight, the things it carries, the people inside it or the forces of wind, water and earthquakes pressing on it. A heap of bricks is not a structure, because it has no arrangement; a brick wall is, because the bricks are placed and bonded so that each one helps hold the others up.

Every object around you that stands up and does a job is a structure. The house on the corner is a structure of walls, roof trusses and foundations. The water tank is a structure, and so is the steel stand under it. The footbridge is a structure of beams and a deck. The jacaranda is a structure of trunk, branches and roots. The weaver's nest is a structure of woven grass. Your own body is a structure, with a skeleton of bones that holds you upright and lets you carry a schoolbag.

Structures are not always big. A chair, a paper cup, an egg shell, a bicycle frame and a drinking straw are all structures. What makes something a structure is not size but the fact that it has a shape it keeps and a load it bears. The science of making structures that stand up safely and economically is called structural engineering, and it begins with asking two questions of any object: what loads does it carry, and how do its parts share them?

Loads on a structure are forces. Forces push or pull, and they are measured in newtons. A static load does not change, such as the weight of the roof on the walls. A dynamic load changes, such as a crowd walking over the footbridge or a gust of wind against the mast. Structures must cope with both, and wind in particular is the load that shapes tall thin structures like masts and towers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a structure, why a heap is not one and the two kinds of load.

## Subtopic: Four Purposes: Contain, Protect, Support, Span

Structures exist to do one or more of four things. To contain means to hold something in: the water tank contains water, a dam wall contains a river, a bottle contains cooldrink, a mealie bag contains grain. Containing structures must resist the outward push of what is inside, which is why a dam wall is thick at the bottom where the water pressure is greatest.

To protect means to keep something safe from harm, usually from weather, impact or attack. The house protects its family from rain, cold and intruders. A bicycle helmet protects a head. A tortoise shell protects the animal inside. A cell phone case protects the phone. Protecting structures must take blows or weather on the outside without passing them to what is inside.

To support means to hold something up against gravity. The tank stand supports the tank. The chair supports you. A tree trunk supports its branches and leaves. A cell phone mast supports antennas thirty metres in the air so that their signals reach far. Supporting structures must carry weight down to the ground without buckling or tipping.

To span means to bridge a gap. The footbridge spans the stormwater canal. A roof beam spans the room below. A spider web spans the gap between fence posts. A branch spans the air from trunk to leaf. Spanning structures must carry loads across empty space, which makes them bend, and resisting bending is their main task. Many structures do several of these at once: the house protects and contains, and its roof spans and supports. Naming the purposes of a structure is the first step in understanding why it is shaped as it is.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four purposes, an example of each and a structure that does more than one.

## Subtopic: Natural and Man-Made Structures

Structures are classified first by who made them. Natural structures are made by nature without human help: trees, rock arches, honeycombs, bird nests, spider webs, shells, bones, termite mounds and coral reefs. Man-made structures are designed and built by people: houses, bridges, towers, dams, cars, furniture, packaging and clothing. The word man-made is the one the curriculum uses; manufactured or built structures mean the same.

Natural structures are worth studying because they have been tested by millions of years of wind, weather and gravity, and designers borrow from them constantly. The hollow stem of a reed is stiff and light, and so are the steel tubes of a cell phone mast. The honeycomb's hexagonal cells use the least wax to hold the most honey, and the same pattern stiffens aircraft panels and cardboard. The dome of an egg shell spreads a load over its whole surface, and so do the domes of stadiums. The branching of a tree carries the weight of the canopy down to the ground, and the branching legs of an electricity pylon do the same job. A termite mound in the Limpopo bushveld keeps cool through a system of air channels that architects in Harare copied for a shopping centre.

Man-made structures are classified further in the next session, by the way they are built: shell, frame or solid. For now, practise sorting. Walk down the Soweto street again: house, tank, stand, bridge, mast and fence are man-made; tree, nest, web and the ant hill by the gate are natural. Then ask of each what it contains, protects, supports or spans, and you have described the structural life of a whole street.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only big things are structures; a paper cup is a structure. Two: thinking a structure has only one purpose; most have several. Three: saying natural structures are not designed; they are shaped by nature to carry loads, and engineers copy them. Four: calling something made of natural material man-made or natural by its material; a wooden chair is man-made, a tree is natural.

The questions for this section are with you now: natural versus man-made, examples nature teaches designers and sorting a street of structures.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, standing on a street corner in Soweto and looking around — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Everything That Stands Up and Carries Something

A structure is anything made of parts arranged so that it keeps its shape and carries a load. A pile of bricks is not a structure. A brick wall is, because every brick helps hold up the others.

Look along the street. The house. The water tank and the stand under it. The footbridge over the canal. The tree. The bird's nest in it. The spider web on the fence. The cell phone mast on the hill. All structures. Your own skeleton is a structure too.

Size does not matter. A paper cup, an egg shell and a drinking straw are structures. What matters is a shape it keeps and a load it carries.

Loads are forces, pushes and pulls. Some stay still, like a roof on walls. Some move and change, like people on a bridge or wind on a mast.

Keep the street corner in mind and try the questions for this part: what makes something a structure and what a load is.

## Subtopic: Four Jobs

Every structure does at least one of four jobs.

Contain: hold something in. The tank holds water. A dam holds a river. A bag holds mealies.

Protect: keep something safe. The house keeps the family dry and warm. A helmet keeps a head safe. A tortoise shell keeps the tortoise safe.

Support: hold something up. The stand holds up the tank. The chair holds you up. The mast holds antennas high in the air.

Span: reach across a gap. The footbridge crosses the canal. A roof beam crosses the room. A spider web crosses the gap in the fence.

Many structures do more than one. The house protects and contains, and its roof spans and supports.

Hold the four words in mind and try this part's questions: contain, protect, support, span, with an example of each.

## Subtopic: Made by Nature or Made by People

Sort structures by who made them. Natural: trees, nests, webs, shells, bones, honeycombs, termite mounds, rock arches. Man-made: houses, bridges, towers, dams, chairs, cups, cars.

Nature is a brilliant builder, and people copy it. A reed stem is hollow and stiff, like the tubes of a mast. A honeycomb's hexagons hold the most with the least wax, and the same pattern stiffens cardboard and aircraft panels. An egg shell dome spreads the load like a stadium roof. A termite mound stays cool with air channels, and a shopping centre in Harare was built the same way.

Careful: it is who made it, not what it is made of. A wooden chair is man-made. A tree is natural.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A structure is parts arranged to keep a shape and carry a load. Structures contain, protect, support or span, often several at once. Natural structures are made by nature, man-made structures by people, and designers borrow nature's ideas.

Hold the two groups, and take the final questions of the lesson: sorting structures and what nature teaches.
