# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the ideas of stability, strength and rigidity, and investigates practically two ways of stiffening a structural material: forming it into a tube and folding it. The anchor is a classroom desk in Soshanguve with a stack of A4 paper, sticky tape, a ruler, a cup of coins for loads and a pile of textbooks, with the scenario that a cell phone tower must be built for stability, strength and rigidity using as little steel as possible. The governing idea is that a thin material bends easily because its material lies close to the line about which it bends, and that shaping the same material into a tube or a folded section moves material away from that line so that bending is resisted many times more strongly without adding mass. Defining stability, strength and rigidity, carrying out the tubing and folding investigations, recording results and explaining them are examinable, and the results feed directly into the materials chosen for the model tower.

## Subtopic: Stability, Strength and Rigidity

The scenario sets three requirements for a tower, and they are three different things. Stability is the ability of a structure to stay upright and keep its position without toppling or sliding; it depends on the base, the centre of gravity and anchoring, as the last case study showed. Strength is the ability of a material or structure to carry a load without breaking; a thin steel wire is strong, because it takes a large pull before it snaps. Rigidity, also called stiffness, is the ability to carry a load without bending or changing shape; the same steel wire is not rigid, because it bends under its own weight. A cell phone tower must be all three: it must not fall over, it must not break, and it must not sway so much that the antennas lose their aim.

Strength and stiffness are often confused, so hold two objects in mind. A thin plastic ruler is strong enough that you cannot break it by pressing on its ends, but it bends easily, so it is strong but not rigid. A dry biscuit is rigid, holding its shape under a small load, but snaps at once, so it is rigid but not strong. A steel beam is both. Structural materials are chosen and shaped to give the right combination for the job.

Three ways to make a structure stiffer are available to a designer: use a thicker or stiffer material, which adds weight and cost; add more members, as triangulation does in frames; or change the shape of the material so that the same amount resists bending better. The third is the cleverest, because it gives stiffness for free, and it is the subject of this session. The two shapes investigated are the tube and the fold.

Why does shape matter? When a flat sheet bends, the material on the outside of the bend is stretched and the material on the inside is squashed, while the middle is hardly affected. In a thin sheet all the material is close to the middle, so little of it is doing any work to resist the bend. Move material away from the middle, into the walls of a tube or the ridges of a fold, and much more of it is stretched or squashed when bending is attempted, so the section fights back far harder. Thickness in the direction of bending is what counts, and shaping creates thickness without adding material.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining stability, strength and rigidity, telling strong from stiff and why shape resists bending.

## Subtopic: Investigation: Stiffening by Tubing

Test one: the flat sheet. Lay a sheet of A4 paper across a gap between two textbooks about 15 centimetres apart. It sags under its own weight before any load is added. Place a single coin in the middle and the sheet collapses into the gap. Record: load carried, zero or one coin; deflection, total.

Test two: the tube. Roll an identical sheet into a tube about 3 centimetres in diameter along its length, tape the seam, and lay it across the same gap. Add coins to a small paper cup resting on the middle of the tube, one at a time, and record how many it holds before it buckles or sags badly. A well-rolled A4 tube typically carries dozens of coins, and stood on end it can support a textbook. Record the load and watch where it fails: a tube fails by the wall buckling inward at the point of greatest bending, not by tearing.

Test three: tube diameter. Roll two more sheets into tubes of different diameters, one tight and thin, about 1 centimetre, and one wide, about 6 centimetres, and repeat. The wider tube carries more across the gap, because its material is further from the centre, but its thin wall buckles more easily under a point load, so there is an optimum. Compare and record.

Explain the results. In the flat sheet all the paper lies in one thin layer, so bending it stretches and squashes almost nothing. In the tube the same paper is spread around a circle, so to bend the tube the top of the wall must shorten and the bottom must stretch, and paper resists both. The paper has not become stronger; its shape has made it rigid. This is why bicycle frames, scaffolding poles, tent poles, bones and the legs of many cell phone masts are tubes: a tube gives nearly the stiffness of a solid rod of the same diameter at a fraction of the weight. A drinking straw illustrates the same thing, and a solid rod of the same material and weight would be much thinner and far floppier.

The questions for this section are with you now: the tubing investigation, how the tube fails, the effect of diameter and the explanation.

## Subtopic: Investigation: Stiffening by Folding

Test four: the folded sheet. Take another A4 sheet and fold it along its length into a concertina of zigzag pleats about 2 centimetres wide, pressing each fold sharply. Lay it across the same gap and load it with coins. The folded sheet carries far more than the flat one, often as much as the tube, and it keeps carrying until a fold flattens or the paper creases at a support. Record the load and the failure.

Test five: fold size and direction. Fold a sheet into wide pleats of 5 centimetres and another into narrow pleats of 1 centimetre, and test both. Then lay a folded sheet across the gap with the folds running the other way, parallel to the supports, and watch it collapse at once. Record all three. Narrow sharp folds are stiffer than wide shallow ones, and the folds must run in the direction of the span to do any good.

Explain the results. A fold moves paper up and down out of the flat plane, so a pleated sheet is many times thicker than a flat one in the direction of bending, and bending it means stretching and squashing material at the tops and bottoms of the pleats. Folds parallel to the supports add no thickness in the direction that matters, which is why direction counts. The same principle gives us corrugated iron roofing, which spans between purlins because of its ridges and would sag flat without them; the fluted core of corrugated cardboard; the ribs pressed into the floor pan of a car and into the lids of biscuit tins; the pleats of a paper fan; and the folded-plate concrete roofs of some churches and factories. Nature uses it too, in the ribbed leaf of a palm and the pleated shell of a cockle.

For the tower model the lessons are direct: card legs rolled into tubes or folded into channels are far stiffer than flat strips, a folded base resists sagging, and all folds must run along the length of the member they stiffen. Record your results in a table with columns for the shape, the load carried, the way it failed and the explanation, ready for the design stage. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the tube is stronger than the sheet; the material is the same, the tube is stiffer. Two: folding across the span and concluding that folding does not work. Three: assuming a wider tube is always better; the thin wall buckles. Four: confusing stability with rigidity; a stable tower can still bend.

The questions for this section are with you now: the folding investigation, fold size and direction, the explanation and examples in the world.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a stack of A4 paper, some tape and a cup of coins — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Words That Are Not the Same

A tower must be stable, strong and rigid, and those are three different things.

Stable means it does not fall over. That is about the base and the centre of gravity. Strong means it does not break. A thin wire is strong; it takes a big pull to snap it. Rigid, or stiff, means it does not bend or change shape. That same wire is not rigid; it droops under its own weight.

A plastic ruler is strong but not rigid: you cannot break it by pushing on the ends, but it bends easily. A biscuit is rigid but not strong: it holds its shape, then snaps. Steel is both.

To make something stiffer you can use thicker material, which costs weight and money, add more bars, like triangles in a frame, or change the shape. Changing the shape is free stiffness, and that is today. When a flat sheet bends, only the outside is stretched and the inside squashed; the middle does nothing. Move the material away from the middle, into a tube wall or a fold, and much more of it has to stretch and squash, so it fights the bend far harder.

Keep the ruler and the biscuit in mind and try the questions for this part: stable, strong and rigid.

## Subtopic: Roll It Into a Tube

Lay a sheet of A4 across a gap between two books. It sags on its own. Put one coin on it and it falls through.

Roll an identical sheet into a tube about 3 centimetres across, tape it, and lay it across the same gap. Put a paper cup on it and add coins one by one. It holds dozens. Stand it on end and it holds a textbook. Watch how it fails: the wall caves inward at the middle, it does not tear.

Try a tight thin tube and a fat wide tube. The wide one carries more, because its paper is further from the middle, but its thin wall caves in more easily. There is a best size in between.

The paper is exactly the same. It is not stronger. Its shape makes it rigid. That is why bicycle frames, tent poles, scaffolding, your bones and the legs of masts are tubes: nearly as stiff as a solid rod, at a fraction of the weight. A drinking straw is the proof in your lunch box.

Hold the tube holding a textbook in mind and try this part's questions: what happens, how it fails and why.

## Subtopic: Fold It Like a Fan

Take another sheet and fold it along its length into a zigzag of pleats about 2 centimetres wide, pressing each fold sharp. Lay it across the gap and load it. It carries far more than the flat sheet, about as much as the tube, until a fold flattens.

Try wide pleats and narrow pleats: narrow sharp folds are stiffer. Then turn a folded sheet so the folds run the same way as the books, and it collapses at once. Folds only work when they run along the gap.

Why? Folds move paper up and down out of the flat, so the pleated sheet is many times thicker in the bending direction. You see it everywhere: corrugated iron roofs, the wavy middle of cardboard, ribs pressed into a car floor and a biscuit tin lid, a paper fan, a palm leaf, a cockle shell.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Stable means it does not topple, strong means it does not break, rigid means it does not bend. Rolling a sheet into a tube or folding it into pleats moves the material away from the middle so it resists bending many times better, with no extra weight. Wider tubes and sharper folds are stiffer, but thin walls buckle and folds must run along the span. Tubes and folds are the cheapest stiffness there is, and your tower legs should use them.

Hold the pleated sheet and the corrugated roof, and take the final questions of the lesson: folding, direction and examples.
