# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the practical investigation of stiffening a frame structure by triangulation. The anchor is a Technology bench in Kimberley with strips of thick card, split pins, drinking straws, pipe cleaners, a hole punch and a small weight, where a class builds a pinned square, adds diagonals, extends the frame into a cube and a tapered tower section, and tests each stage by pushing and loading it. The governing idea is that a frame with pinned joints is rigid only when it is made entirely of triangles, that each diagonal added converts a mechanism into a structure, that diagonal members carry tension or compression depending on the direction of the load, and that bracing must be placed in every plane in which the frame could distort, including across the inside against twist. Carrying out the investigation, explaining why triangles are rigid, identifying members in tension and compression and applying triangulation to a model tower are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Square That Will Not Stand

Cut four strips of card 150 millimetres long and 20 wide, punch a hole near each end, and join them into a square with split pins so that each corner can turn freely. Stand it on the bench and push the top sideways. It folds into a parallelogram and then flat, with no resistance except the slight friction of the pins. The square is a mechanism, a thing designed to move, not a structure. This is what a tower panel without diagonals would do in the wind.

Now take a fifth strip, measure the diagonal of the square, which for 150 millimetre sides is about 210 millimetres between pin centres, cut it to length, punch it and pin it from one corner to the opposite corner. Push the top again. The frame is rigid. To fold now, the diagonal would have to get longer or shorter, and card is very hard to stretch or squash along its length. One strip has turned a mechanism into a structure by dividing the square into two triangles.

Build a triangle on its own, three strips pinned at three corners, and try to distort it. You cannot, however hard you push, unless a strip bends or a pin tears out. Build a pentagon of five strips and it flops like the square. The triangle is the only polygon whose shape is fixed by the lengths of its sides. Every other shape needs extra members to divide it into triangles before it becomes rigid, and the count is simple: a shape with n sides needs n minus 3 diagonals.

Record the investigation as you go: a sketch of each frame, what happened when it was pushed and how many extra members it took to make it rigid. This record is the evidence that your tower design was based on testing.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the pinned square folds, what one diagonal does and why the triangle alone is rigid.

## Subtopic: Tension, Compression and Crossed Diagonals

With the single diagonal in place, push the top of the square to the right and watch the diagonal. If it runs from bottom left to top right, your push tries to pull its two ends apart: the diagonal is in tension, being stretched, and card resists stretching well. Now push the top to the left. The same diagonal is being pushed end to end, in compression, and a thin card strip under compression bows and buckles, so the frame gives slightly. A diagonal that is strong in tension may be weak in compression if it is slender.

The solution used in towers is to add the second diagonal, making an X. Whichever way the frame is pushed, one of the two diagonals is in tension and holds the shape, while the other is in compression and may buckle harmlessly. Because the tension diagonal does all the work, both diagonals can be thin, even wire or string. Test this: replace the card diagonals with two pieces of string pulled tight across the square, and the frame is rigid in both directions despite string having no stiffness at all in compression. Engineers call these tension-only members ties, and a member that must resist compression a strut.

Horizontal and vertical members in a loaded frame are also in tension or compression. Load the top of a triangulated tower section with a weight and the legs are in compression, carrying the load down, while the horizontals stop the legs bowing outward and may be in tension. Members in compression must be stiff enough not to buckle, which is why the legs of a tower are the heaviest members and why tubing and folding, from the last session, matter most for them. Members in tension can be slender. A good frame puts thin ties where there is only pulling and sturdy struts where there is pushing, and that is where its lightness comes from.

A short cut for identifying tension and compression: imagine removing the member and ask what the frame would do. If the two ends would move apart, the member was holding them together and is in tension. If the ends would move together, the member was holding them apart and is in compression.

The questions for this section are with you now: which diagonal is in tension, why an X allows thin members and how to tell a tie from a strut.

## Subtopic: Bracing a Tower in Every Plane

Join four triangulated squares edge to edge into a tube to make a cube-shaped tower section. Each face is now rigid in its own plane. Hold the base and twist the top, and the section still distorts, because the top square and the bottom square can rotate relative to each other without any face changing shape. The faces are braced but the section is not. Add one diagonal across the inside, from a top corner to the diagonally opposite bottom corner, or a triangulated horizontal frame across the top, and the twist stops. Bracing must be provided in every plane in which the frame could move: each face, and across the section against twist.

Now make a tapered section, with the legs sloping inward so the top square is smaller than the bottom. The sloping legs stiffen the frame because each face is itself a tall trapezium with a triangle built in, and the structure is more stable because its centre of gravity is lower and its base wider. Stack two sections and you have a model tower that can be tested with a weight on top and a sideways push partway up. Compare it with an unbraced version to feel the difference.

Finally, consider how many diagonals are enough. A tower of six panels with single diagonals alternating in direction, a zigzag, is rigid, because each panel is two triangles and the alternating directions share the tension work between panels. A tower with an X in every panel is stiffer and symmetrical but uses twice the material. A tower with diagonals only in the bottom panels is rigid at the base and floppy at the top. Designers choose the pattern to put material where the wind's bending is greatest, at the bottom, and to keep the top light.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding more horizontals to stiffen a frame; horizontals divide the panel into smaller rectangles, which still fold. Two: using a single slender diagonal and expecting it to work in both directions; in compression it buckles. Three: bracing the faces of a tower and forgetting the inside, leaving it free to twist. Four: thinking more diagonals are always better; enough, placed where the forces are, is the goal.

The questions for this section are with you now: bracing against twist, the effect of taper and choosing a diagonal pattern.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with card strips, split pins and a few pieces of string — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Square Falls Flat

Pin four card strips into a square so the corners can turn. Stand it up and push the top. It leans into a diamond and falls flat. Nothing stops it except the squeak of the pins. That square is a mechanism, a thing that moves. A tower panel without diagonals would do the same in the wind.

Measure corner to corner, cut a fifth strip, pin it across. Push again. Solid. To fold now, that strip would have to stretch or shrink, and card will not. One strip turned a floppy square into two triangles.

Pin three strips into a triangle. Try to squash it. You cannot. Pin five into a pentagon. Floppy again. Only the triangle is fixed by the lengths of its sides. Every other shape needs extra strips to cut it into triangles: a shape with n sides needs n minus 3.

Sketch each frame, write what happened when you pushed it, and count the strips it took to make it stiff.

Keep the collapsing square in mind and try the questions for this part: mechanism or structure, and why triangles.

## Subtopic: Pulled or Pushed

Put one diagonal in the square and push the top to the right. The diagonal is being pulled, stretched end to end: tension. Card is good at that. Now push the top to the left. The same diagonal is being pushed end to end: compression. A thin card strip under compression bows and buckles, so the frame gives a little.

Fix it with a second diagonal, making an X. Whichever way you push, one diagonal is pulled and holds, while the other is pushed and bows harmlessly. The pulled one does all the work, so both can be thin. Prove it: replace the card diagonals with two tight pieces of string. String has no stiffness at all when pushed, yet the square is rigid both ways. Pulled-only members are called ties; pushed members are struts.

Load the top of a braced tower section with a weight. The legs are pushed, carrying the load down, so they must be stiff and not buckle; that is why tower legs are the heaviest members and why tubes and folds matter for them. The horizontals mostly stop the legs bowing. Quick test: imagine taking a member out. If the ends would spring apart, it was pulled. If they would move together, it was pushed.

Hold the string diagonals in mind and try this part's questions: tension, compression and the X.

## Subtopic: Brace Every Direction

Join four braced squares into a cube section. Each face is stiff. Hold the bottom and twist the top. It still twists, because the top square can turn against the bottom square without any face changing shape. Add one diagonal through the inside, or a braced frame across the top, and the twisting stops. You must brace every direction the frame could move, including inside against twist.

Make the legs slope inward so the top is smaller than the bottom. The sloping faces are stiffer and the tower is more stable, with a wider base and a lower centre of gravity. Stack two sections, put a weight on top, push it sideways, and compare with an unbraced one.

How many diagonals? A zigzag of single diagonals, alternating direction up the tower, is rigid and light. An X in every panel is stiffer but uses twice the card. Diagonals only at the bottom leave the top floppy. Put the material where the wind bends the tower most, at the bottom, and keep the top light.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A pinned frame is rigid only when it is all triangles; one diagonal turns a square mechanism into a structure. A diagonal is in tension when pulled and compression when pushed; an X lets one diagonal always be in tension so both can be thin. Legs are in compression and must be stiff. Brace every face and the inside against twist, taper the tower, and put the most bracing at the bottom.

Hold the twisting cube, and take the final questions of the lesson: twist, taper and diagonal patterns.
