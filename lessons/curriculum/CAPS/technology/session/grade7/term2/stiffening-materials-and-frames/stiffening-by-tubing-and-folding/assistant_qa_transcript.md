### subtopic_1 — Stability, Strength and Rigidity
**Assistant:** Why does a hollow tube hold almost as much as a solid rod of the same diameter? Surely the missing middle must count for something.

**Tutor:** The missing middle counts for very little, because when a rod bends, the material near its centre line barely stretches or squashes and so contributes almost nothing to resisting the bend. Picture the rod as a bundle of fibres. Bend it and the fibres on the outside of the curve are pulled longer, the fibres on the inside are pushed shorter, and the fibres along the centre keep the same length. The resistance comes from the fibres that change length, and how much they resist depends on how far they are from the centre: a fibre twice as far out is stretched twice as much and, because it is further from the centre, has twice the leverage, so it contributes four times as much. Material at the centre contributes nothing at all. A tube removes exactly that useless central material and keeps the hard-working outer layer, so for the same diameter it keeps most of the stiffness at a fraction of the weight. Engineers measure this with a quantity called the second moment of area, which you will meet in later years, but the idea is simply that material far from the centre does the work. This is why your bones are hollow, why bamboo is hollow, why bicycle frames and scaffolding are tubes, and why a very large diameter thin-walled tube is stiffer still, up to the point where its wall is so thin that it buckles inward. The investigation in this lesson shows all of this with a sheet of paper, which is a good reason to remember it.

### subtopic_2 — Investigation: Stiffening by Tubing
**Assistant:** Does folding work on metal and plastic too, or only on paper and card?

**Tutor:** It works on every material that can be shaped, and much of the manufactured world depends on it. The most familiar example in South Africa is corrugated iron, thin galvanised steel sheet pressed into a wave profile; flat, the same sheet would sag between roof purlins a metre apart, but corrugated it spans easily and has roofed homes and farm buildings for over a century. Modern roofing and cladding profiles are folded into sharper, deeper shapes for the same reason. Car bodies are pressed sheet steel, and if you look under the carpet at a floor pan or inside a bonnet you will see ribs and swages pressed into the panel purely to stiffen it; a flat panel of the same thickness would drum and flex. Steel shelving, filing cabinets, lockers and the sides of taxis and trucks have folded edges and ribs for stiffness. In plastics, the ribs moulded into the underside of a crate or a chair seat let the maker use thin material and still get a rigid product, and corrugated plastic sheet is used for signs and boxes. Aluminium drinks cans have a domed, ribbed base for the same reason. Even concrete is folded: folded-plate roofs and the ribbed slabs of multistorey car parks span further with less material than flat slabs. The principle is independent of material because it depends on geometry, moving material away from the line of bending. What changes between materials is how you make the fold, by pressing, rolling, moulding or casting, and how sharp a fold the material allows before it cracks.

### subtopic_3 — Investigation: Stiffening by Folding
**Assistant:** If tubes and folds give free stiffness, why are the legs of big lattice towers often made of steel angle, which is L-shaped, rather than tubes?

**Tutor:** Because free stiffness is only one of the things a tower designer is paying for, and angle sections win on several others. An L-shaped angle is itself a folded section, with its material spread into two flanges away from the centre, so it is far stiffer than a flat bar of the same weight, and that is why it is used at all. Compared with a tube of equal stiffness it has three practical advantages. It is easy to bolt: a tube has to be crushed, slotted or welded to a plate before it can be joined, while a flat flange simply takes a bolt through it, and a tower may have thousands of bolted joints assembled by a small crew on a hilltop. It is easy to galvanise and inspect, because every surface is on the outside where the zinc coating can reach it and a rigger can see corrosion; a tube rusts invisibly from the inside if water gets in. And it packs flat for transport, so a whole tower arrives on one truck. Tubular towers and monopoles are used where their smoother appearance, lower wind resistance or compact footprint justify the extra cost of welded joints, especially in cities. So the choice is a fitness-for-purpose decision: the angle gives enough stiffness from its folded shape while winning on cost, joining, maintenance and transport. For your card model the same trade-off appears in miniature. Rolled tubes are stiffest, but folded channels and angles are easier to glue flat to a base and to join to each other, and that may matter more at the corners.
