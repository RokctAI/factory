# Part 1 — Expert

This session consolidates the content of Terms 1 and 2 in preparation for the formal mid-year test. The anchor is two completed design records side by side on a desk in Bloemfontein, the rescue device project and the cell phone tower project, opened to their briefs, investigation tables, drawings and evaluations, with a past test paper beside them. The governing idea is that the two projects together cover the first half of the Grade 7 curriculum, that the test will ask for definitions, explanations, classifications, calculations and short evaluations drawn from both, and that the ideas form connected chains, a need leads to a brief leads to a tested product, and a load leads to a structure leads to a stiffening technique, which are easier to remember than isolated facts. All concepts listed are examinable and the consolidation also revisits stiffening of materials and frame structures as the curriculum asks.

## Subtopic: The Design Process and the Two Briefs

Both projects followed IDMEC: investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate, a loop rather than a line. Investigation in Term 1 meant the scenario, levers and the syringe tests; in Term 2 it meant examining towers, analysing existing products and the stiffening and triangulation tests. Design in both meant a brief with specifications and constraints, several sketches, group evaluation, a chosen idea and drawings. Make meant a planned sequence, safe tool use and a model. Evaluate meant testing against each specification with a measured result. Communicate ran throughout as records, labelled sketches, drawings and presentations.

A design brief states the need, the product, the user and the purpose in a few sentences, written before sketching. Specifications are testable features of the product; constraints are limits on the project. Fitness for purpose is judged on users, purpose, function, cost-effectiveness, safety, ergonomics, aesthetics and impact on society and the environment. Analysing an existing design, such as the school desk or the radio, means listing features, explaining their purpose, evaluating strengths and weaknesses and reconstructing the brief.

Evaluation in a group criticises the design not the designer, gives reasons from the brief and the investigations, lists advantages and disadvantages, and leads to recorded improvements with the original kept visible. Technology responds to the needs of a society, which designers find out by asking, and it has positive and negative impacts on society and the environment that must both be listed and weighed; the landline and mobile comparison and the tower case study are the examples to quote.

Practise: write a brief with two specifications and two constraints for any object in the room; list the eight considerations; name three positive and three negative impacts of mobile networks; explain IDMEC in five sentences.

The questions for this section are with you now: IDMEC, briefs, specifications and constraints, fitness for purpose, analysis and impact.

## Subtopic: Mechanisms: Levers and Fluids

A lever is a rigid bar turning on a pivot; the load is the force moved, the effort is the force applied. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort, and a longer effort arm relative to the load arm gives a greater advantage at the cost of the effort moving further. Classes are sorted by what is in the middle: pivot, first class, reverses direction, any MA; load, second class, always MA above 1; effort, third class, always MA below 1 but gives speed and reach. Linked levers share a pivot; linkages with several pivots pass movement along, trading force for distance at each one.

Pneumatics uses a gas and hydraulics a liquid to transmit pressure, force over area, through a sealed system. Unequal pistons multiply force in proportion to their areas while reducing movement in the same proportion. Gases compress, so pneumatic systems lag and feel springy; liquids do not, so hydraulic systems respond at once and hold under load. The syringe tests showed equal syringes giving equal force and distance with water, unequal syringes trading force for distance, and air giving late, soft movement.

Practise: classify ten tools; calculate the MA when a 600 newton load is lifted by 150 newtons; predict what moving a pivot closer to the load does; explain why a rescue tool is hydraulic and a bus door pneumatic; describe the equal-syringe water test and its result.

A typical test question gives a diagram of a tool and asks for the class with a reason, or gives two forces and asks for the MA, or asks for one advantage and one disadvantage of hydraulics compared with pneumatics. Answer each with the rule, then the application: the pivot is between the effort and the load, so it is first class; MA is load over effort, 600 over 150, which is 4; liquid does not compress, so the response is immediate, but it can leak and is heavier than air.

The questions for this section are with you now: lever parts and classes, mechanical advantage, pneumatics and hydraulics.

## Subtopic: Structures, Drawing and the Confusions

A structure is parts arranged to keep a shape and carry a load, to contain, protect, support or span, natural or man-made, and shell, frame or solid by construction: shells take strength from a thin curved skin, frames from members arranged in triangles, solids from mass. Stability depends on the centre of gravity staying over the base, improved by a wider base, a lower centre of gravity or anchoring. Strength is resisting breaking, rigidity is resisting bending, and they are different. Materials are stiffened by tubing and folding, which move material away from the line of bending; frames are stiffened by triangulation, because only a triangle is rigid with pinned joints, with ties in tension and struts in compression, braced in every plane including against twist. Reinforcing uses gussets at joints, webs in corners and cross-bracing on faces and internally. Towers are tapered, wide at the base, and their biggest load is wind. Visual pollution is answered by siting, form, disguise, sharing and colour, each with a structural cost.

Drawings use dark outlines, feint construction, dashed hidden detail and chain centre lines, a stated scale and real dimensions in millimetres each given once. Free-hand sketches are quick and labelled; a 2D working drawing of one face is for making; a 3D oblique drawing has a true front face and depth at 45 degrees, half length, for showing. A resource list gives materials and tools with quantities and reasons, and a parts schedule is lettered to match the drawing.

The confusions that cost marks, in pairs. Strength and rigidity: breaking versus bending. Stability and rigidity: toppling versus bending. Shell and frame: skin versus skeleton. Natural and man-made: who made it, not the material. Specification and constraint: product target versus project limit. Load and effort: moved versus applied. Hydraulics and pneumatics: liquid versus gas. Working drawing and oblique drawing: for making with sizes versus for showing with depth. Cost-effective and cheap: value versus price. Evaluation and praise: measured against the brief versus a compliment.

The error museum, four exhibits for the half year. One: judging a lever by its shape; judge by what is in the middle. Two: adding horizontals to stiffen a frame; only diagonals make triangles. Three: saying a tube is stronger than a flat sheet; it is stiffer, the material is the same. Four: an evaluation that lists benefits only; both sides are always required.

The questions for this section are with you now: structures, stability, stiffening, reinforcing, drawing conventions and the confusing pairs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, walking back through two project books page by page — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Projects, One Method

Both projects used the same five steps: investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate, round and round. Find out, draw ideas and choose, build, test against what you promised, write and explain all the way through.

Both started with a brief: who needs it, what you will make, who it is for, what it must do. Then specifications you can test and constraints you must stay inside. Fit for purpose means passing eight checks: users, purpose, function, value, safety, ergonomics, looks, impact.

Both used evaluation: of existing products like the desk and the radio, and of each other's sketches, always against the brief with reasons, always design not designer, always both good and bad.

And both asked who the technology is for and what it costs the world: ask the community, list the good and the bad, shrink the bad by design. Landline versus mobile, and the tower on the hill, are your examples.

Keep the two project books in mind and try the questions for this part: the method, the brief and fitness for purpose.

## Subtopic: Pushes Made Bigger

Levers: a bar on a pivot. Load is what you move, effort is your push. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. Push far from the pivot and you get stronger but move further. Sort by what is in the middle: pivot, first class, flips direction; load, second class, always stronger; effort, third class, never stronger but fast.

Fluids: push through a pipe. Air is pneumatics, springy and late. Liquid is hydraulics, firm and instant. A small piston pushing a big piston multiplies the force by the area and shrinks the movement the same amount. Water in syringes proved it; air in syringes lagged.

Test habits: name the rule, then apply it. The pivot is in the middle, so first class. Load over effort, 600 over 150, equals 4. Liquid does not squash, so the jaws move at once.

Hold the lever and the two syringes in mind and try this part's questions: classes, MA and air versus water.

## Subtopic: Things That Stand Up, and Not Getting Mixed Up

A structure keeps its shape and carries a load, to contain, protect, support or span. Shell: thin curved skin. Frame: bars in triangles. Solid: mass. Stable means it does not topple: wide base, low centre of gravity, anchored. Strong means it does not break. Rigid means it does not bend. Stiffen a material by rolling it into a tube or folding it. Stiffen a frame with triangles, braced in every direction including against twist. Reinforce joints with gussets and webs, panels with cross-bracing. Towers taper, and wind is their big load. Hide a tower as a tree and pay for it with a stronger base.

Drawings: dark outlines, feint construction, dashed hidden lines, scale on, real sizes in millimetres once. Working drawing to make, oblique to show.

The pairs people mix up: strong and rigid, stable and rigid, shell and frame, natural and man-made, specification and constraint, load and effort, hydraulics and pneumatics, working and oblique, cheap and cost-effective, praise and evaluation. One sentence each.

Pocket summary of the half year. A need becomes a brief; IDMEC turns it into a tested product judged on eight considerations and on both sides of its impact. Levers and fluids multiply force by trading distance. Structures contain, protect, support or span as shells, frames or solids; they are kept stable by base and centre of gravity, and stiff by tubes, folds and triangles. Drawings speak a shared language. Know the pairs.

Hold the pocket summary, and take the final questions of the lesson: structures, drawings and the pairs.
