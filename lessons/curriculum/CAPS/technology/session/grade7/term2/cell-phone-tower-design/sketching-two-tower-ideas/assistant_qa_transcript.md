### subtopic_1 — Drawing a Lattice Tower in Oblique
**Assistant:** Why does the curriculum insist on free-hand sketches rather than letting me use a ruler for the tower? The lines would be so much straighter.

**Tutor:** Because at this stage the purpose of the drawing is to think and to compare, not to build, and a ruler slows thinking down and tempts you to commit too early. Sketching free-hand lets you draw a tower in a few minutes, look at it, see that the base is too narrow or the bracing too sparse, and draw another one beside it. With instruments, each drawing takes so long that you make one and defend it. Professional designers work this way deliberately; architects and engineers fill pages with quick free-hand studies before a single line is drawn with instruments or on a computer, because the fast rough drawings are where options get explored and rejected cheaply. Squared paper is the compromise the curriculum offers: the grid keeps your verticals vertical and gives you the 45 degree diagonals for free, so a free-hand sketch on it looks tidy without a ruler. The straight, instrument-drawn version comes later, when the chosen idea is turned into a working drawing for making, and there the ruler is required. There is also an assessment reason. Examiners can see design thinking in a free-hand sketch, the hesitations, the second attempt at a base, the added note, in a way a clean ruled drawing hides. A slightly wobbly sketch with clear structure and good annotations earns more than a ruler-straight picture with nothing explained. Practise long confident strokes from the shoulder, accept a little wobble, and spend the saved time on the notes.

### subtopic_2 — Making Two Ideas Genuinely Different
**Assistant:** What if my two ideas are both good? How do I choose between them without just picking my favourite?

**Tutor:** You do not choose alone, and you do not choose by feeling; you compare them against the specifications, and next week your group does the same, which is why the notes under each sketch matter so much. Draw up the table you used for the rescue device: specifications down the side, the two ideas across the top, and a tick, cross or question mark in each cell with a one-word reason. Height and footprint are easy ticks or crosses. Stability: which base is wider, which top is heavier? Strength and rigidity: which bracing pattern and member type did the investigations show to be stiffer? Mass: which uses less material? Visual impact: which softening is more convincing and better suited to the community in the brief? Buildability within the constraints: which can actually be made in two lessons from the materials given? Often the table shows that each idea wins some rows, and then the right answer is usually a third design that combines the strengths of both, such as the four-legged lattice for stability with the second idea's canopy and weighted base. Designers call this synthesis, and it is encouraged; write down which features came from which idea and why. If the table is genuinely tied, pick the one that is simpler to build, because a simple design made well beats a clever design made badly, and record that reasoning. What examiners penalise is a choice with no reasons, so whichever you pick, the table and a few sentences are what earn the marks.

### subtopic_3 — Sketching Habits That Earn Marks
**Assistant:** My tower sketch looks flat even though I used 45 degree lines. What am I doing wrong?

**Tutor:** Three small things usually cause a flat-looking oblique sketch, and all are quick to fix. First, depth lines that are too short or missing from key corners. The eye reads depth from the receding edges of the base and the platform, so make sure both have clear 45 degree lines of a sensible length, about half the real depth, and that the back legs connect them; if the platform has no depth the top of the tower collapses into a flat triangle. Second, no difference between the two visible faces. In oblique the front face is drawn true and the side face is drawn receding, so the horizontals on the side face must run at 45 degrees, not across the page, and the diagonals on that face must fit inside those slanted panels. If both faces look the same the tower reads as a flat silhouette. Third, line weight. Darken the four legs and the outlines of the base and platform more than the internal bracing, and draw the hidden back members faint, dashed or not at all; when every line is equally dark the brain cannot tell front from back. A quick test is to cover the drawing except the base: if the base alone reads as a square seen from the corner, with a true front edge and a receding side edge, the rest will follow. Tapered legs also help, because a trapezium face with its mirror receding at 45 degrees gives a strong sense of a three-dimensional pyramid-like form that vertical legs do not.
