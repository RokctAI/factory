### subtopic_1 — Writing the Tower Brief
**Assistant:** How do I decide the numbers in my specifications, like 200 grams or 10 millimetres? They feel made up.

**Tutor:** They are chosen, not made up, and the difference is that a chosen number has a reason you can state. Start from what the model has to demonstrate and from what you learned in the investigations. The load on the platform stands in for the antennas and the wind on them, so it should be heavy enough to stress a card tower but light enough that a well-designed one passes: 200 grams, about a cup of water, is a sensible stand-in because your stiffening tests showed that a single straw buckles under far less while a triangulated section of four straws carries much more. The sideways movement limit stands in for antenna alignment: a real tower's antennas lose signal if the top sways more than a small fraction of the height, so you might allow 2 percent of 500 millimetres, which is 10 millimetres. The base size comes from the constraint of the drawing sheet and the bench space and from the stability investigation, which showed that a base a quarter to a third of the height resists tipping well. The mass limit comes from the point of the whole term, that good structures are light: weigh a few sheets of card and a bundle of straws and set a figure that forces you to leave material out. Write a one-line reason next to each number in your design record. When the tower is tested, you may find a number was too strict or too generous, and revising it openly with a reason is good practice. What examiners do not want is a number with no reason, or a specification with no number at all.

### subtopic_2 — Reinforcing: Webs, Gussets and Internal Bracing
**Assistant:** Is it cheating to make my tower look like a tree just so it is not ugly? It feels like decoration rather than engineering.

**Tutor:** It is engineering, because the brief asks for it, the community needs it and it changes the structure, and the fact that it also looks pleasant does not make it less serious. Recall the fitness-for-purpose considerations from the first term: aesthetics and impact on society are two of the eight, and a tower that is refused planning permission because residents hate the look of it serves nobody, however perfectly triangulated. Real networks spend considerable money disguising towers for exactly this reason. What turns the tree from decoration into engineering is accounting for its consequences. A canopy of card leaves at the top adds mass high up, which raises the centre of gravity and makes the tower easier to tip; it also catches more air when pushed, which is the model's version of wind load. Your design must therefore respond, with a wider or heavier base, stronger lower panels or guy strings, and your record must say so. That chain, a social requirement leading to an aesthetic choice leading to a structural change, is precisely the kind of thinking the subject is trying to teach, and it is what the engineer at a public meeting has to explain. A tree tower with a flimsy base that topples is decoration; a tree tower whose base was redesigned to carry the canopy is design. Choose the disguise that fits the community you described in the brief, build the structure to carry it, and explain the trade-off.

### subtopic_3 — Disguising the Tower
**Assistant:** Why does the brief say self-supporting? Could I not just hold the tower up with string, like a guyed mast?

**Tutor:** You could design a guyed mast, and it would be a legitimate structure, but the brief specifies self-supporting for two reasons that are worth understanding. First, the learning goal: this term investigated how frames achieve stability, strength and rigidity through their own shape, by triangulation, tapering, reinforcing joints and stiffening members. A self-supporting tower makes you use all of those, because nothing else is holding it up. A guyed mast moves most of the stability problem into the guy strings and their anchors, so a flimsy central pole can stand if the strings are tight, and the investigation findings would go largely untested. Second, the real-world situation in the brief: a tower above a settlement has little spare land for guy anchors, which must be placed far from the base, and guys are a hazard to people and animals moving around the site. Self-supporting lattice towers are therefore the normal choice near housing, and guyed masts are used where there is open land, such as on farms and for very tall broadcast masts. If you want to use string, use it as internal cross-bracing, pulled tight through the inside of the frame, where it works as a tie within a self-supporting structure rather than as an external prop. If your design truly needs guys to meet the stability specification, that is worth recording honestly as a finding: it would tell you that the base is too small or the top too heavy for a self-supporting frame, and the next design should fix that.
