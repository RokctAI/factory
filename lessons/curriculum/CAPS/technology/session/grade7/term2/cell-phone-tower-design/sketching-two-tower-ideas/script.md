# Part 1 — Expert

This session produces free-hand 3D sketches of two design ideas for the cell phone tower, drawn in oblique projection. The anchor is an A3 sheet of squared paper on a desk in Pietermaritzburg, with the tower brief pinned above it and a pencil, an eraser kept out of reach, and the results tables from the investigations at hand, as a learner draws a tapered four-legged lattice and then a slender three-legged monopole-style tower with a tree canopy. The governing idea is that design ideas must differ in structure, not only in decoration, that each sketch must show how the design meets the specifications for stability, strength, rigidity and visual impact, and that oblique projection on squared paper is the quickest way to make a tall frame readable in three dimensions. Producing two labelled oblique sketches of different tower ideas with annotations tied to the specifications is an examinable design mark.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Lattice Tower in Oblique

A tall frame is easier to draw than it looks if you build it from a simple box. On squared paper, at a scale that fits the sheet, draw the front face of the tower as a tall trapezium: a base line, two legs sloping inward, a short top line for the platform. Keep these feint. Mark the levels of the horizontals with light ticks up both legs, equally spaced or closer together towards the base where the bracing is denser.

Add depth. From the two right-hand corners, bottom and top, draw 45 degree lines back along the grid diagonals, each half the real depth of the base at the bottom and of the platform at the top. Join their ends to make the back legs, which slope just as the front ones do. You now have the outline of a tapered four-legged tower in oblique, with the left face and the front face visible and the two back faces hidden. Darken the four visible legs and the base and platform outlines.

Fill in the members. Draw the horizontals on the two visible faces at the marked levels, remembering that those on the side face run back at 45 degrees. Then the diagonals: a zigzag or an X in each panel, drawn with a single confident line each. Add the antenna platform as a small braced frame at the top with three panel antennas and a dish, and the concrete base as a shallow block under the legs. Leave the hidden members out or draw one or two dashed to show the back face is also braced.

Then annotate. Label the legs, horizontals, diagonals, platform and base. Add notes with leader lines: legs as rolled card tubes for stiffness; X bracing on every face; internal string bracing at two levels, with a dashed line to show it; webs at the base corners; gussets at each leg-horizontal joint. Write the overall height and base size, and the scale. A sketch like this communicates the whole structural idea on one page.

The questions for this section are with you now: building a tower sketch from a trapezium, adding depth and members, and annotating it.

## Subtopic: Making Two Ideas Genuinely Different

The PAT asks for two ideas, and two sketches of the same tower in different colours are not two ideas. Ideas differ when they solve the brief by a different structural route. Vary the number of legs: a four-legged square lattice is stable and simple to brace; a three-legged triangular lattice uses fewer members, is rigid in plan without internal bracing because its horizontal section is already a triangle, and has a smaller footprint. Vary the taper: a strongly tapered tower with a wide base is stable and stiff but uses a lot of base material; a slimmer tower needs a heavier base plate or ribs.

Vary the bracing pattern: a zigzag of single diagonals is light; an X in every panel is stiffer; K bracing, where two diagonals meet at the middle of a horizontal, shortens the compression members. Vary the member type: card tubes for legs and straws for diagonals, or folded card channels throughout, or straws for everything with card gussets. Vary the stability strategy: a wide base, a weighted base plate, or a base with outriggers. And vary the softening: a tree canopy, a flagpole form, a painted colour scheme, a shared platform.

For the second sketch, then, draw something structurally distinct. If the first was a four-legged tapered lattice with X bracing and card-tube legs, the second might be a three-legged tower with K bracing, folded channel legs, a weighted base and a canopy of card leaves hiding the antennas. Draw it the same way, trapezium, depth, members, annotations, and give it the same scale so the two can be compared side by side.

Under each sketch write two or three sentences on how it meets the brief and where it might fall short. The four-legged lattice: very stable and stiff, every face easily braced, but heavier and taller to draw and the most material. The three-legged canopy tower: lighter, naturally rigid in plan, hides the antennas, but the canopy adds top weight and the three-leg base is less stable against a push along one face. These notes are the raw material for the group evaluation next week.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ways two tower ideas can differ structurally and the notes written under each sketch.

## Subtopic: Sketching Habits That Earn Marks

Examiners assess the sketches on clarity, on whether the structure can be understood, on the use of conventions and on the link to the specifications. Clarity comes from the feint-then-dark habit: set out with light construction lines, check the proportions against the brief, and only then darken the outlines and members. Keep construction lines on the page; they show the method. Use a single stroke for each member rather than a hairy line; practise a few straight lines on scrap first.

Readability comes from restraint. A tower has many members, and a sketch that shows every one of forty diagonals in both faces becomes a grey tangle. Show the pattern fully in the lowest two or three panels and the top panel, and indicate the rest with lighter lines or a note saying pattern continues. Draw what the maker needs to understand and no more.

The link to the specifications comes from the annotations. Each note should answer a specification: tubes for rigidity, wide base for stability, X bracing for strength against sideways push, canopy for visual impact. A sketch with structure but no notes shows drawing skill; a sketch with notes tied to the brief shows design. Add the key numbers, height and base, so the sketch can be checked against the brief at a glance.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: two sketches of one idea; the second must differ in structure. Two: drawing every member so the sketch cannot be read; show the pattern and indicate the rest. Three: no annotations; the structure must be explained in words on the page. Four: depth lines at random angles and lengths so the tower looks twisted; all at 45 degrees, all in proportion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the habits that make a sketch clear, readable and tied to the brief.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a sheet of squared paper and a pencil — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Draw the Tower From a Box

Start with the front face as a tall trapezium: a base line, two legs leaning inward, a short line at the top for the platform. Keep it feint. Tick the levels where the horizontals will go, closer together near the bottom.

Add depth: from the two right-hand corners, draw lines back at 45 degrees, one square across and one up, about half the real depth. Join the ends to make the back legs, sloping the same way. Darken the four visible legs and the base and top.

Now the members. Horizontals at your ticks on the two faces you can see, the side ones running back at 45 degrees. Diagonals as a zigzag or an X in each panel, one clean line each. A small braced platform on top with antennas, a shallow concrete block at the bottom.

Label it: legs, horizontals, diagonals, platform, base. Add notes: tube legs for stiffness, X on every face, string inside at two levels, webs at the base corners, gussets at the joints. Write the height, the base size and the scale.

Keep the trapezium and the 45 degree lines in mind and try the questions for this part: the steps of the tower sketch.

## Subtopic: Two Ideas, Not One Idea Twice

Two sketches of the same tower in different colours are not two ideas. Ideas differ in how they solve the problem.

Change the legs: four legs make a square tower, easy to brace; three legs make a triangle tower, lighter, and already stiff in plan. Change the taper: wide and stable, or slim with a heavy base. Change the bracing: zigzag, X, or K where two diagonals meet in the middle of a horizontal. Change the members: card tubes, folded channels or straws. Change the stability trick: wide base, weighted plate, or feet that stick out. Change the softening: tree canopy, flagpole, paint, shared platform.

So if sketch one is a four-legged tapered lattice with X bracing and tube legs, sketch two could be a three-legged tower with K bracing, folded legs, a weighted base and a canopy of card leaves. Same scale, side by side.

Under each, write two or three sentences: how it meets the brief and where it might fail. Four legs: very stable, but the most material. Three legs with canopy: light and hidden, but top-heavy and less stable along one face. Those notes feed next week's evaluation.

Hold the two different towers in mind and try this part's questions: how ideas can differ and what to write under them.

## Subtopic: Sketching Habits That Score

Feint first, check against the brief, then dark. Leave the feint lines; they show your method. One clean stroke per member; practise on scrap.

Do not draw every one of forty diagonals; it becomes a grey mess. Show the pattern fully in the bottom panels and the top one, and write pattern continues for the rest.

Every note should answer a specification: tubes for rigidity, wide base for stability, X for strength against a push, canopy for the view. Write the height and base size so the sketch can be checked against the brief at a glance.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Draw the tower from a trapezium, add 45 degree depth, fill in horizontals and diagonals, label and annotate. Make the second idea differ in legs, taper, bracing, members, stability or softening. Feint then dark, show the pattern not every member, and tie each note to a specification.

Hold the two annotated sketches, and take the final questions of the lesson: the habits that earn marks.
