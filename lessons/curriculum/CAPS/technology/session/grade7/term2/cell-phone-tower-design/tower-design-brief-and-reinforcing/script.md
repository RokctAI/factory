# Part 1 — Expert

This session moves the term's investigation into design: writing a design brief with specifications for a new cell phone tower, planning its reinforcement with triangular webs, gussets and internal cross-bracing, and considering how to disguise it to avoid visual pollution. The anchor is a hilltop above a growing settlement outside Rustenburg where a network wants a tower and residents want their view, with a learner's project book open to a blank brief page and the results tables from the stiffening and triangulation investigations beside it. The governing idea is that a brief for a structure must state the need, the structure, the users and the purpose, that its specifications translate the term's findings about stability, strength and rigidity into testable requirements for a model, and that reinforcing and disguising decisions are made with reasons from the investigations. Writing a brief with specifications and constraints for the tower, selecting and justifying reinforcing techniques, and proposing disguise options are examinable design marks.

## Subtopic: Writing the Tower Brief

The brief for a structure follows the same shape as the brief for the rescue device: the need, the thing to be made, the users and the purpose, in a few sentences, written before any sketching. For the Rustenburg hill: The settlement has grown and residents have no reliable mobile signal, so the clinic, the school and local businesses cannot be reached. I am going to design and make a model of a self-supporting frame tower at least 500 millimetres tall that holds a model antenna head steady against a sideways push, to show how a real tower would be built for stability, strength and rigidity. The model is for the Technology class and must be built from the card, straws and glue provided. It must also show one way of reducing the tower's visual impact on the people below it.

Each sentence does its job: the first names the people and the need, the second the product and its purpose with the three structural words from the scenario, the third the user and the main constraint, the fourth brings in the social consideration. A brief that leaves out the community is a brief for a steel frame, not for a cell phone tower.

Now the specifications, each testable. Height: at least 500 millimetres from base to antenna platform. Footprint: base fits inside a 150 by 150 millimetre square. Stability: stands unaided and does not topple when the top is pushed sideways with a force that moves it 20 millimetres, returning upright when released. Strength: carries a 200 gram load on the antenna platform without any member breaking. Rigidity: the platform moves less than 10 millimetres sideways under a push of a chosen size at the top. Structure: a frame, triangulated in every face and braced against twist. Mass: no more than 150 grams of material, to reward efficient design. Visual impact: at least one disguise or softening feature is included and explained.

Then the constraints: materials limited to the card, drinking straws, string, split pins and glue provided; made in two lessons; no sharp or dangerous parts; A3 drawing sheet for the design; a cost limit if extra materials are allowed. Check every specification against a question: could a classmate test this with a ruler, a weight or a push and give a yes or no? If not, rewrite it until they could.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four sentences of the tower brief and writing testable specifications for stability, strength and rigidity.

## Subtopic: Reinforcing: Webs, Gussets and Internal Bracing

The investigations told us where frames fail: at joints that rotate, in panels that lean, in slender members that buckle and in sections that twist. Reinforcing techniques answer each. Triangular webs are small triangles of card glued into the corner where two members meet, filling the angle so that the joint cannot open or close. In a card model they are cut from offcuts and glued flat across the inside of each corner of the base and the top platform, and they make a corner that would otherwise hinge behave as part of a triangle.

Gussets are plates fixed across a joint so that the members are connected through an area rather than a point. In the model, a gusset is a square or triangle of card glued over a joint where a leg meets a horizontal, with the leg and the horizontal both glued to the gusset over a good length. Gussets spread the glue area, stop the members twisting relative to each other and allow a leg to be joined to two or three members at once at the same level. Real towers use steel gusset plates with several bolts, and the model does the same job with glue.

Cross-bracing is the X of diagonals across each panel from the triangulation investigation, and internal cross-bracing is the diagonal that runs through the inside of the tower from a corner at one level to the opposite corner at the next, which stops the section twisting. In a card tower, the face diagonals can be straws or card strips, and internal bracing can be string pulled tight from corner to opposite corner, which is light and almost invisible. A horizontal triangulated frame at the platform and at one level lower does the same job and gives the antennas a stiff mount.

Plan the reinforcement on a sketch before building: mark every corner that will have a web, every joint that will have a gusset, every panel that will have an X or a single diagonal, and the two or three levels where internal bracing or a horizontal frame will go. Give a one-line reason for each from the investigations: webs because pinned corners rotate, gussets because glued point joints fail, face diagonals because rectangles lean, internal bracing because braced faces still twist. This plan is design evidence and it also becomes the parts list.

The questions for this section are with you now: what webs, gussets and internal bracing do and where each goes on the tower plan.

## Subtopic: Disguising the Tower

The brief asks for one way to reduce visual impact, so the design must choose from the options the case study gave. Disguise: build the tower as a tree, with the platform hidden in a canopy of card leaves, or as a flagpole, a windmill or a water tower. Form: use a slender tapered shape rather than a wide boxy one, which reads as a graceful object. Colour: paint or cover the tower in a grey-green or sky-blue that fades against its background. Siting: present the model on a base that shows it placed behind a stand of trees or on an existing structure such as a school hall roof. Sharing: show two or three sets of antennas on one platform to make the point that one tower can serve several networks.

Each option has a structural cost that the design must account for. A tree disguise adds weight at the top, which raises the centre of gravity and increases wind load, so the base and the lower panels must be stronger. A slender form has a smaller base, so stability must be won with a heavier base plate or guy strings. Paint adds little weight but takes time. The design record should state the option chosen and the structural change made to pay for it, because that trade-off is exactly what the real engineer at the Pinetown meeting had to explain.

The best designs connect the disguise to the community. A tower near a school might become a flagpole for the school's flag; a tower in a farming area might be shaped like the wind pumps already on the horizon; a tower in a township might carry a sign or a light that residents asked for. This is the needs-of-society work made concrete in a model, and it is the part of the project most likely to be remembered.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a brief that mentions only the steel and not the community. Two: a specification such as the tower must be strong; it must say what load, where. Three: adding gussets and webs after the tower is built, where there is no room; plan them on the sketch. Four: choosing a disguise and ignoring its weight; every softening feature must be paid for in the structure.

The questions for this section are with you now: disguise options, the structural cost of each and linking the design to the community.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, on a hill above a settlement with a blank page of a project book — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Brief for Our Tower

Same four sentences as before. The need: the settlement has grown and has no signal, so the clinic and the school cannot be reached. The product: I will design and make a model frame tower at least 500 millimetres tall that holds a model antenna steady against a sideways push, to show stability, strength and rigidity. The user and main limit: for the Technology class, made from the card, straws and glue provided. The social part: it must show one way of not spoiling the view.

Now specifications you can test. At least 500 millimetres tall. Base inside a 150 by 150 square. Stands on its own and comes back upright after a push. Holds 200 grams on top without a member breaking. Top moves less than 10 millimetres when pushed sideways. Every face triangulated, and braced against twist. No more than 150 grams of material. At least one disguise feature, explained.

Constraints: only the materials given, two lessons, nothing sharp, an A3 sheet for the drawing. For every specification ask: could a friend test this with a ruler, a weight or a push? If not, rewrite it.

Keep the four sentences in mind and try the questions for this part: the brief and testable specifications.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Strengthen It

The investigations showed where frames fail: corners that turn, panels that lean, thin members that buckle, sections that twist. Three reinforcements fix them.

Triangular webs: small card triangles glued into a corner so it cannot open or close. They turn a hinge into part of a triangle. Use them at the corners of the base and the platform.

Gussets: card plates glued over a joint so the members are stuck together over an area, not a point. They stop members twisting against each other and let a leg join two or three members at one level.

Cross-bracing: the X on each face, in straws or card. Internal bracing: a string pulled tight from one corner to the opposite corner at the next level, through the middle of the tower, so it cannot twist. A braced horizontal frame at the top does the same and gives the antenna a stiff seat.

Plan it all on a sketch before building, with a reason for each: webs because corners turn, gussets because glued points fail, diagonals because rectangles lean, internal bracing because faces alone still twist.

Hold the three reinforcements in mind and try this part's questions: what each one fixes and where it goes.

## Subtopic: Hiding It in Plain Sight

The brief asks for one way to reduce the visual impact. Options: dress the tower as a tree with a card canopy, or as a flagpole, a windmill or a water tower. Make it slim and tapered so it looks graceful. Paint it grey-green or sky-blue to fade away. Show it placed behind trees or on a school hall roof. Put several networks' antennas on one platform so fewer towers are needed.

Each option costs something in the structure. A tree canopy adds weight at the top, so the base and bottom panels must be stronger. A slim tower has a small base, so it needs a heavier base plate or guy strings. Write down which option you chose and what you changed to pay for it.

The best ideas fit the place. A flagpole for the school's flag. A wind pump shape in farming country. A light the residents asked for. That is the needs of society turned into card.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The tower brief names the need, the model, the users and the purpose, and includes the community. Specifications turn stability, strength and rigidity into tests with numbers. Webs lock corners, gussets strengthen joints, face and internal cross-bracing stop leaning and twisting, all planned before building. A disguise reduces visual impact and must be paid for in the structure.

Hold the tree tower and its heavier base, and take the final questions of the lesson: disguise and its cost.
