### subtopic_1 — Listing the Resources
**Assistant:** How do I work out the length of each horizontal on a tapered tower without building it first?

**Tutor:** You read it off the working drawing, which is one of the reasons the drawing is made to scale, or you calculate it with a simple proportion. On the drawing, the width of the tower at any level is just the horizontal distance between the two legs at that height; measure it on the paper with a ruler and multiply by the scale factor, so a width of 66 millimetres on a 1 to 2 drawing is a 132 millimetre horizontal. That is quick and accurate enough for straws. If you prefer arithmetic, the taper is straight, so the width shrinks evenly with height. A tower 150 wide at the base and 60 at the platform narrows by 90 millimetres over 500 millimetres of height, which is 18 millimetres for every 100 millimetres you go up. At 80 millimetres up, the width is 150 minus 14.4, about 136; at 170, about 119; at 270, about 101; at 380, about 82; at 500, 60. Write these in the parts schedule against each level. A third approach, which many makers use for the first tower, is to write cut to fit against the horizontals and measure each one against the legs as the face is assembled on the drawing; it is slower but forgiving of small errors. Whichever you choose, the diagonals are best cut to fit, because their lengths depend on both the panel width and height and a few millimetres matter less for a diagonal glued across a panel than for a horizontal that sets the width. Record your method in the design record.

### subtopic_2 — The Working Drawing of One Face
**Assistant:** Why do we draw only one face when the real tower has four? Would a maker not need to see how they join?

**Tutor:** Because the four faces of a square tapered tower are identical, so one dimensioned face carries all the information needed to make each of them, and the joining is simple enough to describe in a note and a plan sketch rather than another full drawing. Professional drawings follow the same economy: when parts repeat, they are drawn once with a note saying four required, and a general arrangement drawing shows how the repeated parts relate. For your tower, the one-face drawing is the manufacturing drawing for the faces. To show the joining, a small plan view, the tower seen from above as a square with a leg at each corner and the internal string as a cross, is enough, and it can go in a corner of the sheet. The oblique sketch you already have is your general arrangement, showing the whole assembled tower. There is a practical reason too: the one-face drawing at 1 to 2 doubles as a jig for building faces flat, which only works if the face is drawn flat and true. If the tower were three-legged, the same logic applies with three identical faces and a triangular plan. If a design has different faces, for instance a ladder on one side or an asymmetrical canopy, then that face needs its own drawing, and the parts schedule would list the extra parts. The rule is to draw as many views as the maker needs to make and assemble the object correctly, and no more; for a square lattice tower, one face, one plan and the schedule meet that test.

### subtopic_3 — Parts Schedule and Checking the Drawing
**Assistant:** Why does the curriculum ask for a cost estimate for a cardboard tower that uses scrap? It feels pointless.

**Tutor:** Because the habit of attaching a cost to materials is what later lets a designer compare options sensibly, and a model built from scrap is the safest place to learn it. Real towers are built by companies that must decide between a lattice tower and a monopole, between galvanised angle and tube, between one tall shared tower and three short ones, and every one of those decisions is partly a cost decision. Nobody can make such comparisons without the reflex of asking what each option costs. Your estimate does not have to be precise. If a sheet of card is a few rand, a pack of straws a few rand more, and glue and string shared across the class, your tower might cost ten or fifteen rand if bought, and simply writing that figure down teaches several things: that materials have a price even when they are given to you, that a lighter design is usually a cheaper one, which links cost to the mass specification, and that waste from poor marking out is money as well as material. The estimate also feeds the fitness-for-purpose considerations, where cost-effectiveness is one of eight headings, and the impact considerations, where reused and recyclable materials score well. In the Practical Assessment Task the cost line is a small part of the marks, but a learner who can say my tower uses two sheets and forty straws, about twelve rand, and the three-legged version would use a third less, is demonstrating exactly the kind of thinking the subject exists to build.
