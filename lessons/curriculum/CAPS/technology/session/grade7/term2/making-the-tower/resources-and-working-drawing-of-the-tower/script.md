# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the making skills that come before any cutting: listing the resources, materials and tools, to be used, and drawing a dimensioned 2D working drawing of one face of the cell phone tower. The anchor is a Technology room in Uitenhage the week before the making lesson, where a learner has an improved oblique sketch, a sheet of A3 squared paper and the materials box open on the bench, and must produce the list and the drawing that will turn the sketch into a tower. The governing idea is that making is planned on paper, that a resource list prevents waste and delay and reveals cost, and that a working drawing of one face, drawn to scale with full dimensions, line conventions and a parts schedule, is the instruction from which a frame is marked out and cut. Producing a complete resource list with reasons and a dimensioned working drawing of one face are examinable making-skill marks.

## Subtopic: Listing the Resources

A resource list has two parts, materials and tools, and each item carries a quantity and a reason. Start with the materials, working through the improved sketch part by part. Legs: four, rolled card tubes 520 millimetres long from A3 card, because the tubing investigation showed tubes carry far more than flat strips; count the sheets needed. Horizontals: for a four-legged tower with six levels, twenty-four straws cut to length, decreasing with the taper. Diagonals: X bracing in the lower three panels on four faces needs twenty-four straws, zigzag in the upper panels another twelve. Internal bracing: two metres of string. Base plate: one piece of double corrugated card 150 by 150. Platform: card 60 by 60 with four straw edges. Webs: sixteen small card triangles from offcuts. Gussets: twenty-four card squares 20 by 20. Canopy, if chosen: green card, about one A4 sheet. Glue: one bottle of PVA. Fixings: eight split pins for the base corners, a roll of masking tape.

Then the tools: a cutting mat, a craft knife, a steel ruler, scissors for straws, a pencil and a set square for marking out, a hole punch for the base corners, a small set of pegs or clips to hold glued joints while they dry, a damp cloth for glue. Each tool has a job; listing them stops the lesson being lost to searching for a punch.

Add up the material. Weigh a sheet of card and a bundle of straws and estimate the total mass of the tower, checking it against the 150 gram specification; if it is over, the design must lose material now, not after it is built. Estimate the cost if the materials were bought, even roughly, because cost is a design consideration. Note which materials are reused or recyclable, because impact on the environment is too.

Finally, check the list against the constraints: only the materials provided, no sharp parts. If the design needs something not provided, such as green card for a canopy, record the substitution or the request. The resource list is also the first draft of the parts schedule that will sit beside the working drawing.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing materials with quantities and reasons, listing tools with their jobs and checking mass and cost.

## Subtopic: The Working Drawing of One Face

A frame tower is drawn for making as one face, because the four faces of a square tapered tower are identical, and one dimensioned face tells the maker everything. Choose the scale: a 520 millimetre tower at 1 to 2 is 260 millimetres on paper, which fits A3 with room for dimensions. Write the scale in the title block at once.

Draw feint construction lines first. A vertical centre line. The base line, 150 millimetres wide in real size, so 75 on paper, centred on the centre line. The platform line, 60 real, 30 on paper, at the top. The two legs joining base corners to platform corners, sloping inward symmetrically. Then the horizontal levels: measure up the centre line and tick each level, for example at 0, 80, 170, 270, 380 and 500 millimetres real, closer together at the base, and draw the horizontals between the legs at each level.

Add the diagonals as they will be built: X in the three lowest panels, single alternating diagonals above. Draw the platform as a small frame at the top. Draw the base plate as a thin rectangle under the legs, wider than the base if it has outriggers. Then darken the outlines: legs, horizontals, diagonals, platform, base plate, all as firm continuous lines. Leave the construction lines feint. Add a dashed line for the internal string bracing, which is hidden behind the face, at the two levels where it runs.

Dimension it in real millimetres, each once. Overall height 520. Base width 150. Platform width 60. Each horizontal level as a running dimension from the base line: 80, 170, 270, 380, 500. The width of the tower at each level if the maker needs to cut horizontals to length, or a note that horizontals are cut to fit between legs. The leg tube diameter, 12. The base plate 150 by 150 by double card. Use thin dimension lines with arrowheads and extension lines, placed outside the drawing where they do not cross the members, and a note where a dimension repeats on all four faces.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps for the one-face working drawing, choosing scale and placing dimensions.

## Subtopic: Parts Schedule and Checking the Drawing

Beside the drawing goes the parts schedule, a table built from the resource list: part, quantity, material, size. Leg, 4, rolled card tube, 520 long by 12 diameter. Horizontal level 1, 4, straw, 150. Horizontal level 2, 4, straw, 132. And so on up the tower, with the diagonal lengths calculated or marked to be cut to fit. Base plate, 1, double corrugated card, 150 by 150. Platform, 1, card and straw, 60 by 60. Web, 16, card triangle, 20 by 20. Gusset, 24, card, 20 by 20. String, 2 metres. Split pin, 8. Letter each part on the drawing and use the same letter in the schedule, so that a maker can find part D on both.

Then check the drawing as a maker would. Can every member be cut to length from the drawing or the schedule? Is the scale written on? Does every dimension give a real size, once, in millimetres? Are the line types correct, dark outlines, feint construction, dashed hidden string? Is there a title block with the tower's name, the scale, your name and the date? Could a classmate who has never seen your sketch build this face, and then three more like it, and join them? If any answer is no, fix the drawing now.

Add a note on the sequence of manufacture in outline, because the working drawing and the sequence are read together: roll and dry the leg tubes first; cut all straws to the schedule; build two faces flat on the drawing, using it as a template with the drawing under a sheet of plastic; join the faces with the remaining horizontals; add the base plate, webs and gussets; thread the internal string; fit the platform; test. Building faces flat on the drawing is a trick worth knowing: the drawing becomes a jig that guarantees every face comes out identical.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a resource list with items but no quantities, so the materials run out in the lesson. Two: dimensioning the oblique sketch instead of the flat face; sizes belong where lines are true length. Three: forgetting that horizontals get shorter up a tapered tower; each level has its own length. Four: no letters linking the drawing to the parts schedule, so the maker cannot tell which strip is which.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts schedule, checking the drawing as a maker and using the drawing as a jig.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a materials box open on the bench and a sheet of A3 — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What We Need, and How Many

Go through your sketch part by part and write down the materials, how many, and why. Four legs, rolled card tubes 520 long, because tubes carry more than flat strips. Twenty-four straws for horizontals, getting shorter up the tower. Thirty-six straws for diagonals, X at the bottom, zigzag above. Two metres of string for inside bracing. A base plate of double card, 150 by 150. A small platform. Sixteen card triangles for webs, twenty-four card squares for gussets. Green card for a canopy if you chose one. Glue, split pins, tape.

Then the tools: cutting mat, craft knife, steel ruler, scissors, pencil and set square, hole punch, pegs to hold joints while glue dries, a damp cloth. Each one has a job, and listing them means no hunting during the lesson.

Weigh a sheet of card and a bundle of straws and estimate your tower's mass. Over 150 grams? Lose material now, on paper. Guess the cost if bought. Note what is recycled.

Check the list against the rules: only the materials given, nothing sharp. If you need something extra, write it down and ask.

Keep the materials box in mind and try the questions for this part: materials, tools, quantities and reasons.

## Subtopic: One Face, Drawn to Build From

The four faces of a square tapered tower are the same, so draw one face flat with all its sizes. Scale 1 to 2: the 520 tower is 260 on the paper. Write the scale on.

Feint lines first: a centre line, a base line 150 real so 75 on paper, a platform line 60 real so 30 on paper, the two legs leaning in to join them. Tick the horizontal levels up the centre line, closer near the bottom: 80, 170, 270, 380, 500. Draw the horizontals across at each tick. Add the X's in the bottom three panels and single diagonals above. The platform at the top, the base plate below.

Darken the legs, horizontals, diagonals, platform and base plate. Leave the feint lines. Draw the inside string as a dashed line, because it is hidden behind this face.

Dimensions in real millimetres, each once: height 520, base 150, platform 60, each level from the base, the leg diameter 12, the base plate size. Thin lines, arrowheads, kept outside the drawing.

Hold the one dimensioned face in mind and try this part's questions: scale, levels and dimensions.

## Subtopic: The Parts Table and the Check

Next to the drawing, a parts table: part, how many, material, size. Leg, 4, card tube, 520 by 12. Horizontal level 1, 4, straw, 150. Level 2, 4, straw, 132. Up to the top. Base plate, 1, double card, 150 by 150. Webs, 16. Gussets, 24. String, 2 metres. Letter each part on the drawing and in the table so they match.

Now check like a maker. Can every piece be cut from the drawing or the table? Is the scale on? Every size real, once, in millimetres? Right line types? Title, name, date? Could a classmate build this face, then three more, and join them? If not, fix it now.

Add the order of work: roll the leg tubes first and let them dry; cut all the straws; build two faces flat on top of the drawing, with plastic over it, so the drawing is a template; join the faces; add the base plate, webs and gussets; thread the string; fit the platform; test. Building on the drawing makes every face identical.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The resource list names every material and tool with quantity and reason, and checks mass, cost and the constraints. The working drawing shows one face to scale with every real dimension once, dark outlines, feint construction and dashed hidden string. The parts table matches the drawing by letters, and the drawing doubles as a jig for building identical faces.

Hold the lettered parts table, and take the final questions of the lesson: the table, the check and the jig.
