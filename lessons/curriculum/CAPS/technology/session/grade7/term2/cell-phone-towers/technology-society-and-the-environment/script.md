# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how designers consider the needs of society and how technology affects society and the environment, using the cell phone tower and the mobile network it belongs to as the case. The anchor is a village in the Eastern Cape near Lusikisiki where a tower went up in 2015: the clinic can now call an ambulance, the school has internet, young people send money home, a shop owner takes card payments, and at the same time an elder complains about the mast over the hill, the diesel generator that runs when Eskom cuts the power, and children spending the evening on screens. The governing idea is that technology is developed in response to the needs of a society, that its effects spread far beyond the users to the whole community and the natural world, that these effects are both positive and negative, and that a responsible designer identifies and weighs them. Explaining how designers consider society's needs, describing positive and negative impacts on society and the environment and evaluating a technology from several points of view are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Designers Find Out What Society Needs

Technology does not appear from nowhere. Every structure and product is a response to a need that someone identified, and the first job of a designer is to find out what that need really is, from the people who have it. For a network planning towers, that means asking where people live and travel, where signals currently fail, what people want to do with a phone, and what they can afford. In South Africa the answers shaped the whole network: far more people had access to a mobile phone than ever had a landline, because towers are cheaper than copper wires to every house, and a prepaid airtime system was invented so that people without bank accounts could use them.

Designers gather needs in several ways. They survey and interview communities. They study how existing technology is used and misused. They look at data, such as where emergency calls fail. They hold public meetings, like the one in the Pinetown hall, where residents say what they want and fear. They consult local leaders, municipalities and health services. The result is a list of needs ranked by importance, which becomes the basis of a design brief, exactly as in the Practical Assessment Task but for a whole region.

Needs differ between communities, and a good designer does not assume. A city suburb may need capacity for thousands of users streaming video; a rural village may need basic coverage for calls and mobile money over a wide area with no mains electricity. The suburb's tower is short and disguised; the village's is tall, bare and runs on solar panels and a battery. Both are correct responses to the needs identified. The indigenous knowledge of a community also counts: where people gather, which hilltops are sacred, which land floods.

The curriculum's point is that technology is shaped by society's needs, and that identifying those needs properly is a technological skill. A tower built where nobody needs it, or in a way a community rejects, is a design failure however well it is engineered.

The questions for this section are with you now: how designers identify needs, why needs differ between communities and the link to the design brief.

## Subtopic: Impact on Society: Good and Bad

Once a technology exists it changes the lives of people who use it and people who do not. The cell phone tower near Lusikisiki shows both sides. Positive impacts: the clinic calls an ambulance in minutes instead of sending someone to walk to the police station; a nurse receives test results by message; the school downloads textbooks; a young man working in Durban sends money home by phone in seconds; the shop owner takes card payments and orders stock; a farmer checks prices before selling cattle; people in a flood get a warning. Mobile networks have been credited with lifting economic activity across Africa because they connect sellers to buyers and families to each other.

Negative impacts exist too, and listing them honestly is part of design. The mast changes the view and is lit at night. Some residents fear health effects from radio waves, and although the scientific evidence shows exposure at ground level near a tower is far below safety limits, the fear is real and must be addressed with information and respect. Children and adults spend hours on screens, with effects on sleep, schoolwork and face-to-face life. Scams and misinformation travel on the same network. Jobs change: public telephones and some postal services shrank, while new jobs in airtime sales, phone repair and tower maintenance grew. Theft of batteries and copper from tower sites has become a crime that leaves whole areas without signal.

Impacts also fall unevenly. A tower serves people within a few kilometres; those just beyond still have no signal and may pay more for everything as a result. People who cannot afford a smartphone or data are left further behind as services move online, which is called the digital divide. A designer who thinks about society asks not only what the technology does but for whom, and who is left out.

Weighing impacts means listing them side by side, positive and negative, for different groups, and then asking whether the negatives can be reduced by design: a disguised tower, a solar power supply instead of a diesel generator, shared towers to cover more area, or community education sessions. The goal is not a technology without downsides, which does not exist, but one whose benefits clearly outweigh its costs and whose costs are reduced as far as possible.

## Subtopic: Impact on the Environment

The natural world pays for every technology, and the designer's job is to know how and to reduce it. A tower's environmental impact starts before it is built: iron ore is mined and smelted into steel, which releases carbon dioxide; concrete for the foundation does the same; the parts are trucked to a hilltop and a road may be cut to get them there, disturbing soil and plants. During its life the tower's equipment uses electricity day and night, and where the grid is unreliable, a diesel generator burns fuel and makes noise and fumes; lights at night attract and disorientate insects and birds, and guy wires can kill birds in flight. At the end of its life the steel is usually recycled, but batteries contain lead or lithium that must be handled carefully, and old electronics are a growing waste problem.

Designers reduce these impacts in ways that mirror the stability and visual pollution solutions. Siting on existing roads and disturbed ground limits new damage. Sharing one tower between networks means fewer towers. Solar panels and batteries replace or reduce diesel. Low-intensity or shielded lights reduce harm to wildlife, and markers on guy wires make them visible to birds. Choosing recyclable materials and planning for the tower's removal at the end of its life closes the loop. These are the same environmental questions you will ask of your own model: how much card and glue did it use, can the materials be reused, and did you waste any?

The wider mobile network has a larger footprint still: the manufacture of millions of handsets, their short lives and the e-waste they become, the energy used by data centres. Against this stands a benefit that is also environmental: a phone call replaces a journey, a message replaces paper, and a sensor on a tower can warn of fire or flood. As with society, the honest method is to list both sides and to design so the balance improves.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: assuming technology is neutral and only its use has impacts; the making and disposing of a tower have impacts of their own. Two: listing only the benefits in an evaluation; examiners expect both sides. Three: treating a community's fears as ignorance to be dismissed; needs and fears are data that shape the design. Four: thinking the environment is somebody else's problem; the material choices in your own model are environmental choices.

The questions for this section are with you now: environmental impacts across a tower's life, design responses and the two-sided method.

## Subtopic: Asking People What They Need

Technology starts with a need, and designers have to find out what that need is from the people who have it. For a phone network that means asking: where do people live, where does the signal fail, what do they want to do with a phone, what can they pay?

In South Africa the answers changed everything. Far more people got a cell phone than ever had a landline, because towers are cheaper than wires to every house. Prepaid airtime was invented so people without bank accounts could join in.

Designers find out by surveys, interviews, public meetings, studying how people already use things and talking to clinics, schools and local leaders. The list of needs becomes the design brief, just like in your project.

Different places need different things. A city suburb needs a short, disguised tower for thousands of video users. A rural village needs a tall bare tower on solar power covering a huge area for calls and money. Both are right.

## Subtopic: Who Wins and Who Loses

When the tower went up near Lusikisiki, good things happened. The clinic can call an ambulance. The school has internet. Money comes home from Durban in seconds. The shop takes cards. The farmer checks cattle prices. People get flood warnings.

But not everything is good. The mast sits in the view and glows at night. Some people worry about health, and even though exposure near a tower is far below safety limits, their worry deserves a proper answer. Children stare at screens all evening. Scams spread. Some old jobs vanished while new ones appeared. Thieves steal tower batteries and leave everyone without signal.

And the good is spread unevenly. People just over the hill still have no signal. People who cannot afford a smartphone fall behind as everything moves online. That gap is called the digital divide.

So designers list the good and the bad for each group, then ask: can design shrink the bad? Disguise the tower, use solar instead of diesel, share towers, teach people about safety.

Hold the two columns, good and bad, in mind and try this part's questions: impacts on different people.

## Subtopic: What Nature Pays

The tower costs the environment before it exists: mining and smelting steel, making concrete, trucking parts up a hill and cutting a road. During its life it uses electricity all day, and when the power fails a diesel generator runs, smoking and roaring. Night lights confuse insects and birds, and birds fly into guy wires. At the end, steel is recycled but batteries and electronics are dangerous waste.

Designers shrink this: use existing roads, share towers, run on solar and batteries, shield the lights, mark the wires for birds, choose recyclable parts and plan to take the tower down one day.

The whole network has a bigger footprint: millions of handsets thrown away, data centres burning power. But a call replaces a journey and a message replaces paper. Honest method: list both sides, then design for a better balance.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Designers find out what society needs by asking the people who have the need, and needs differ between places. A technology brings benefits and harms, spread unevenly, and design can reduce the harms. The environment pays through materials, energy and waste, and good design reduces that too. Always list both sides.
