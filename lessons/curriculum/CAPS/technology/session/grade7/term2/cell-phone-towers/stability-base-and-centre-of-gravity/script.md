# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of the structural elements, reinforcing techniques and design issues of cell phone towers: visual pollution, stability, base size and centre of gravity. The anchor is a public meeting in a church hall in Pinetown where residents object to a proposed tower, and a demonstration table at the front with a broom, a traffic cone, a stack of books and a cardboard tower model that the engineer uses to explain what keeps a tower upright. The governing idea is that a structure is stable when its centre of gravity stays above its base, that stability is increased by widening the base, lowering the centre of gravity or anchoring the structure, and that reinforcing techniques such as gussets, webs and cross-bracing strengthen the joints and panels where forces concentrate. Explaining stability in terms of base and centre of gravity, describing reinforcing techniques and discussing visual pollution as a design issue are examinable.

## Subtopic: Centre of Gravity and the Base

Every object has a centre of gravity, the single point at which its whole weight can be thought to act. For a symmetrical, evenly made object it is at the geometric middle: the centre of a brick, the middle of a broom handle's length if the head and handle balanced, in practice nearer the heavy head. You can find the centre of gravity of a ruler by balancing it on a finger; the balance point is where the weight acts.

An object stands up as long as the vertical line down from its centre of gravity falls inside its base, the area enclosed by the points touching the ground. Tilt a traffic cone and it rocks back, because the line from its low centre of gravity still lands inside the wide base. Tilt it far enough that the line passes outside the base, and it topples. A stack of books leaning like a tower at Pisa stands until the top book's weight shifts the stack's centre of gravity past the edge of the bottom book, and then the stack falls.

Three things decide how stable an object is. The size of the base: a wider base means the centre of gravity must move further before its line leaves the base. The height of the centre of gravity: a lower centre of gravity means a given tilt moves it less sideways, so a cone is stable and a broom balanced on its handle end is not. The weight and its distribution: heavier at the bottom lowers the centre of gravity. A fourth, for structures fixed in place, is anchoring: bolting the feet down or adding guy wires means the structure cannot topple even if the line leaves the base, because the ground holds it.

A cell phone tower is tall, so its centre of gravity is high, and the wind pushes it sideways, which acts like a tilt. Designers answer with a wide base, a taper that keeps most of the steel low, heavy concrete foundations that both lower the centre of gravity of the whole assembly and anchor the feet, and on slender masts, guy wires. A tower with a base too small for its height is a tower waiting to fall.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining centre of gravity, the rule for staying upright and the three ways to improve stability.

## Subtopic: Reinforcing the Frame

Even a well-shaped tower has places where forces concentrate, mainly at the joints where members meet and in panels that carry the greatest bending. Reinforcing techniques add material exactly there. A gusset is a flat plate, usually triangular, fixed across a joint so that the members are connected through a large area of plate rather than at a point, spreading the force and stiffening the corner. Look at the corners of a steel gate or the joint between a tower leg and a horizontal and you will see gusset plates with several bolts in each.

A web is a thin plate or a lattice filling the space between two members so that they act together as one deeper, stiffer piece. A steel I-beam is two flat flanges joined by a vertical web; a triangular web in the corner of a frame does the same job as a gusset but reaches further along the members. In towers, webs often appear as small triangular plates bracing the junction of diagonals.

Cross-bracing is the pair of diagonals forming an X across a panel, which we met as triangulation. Internal cross-bracing runs through the inside of the tower from one face to the opposite face, tying the whole section together so that the tower cannot twist. A tower that is braced on its faces but not internally can twist about its own axis in gusty wind, which loosens bolts and misaligns antennas. Horizontal diaphragms, flat triangulated frames at intervals up the height, do the same job.

The principle behind all three is to add stiffness where it is needed and nowhere else. Reinforcing everywhere would make the tower heavy and expensive; reinforcing at joints, in the lowest panels where bending is greatest and internally against twist gives strength for the least material. The same ideas appear in the model tower you will design: card gussets at the corners, a web of card across a weak panel and a diagonal running through the inside.

The questions for this section are with you now: gussets, webs and cross-bracing, where each is used and why reinforcement is targeted.

## Subtopic: Visual Pollution and the Design Issue

The residents in the Pinetown church hall are not worried about triangulation. Their objection is that a thirty metre steel lattice will dominate the view from their homes, that it will look industrial in a residential area, and that it will be lit at night. This is visual pollution: a structure that spoils the appearance of a place. It is a real design issue, because a tower that is refused planning permission connects nobody, and because the people who live under a structure have a right to a say in how it looks.

Designers have several answers. Siting: placing the tower behind trees, on an existing industrial site or on the roof of a tall building where it is less obtrusive. Form: using a slender monopole instead of a lattice where the budget allows. Disguise: towers built to look like pine trees, palm trees, church steeples, minarets, flagpoles or water towers are now common; the antennas sit inside fibreglass branches or behind panels that pass radio signals. Sharing: several phone networks placing their antennas on one tower instead of building three. Colour: painting the structure grey-green to fade against the hills or sky.

Each answer involves a trade-off with the other fitness-for-purpose considerations. A tree disguise costs more and the fibreglass branches add wind load. Siting behind trees may put the tower further from the people it serves. Sharing a tower means a heavier, taller structure. The engineer's job at the meeting is to explain these trades honestly and to show the residents the options, which is the communicate step of the design process applied to a whole community.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a heavy tower is automatically stable; what matters is where the centre of gravity is relative to the base. Two: adding reinforcement everywhere; it is targeted at joints and the most loaded panels. Three: dismissing visual pollution as not a technical problem; it decides whether the tower is built at all. Four: believing a disguised tower has no costs; it adds expense and wind load.

The questions for this section are with you now: what visual pollution is, the design answers to it and the trade-offs each involves.

## Subtopic: Why Things Topple

Every object has a balance point, its centre of gravity, where all its weight seems to act. Balance a ruler on your finger and you have found it.

A thing stays standing as long as a straight line down from that balance point lands inside its base, the patch where it touches the ground. Tilt a traffic cone and it rocks back, because the line still lands inside its wide bottom. Tilt it too far and the line goes outside, and over it goes. A leaning stack of books falls the moment its balance point passes the edge of the bottom book.

Three ways to make something harder to topple: give it a wider base, make its balance point lower by putting the weight at the bottom, or fix it to the ground. A broom standing on its bristles is stable; standing on the end of its handle it is not.

A tower is tall, so its balance point is high, and wind pushes it like a tilt. So it gets a wide base, most of its steel low down, a heavy concrete foot that also bolts it down, and on thin masts, guy wires.

Keep the cone and the broom in mind and try the questions for this part: the balance point, the base and the three ways to be stable.

## Subtopic: Strengthening the Weak Spots

Even a good tower has weak spots: the corners where bars meet, and the lowest panels where the wind bends it most. Designers add strength just there.

A gusset is a flat triangular plate bolted across a corner, so the bars join through a big plate instead of at one point. Look at a steel gate's corners.

A web is a thin plate or lattice filling the space between two bars so they act as one deeper, stiffer piece, like the upright part of an I-beam.

Cross-bracing is the X of two diagonals across a panel. Internal cross-bracing runs through the middle of the tower so it cannot twist in gusts.

Reinforce only where needed. Everywhere would be heavy and costly. In your card model: card gussets at the corners, a web across a weak panel, a diagonal through the inside.

Hold the gusset, the web and the X in mind and try this part's questions: what each one does and where it goes.

## Subtopic: Nobody Wants to Look at It

At the public meeting the neighbours are not asking about triangles. They do not want a thirty metre steel lattice over their houses. That is visual pollution, and it is a real design problem, because a tower that is refused connects nobody.

Answers: put it somewhere less obvious, behind trees or on an industrial site. Use a slim single pole. Disguise it as a pine tree, a palm, a church steeple or a flagpole, with the antennas hidden inside. Let several phone companies share one tower. Paint it to fade into the sky.

Every answer costs something. A tree disguise is expensive and catches more wind. A hidden site may be further from the people who need the signal. A shared tower is bigger. The engineer's job is to explain these trades honestly.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A structure stands while the line from its centre of gravity falls inside its base; widen the base, lower the centre of gravity or anchor it to make it stable. Gussets, webs and cross-bracing reinforce joints and weak panels. Visual pollution is a design issue answered by siting, form, disguise, sharing and colour, each with a trade-off.
