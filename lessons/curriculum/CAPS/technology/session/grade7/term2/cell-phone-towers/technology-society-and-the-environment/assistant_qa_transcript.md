### subtopic_1 — How Designers Find Out What Society Needs
**Assistant:** Are cell phone towers actually dangerous to health? People in my area say they cause cancer.

**Tutor:** The scientific evidence, gathered over more than twenty years and reviewed by the World Health Organization and national health bodies including South Africa's, does not show that living near a cell phone tower causes cancer or other illness. A tower's antennas send out radio waves, which are a form of non-ionising radiation: unlike X-rays or the ultraviolet in sunlight, they do not carry enough energy to break chemical bonds in cells, which is how radiation causes cancer. The antennas also point their beams outwards and slightly downwards towards the horizon, so the exposure directly under a tower is usually lower than a few hundred metres away, and at ground level it is typically hundreds or thousands of times below the international safety limits, which themselves include a large margin. Your own handset, held against your head, exposes you to far more radio energy than any tower, and even that has not been shown to cause harm, although researchers continue to study heavy long-term use. None of this means the fear should be brushed aside. The curriculum asks designers to consider the needs and worries of society, and a worried community is a design input. The right response is honest information from independent sources, measurements that residents can see, siting that keeps antennas away from classrooms and bedrooms where it costs nothing to do so, and respect for the fact that people have the right to ask. Dismissing a community's concern as ignorance usually results in the tower being refused, which helps nobody.

### subtopic_2 — Impact on Society: Good and Bad
**Assistant:** If the benefits of the network are so large, why should a designer worry about one elder who dislikes the view?

**Tutor:** Because the elder is a member of the society the technology is supposed to serve, and because dismissing minority objections has a long history of producing bad technology and bitter communities. The benefits of connectivity are real and large, and in the end they usually justify building the tower. But a tower that could have been placed behind the ridge, painted to fade into the hill or disguised at modest cost, and was instead put in the most visible spot because nobody asked, is a worse design than it needed to be. The elder's objection points to a cost that was avoidable, and good design is partly the craft of avoiding avoidable costs. There is also a question of fairness. The benefits of the tower flow to many people across the valley, but the view belongs to the few households below it, so one small group carries a cost for everyone else's gain. Societies have learned to handle this through consultation, compensation and compromise, which is why planning law requires public notice and a chance to object before a tower is approved. Finally, the elder may know things the engineer does not: where the land floods, which path cattle use, which hill is sacred. Listening to the objection is how the designer finds out. The curriculum's phrase is that designers consider the needs of society, and society includes the people who are not cheering.

### subtopic_3 — Impact on the Environment
**Assistant:** How can I evaluate the impact of a technology in an exam if I do not know all the facts about it?

**Tutor:** You are not expected to know every fact; you are expected to show a method, and the method works on any technology with a little general knowledge. Start by naming the need the technology answers and who has it. Then draw two columns, positive and negative, and fill each with impacts on society and on the environment, aiming for three or four points in each column. Think across the whole life of the technology: the materials and energy used to make it, what happens while it is used, and what happens when it is thrown away. Think across different groups: users, non-users nearby, people far away, future generations. Then add the sentence that examiners most want to see: a judgement about whether the benefits outweigh the costs, with a reason, and one or two design changes that would reduce the negatives. For a cell phone tower you can write about ambulances, mobile money and internet on one side and visual pollution, diesel, e-waste and the digital divide on the other, and propose solar power, shared and disguised towers and community information sessions. For a technology you have never studied, say a new type of taxi or a plastic water bottle, the same columns and the same questions apply, and sensible general knowledge fills them in. Markers reward balance, structure and a reasoned judgement far more than a long list of memorised facts, because that is what thinking like a designer looks like.
