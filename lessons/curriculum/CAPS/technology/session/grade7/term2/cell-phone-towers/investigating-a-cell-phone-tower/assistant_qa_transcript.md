### subtopic_1 — Reading a Cell Phone Tower
**Assistant:** If a triangle is so strong, why don't engineers just make the whole tower one giant triangle instead of lots of small ones?

**Tutor:** In a sense they do: the taper of a tower makes each face one tall triangle, and that is part of its stiffness. But a single giant triangle of three long members would fail for a different reason, which is buckling. A long slender steel member under compression does not fail by being crushed; it fails by bowing out sideways and collapsing, the way a plastic ruler pressed from both ends suddenly snaps into a curve. The longer and thinner a member is, the smaller the load that makes it buckle, so a leg thirty metres long with nothing holding it along its length would buckle under a modest push. The horizontals and diagonals solve this. Every few metres they hold the leg in position, so that instead of one thirty metre column the leg behaves like ten three metre columns stacked, each far too short to buckle under the loads it sees. The small triangles therefore do two jobs at once: they stop the panels from leaning, and they brace the legs against buckling. This is also why the members of a tower can be thin and the whole thing light. If you build a model tower from drinking straws, you will see this clearly: a long single straw bows under a small push, but the same straw braced at three points along its length carries far more. Engineers describe this as reducing the effective length of the member, and it is as important to tower design as triangulation itself.

### subtopic_2 — Why Triangles
**Assistant:** Why are some cell phone masts a single thin pole with no lattice at all? Do they not need triangles?

**Tutor:** Monopoles, the single tubular masts you see beside highways and in suburbs, get their stiffness a different way, and the choice between a lattice tower and a monopole is a good example of designers weighing the fitness-for-purpose considerations. A monopole is a tapered steel tube, thick-walled and often more than a metre across at the base, and it resists wind the way a solid flagpole does: by the sheer bending strength of a large hollow tube, which is a very efficient shape against bending, as the stiffening-by-tubing investigation later this term will show. It needs no diagonals because there are no panels to lean; it is one continuous member. The cost is material. A monopole uses far more steel than a lattice tower of the same height, needs a heavier foundation and is harder to transport, so it is more expensive. Its advantages are a small footprint, quick erection, a cleaner appearance that planners and neighbours prefer, and fewer places for birds to perch and people to climb. In a city, where land is costly and visual pollution is a real objection, those advantages win and monopoles are common. In open country or for very tall structures, the lattice tower's lightness and lower cost win. Some masts are a hybrid: a monopole for the lower section and a short lattice at the top, or a monopole disguised as a pine tree. The lesson is that triangulation is one way to make a tall structure rigid, not the only way, and that the right choice depends on the site.

### subtopic_3 — Pylons, Windmills and Headgear
**Assistant:** Why does the curriculum include old mine headgear in a lesson about cell phone towers? Mines seem to have nothing to do with phones.

**Tutor:** Because headgear is the most dramatic example in South Africa of the same engineering problem, a tall frame that must carry a load against pull and wind, and because it connects the technology to the country's own history. Johannesburg exists because of gold, and the headgears that still stand along the Reef were, for a century, the tallest structures most people saw. They show triangulation on a heroic scale: every panel braced, the back legs sloped to take the enormous pull of the winding cable, the whole frame bolted to deep concrete foundations. Looking at one teaches the same lessons as a cell phone tower but with the loads made visible, since you can see the cable, the wheel and the direction of the pull. The case study also fits the curriculum's wider aim, which is to show that technology develops in response to the needs of a society at a particular time. In the 1890s the need was to lift ore and men from deep shafts, and the answer was headgear; in the 2000s the need was to connect millions of people without copper wires, and the answer was the cell phone tower. The engineering principles did not change; the need did. Noticing that is part of understanding how technology and society shape each other, which is the subject of a later session this term.
