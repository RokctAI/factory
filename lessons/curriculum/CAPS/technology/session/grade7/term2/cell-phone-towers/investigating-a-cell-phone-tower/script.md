# Part 1 — Expert

This session is an investigation of the cell phone tower as a frame structure and a case study of triangulation in towers, including electricity pylons, wind pump windmills and mine headgear. The anchor is a thirty metre lattice tower on the edge of Kagiso on the West Rand, viewed from its fenced base, with an Eskom pylon marching past in the distance, a rusting Climax wind pump on a nearby smallholding and the headgear of a disused gold mine on the skyline. The governing idea is that a tall frame survives wind and its own weight only if its rectangles are broken into triangles, because a triangle is the one shape that cannot distort without a member changing length, and that every successful tower in South Africa's landscape shows this principle in a slightly different form. Describing the structural members of a tower, explaining triangulation and recognising it in pylons, windmills and headgear are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading a Cell Phone Tower

Start at the base. The tower stands on a concrete foundation, a solid structure buried in the ground, with steel bolts rising out of it to hold the feet of the legs. The legs are the main vertical or slightly sloping members, usually three or four of them, made of steel angle or tube, and they carry the weight of the tower and the antennas down to the foundation. Because wind pushes the tower sideways, the legs on the windward side are pulled upward and those on the leeward side are pushed down, so the bolts must hold the feet down as well as up.

Between the legs run horizontal members, called horizontals or bracing, which hold the legs apart at a fixed distance and stop them bowing inward or outward. Joining the horizontals and legs are the diagonal members, which run from the top of one horizontal to the bottom of the next on the neighbouring leg, forming a zigzag or a series of crosses up each face. The diagonals are the key: they divide each rectangular panel between two legs into triangles.

At the top sits the head frame carrying the antennas: flat panel antennas for the phone signals pointing in three directions, and round dish antennas that link the tower to the rest of the network. Cables run down inside the tower to equipment cabinets at the base. The tower is wider at the bottom than at the top, because the bending caused by wind is greatest at the base, and a wide base also lowers the risk of the whole thing tipping. Many taller masts are thinner still and are held up by guy wires, steel cables anchored to the ground, which turn an unstable pole into a stable braced structure.

Make a labelled sketch as part of the investigation: foundation, legs, horizontals, diagonals, head frame, antennas, cabinets, and if present the guys. Count the panels and note which members are thick and which are thin. This record becomes evidence in your own tower design.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming the members of a tower, what each one does and why the base is wide.

## Subtopic: Why Triangles

Imagine a single panel of a tower with the diagonals removed: two legs and two horizontals forming a rectangle, joined at the corners. Push it sideways, as the wind does, and the rectangle leans into a parallelogram, because each joint can rotate and no member has to stretch or shorten for the shape to change. A tower built of such panels would sway, lean further and collapse in the first strong wind. It is said that a four-sided frame is not a structure but a mechanism.

Now add one diagonal from corner to corner. The rectangle becomes two triangles. To lean, the panel would now have to stretch the diagonal or squash it, and steel resists both enormously. The shape is locked. This is triangulation: dividing a frame into triangles so that no part can change shape without a member changing length. Because the diagonal is in tension when the wind blows from one side and in compression from the other, many towers use two crossing diagonals so that one is always being pulled, allowing both to be thin.

Triangulation is why the tower can be light. The members are slender steel sections, mostly air between them, yet the frame is as rigid as a solid column many times heavier. The principle works at every scale: a wire coat hanger, a roof truss, the gusset in the corner of a gate, the diagonal brace on a bookshelf and the cross-bracing of a skyscraper all do the same thing. Triangulation also appears in nature, in the branching of a tree's forks and in the bony struts inside a bird's wing bone.

A second use of triangles is in the tower's taper. Because the legs slope inward towards the top, each face is itself a long tall triangle, which stiffens the whole tower against bending in the same way that a tent with sloping poles stands more firmly than one with vertical poles.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a rectangle is a mechanism, how a diagonal locks it and why crossing diagonals are used.

## Subtopic: Pylons, Windmills and Headgear

Electricity pylons are the most common triangulated towers in South Africa, carrying high-voltage lines across hundreds of kilometres of veld. A pylon has four sloping legs, horizontal and diagonal bracing in every panel, and cross-arms at the top from which the conductors hang on insulators. The load is mostly the sideways pull of the heavy cables and the wind on them, so pylons at corners of the line, where the pull is unbalanced, are built heavier and often held by guy wires. Look closely and every face is triangles from the ground to the arms.

The farm windmill, properly a wind pump, is a smaller cousin that has stood on South African farms since the 1870s. A four-legged lattice tower, perhaps ten metres high, carries a wheel of blades and a tail vane at the top and drives a pump rod down to a borehole. The tower is triangulated in every panel, tapers strongly from base to top and is bolted to concrete footings, because the spinning wheel in a Karoo gale puts a vibrating, changing load on it. The wind pump is a frame supporting a working machine, exactly as a cell phone tower supports antennas.

Mine headgear is the tall steel frame above a mine shaft that carries the winding wheels, called sheaves, over which the cables run that lift the cage and the ore from kilometres underground. Headgear takes enormous loads, the weight of the cage and its cargo plus the pull of the winding engine, and it is built as a massive triangulated frame, often with a sloping back leg that leans towards the winding house to resist the pull of the cable. The headgears of Johannesburg's old mines are so much a part of the city's identity that they appear on its emblems.

The case study teaches one lesson three times: a tall frame carrying a load against wind and pull is triangulated, tapered and anchored. The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the horizontals alone stiffen a tower; without diagonals the panels still lean. Two: thinking the diagonals are decoration; they are the members that lock the shape. Three: believing a tower must be heavy to be strong; triangulation gives rigidity with little material. Four: assuming the biggest load on a tower is the antennas' weight; it is the wind.

The questions for this section are with you now: how pylons, wind pumps and headgear use triangulation and the common lesson.

## Subtopic: The Parts of a Tower

Stand at the fence and look up. At the bottom, a concrete block in the ground with bolts holding the feet: the foundation. Rising from it, three or four long steel members: the legs. They carry the weight down to the ground, and when the wind blows, one side of the legs is pulled up while the other is pushed down, so the bolts matter.

Between the legs, flat bars go across: the horizontals. They keep the legs the right distance apart. Then slanting bars zigzag up each face: the diagonals. They are the important ones, and the next part says why.

At the top sit the antennas, flat panels facing three ways and round dishes talking to other towers. Cables run down to cabinets at the bottom. The tower is wide at the base and narrow at the top, because wind bends it most at the bottom and a wide base is hard to tip over. Tall thin masts are held up by guy wires, steel cables pegged to the ground.

## Subtopic: The Triangle Trick

Take one panel of the tower: two legs, two horizontals, a rectangle. Push it sideways like the wind does. It leans over into a diamond shape, because the corners can turn and no bar has to change length. A tower made only of rectangles would sway and fall.

Now add one slanting bar from corner to corner. The rectangle is now two triangles. To lean, the panel would have to stretch or squash that slanting bar, and steel will not let it. Locked. That is triangulation.

Many towers put two slanting bars in a cross, so whichever way the wind blows, one of them is being pulled, and pulled bars can be thin. That is why a tower can be mostly air and still stand rigid in a storm. Coat hangers, roof trusses, gate braces and skyscrapers use the same trick. Even the sloping legs make each face a big triangle, which stiffens it more.

Hold the leaning rectangle in mind and try this part's questions: why it leans, what the diagonal does and why crosses are used.

## Subtopic: Three Cousins of the Tower

Electricity pylons march across the veld carrying power lines. Four sloping legs, triangles in every panel, arms at the top for the cables. The big load is the sideways pull of the heavy cables and the wind on them.

Farm windmills, really wind pumps, have stood on farms since the 1870s. A small four-legged lattice tower with triangles everywhere, a wheel on top, a pump rod down to the borehole. The spinning wheel in a gale shakes it, so it is bolted to concrete feet.

Mine headgear stands over a mine shaft, holding the big wheels that lift the cage from kilometres down. Huge loads, so a huge triangulated frame, often with a back leg sloping towards the winding house to resist the cable's pull. Johannesburg's old headgears are on the city's emblems.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A tower has a foundation, legs, horizontals, diagonals and a head carrying antennas, wide at the base and narrow at the top. Rectangles lean; diagonals make triangles that lock the shape, so the tower is light and rigid. Pylons, wind pumps and headgear are all tall triangulated frames carrying a load against wind and pull.
