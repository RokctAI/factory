### subtopic_1 — Evaluating Fairly Against the Brief
**Assistant:** What if the group is wrong about my design and the thing they criticised actually works?

**Tutor:** Then you keep your feature, record the comment and your reason for rejecting it, and you have done exactly what the process asks. Group evaluation is not a vote that binds you; it is a way of collecting more eyes and more arguments than you have alone. Sometimes those extra eyes are mistaken, because they have not seen the investigation result you are relying on or have misread the sketch. The right response is not to defend in the moment, which tends to turn into an argument nobody learns from, but to write the comment down, go back to your evidence and decide. If your stiffening results show that a folded channel leg is stiffer than the group assumed, you can note next to the comment: rejected, folded channel carried 40 coins in the stiffening test, see results table. That single line turns a disagreement into evidence, and examiners reward it as highly as an accepted comment, because it shows you evaluated the criticism rather than either swallowing or ignoring it. Two cautions. First, be honest with yourself about whether you are rejecting the comment because the evidence supports you or because you are attached to the idea; if you cannot point to a result, build a quick mock-up and test it before deciding. Second, notice if several people make the same comment, because that usually means the sketch is unclear even if the design is sound, and the fix is better annotation. Designers in professional practice keep exactly this kind of log, called a design review record, with every comment and its disposition, and it protects both the design and the designer.

### subtopic_2 — Giving and Receiving Criticism
**Assistant:** Why do we evaluate sketches before building? Surely we can only know if a tower works by making it.

**Tutor:** You can only be certain by testing the built model, which is why the make and evaluate steps follow, but evaluating sketches first catches most of the problems while they are still free to fix. A line on paper costs nothing to move; a glued card leg costs the material, the glue, the drying time and a lesson. If the group spots on the sketch that the base is 170 millimetres wide against a footprint limit of 150, the fix is a redrawn line. If nobody looks until the tower is built, the fix is a rebuilt base. Engineers put it as a rule of thumb: a mistake found at the drawing stage costs one unit, at the building stage ten, and after the structure is in use a hundred. Design reviews on paper are how every large project works, from bridges to aircraft, with experienced people examining drawings for weaknesses before any steel is cut. Your group evaluation is the school-sized version. It also does something a built model cannot: it lets you compare eight different approaches side by side in half an hour, which no class could do by building all eight. There are things paper cannot tell you, such as whether a particular glue joint will hold, and those honest question marks in the table are precisely the items you will test on the model. The goal is to arrive at making with a design that has already survived everyone's best objections, so that the building lessons are spent on construction rather than on rescuing a flawed idea.

### subtopic_3 — Adapting and Improving Your Design
**Assistant:** How is it fair that I get marks for evaluating other people's work, which has nothing to do with my own tower?

**Tutor:** Because evaluation is a skill the subject is teaching, as valuable as sketching or gluing, and the fairest way to assess it is to watch you do it. Every designer spends a large part of their working life assessing other people's ideas: a senior engineer checks a junior's drawings, a committee reviews a proposal, a team decides between options. Being able to look at a design, judge it against a brief with reasons and explain the judgement constructively is what makes a designer useful to others, and it is tested in the curriculum's evaluation outcomes alongside the design outcomes. There is also a direct benefit to your own tower that makes the time well spent. When you explain why a classmate's single straw diagonal will buckle in compression, you understand your own bracing better; when you notice that another learner's weighted base solves a stability problem elegantly, you have found an idea you can borrow with acknowledgement. People who evaluate carefully produce better designs, which is why the group session comes before the individual improvement. Finally, fairness in the other direction: your own sketch gets three thoughtful critics instead of none, which is a gift most designers would pay for. The marks for participation and reasoning simply recognise that giving that gift well is work, and that doing it badly, by flattering or by sneering, helps nobody. Take it as seriously as you take your own drawing and both will improve.
