# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers group evaluation of individual design sketches and the individual adaptation and improvement that follows. The anchor is a group of four learners around a desk in Mahikeng, each with two annotated oblique sketches of a cell phone tower, a copy of the shared brief and specifications, and an evaluation table; the group works through all eight ideas, then each learner returns to their own page to revise. The governing idea is that evaluation against a written brief turns opinion into evidence, that advantages and disadvantages must be stated with reasons drawn from the investigations, that criticism is of the design and not the designer, and that improvement means a recorded change with a reason, not a fresh start. Evaluating designs against specifications, giving and receiving constructive criticism, and adapting a design idea with justified changes are examinable and form part of the design process marks.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Fairly Against the Brief

Evaluation in Technology always begins with the brief. The group places the shared specifications in the first column of a table and the ideas across the top, one column per sketch. For each idea and each specification the group decides whether the design as drawn is likely to meet it, with a tick, a cross or a question mark, and writes a short reason in the language of the term. Height 500 millimetres: tick, drawn at 520. Footprint 150 by 150: question mark, base drawn 170 wide. Stability: tick, strongly tapered with a wide base. Rigidity: cross, single diagonals in the upper panels and no internal bracing, so twist is likely. Mass under 150 grams: question mark, X bracing in every panel may be heavy. Visual impact: tick, tree canopy, but note the extra top weight.

The reasons are what make the evaluation fair. A cross that says I do not like it teaches nothing; a cross that says the base is narrow for the height so it may topple under a push can be checked against the stability investigation and fixed. The group should be able to point to a lesson, an investigation or a specification for every judgement.

Advantages and disadvantages are then summarised in two short lists under each idea. Advantages of the four-legged X lattice: stable, every face easily braced, simple joints. Disadvantages: the most material, the heaviest, square section needs internal bracing against twist, no softening feature. Advantages of the three-legged canopy tower: light, rigid in plan, antennas hidden, fits the farming landscape in the brief. Disadvantages: top-heavy, less stable against a push along one face, canopy catches wind, harder to glue three-way joints. Each list should have at least two items on each side, because every design has both.

The group then chooses, for each learner, which of their two ideas is the stronger starting point, with reasons, and notes any feature from the other idea worth carrying across. The group does not design for the learner; it evaluates and advises, and the learner keeps ownership of the decision.

The questions for this section are with you now: building the evaluation table, writing reasons, and listing advantages and disadvantages.

## Subtopic: Giving and Receiving Criticism

Evaluation in a group only works if people can speak honestly and hear honestly. A few rules make that possible. Criticise the design, never the designer: the base is narrow, not you made the base too narrow. Start with what works, because every sketch has strengths and the designer needs to know which features to keep. Be specific and tie every comment to the brief or to an investigation: this diagonal will be in compression when pushed from the left and a straw will buckle, so consider an X. Suggest rather than instruct: have you thought about, rather than you must. Give everyone the same time and the same seriousness, whether their drawing is neat or scrappy.

Receiving criticism is a skill too. Listen to the whole comment before answering. Write it down, even if you disagree, because you will think about it more calmly later. Ask a question to understand it rather than defending: do you mean the whole base or just the front edge? Thank the person. Decide afterwards, alone, what to change; you are not obliged to accept every comment, but you are obliged to consider each one and to record your reason if you reject it. A designer who cannot be criticised stops improving, and a designer who accepts every comment without judgement ends up with nobody's design.

The teacher watches the process and the records as much as the designs. In the Practical Assessment Task, marks are available for taking part in evaluation, for the quality of the reasons given and for the evidence that comments were considered. A sketch with a column of neat notes from the group, some ticked as accepted and some marked rejected with a reason, is strong evidence that the design process was followed.

Groups also learn from each other's ideas. A learner who sees a weighted base plate on a classmate's sketch and borrows it, with acknowledgement, is doing what designers everywhere do. The curriculum encourages this provided the borrowing is recorded and the final design remains the learner's own synthesis.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rules for giving criticism, the skills of receiving it and what the teacher assesses.

## Subtopic: Adapting and Improving Your Design

After the group session each learner returns to their own sketches with the table and the comments. Improvement is a set of specific changes, each recorded with its reason. Work through the comments one at a time. Base too wide for the footprint: redraw the base at 150 and slightly increase the taper; reason, footprint specification. Upper panels have single diagonals and no internal bracing: add X bracing to the lower three panels, keep zigzag above, add string bracing through the inside at two levels; reason, rigidity specification and the twist finding from the triangulation investigation. Canopy adds top weight: thicken the base plate to double card and add webs at all four base corners; reason, stability specification and the centre-of-gravity case study.

Draw the improved design as a third sketch beside the first two, or annotate the chosen sketch clearly in a different pencil pressure with the changes labelled as revisions. Do not erase the original; the improvement is only visible if the before and the after both exist. Add a short paragraph: which idea was chosen and why, which comments were accepted and how, which were rejected and why, and which features were borrowed from the other idea or from a classmate. This paragraph is the record of the design evolving, and it is exactly what examiners want to read.

Check the improved design against the full specification list once more, in a fresh column of the table. The aim is that every row is now a tick or an honest question mark that only testing can resolve. Where a specification cannot be met without breaking a constraint, say so and propose a revision of the specification with a reason; that is better than pretending.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: evaluating by taste rather than against the brief. Two: criticising the person instead of the design, or praising everything to be kind. Three: starting a completely new design after criticism instead of adapting the existing one; the process values improvement, not abandonment. Four: erasing the original so that the improvement cannot be seen.

The questions for this section are with you now: turning comments into recorded changes, re-checking against the brief and keeping the before and after.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, four learners around a desk with eight sketches and one brief — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Judging Against the Brief, Not Against Taste

Put the specifications down the side of a table and the sketches across the top. For each box, decide: will this design meet this specification? Tick, cross or question mark, and a short reason in the words of the term. Tall enough? Tick, 520. Base inside 150? Question mark, drawn 170. Stable? Tick, wide base. Rigid? Cross, single thin diagonals up top and nothing inside, so it will twist. Light enough? Question mark. Spares the view? Tick, tree canopy, but heavy on top.

The reason is what makes it fair. I do not like it helps nobody. The base is narrow for the height so it may topple points to an investigation and can be fixed.

Then two short lists under each sketch: advantages and disadvantages, at least two of each. Four-legged X lattice: stable, easy to brace, simple joints; but heavy, uses the most card, needs inside bracing, nothing to soften the look. Three-legged canopy: light, already stiff in plan, hidden antennas, fits the farms; but top-heavy, weaker along one face, canopy catches wind, tricky three-way joints.

The group says which of each person's two ideas is the stronger start and why. The owner decides.

Keep the tick-cross table in mind and try the questions for this part: judging against the brief with reasons.

## Subtopic: How to Say It and How to Hear It

Giving criticism: talk about the design, not the person. The base is narrow, not you made it wrong. Say what works first. Be specific and point to the brief or an investigation. Suggest, do not order. Give everyone equal time.

Receiving criticism: listen to the end. Write it down even if you disagree. Ask a question to understand it. Say thank you. Decide later, on your own, what to change. You do not have to accept everything, but you must think about everything and give a reason if you reject something.

The teacher marks the process too: did you take part, were your reasons good, is there evidence you considered the comments? A sketch with the group's notes down the side, some ticked, some crossed out with a reason, is strong evidence.

Borrowing a good idea from a classmate is fine, as long as you say where it came from and the final design is still your own mix.

Hold the two lists of rules in mind and try this part's questions: giving and receiving criticism.

## Subtopic: Making It Better, Not Starting Over

Back at your own desk, go through the comments one at a time and make a change for each, with a reason. Base too wide: redraw at 150, taper a bit more; reason, footprint. Weak at the top and twists: X bracing in the bottom three panels, zigzag above, string through the inside at two levels; reason, rigidity and the twist test. Canopy heavy: double card base plate and webs at all four corners; reason, stability.

Draw the improved tower as a third sketch, or mark the changes clearly on the chosen one. Never rub out the original; the improvement only shows if before and after both exist. Write a short paragraph: which idea you chose and why, which comments you took and how, which you rejected and why, what you borrowed.

Check the improved design against every specification again in a new column. If one cannot be met without breaking a constraint, say so and suggest a revised specification with a reason.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Evaluate against the brief with a tick-cross table and reasons from the term's investigations. List advantages and disadvantages for every idea. Criticise the design, not the designer; listen, write it down, decide later. Improve by recorded changes with reasons, keep the original visible, and re-check against the brief.

Hold the before-and-after sketches, and take the final questions of the lesson: adapting and improving.
