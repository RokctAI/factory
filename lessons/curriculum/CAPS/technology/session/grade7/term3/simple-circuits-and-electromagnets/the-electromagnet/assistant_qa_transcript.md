### subtopic_1 — A Wire Becomes a Magnet
**Assistant:** Why does the nail have to be iron? Would a steel bolt from the hardware shop work as well?

**Tutor:** An ordinary steel bolt will work, often very well, because the mild steel that most bolts, nails and screws are made of is mostly iron with a little carbon and is magnetically soft enough for the job. The core has two tasks. While current flows it must magnetise strongly, which any ferrous material does, and when the current stops it must demagnetise almost completely, so that the clips fall. Soft iron does this best; mild steel does it well enough that you will see only a clip or two linger; hardened steel, such as a masonry nail, a drill bit or a spring, is the one to avoid, because its domains lock in place and it becomes a permanent magnet that keeps holding after the switch opens. Test a candidate core before you wind it: hold it against a permanent magnet for a few seconds, pull it away and see whether it will still pick up a clip on its own. If it does, it is too hard. The size matters too. A longer, thicker core gives more iron to magnetise and lets you wind more turns, but a very long core puts the pole far from the coil and wastes some field. For a model crane a round 100 to 150 millimetre mild steel nail or a soft iron bolt of about 8 millimetres diameter is ideal, and a stainless steel bolt should be avoided because many stainless grades are non-magnetic, as the metals investigation showed. Whatever you choose, the test is the same as everywhere this term: build it, count the clips, and write it down.

### subtopic_2 — Building and Testing the Electromagnet
**Assistant:** Why does the wire have to be insulated? Surely bare copper wire would conduct better?

**Tutor:** Bare wire would conduct just as well, but it would not make an electromagnet. The field of a solenoid comes from the current going round and round the core, turn after turn, with every turn adding to the total. If the wire were bare, each turn would touch the turn beside it and the nail itself, and the current would take the shortest path, straight along the outside of the coil from one end to the other, instead of circling. You would have a short circuit, a hot wire, a flat cell and almost no magnetism. The thin plastic or enamel coating on the wire forces the current to follow the full spiral path, so every turn counts. This is why electromagnet wire is sold as enamelled copper wire, with a coating so thin that hundreds of turns fit on a small core, and why the only places you strip it are the two ends that join the circuit. It also explains a fault that catches many groups: a coil wound with wire whose insulation has been scraped or cracked in a few places, perhaps by pulling it tight over the sharp head of the nail, will leak current between turns and be mysteriously weak. If your electromagnet lifts far fewer clips than your neighbour's with the same turns and cell, look for bare patches, and pad the nail head with a turn of tape before winding.

### subtopic_3 — Why the Crane Wants an Electromagnet
**Assistant:** Could I use the electromagnet to sort the scrap even faster by making it very strong, say with a car battery?

**Tutor:** You could make it very strong, and you would very quickly learn why real lifting magnets are built the way they are. A car battery pushes with twelve volts and can deliver hundreds of amps into a short circuit, and a coil of thin wire has very little resistance, so the current would be enormous, the wire would get red hot within seconds, the insulation would melt and the battery could even be damaged or spit acid. Electromagnet design is a balance. Strength rises with turns and with current, but current through thin wire makes heat, and heat is what limits every electromagnet, from your nail to the scrap-yard disc. Industrial magnets use many turns of thick wire so that the resistance limits the current to a safe value, they are run from a controlled supply rather than a raw battery, and the big ones have cooling fins or even oil inside the case to carry heat away. Some are rated for a duty cycle, which means they may be on for only so many seconds in each minute. For your model, one or two cells and a push switch are the right supply, and the way to make the magnet stronger within that budget is more turns, a good soft iron core and tidy winding, not more voltage. Try the experiment on paper instead: estimate how many clips a hundred-turn coil lifts on one cell and on two, and notice that the second cell helps less than doubling the turns did, because the extra current also warms the wire and the cell cannot keep it up. That is the designer's lesson in miniature.
