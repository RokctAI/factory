### subtopic_1 — Building a Circuit That Works
**Assistant:** Which way does the current actually flow, and does it matter for building the circuit?

**Tutor:** There are two answers that sound contradictory and are both used. Historically, before anyone knew what was moving inside a wire, scientists agreed to say that current flows from the positive terminal of the cell, round the circuit, to the negative terminal, and every circuit diagram, arrow and rule in electronics still uses that convention; it is called conventional current. Later it was discovered that what actually moves in a metal wire is electrons, which carry negative charge and therefore drift the other way, from the negative terminal toward the positive. Because the mathematics works identically whichever direction you choose, engineers simply kept the old convention, so when a textbook says current flows from plus to minus, it is describing conventional current and is correct within that agreement. For building a simple circuit with a bulb, the direction does not matter at all, because a filament heats up the same whichever way charge passes through it. It matters for one-way components. A buzzer, a light-emitting diode or a motor that must turn a particular way each has a positive terminal that must connect toward the positive end of the cell, and the diagram shows this with a plus sign or a symbol that has a direction built in. In the next session the direction will matter for a different reason: the direction of current in the coil of an electromagnet decides which end of the nail becomes north, and reversing the cell swaps the poles. So learn the convention, plus to minus round the loop, and know that it is an agreed language rather than a description of the electrons.

### subtopic_2 — Conductors, Insulators and the Group Build
**Assistant:** Why do two bulbs in one loop glow dimmer, and is there a way to add a bulb without dimming the first?

**Tutor:** When two bulbs are joined one after the other in a single loop, called a series connection, the same current has to pass through both filaments, and each filament resists the flow; two resistances in a row make the cell's push work twice as hard, so less current flows and both bulbs are dim. Unscrew either bulb and the other goes out too, because the loop is broken. There is another arrangement called parallel, in which each bulb gets its own loop back to the cell: a wire from the positive terminal splits into two branches, one through each bulb, and the branches rejoin at the negative terminal. Now each bulb has the full push of the cell across it and glows as brightly as it would alone, and unscrewing one leaves the other lit, which is why house lights are wired this way so that one failed bulb does not darken the whole house. The price is that the cell supplies the current for both branches and runs down faster. For your crane the question will come up when you add an indicator lamp to the electromagnet circuit: in series the lamp steals push from the coil and weakens the magnet, so in a later session you will see the lamp wired in a way that lights whenever the coil is on without starving it. For now, build both arrangements if you have time and draw each as a diagram; the parallel one needs junction dots where the wires split and rejoin, which is good practice with the symbols.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing the Circuit Diagram
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a cell and a battery, and why do we say volts?

**Tutor:** Strictly a cell is a single unit, the kind of thing sold as an AA or a D size, with one positive and one negative terminal and a push of about 1.5 volts from the chemicals inside. A battery is two or more cells joined together; the nine-volt rectangular battery for a smoke alarm contains six small cells in series inside its case, and a car battery contains six two-volt cells, which is why it gives twelve volts. Everyday language calls a single cell a battery, which is fine in the shop but not in your diagram or your written work, where the examiner expects the correct word; two cells drawn touching are a battery of two cells. The volt is the unit of the electrical push, properly called potential difference, named after the Italian scientist Alessandro Volta who built the first chemical battery around 1800 from stacked discs of zinc and copper separated by cloth soaked in salt water. A higher voltage means a stronger push, which drives more current through the same bulb and makes it brighter until the filament overheats. The symbol for the volt is a capital V, and a cell's voltage is written beside its symbol on the diagram. One more word you will meet soon is the ampere, or amp, the unit of current, the amount of charge flowing per second; a torch bulb takes a fraction of an amp, while a car starter takes hundreds, which is why its cables are as thick as your thumb.
