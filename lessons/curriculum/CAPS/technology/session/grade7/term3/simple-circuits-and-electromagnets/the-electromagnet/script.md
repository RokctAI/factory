# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the demonstration and explanation of a simple electromagnet. The anchor is a Technology bench in Bloemfontein with a 100 millimetre soft iron nail, two metres of thin insulated copper wire, a 1.5 volt cell in a holder, a push switch, crocodile-clip leads, a plotting compass and a dish of paper clips, where a class winds a coil, tests it and then varies the turns, the current and the core. The governing idea is that a current in a wire produces a magnetic field around the wire, that winding the wire into a coil, a solenoid, concentrates the field so the coil behaves like a bar magnet with a north and a south end, that an iron core inside the coil is magnetised by that field and amplifies it many times, that the electromagnet can be switched off and reversed, and that its strength grows with the number of turns and the current. Building an electromagnet, explaining the role of the core, listing ways to strengthen it and comparing it with a permanent magnet are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Wire Becomes a Magnet

Lay a single straight wire across the bench, place a plotting compass beside it and connect the wire briefly across a cell through a bulb so the current is limited. The compass needle twitches and swings to lie across the wire. Disconnect, and it swings back to north. A current flowing through a wire creates a magnetic field around the wire, in circles round it, and the field vanishes the moment the current stops. This was discovered by accident in 1820 by a Danish teacher named Oersted during a lecture, and it is the link between electricity and magnetism on which every electric motor, generator and electromagnet depends.

A single wire makes a weak field. Wind the same wire into a coil of twenty turns round a pencil and the circular fields of all the turns add up inside the coil, so the coil now has a field like a bar magnet, with a north end and a south end. A coil like this is called a solenoid. Bring the compass to one end and the needle points in; bring it to the other end and the needle points out. Swap the cell round so the current flows the other way, and the ends swap too: the north end becomes south. An electromagnet can be reversed by reversing the current, something no permanent magnet can do.

Slide the pencil out and the solenoid still works, but weakly; with a cell and twenty turns it will barely move a paper clip. Slide an iron nail in and the coil suddenly lifts a chain of clips. Everything that follows depends on understanding why that nail makes such a difference.

The questions for this section are with you now: the compass beside the wire, what a solenoid is and how its poles are reversed.

## Subtopic: Building and Testing the Electromagnet

Take the iron nail and the insulated wire. Leave about 150 millimetres free at one end, then wind the wire tightly round the nail in neat turns, side by side, all in the same direction, from near the head toward the point. Fifty turns is a good first coil. Leave another 150 millimetres free at the other end, scrape the plastic insulation off the last 10 millimetres of each free end with sandpaper or a blade so the copper is bare, and connect the bare ends to the cell holder through the push switch. Press the switch and lower the nail point into the dish of clips. They jump to it. Release the switch and they fall.

Now test what the iron core does. The coil without the nail was weak because air does not help the field; iron does. The field of the coil lines up the domains in the iron, as you learned with the clips in the magnets session, so the nail becomes a strong temporary magnet while the current flows, and its field adds to the coil's own. The iron multiplies the field hundreds of times. The nail must be soft iron or mild steel so that its domains let go when the current stops, otherwise the electromagnet would keep holding its load with the switch open, which a scrap crane cannot allow.

Test what the number of turns does. Count the clips the fifty-turn coil lifts, then wind fifty more turns on top in the same direction and count again. More turns, more clips, roughly in proportion, because each turn adds its own field to the total. Test what the current does. Add a second cell in series to double the push, and the clip count rises again; but feel the coil after half a minute, because more current also means more heat, and a thin wire on two cells gets warm quickly. Record each test in a table: turns, cells, clips lifted, three trials each.

Two things weaken an electromagnet and are worth testing too. Winding half the turns one way and half the other cancels their fields. And a core of copper or aluminium instead of iron does nothing at all, which is the same fact as the scrap yard: only ferrous metal responds.

The questions for this section are with you now: the winding method, what the core does and the three tests of strength.

## Subtopic: Why the Crane Wants an Electromagnet

Compare the two kinds of magnet for the scrap-yard job. A permanent magnet is always on; it will lift the steel but never release it, it cannot be made stronger or weaker, and it snatches tools and steel from anywhere near it. An electromagnet is on only while the switch is closed, so the operator lifts, swings and drops with one finger; its strength can be adjusted by changing the current; and it can be reversed. The one disadvantage is that it needs a supply of electricity and stops working if the supply fails, which is why a real crane magnet has its own generator and why a load should never be swung over people.

In your model, the electromagnet will hang on a string from the end of the crane arm, with its two wires running up the arm to a switch and a cell at the base. Decide now how many turns you can wind on the nail, roughly a hundred for a 100 millimetre nail with thin wire, and whether one or two cells will be used. Keep the switch a push switch, so that the magnet is off whenever nobody is pressing; a crane that could be left on by accident is a hot wire and a flat cell.

Real electromagnets are everywhere. The scrap-yard lifting magnet is a flat coil in a steel case a metre across. The electric bell, the door lock that buzzes open, the relay that lets a small switch control a big current, the loudspeaker that moves a cone to make sound, the maglev train, the hospital scanner: every one is a coil with an iron core, switched or varied by a current. The electric motor is a set of electromagnets switched in turn so that a magnet in the middle is forever chasing a pole that moves away.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: winding the wire loosely and in both directions, so the turns cancel; wind tight, neat and one way. Two: forgetting to scrape the insulation off the wire ends, so no current flows at all. Three: using a copper or aluminium rod as the core because it is a metal; only iron works. Four: leaving the switch closed for minutes to show off; the wire heats, the cell drains and the lesson ends early.

The questions for this section are with you now: permanent versus electromagnet, the model crane's magnet and where electromagnets are used.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a nail, a reel of wire and a dish of paper clips — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Current Makes Magnetism

Put a compass next to a wire and send current through the wire. The needle swings. Stop the current and it swings back. Electricity flowing in a wire makes a magnetic field around the wire. A teacher named Oersted found this by accident in 1820.

One wire is weak. Coil it twenty times round a pencil and all the little fields add up, so the coil acts like a bar magnet with a north end and a south end. That coil is called a solenoid. Turn the cell round and the ends swap over. You can reverse an electromagnet; you cannot reverse a bar magnet.

Take the pencil out and the coil is weak. Slide an iron nail in and it suddenly lifts a chain of clips. The nail is the secret.

Keep the twitching compass in mind and try the questions for this part: current, the coil and swapping the ends.

## Subtopic: Wind It and Test It

Leave a hand-length of wire free, wind fifty tight neat turns round the nail all one way, leave another free end, scrape the plastic off the tips and connect through a push switch to the cell. Press: clips jump to the nail. Let go: they drop.

Why the nail? The coil's field lines up the tiny domains inside the iron, so the nail becomes a strong magnet while current flows and adds its field to the coil's. Iron multiplies the field hundreds of times. Use soft iron so it lets go when the switch opens.

Make it stronger three ways: more turns, more current with a second cell, and an iron core. Count the clips for each and write a table. Watch for heat with two cells. Half the turns one way and half the other cancel. A copper rod in the middle does nothing.

Hold the clips dropping in mind and try this part's questions: winding, the core and the three ways to strengthen.

## Subtopic: Why Not a Permanent Magnet

A permanent magnet is always on: it grabs the steel and never lets go, cannot be adjusted and snatches tools. An electromagnet is on only while you press the switch, can be made stronger or weaker and can be reversed. Its weakness is that it needs electricity, so never swing a load over people.

In your crane the nail hangs on a string from the arm, wires run to a push switch and a cell at the base. About a hundred turns on a long nail is a good target. A push switch means it is off the moment nobody presses.

Electromagnets are in bells, door locks, loudspeakers, relays, scanners, maglev trains and every electric motor.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Current in a wire makes a magnetic field. A coil, or solenoid, acts like a bar magnet and reverses when the current reverses. An iron core multiplies the field hundreds of times and lets go when the current stops. More turns and more current make it stronger. An electromagnet can be switched off, which is why the scrap crane needs one.

Hold the push switch, and take the final questions of the lesson: the comparison, the crane magnet and everyday uses.
