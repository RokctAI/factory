# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the introduction to simple electric circuits and circuit diagrams. The anchor is a Technology bench in Pietermaritzburg with a 1.5 volt cell in a holder, crocodile-clip leads, a push switch, a torch bulb in a holder and a small buzzer, where groups build, break and rebuild a circuit and then draw it. The governing idea is that a circuit needs a source of energy, a conductor forming a complete closed loop, a switch to control the flow and an output such as a bulb or buzzer, that current flows only when the loop is complete and stops at any gap, that conductors carry current while insulators do not, and that a circuit diagram uses standard symbols drawn with a ruler in a rectangle so that it can be read and built by anyone. Building a working circuit, explaining a fault, naming components and drawing a correct diagram are examinable.

## Subtopic: Building a Circuit That Works

Start with the four parts every simple circuit needs. The cell is the source of energy; its chemicals push electric charge round the circuit, and the push is measured in volts, 1.5 volts for a single torch cell. The conductors are the wires, copper inside plastic insulation, that give the charge a path. The switch is a gap that can be opened and closed. The output is the component that does the job: a bulb that glows or a buzzer that sounds. Connect them in a ring, cell to switch to bulb and back to the cell, press the switch, and the bulb lights.

Now break it on purpose. Unclip one lead and the bulb goes dark at once, even though the cell is still there and the bulb is still good. Electricity flows only round a complete, closed loop; the moment there is a gap anywhere in the ring, the whole circuit stops. The switch is simply a gap that you control. Open, no light; closed, light. A loose crocodile clip, a broken wire inside its insulation or a blown bulb filament all act as unplanned switches, and finding the gap is what fault-finding means.

Try the buzzer in place of the bulb. It sounds with one connection and stays silent if you swap the two leads over, because a buzzer, unlike a bulb, works in only one direction; it has a positive and a negative terminal marked on it, and the positive must connect toward the positive end of the cell, the end with the little button. The bulb does not care which way round it goes. Record this difference; it matters when you draw the diagram.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four parts of a circuit, why a gap stops everything and the one-way buzzer.

## Subtopic: Conductors, Insulators and the Group Build

Open the switch and bridge the gap with different materials: a steel nail, a strip of aluminium foil, a copper coin, a pencil lead, a plastic ruler, a rubber band, a piece of wood, a wet string. The bulb lights for the metals and faintly for the pencil lead; it stays dark for plastic, rubber, dry wood and dry string. Materials that let current through are conductors, and all metals conduct; materials that block it are insulators. This is why wires are copper, the best cheap conductor, wrapped in plastic, a good insulator, so that the current goes where it is meant to and not into your fingers.

Build the circuit in your group with a job for each person: one holds the cell holder, one connects the leads, one works the switch, one records. Then rotate jobs so everyone has connected a circuit. If the bulb will not light, work through the loop: is the cell the right way round in its holder, is each clip gripping bare metal and not insulation, is the bulb screwed fully home, is the switch actually closing? Nine faults in ten are a clip on plastic or a bulb not screwed in.

Add a second bulb in the same loop, one after the other, and both glow dimmer than one did alone, because the one cell is now pushing through two bulbs. Add a second cell end to end, positive to negative, and the bulbs brighten, because two cells push with three volts. Do not put more cells than the bulb is rated for, or the filament burns out in a flash; a torch bulb marked 2.5 volts is happy with two cells and dead after three.

Safety note for the record: cells at 1.5 volts are safe to touch, but never connect a wire directly from one end of a cell to the other with no bulb or buzzer between, a short circuit, because the wire and the cell get hot very quickly and the cell is ruined. Never experiment with mains electricity at 230 volts; it kills.

The questions for this section are with you now: conductors and insulators, the fault-finding sequence and the short-circuit warning.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Circuit Diagram

A photograph of your tangle of leads is useless to another builder. A circuit diagram shows the same circuit as a neat rectangle drawn with a ruler, with each component shown by its standard symbol and the wires as straight lines meeting at right angles. The cell is a long thin line and a short thick line side by side, the long line being the positive terminal. A switch is a line with a hinged gap. A bulb is a circle with a cross inside. A buzzer is a half-circle on a flat base. A wire is a plain straight line, and a junction where wires join is a dot.

Draw your circuit: start with the cell on the left side of the rectangle, the long positive line at the top, run a line from the positive terminal along the top to the switch, on to the bulb on the right side, and back along the bottom to the negative terminal. Label each component with its name and, for the cell, its voltage. Two cells end to end are drawn as two cell symbols touching, long line to short line. If your output is a buzzer, mark the positive terminal with a small plus sign so the builder connects it the right way.

The reason for conventions is the same as for the drawing conventions you learned in Term 1: a standard symbol means one thing to every reader. An electrician reading your diagram in another country does not need your language, only the symbols. Keep the lines straight and the corners square, keep the symbols the size you were shown, and do not draw the wires as wavy or crossing one another; a crossing line without a dot means the wires do not join.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing a picture of the real cell and bulb instead of the symbols; a diagram is not a sketch. Two: leaving a gap in the drawn loop by accident, which shows an open circuit. Three: getting the cell symbol backwards, so the long line marks the negative terminal. Four: curved, freehand wires; use a ruler, square corners.

The questions for this section are with you now: the standard symbols, drawing the loop and why conventions matter.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a cell, a bulb, a switch and a pile of crocodile clips — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Make the Bulb Light

Four parts: a cell to push, wires to carry, a switch to control and a bulb or buzzer to do the job. Join them in a ring, press the switch, and the bulb lights.

Unclip one lead. Dark. Electricity only flows round a complete loop; one gap anywhere and the whole thing stops. The switch is just a gap you control. A loose clip or a blown bulb is a gap you did not plan.

Try the buzzer. It sounds one way round and is silent the other way, because a buzzer has a plus and a minus side. The bulb does not care which way it goes. Write that down.

Keep the ring in mind and try the questions for this part: the four parts, the gap and the buzzer.

## Subtopic: What Lets Current Through

Open the switch and put things across the gap. Nail, foil, coin: the bulb lights. Pencil lead: faint light. Plastic, rubber, dry wood: dark. Metals are conductors; plastic and rubber are insulators. Wires are copper inside plastic so the current goes where you want and not into you.

Build in your group with a job each, then swap. If the bulb does not light, check the loop: cell the right way round, clips on bare metal, bulb screwed in, switch really closed. Nearly every fault is a clip on plastic or a loose bulb.

Two bulbs in a row: both dim. Two cells end to end: brighter. Too many cells and the bulb blows. Never join a wire straight from one end of a cell to the other with nothing in between; it gets hot fast. Never touch mains electricity.

Hold the nail across the gap in mind and try this part's questions: conductors, fault-finding and the short circuit.

## Subtopic: Draw It So Anyone Can Build It

A circuit diagram is a neat rectangle with symbols. Cell: a long thin line and a short thick line, the long line is plus. Switch: a line with a hinged gap. Bulb: a circle with a cross. Buzzer: a half-circle on a flat base. Wires: straight lines, ruler, square corners.

Draw the cell on the left, plus at the top, a line along the top to the switch, down to the bulb on the right, back along the bottom to minus. Label every part. Two cells touch long line to short line. Mark the buzzer's plus side.

Symbols are a language everyone reads. Straight lines, no wavy wires, and a crossing without a dot means the wires do not join.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A circuit needs a cell, conductors, a switch and an output, joined in a closed loop. A gap anywhere stops the current. Metals conduct; plastic and rubber insulate. Buzzers work one way round; bulbs do not care. Never short a cell. Draw the circuit as a rectangle with standard symbols and a ruler.

Hold the rectangle of symbols, and take the final questions of the lesson: symbols, the loop and conventions.
