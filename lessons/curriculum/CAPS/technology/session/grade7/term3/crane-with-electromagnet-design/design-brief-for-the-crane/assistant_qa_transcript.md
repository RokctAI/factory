### subtopic_1 — From Scenario to Design Brief
**Assistant:** Why can we not just write "make a crane that lifts metal" as the brief? It says what we are doing.

**Tutor:** It says what you are making, but it fails the other two questions a brief must answer and it fails the investigation. First, who is it for? A crane for a scrap dealer sorting metal has different needs from a crane for a harbour or a building site: the dealer wants to lift only steel, to let go on command and to work safely next to people in a yard. Leave the dealer out and nothing in the brief points at those needs. Second, what must it do? "Lifts metal" is wrong on the facts you established in the investigation, because a magnet lifts only ferrous metal, and that is the whole point of the device; the dealer wants steel separated from copper and aluminium, so the brief must say ferrous or steel. Third, the brief says nothing about releasing the load, and a crane that cannot drop what it has picked up cannot sort anything into a second bin. The one-sentence version that passes is: design and make a model crane for a scrap-metal dealer that uses an electromagnet to lift ferrous scrap from a mixed pile, swing it to a separate bin and release it, quickly and safely. Every phrase in it earns its place: dealer, electromagnet, ferrous, swing, release, quickly, safely. The PAT rubric gives marks for a brief that identifies the user, the purpose and the product, so a four-word brief is a quick way to lose them.

### subtopic_2 — Specifications: What the Crane Must Do
**Assistant:** Our team wants a specification that says the crane must be able to lift a real steel can. Is that a good specification?

**Tutor:** It is a good instinct and a risky number, and the way to decide is to work it through. A good specification is measurable, achievable within the constraints and linked to the need. A steel food can is measurable, you can say whether it was lifted or not, and it is linked to the need, because tins are exactly what a gleaner brings to the dealer. The question is whether it is achievable with a nail electromagnet on one or two cells. An empty 410 gram food can weighs about 40 to 50 grams, and a hundred-turn coil on a 100 millimetre nail will typically hold between 20 and 60 grams of clean steel depending on the cell, the winding and how flat the contact is. A can is a curved surface, so the nail touches it along a line rather than a face, which weakens the grip. It might work; it might just fail on evaluation day in front of the class. The disciplined move is to set the specification at a load you have already measured in the investigation, say ten paper clips or one 20 gram washer, and to add a stretch target: "should lift an empty steel can" as a desirable rather than a must. Rubrics distinguish between essential specifications, which the model must meet, and desirable ones, which earn credit if met. Set your essentials where you are confident and let the can be the extra. And keep a note of the test: you will lift the can on a flat lid, not the curved side, because that is how a real lifting magnet is used.

### subtopic_3 — Constraints: The Limits You Work Within
**Assistant:** If we can only use card and string, how will the crane be strong enough to hold a load without bending? Is that constraint not making the task impossible?

**Tutor:** It is making the task interesting, which is different, and the answer lies in term two. You spent six weeks on structures: how card is stiffened by folding it into tubes, L-sections and box sections; how a frame is made rigid by triangulation; how a tower is kept stable by a wide base and a low centre of gravity. The crane is a tower with an arm, so every one of those techniques applies. A card tube rolled tightly round a pencil and glued will carry a 50 gram load at its tip over 200 millimetres with almost no bending, where a flat strip of the same card droops under its own weight. A mast of four skewers braced with card triangles at each corner will not sway. A base of double card with a weight, a stone or a cell holder, placed opposite the arm will stop the crane tipping when the load is lifted. The constraint on materials does not stop you being strong; it forces you to use shape and structure instead of mass, which is exactly how real cranes are built. Look at a tower crane on a building site: it is a lattice of thin steel members, not a solid block, and it balances its load with a counterweight. The constraint is also fair, because every team has the same card, so the marks go to the team that uses it most cleverly. When you write the constraint, write it as a challenge: "made from recycled card, skewers and string using stiffening and triangulation techniques", and the sentence itself reminds you where the strength will come from.
