# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the design stage of the second Practical Assessment Task, the crane with an electromagnet. The anchor is a Technology classroom in Polokwane where a team has finished its investigation of magnets, metals and circuits and now has one period to write a design brief with specifications and constraints before any sketching begins. The governing idea is that a design brief is a short statement of what is to be designed, for whom and for what purpose, that specifications describe what the product must do and how well, that constraints describe the limits on materials, size, cost, time and safety, and that a good brief is measurable, so that the finished model can be tested against it rather than admired. Writing a brief, listing specifications and constraints, and telling the two apart are examinable and carry marks in the PAT.

## Subtopic: From Scenario to Design Brief

Reread the scenario from the start of term. A scrap-metal dealer in an industrial area buys mixed metal from gleaners and must sort the magnetic steel from the non-magnetic copper, brass and aluminium before selling it on. Sorting by hand is slow and the sharp edges cut hands. The dealer wants a crane with a magnet that lifts the steel out of the pile, swings it over a second bin and drops it there. That is the need. A design brief turns a need into a task, and it is written in one or two sentences that answer three questions: what is to be designed, who is it for, and what must it do. Here is one: "Design and make a model crane for a scrap-metal dealer that uses an electromagnet to lift ferrous scrap from a mixed pile, swing it to a separate bin and release it, so that steel is sorted from non-magnetic metals quickly and safely."

Notice what the brief does not say. It does not say how tall the crane is, what it is made of or whether the arm pivots or lifts. Those are decisions still to be made, and a brief that fixes them too early kills the design ideas you need next week. Notice also what the brief does say: ferrous scrap, because the investigation showed only iron and steel respond to a magnet; electromagnet, because the magnet must release its load; and quickly and safely, because those are the dealer's two complaints about sorting by hand. Every word in a brief should trace back to the investigation. If you cannot say why a word is there, cut it.

A brief is written in the IDMEC sequence you learned in term one: Investigate, Design, Make, Evaluate, Communicate. The investigation is done and its findings feed the brief. The design stage begins with the brief and continues through specifications, constraints, two sketches and a chosen idea. The PAT awards marks for a brief that names the user, the purpose and the product, so write all three or lose marks for a sentence that only says "make a crane".

The questions for this section are with you now: the three questions a brief answers, what the brief leaves open and the words that trace back to the investigation.

## Subtopic: Specifications: What the Crane Must Do

Specifications are the measurable requirements the product must meet. They answer the question "how will we know it works?" For the crane, write them as numbered statements that can each be tested with a ruler, a stopwatch, a count or a yes-or-no. One: the electromagnet must lift at least ten steel paper clips or one 20 gram steel washer. Two: it must release the entire load within one second of the switch being opened. Three: the arm must raise the load at least 150 millimetres above the base and swing or move it at least 200 millimetres sideways to a second bin. Four: the crane must stand on its own without being held while loaded. Five: the electromagnet must pick up no copper, aluminium or brass pieces from the mixed pile. Six: an indicator bulb must light whenever the electromagnet is on.

Each of those is a test, not a wish. "The crane must be strong" is a wish; "the crane must lift a 20 gram washer without the arm bending more than 10 millimetres" is a specification. "The magnet must work well" is a wish; "must lift ten clips and drop them all within a second" is a specification. When your team writes a specification, ask immediately: with what instrument, and what number, will we check this on evaluation day? If there is no answer, rewrite it.

Specifications come from the user's needs, so order them by importance to the dealer. Sorting correctly, the magnet lifting only ferrous metal, matters more than how high the arm goes, and releasing the load matters as much as lifting it, because a crane that cannot let go sorts nothing. Put the sorting and releasing specifications first. Marks in the PAT are awarded for specifications that are specific, measurable and clearly linked to the scenario, usually four to six of them, so do not pad the list with trivia such as the colour of the base.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes a statement a specification, the six crane specifications and how to order them.

## Subtopic: Constraints: The Limits You Work Within

Constraints are the limits placed on the designer. They do not describe what the product does; they describe what you may and may not use to make it. For the crane the usual constraints are these. Materials: only recycled card, dowel or skewers, string, a nail, insulated copper wire, one or two 1.5 volt cells, a push switch, a bulb, glue and tape, as supplied or brought from home at no cost. Size: the base must fit on an A4 sheet and the crane must be no taller than 400 millimetres so it fits in the storage cupboard. Time: the model must be finished in four making periods. Cost: nothing may be bought beyond the cells. Safety: no mains electricity, no cells connected directly to the coil without a switch, no sharp metal edges exposed, and no load swung over a person. Skills: the team must be able to make it with the tools in the room, which are scissors, a craft knife on a cutting mat, a ruler and a hot-glue gun used by the teacher.

The test for whether something is a specification or a constraint is to ask who it is about. If the statement is about the product's performance, lifting, releasing, swinging, lighting, it is a specification. If it is about the designer's resources and limits, materials, money, time, size, safety rules, it is a constraint. Learners lose marks every year for writing "must be made of card" under specifications and "must lift clips" under constraints. Two columns on one page, headed Specifications and Constraints, with four to six items in each, is the format examiners want to see.

Constraints are not the enemy of design; they are what make design a skill. A crane built from anything, at any size, with unlimited time, is not a design problem. A crane built from card, string and a nail, standing on an A4 sheet and finished in four periods, is a problem worth solving, and the engineer who solves it has done more than the one with a blank cheque. Write your constraints honestly. If the class has only one hot-glue gun, that is a constraint on time, and a good team plans around it rather than queueing for twenty minutes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a brief that says only "make a crane", with no user or purpose. Two: specifications that cannot be measured, such as "must be strong" or "must look good". Three: constraints and specifications muddled into one list. Four: a brief that already fixes the design, "a tall yellow crane with a pivoting arm", so there is nothing left to sketch two ideas for.

The questions for this section are with you now: the crane's constraints, the test that separates them from specifications and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a pile of scrap, a pencil and one sheet of paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Writing the Brief

The dealer needs steel sorted out of a mixed pile. Our brief says it in one sentence: design and make a model crane for a scrap dealer that uses an electromagnet to lift steel from a mixed pile, swing it to another bin and drop it there.

A brief answers three questions. What? A model crane with an electromagnet. Who for? The scrap dealer. What must it do? Sort steel from the other metals quickly and safely. Say all three.

A brief does not say how tall, what it is made of or how the arm moves. That is next week's job. Every word in the brief should come from something you found out in the investigation.

Keep the dealer's pile in mind and try the questions for this part: the three questions and what a brief leaves out.

## Subtopic: Specifications You Can Test

A specification is something you can test with a ruler, a stopwatch, a count or a yes-or-no. Lift ten clips. Drop them all within one second of letting go of the switch. Lift them 150 millimetres and move them 200 millimetres sideways. Stand up on its own. Pick up no copper or aluminium. Bulb on when the magnet is on.

"Must be strong" is a wish, not a specification. If you cannot say what instrument and what number you will use to check it, rewrite it.

Put the important ones first: sorting correctly and letting go matter most, because a crane that cannot drop its load sorts nothing. Four to six specifications are enough.

Hold the ten paper clips in mind and try this part's questions: what makes a test, the list and the order.

## Subtopic: Constraints You Must Obey

Constraints are the limits on you, the maker. Materials: card, dowel, string, a nail, wire, cells, a switch, a bulb, glue and tape. Size: base on an A4 sheet, no taller than 400 millimetres. Time: four periods. Cost: nothing bought but cells. Safety: no mains, always a switch, no sharp edges, never a load over a person.

Ask who the statement is about. About what the crane does: specification. About what you may use: constraint. Two columns on one page, and never mix them.

Constraints are what make this a real design problem. Anyone can build a crane with unlimited money. Building one from card and string in four periods takes skill.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A brief says what, who for and what it must do, in one or two sentences, without fixing the design. Specifications are measurable things the crane must do: lift, release, move, stand, sort, light. Constraints are the limits on materials, size, time, cost and safety. Keep them in two columns and make every one testable.

Hold the two columns, and take the final questions of the lesson: the constraints, the sorting test and the summary.
