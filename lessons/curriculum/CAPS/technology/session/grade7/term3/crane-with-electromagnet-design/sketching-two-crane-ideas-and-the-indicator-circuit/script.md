# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the second half of the design stage for the crane with an electromagnet: producing two possible designs and the circuit diagram. The anchor is a design folder on a desk in Kimberley holding last week's brief, specifications and constraints, a sheet of squared paper, a pencil, a ruler and the pictures of real cranes studied in week two. The governing idea is that designers generate more than one idea before choosing, that each sketch is a freehand labelled drawing showing the main parts, the materials and how the arm moves, that the two ideas must differ in a real way, such as a pivoting jib versus a luffing arm, and that the electromagnet's circuit is a series loop of cell, push switch, coil and indicator bulb, drawn with the standard symbols. Two annotated sketches and a correct circuit diagram are examinable and carry PAT marks.

## Subtopic: Two Ideas, Not One

Designers never settle for the first idea, because the first idea is usually the one everyone else had too. The PAT asks for two possible designs, and the two must differ in how they solve the problem, not in colour. Look back at the crane pictures from week two and you will find the two classic families. The first is the tower or jib crane: a tall vertical mast with a horizontal arm that pivots round the mast, so the load is lifted straight up by a string over the arm's end and then swung sideways by turning the arm to the second bin. The second is the luffing or boom crane: an arm hinged at its base that is raised and lowered, so the load moves in an arc as the arm tips up, and a second movement swings or slides it to the bin. A third family, the gantry, runs a trolley along a horizontal beam between two legs, so the load slides sideways rather than swinging.

Sketch idea A on the left half of the page and idea B on the right. Each is a freehand drawing, no ruler, in 3D if you can manage it or a clear side view if not, and each is labelled. Labels are not decoration; they are where the marks are. Label the base and its material, the mast or boom and its material, the pivot or hinge and how it is made, the string and where it runs, the position of the electromagnet, where the cells and switch sit, and the counterweight if there is one. Add a short note under each sketch saying how the arm moves and how the load gets from the pile to the bin.

Check both ideas against the brief while the pencil is still in your hand. Will idea A lift the load 150 millimetres? Where does the string go over the arm, and will the arm bend? Can idea B swing 200 millimetres to the bin, and what stops it tipping over when the boom is up? Write the checks as ticks and crosses beside each specification. A sketch that fails a specification is not wasted; it is the reason you will choose the other one, and the PAT rewards a choice with reasons.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three crane families, what each sketch must label and checking ideas against the brief.

## Subtopic: Making the Sketches Honest

A design sketch is a thinking tool, so it must show how the thing works, not just what it looks like. For the pivoting jib crane, draw the mast as a card tube set into a hole in a wide card base, with a second tube rotating round it or a dowel turning in a cardboard bearing, and show the string running from a winding drum at the base, up the mast, along the arm and down to the magnet. For the luffing crane, draw the boom hinged on a dowel through two upright supports, with a second string from the boom tip to a winder that tips the boom up, and the magnet's string over the tip. In both, show the counterweight: a cell holder or a stone at the back of the base or at the back of the arm, because the investigation of towers in term two taught that a load at the end of an arm shifts the centre of gravity toward the tipping point.

Use the drawing conventions you know. Thick lines for outlines, thin lines for detail, dotted lines for hidden parts such as a dowel inside a tube, and label lines that end in an arrowhead on the part named. Add rough sizes: base 210 by 297, mast 350, arm 250. These are not final dimensions, which belong on the working drawing in two weeks, but they prove that the sketch obeys the size constraints. A sketch with no sizes could be any crane; a sketch with a 350 millimetre mast on an A4 base is your crane.

Borrow from the real cranes but do not copy. The lattice boom of a crawler crane tells you that triangulated skewers will make a light, stiff arm. The counterweight slabs on a tower crane tell you to put mass opposite the load. The hook block on a harbour crane tells you to hang the magnet on a swivel so it does not twist the wires. Note beside each borrowed idea where it came from; the rubric gives credit for design ideas that show the influence of the investigation, and it is also the honest thing to do.

The questions for this section are with you now: showing how each idea works, line types and rough sizes, and borrowing from real cranes.

## Subtopic: The Circuit Diagram with an Indicator Light

The electromagnet needs a circuit, and the brief adds one requirement: an indicator light that shows when the magnet is on, so the operator can see at a glance whether the load is held. The components are a cell or two in series, a push switch, the coil of the electromagnet and a bulb. Connected in one loop, in series, the current flows through every component in turn, so when the switch is pressed the bulb lights and the coil is energised together, and when it is released both go off. That single loop is the simplest and most reliable arrangement for the model.

Draw it with the standard symbols from week three. A cell is a long thin line and a short thick line side by side, the long line the positive terminal; two cells in series are two such pairs end to end. A switch is a gap in the line with a short slanted line that can close it, drawn open. A bulb is a circle with a cross inside. The coil has no everyday Grade 7 symbol in some textbooks, so it is drawn either as a row of loops or as a rectangle labelled "electromagnet" or "coil", and the label is what matters. Join the symbols with straight ruled lines, horizontal and vertical, with right-angled corners, into a closed rectangle. Label each component. Draw the switch open, which is the state of the real circuit when nobody is pressing.

A design detail worth knowing: because the bulb and coil are in series, the bulb's filament limits the current, which keeps the coil cooler and the cells alive longer but makes the magnet weaker than with the coil alone. If the test shows the magnet too weak with the bulb in series, the alternative is to put the bulb in parallel with the coil, across the coil's two ends, so that each gets the full cell voltage; draw that as a second branch beside the coil. Either is acceptable in the PAT as long as the diagram matches the model you build, so decide, draw it once and wire exactly what you drew.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: two sketches that are the same crane in two colours; they must differ in how the arm moves. Two: sketches with no labels and no sizes, so the marker cannot tell card from wood or whether it fits the A4 base. Three: a circuit diagram drawn freehand with curved wires and a bulb symbol that is just a circle; use a ruler and the full symbol. Four: a circuit with no switch, which means a magnet that is always on and cells that are flat by the end of the period.

The questions for this section are with you now: the series loop, the four symbols and the series or parallel indicator.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a pencil, a page folded in half and a circuit of four symbols — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Draw Two Different Cranes

Never stop at one idea. Fold the page in half. On the left, idea A: a tall mast with an arm that swings round it; the load goes up on a string and then the arm turns to the bin. On the right, idea B: an arm hinged at the bottom that tips up and down like a seesaw, with a second string to lift it.

Label everything: base, mast, arm, hinge, string, magnet, cells, switch, counterweight. Say what each part is made of. Write under each sketch how the load gets from the pile to the bin.

Tick or cross each specification beside each sketch. Lifts 150 millimetres? Swings 200 millimetres? Stands up? The crosses tell you which idea to choose, and the PAT wants a choice with reasons.

Keep the folded page in mind and try the questions for this part: two ideas, labels and the ticks.

## Subtopic: Make the Sketch Work

A sketch must show how the crane works. Draw the string from a winder at the base, up the mast, along the arm and down to the magnet. Draw the hinge as a dowel through two uprights. Draw the counterweight opposite the load so the crane does not tip.

Thick lines for outlines, thin for detail, dotted for hidden parts, arrows for labels. Add rough sizes: mast 350, arm 250, base A4.

Borrow from real cranes: a lattice arm from a crawler crane, a counterweight from a tower crane, a swivel hook from a harbour crane. Write where each idea came from.

Hold the string running up the mast in mind and try this part's questions: how it works, line types and borrowing.

## Subtopic: The Indicator Circuit

One loop: cell, push switch, coil, bulb. Press the switch and the bulb lights while the magnet grabs. Let go and both stop. The bulb tells the operator the magnet is on.

Symbols: a cell is a long thin line and a short thick line; a switch is a gap with a slanted line; a bulb is a circle with a cross; the coil is a row of loops or a labelled box. Ruler, straight lines, square corners, switch drawn open, every part labelled.

If the magnet is too weak with the bulb in the loop, put the bulb in parallel across the coil instead, and draw what you build.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Two sketches that differ in how the arm moves, each labelled with parts, materials and rough sizes, each checked against the brief, with borrowed ideas credited. One circuit diagram: cell, switch, coil and bulb in a loop, standard symbols, ruled lines, switch open, labels on.

Hold the four symbols, and take the final questions of the lesson: the loop, the symbols and the summary.
