### subtopic_1 — Two Ideas, Not One
**Assistant:** If we already know which crane we want to build, why waste time sketching a second idea we are going to throw away?

**Tutor:** Because you do not yet know which crane you want to build; you know which one you thought of first, and those are different things. The whole history of design says that the first idea is rarely the best, because it arrives before you have understood the problem fully. Sketching a second, genuinely different idea forces you to look at the problem from another side. Suppose your first idea is a tall mast with a swinging arm. Drawing the hinged boom alternative makes you ask how the swinging arm will be held up, where the counterweight goes, how the string runs round a rotating joint without tangling, and whether a mast 350 millimetres high on an A4 base will stand when a load hangs 250 millimetres out. Some of those questions you would not have asked until the model fell over in week eight. Very often the second sketch also lends the first one a part: the triangulated boom from idea B becomes the arm of idea A, and the chosen design is better than either sketch. There is also the matter of marks. The PAT rubric awards marks for two possible designs and for a reasoned choice between them, and a team that submits one sketch, or two sketches that are the same crane recoloured, loses those marks whatever it builds. Treat the second sketch as the cheapest experiment you will ever run: ten minutes of pencil to find a problem that would otherwise cost you a period of card and glue.

### subtopic_2 — Making the Sketches Honest
**Assistant:** How do we show the pivot in the sketch so the marker can see how the arm actually turns?

**Tutor:** You show the mechanism, not just the shape, and you do it with a detail drawing. Beside your main sketch, draw a small enlarged view of the joint, circled and connected to the main drawing with a thin line, the way a technical drawing shows a detail at larger scale. For a swinging jib the usual model solution is a fixed mast tube glued into the base, with a second, slightly larger card tube slipped over it carrying the arm, so the outer tube rotates on the inner one. Draw the two tubes with a dotted line for the hidden inner mast, label "inner tube fixed, outer tube turns", and add an arrow curving round the top to show rotation. An alternative is a dowel mast turning in a card bearing, two layers of card with a hole, glued to the base, with a bead or washer to reduce friction; draw the hole and the bead. For a hinged boom, draw the dowel passing through the boom and the two upright supports, with a dotted line where it is hidden inside, and a curved arrow showing the boom lifting. Label the dowel, the supports and the material. Then, below the detail, write one sentence: "the arm turns about the mast on an outer card tube" or "the boom tips on a 4 millimetre dowel through two card uprights". The marker is checking two things: that the mechanism will physically work, and that you understood it well enough to draw it. A labelled detail with a movement arrow answers both, and it will also be the drawing you build from in week eight when you cannot remember what you meant.

### subtopic_3 — The Circuit Diagram with an Indicator Light
**Assistant:** Does it matter which side of the cell the switch goes on in the circuit diagram? Our group argued about it.

**Tutor:** In a simple series loop it makes no difference to how the circuit works, and knowing why is worth more than the argument. In a series circuit there is one path, and the same current flows through every component in turn, like water in a single closed pipe. A switch is simply a gap that can be opened or closed in that pipe, so wherever it is placed, opening it stops the current everywhere in the loop at once: the bulb goes out and the coil loses its magnetism together. You could put the switch between the cell and the coil, between the coil and the bulb, or between the bulb and the cell, and the behaviour would be identical. What does matter is that there is exactly one switch, that it is in the loop rather than dangling on a branch, and that it is drawn open, because that is the resting state of a push switch and the convention examiners expect. Two things do depend on position. First, if you move the indicator bulb from the series loop to a parallel branch across the coil, the switch must stay in the part of the circuit that both branches share, otherwise it would switch only one of them; keep it next to the cell. Second, in the real model, position matters for convenience: put the switch near the cells at the base where the operator's hand is, so the wires run short and the hand is far from the swinging load. Draw the diagram neatly with the switch in whichever position makes the rectangle tidy, label it, and spend the saved argument time checking that every symbol is correct.
