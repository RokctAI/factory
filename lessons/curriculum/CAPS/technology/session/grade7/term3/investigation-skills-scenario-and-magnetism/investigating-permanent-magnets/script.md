# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the practical investigation of permanent magnets. The anchor is a Technology bench in Polokwane with two bar magnets, a horseshoe magnet, paper clips, steel pins, a plotting compass, iron filings in a shaker and sheets of paper, where a class tests where the pull is strongest, how two magnets interact and what shape the field takes. The governing idea is that a permanent magnet has two poles, north-seeking and south-seeking, that the magnetic force is concentrated at the poles, that like poles repel and unlike poles attract, that the field can be mapped with filings or a compass and is strongest where the lines are closest, and that a horseshoe magnet brings both poles together to make a stronger pull at one place. Naming poles, stating the law of magnets, drawing the field of a bar magnet and describing a fair test of magnet strength are examinable.

## Subtopic: Where a Magnet Pulls Hardest

Lay a bar magnet on the bench and scatter paper clips near it. Most clips cling to the two ends and almost none to the middle. The ends are the poles, and the magnetic force is concentrated there. Count the clips that each end will hold in a chain, end to end, and you have a simple measure of strength; a school bar magnet will usually hold three or four clips in a chain from each pole and none from its middle. The horseshoe magnet is a bar magnet bent so that both poles sit side by side, and across those two poles it holds a longer chain than either pole of the bar, because both poles pull on the same spot.

Hang a bar magnet from a thread so it can swing freely and wait. It turns and settles pointing roughly north and south. The end that points north is called the north-seeking pole, or north pole for short, and is marked N or painted red; the other end is the south pole, S. A compass is simply a small magnet on a pivot doing the same thing. This is why the Earth itself must be a huge magnet, with its magnetic poles near its geographic poles, and why a compass has guided ships and hikers for centuries.

A fair test of magnet strength changes one thing at a time. If you compare two magnets, use the same clips, the same way of chaining them and the same pole, and repeat each count three times to average out shaky hands. Record the results in a table with columns for the magnet, the pole tested and the number of clips held in each trial. A result without a method is a guess; a result with a method is evidence.

The questions for this section are with you now: the poles, the clip-chain test and why a hanging magnet points north.

## Subtopic: Attract and Repel

Take two bar magnets with their poles marked. Bring the north pole of one toward the south pole of the other and they leap together and hold; this is attraction. Turn one round and bring north toward north, and you feel a push in your hands that grows as they get closer; they refuse to touch, and if you let one go it skids away or flips over to present its south pole. The rule is the law of magnets: unlike poles attract, like poles repel. It is the only test that tells a magnet from a piece of plain iron, because iron is attracted to both poles and never repels.

Float a small bar magnet on a slice of cork in a bowl of water and bring another magnet near. The floating magnet swings, pushed and pulled, until it lines up with the one in your hand, north facing south. The push and pull act across the gap without the magnets touching; magnetism is a non-contact force, like gravity, and it acts through air, water, paper, plastic, wood and card. Prove it by picking up clips through a sheet of paper or the bottom of a plastic cup.

Repulsion has uses. Magnetic levitation trains ride on repelling magnets, fridge-door seals use attraction, and in your scrap-yard model the electromagnet relies on attraction alone. But the law also explains a common mistake with model magnets: two small magnets that keep flying apart are not broken, they are simply like poles facing each other, and turning one over fixes it.

Cutting a bar magnet in half does not give you a separate north and a separate south. Each half becomes a complete magnet with its own north and south pole, however many times you cut it. Poles always come in pairs.

The questions for this section are with you now: the law of magnets, non-contact force and what happens when a magnet is cut.

## Subtopic: Mapping the Invisible Field

Place a bar magnet under a sheet of paper and sprinkle iron filings over the paper, tapping gently. The filings arrange themselves into curved lines that run from one pole round to the other, crowded close together at the poles and spread far apart at the sides and the middle. These are magnetic field lines, a picture of the region in which the magnet exerts a force. Where the lines are crowded the field is strong; where they are sparse it is weak. That is why the clips clung to the ends and not the middle.

Now do the same with a plotting compass. Put the compass near the north pole of the magnet, mark a dot where its needle points, move the compass to that dot and mark again, and follow the chain of dots to the south pole. Join them and you have drawn a field line, and the arrow on it runs from north to south outside the magnet. Repeat from several starting points and the drawing matches the filings. Draw this diagram carefully in your record: a rectangle marked N and S, with curved lines leaving N, looping round and entering S, closest together at the ends.

Put two magnets under the paper, first north to south and then north to north, and sprinkle again. Unlike poles show lines running straight across the gap, joining the magnets. Like poles show lines bending away from each other, leaving an empty space between the poles. The field pattern shows attraction and repulsion without you touching anything.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the middle of a bar magnet is as strong as its ends; the force lives at the poles. Two: calling a piece of iron a magnet because a magnet attracts it; only repulsion proves a magnet. Three: believing a cut magnet gives one north piece and one south piece; every piece has both. Four: drawing field lines evenly spaced everywhere; they crowd at the poles because that is where the field is strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: filings, compass plotting, and the field patterns of attraction and repulsion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two bar magnets, a horseshoe and a shaker of iron filings — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Ends Do the Work

Drop paper clips near a bar magnet. They rush to the two ends and ignore the middle. The ends are the poles, and that is where the pull is. Count how many clips hang in a chain from each end; that is a quick strength test. A horseshoe magnet is a bar bent so both ends sit together, so it holds a longer chain at that one spot.

Hang a bar magnet on a thread. It swings and stops pointing north and south. The north-pointing end is the north pole, marked N or red. The other is south, S. A compass is just a tiny magnet on a pin doing the same. The Earth itself is a giant magnet.

Fair test: same clips, same way of hanging them, same pole, three tries each, written in a table. Method plus result equals evidence.

Keep the clip chain in mind and try the questions for this part: poles, the test and the compass.

## Subtopic: Snap Together, Push Apart

North to south: the magnets snap together. North to north: they push each other away, and the push gets stronger the closer they are. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel. Plain iron is pulled by both ends and never pushes back, so repulsion is the only proof that something is a magnet.

Float a magnet on cork in water and bring another near. It swings round until north faces south, without touching. Magnetism works across a gap and through paper, plastic, wood and water. Pick up clips through a sheet of paper to prove it.

If two little magnets keep jumping apart, nothing is broken: like poles are facing. Turn one over. And if you cut a magnet in half, each half gets its own north and south. Poles always come in pairs.

Hold the floating magnet in mind and try this part's questions: attract, repel and the cut magnet.

## Subtopic: Seeing the Field

Put a bar magnet under paper and sprinkle iron filings. Curved lines appear, running from one end round to the other, bunched tight at the ends and spread thin at the sides. Tight lines mean strong field. That is why clips stick at the ends.

Use a small compass instead: put it near N, dot where the needle points, move the compass to the dot, dot again, and follow the trail to S. Join the dots and you have a field line, arrow from N to S. Draw the whole picture in your book.

Two magnets N to S: lines run straight across the gap. N to N: lines bend away and leave an empty space. The pattern shows attraction and repulsion without touching.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A magnet has a north and a south pole where the force is strongest. A free magnet points north. Unlike poles attract, like poles repel, and only repulsion proves a magnet. Magnetism acts across a gap through non-magnetic materials. Cutting a magnet gives two complete magnets. The field is drawn as lines from N to S, crowded at the poles.

Hold the filings picture, and take the final questions of the lesson: field lines, plotting and two-magnet patterns.
