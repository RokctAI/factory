### subtopic_1 — Testing the Tray
**Assistant:** Why are some stainless steel spoons magnetic and others not, if they are all steel?

**Tutor:** Stainless steel is not one material but a family of alloys, all based on iron with at least about ten percent chromium to stop rust, and the other ingredients decide the magnetism. The common kitchen grade used for sinks, cutlery and cooking pots often contains a lot of nickel as well, around eight percent, and that combination rearranges the iron atoms into a crystal pattern called austenite in which the domains cannot form, so the steel is non-magnetic even though it is mostly iron. Cheaper grades with chromium but little or no nickel keep the ordinary iron crystal pattern, called ferrite, and are magnetic; many spoons, knives and appliance panels are made of these. To add to the confusion, a non-magnetic grade can become slightly magnetic where it has been bent or stamped hard, because the working changes the crystal structure locally, which is why a spoon may hold a magnet at its bowl but not at its handle. For a scrap dealer this is a real nuisance: a magnet crane will lift the magnetic stainless grades with the ordinary steel and leave the nickel-rich grades in the non-ferrous pile, where they are valuable precisely because of their nickel. The lesson for your investigation is the one you already drew: do not predict from the name, test every object, and write the surprise in the note column.

### subtopic_2 — Why Only Iron
**Assistant:** Could the dealer use a magnet to find copper, since copper is the valuable metal?

**Tutor:** Not directly, because copper has no magnetic domains and a magnet simply does not notice it, no matter how strong the magnet. What a magnet does is remove everything that is not copper, aluminium or brass, which is most of the volume of a scrap pile, leaving a smaller non-ferrous heap that is much easier to sort by other means. In the non-ferrous heap the dealer uses colour, since copper is reddish and brass is yellow while aluminium is silvery white; weight, since copper is more than three times as dense as aluminium so a copper fitting feels heavy for its size; and sound, since struck copper rings dull while aluminium rings bright. Larger yards use a clever extension of today's lesson called an eddy-current separator. A fast-spinning magnetic rotor under a conveyor belt induces swirling electric currents in any conductor passing over it, and those currents produce their own magnetic field that pushes against the rotor, so non-ferrous conductors are flung forward off the belt while plastic and glass drop straight down. That is magnetism being used to sort copper and aluminium after all, but by inducing currents rather than by attraction, and it only works once the steel has been pulled out first by an ordinary magnet, otherwise the steel would stick to the rotor. So the magnet crane is the first step in a chain, and it earns its keep by making every later step smaller and safer.

### subtopic_3 — Writing Up a Fair Materials Test
**Assistant:** If a nail becomes a temporary magnet when near a magnet, why does it not stay a magnet afterwards like the stroked needle does?

**Tutor:** The difference is how hard the material is, in the magnetic sense. In soft iron, which includes the mild steel of an ordinary nail, the domains swing into line very easily when a field is applied and swing back out of line just as easily when the field is removed, because the internal structure offers little resistance to their movement. That is why the nail is attracted strongly while the magnet is near and goes back to being plain iron almost completely when the magnet is taken away, keeping only a faint trace. A sewing needle or a screwdriver tip is made of hardened steel, with more carbon and a heat treatment that locks the crystal structure, and in that material the domains resist being moved; it takes repeated stroking in one direction to line them up, but once lined up they stay, so the needle becomes a permanent magnet. Magnetically soft and hard materials each have their job. Permanent magnets, fridge magnets and compass needles need hard materials that keep their alignment. Electromagnet cores, transformer cores and the keeper bar across a horseshoe magnet need soft iron that magnetises instantly and demagnetises instantly, so that the electromagnet lets go the moment the switch is opened. When you wind your electromagnet on an iron nail in a few sessions, you will be relying on exactly this softness, and if you ever find your electromagnet still holding a clip after switching off, a slightly harder nail is the reason.
