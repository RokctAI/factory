# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens PAT 2 with the scenario and the case study of cranes. The anchor is a scrap yard in Germiston on the East Rand, where a dealer buys mixed scrap by the bakkie load and must sort it into ferrous metal, which contains iron and is attracted by a magnet, and non-ferrous metal such as copper, aluminium and brass, which is not. The governing idea is that a technology project begins with a careful investigation of the scenario, identifying the user, the need, the situation and the conditions, and continues with a case study of existing products to borrow proven ideas before any design is attempted. Reading a scenario for need and constraints, describing the main parts of a crane and recording observations from pictures are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading the Scenario for the Real Need

A scenario is a short story that hides a problem. The story here is a scrap yard where every incoming load is a mixture of metals. Steel pays one price per kilogram, copper pays many times more, and a buyer at the recycling mill refuses a load that is mixed. The dealer currently sorts by hand, which is slow, dirty and dangerous because the edges of cut metal are sharp and heavy pieces fall. The need is not a crane; the need is to separate ferrous from non-ferrous metal quickly and safely. The crane with a magnet is one possible answer, and we have to understand the need before we judge the answer.

Investigation means asking questions of the scenario. Who is the user? The dealer and the workers in the yard. What is the situation? An open yard with piles of scrap on a concrete floor and a truck to load. What must the solution do? Lift magnetic metal from a mixed pile, carry it across to a separate pile or a truck, and release it there. What must it not do? It must not lift the copper and aluminium, it must not drop steel on anyone, and it must not cost more than the dealer can afford. Write these answers down as a list; they become your specifications and constraints later in the term.

Notice the words in the scenario that carry technical meaning. A magnet attracts only some metals, so the sorting depends on a property of materials, which we will test next session. A crane needs to lift, swing and lower, which are three movements, so the crane will need mechanisms. The magnet must switch off to release the load, so a permanent magnet will not do and an electromagnet, which can be turned on and off with a switch, is needed. Each technical word points to one part of the term's work.

The questions for this section are with you now: the user, the need, the situation and the technical words hidden in the scenario.

## Subtopic: A Case Study of Cranes

A case study means examining things that already exist to learn from them. Collect pictures of cranes: the tall tower cranes over city building sites, the mobile cranes on trucks, the small jib cranes bolted to a workshop wall, and the scrap-yard cranes with a round magnet hanging from the hook. Lay the pictures side by side and look for what they have in common. Every crane has a base that keeps it from tipping, a mast or body that gives height, an arm called a jib or boom that reaches out over the load, a hoisting rope or cable that runs over a pulley at the end of the jib, and a lifting device at the end of the cable, a hook or a magnet.

Now look for how each crane moves. Hoisting raises and lowers the load on the cable, usually by winding the cable onto a drum turned by a crank or motor. Luffing raises and lowers the jib itself to change the reach. Slewing swings the whole upper part round on a pivot so that the load can be carried from one pile to another. A scrap-yard magnet crane does all three: it lowers the magnet onto the pile, lifts, swings to the steel heap, lowers and releases. Your model will need at least hoisting and slewing.

Look also at how cranes stay standing. A tower crane has a heavy concrete counterweight on the short side of the jib, opposite the load. A mobile crane spreads outriggers, legs that push out sideways, to widen its base before lifting. A wall jib crane is bolted to a strong wall. Every one of these answers the stability lesson from last term: a wide base, a low centre of gravity and the weight balanced about the pivot. Make a note of the counterweight idea in particular, because a model crane with a long arm and a magnet on the end tips over very easily.

Record the case study in a table with columns for the crane type, its base, its jib, its lifting mechanism and what you could borrow for your model. This table is evidence for the investigation marks in the PAT.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a crane, its three movements and how it stays standing.

## Subtopic: From Pictures to Design Ideas

The purpose of a case study is to borrow, not to copy. From the tower crane, borrow the counterweight. From the mobile crane, borrow the wide base. From the wall jib, borrow the simple pivot that lets the arm swing. From the scrap crane, borrow the round magnet hanging on a cable. A model made of dowel, card and string can use every one of these ideas at a small scale. Sketch each borrowed idea quickly as a thumbnail, a small rough drawing with a label, so that you have a bank of ideas to draw on in the design stage.

Look at the scale of the real thing and the scale of your model. A real scrap-yard magnet is a steel disc a metre across, powered from the crane's generator, and lifts a tonne of steel at a time. Your electromagnet will be a nail wound with wire and powered by a cell, and it will lift paper clips and small steel washers. The idea is the same; only the size changes. Technologists often test an idea with a small working model before building the real thing, because the model shows whether the idea works and where it fails, cheaply and safely.

Think about safety from the start. In the scenario, the danger is sharp edges and falling metal. In your model, the dangers are smaller but real: a hot wire if the electromagnet is left switched on too long, a sharp nail point, and a glue gun. Writing a safety line into your investigation record is part of good practice and earns credit.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the need is a crane; the need is to sort metal, the crane is one answer. Two: skipping the case study and designing from imagination alone, which repeats mistakes other people have already solved. Three: copying one picture exactly rather than borrowing ideas from several. Four: forgetting the counterweight and building a model that falls on its face the first time it lifts.

The questions for this section are with you now: borrowing ideas, the scale of models and the safety line in the record.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a pile of scrap and a stack of crane pictures — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What the Dealer Really Needs

Picture a scrap yard in Germiston. Bakkies arrive with mixed metal: car parts, wire, gutters, pots, cans. Steel sells for a little; copper sells for a lot; a mixed load sells for nothing because the mill refuses it. Workers sort by hand, which is slow and cuts their hands.

The dealer does not need a crane. The dealer needs steel separated from the other metals quickly and safely. A crane with a magnet is one way to do that. Always find the need before you fall in love with an answer.

Ask the scenario questions. Who uses it? The dealer and workers. Where? An open yard. What must it do? Lift magnetic metal, carry it, drop it on another pile. What must it not do? Lift copper, drop steel on people, cost too much. The magnet must switch off to let go, so it has to be an electromagnet. Write all of this down.

Keep the mixed pile in mind and try the questions for this part: user, need and the magnet that must switch off.

## Subtopic: Looking at Real Cranes

Collect pictures of cranes and lay them out. Every crane has a base so it does not tip, a body for height, an arm that reaches over the load, a cable over a pulley at the end of the arm, and a hook or magnet on the cable.

Cranes move three ways. Hoisting: the cable winds in or out to lift and lower. Luffing: the arm tilts up or down. Slewing: the top swings round on a pivot. The scrap crane lowers the magnet, lifts, swings to the steel pile and lets go. Your model needs at least lifting and swinging.

Cranes stay up with a counterweight on the short end, legs that push out sideways, or bolts into a strong wall. Wide base, low weight, balanced about the pivot. Write the counterweight down twice; a model with a long arm tips easily.

Hold the three movements in mind and try this part's questions: parts, movements and staying upright.

## Subtopic: Borrow, Do Not Copy

Take one idea from each picture: counterweight from the tower crane, wide base from the truck crane, simple pivot from the wall crane, hanging magnet from the scrap crane. Draw each as a small labelled thumbnail. That is your idea bank.

The real magnet is a metre across and lifts a tonne. Yours is a nail with wire and lifts paper clips. Same idea, tiny size. Technologists build small models first to see where the idea fails, cheaply.

Safety from day one: a hot wire if the electromagnet stays on, a sharp nail, a hot glue gun. Write a safety line in your record.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The need is to sort ferrous from non-ferrous metal, not to own a crane. A crane has a base, body, arm, cable, pulley and lifting device, and it hoists, luffs and slews. Cranes stay up with wide bases and counterweights. A case study borrows ideas from several existing products. Models test ideas at small scale, and safety goes in the record.

Hold the idea bank, and take the final questions of the lesson: borrowing, scale and safety.
