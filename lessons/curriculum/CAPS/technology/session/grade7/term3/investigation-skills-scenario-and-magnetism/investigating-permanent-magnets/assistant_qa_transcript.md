### subtopic_1 — Where a Magnet Pulls Hardest
**Assistant:** If the north pole of a compass points north, does that mean the Earth's North Pole is a magnetic north pole?

**Tutor:** It is a good catch, and the answer is that the magnetic pole up in the Arctic is actually a south-seeking pole in the magnetic sense, even though we call that region the north. A compass needle's north pole points north because it is attracted toward it, and unlike poles attract, so whatever is near the geographic North Pole must behave like the south pole of a magnet. Early navigators named the needle's north-pointing end the north pole long before anyone understood what it was pointing at, and the name stuck, which is why textbooks say north-seeking pole to be precise. The Earth's magnetic poles also do not sit exactly on the geographic poles; the magnetic pole in the Arctic is currently in the ocean north of Canada and drifts a few tens of kilometres a year toward Siberia, so a compass points a little off true north by an angle that depends on where you are. In South Africa that angle is currently about twenty degrees to the west, which is why a hiker's map gives a correction. None of this changes the practical rule you need: a freely swinging magnet lines up roughly north and south, the end that points north is labelled N, and N attracts S and repels N.

### subtopic_2 — Attract and Repel
**Assistant:** Why does a magnet pick up clips through paper and plastic but not through a steel plate?

**Tutor:** Magnetism is a non-contact force that passes through any material that is not itself magnetic, so paper, plastic, wood, glass, water, aluminium and copper are all transparent to it; the field lines run straight through them as if they were air, and the clips on the other side feel the full pull. A steel plate is different because steel contains iron, which is magnetic. When the field reaches the steel, the lines are drawn into the plate and run along inside it rather than continuing through to the other side, because iron is a far easier path for field lines than air. The steel becomes temporarily magnetised and clips stick to the steel itself, but very little field escapes to the far side, so the clips there are hardly pulled at all. Engineers use this as magnetic shielding: a sensitive instrument is put inside a soft iron box so that stray fields are channelled round it. It is also why a thick steel cabinet door will hold a fridge magnet firmly but will not let that magnet pull a clip inside the cabinet. For your scrap crane the lesson is the opposite one: your electromagnet works on the steel in the pile because the field is drawn into the steel and holds it, while copper and aluminium let the field pass through untouched and are left behind.

### subtopic_3 — Mapping the Invisible Field
**Assistant:** How can I tell the north pole of a magnet whose paint has worn off?

**Tutor:** You have two reliable methods and both use what you learned today. The first uses a compass or a magnet whose poles are known. Bring one end of the unmarked magnet slowly toward the red, north-marked end of a compass needle. If the needle is pushed away, you are holding the north pole, because like poles repel; if the needle is pulled toward it, you are holding the south pole. Always use the repulsion to decide, because attraction alone could happen with any iron object. The second method needs no other magnet. Tie a thread round the middle of the magnet so it balances level, hang it where nothing steel is nearby, and let it settle; the end that points north, which you can check against the sun's position or a map, is the north-seeking pole. Mark it with a dot of paint or a scratch straight away. A third quick trick for a workshop is to hold the unknown magnet near a floating cork magnet whose N is known and watch which way the cork turns. Whatever method you use, label the magnet, because in the next sessions and in the electromagnet work you will want to know which way the field points, and relabelling takes a minute while guessing wastes a whole investigation.
