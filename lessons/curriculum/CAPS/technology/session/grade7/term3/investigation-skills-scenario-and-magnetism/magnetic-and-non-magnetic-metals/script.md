# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the practical investigation of magnetic and non-magnetic metals. The anchor is a Technology bench in Mthatha with a bar magnet and a tray of labelled samples: steel nails and washers, copper wire, aluminium foil and a cooldrink can, a brass key, a food tin, galvanised fence wire, a stainless steel spoon, a lead fishing sinker and a few coins, together with a results table. The governing idea is that only iron, nickel, cobalt and alloys containing them, chiefly steel, are attracted by a magnet, that these are called ferrous or magnetic metals, that copper, aluminium, brass, lead, zinc, gold and silver are non-ferrous and non-magnetic, that coatings and alloying can disguise a metal so testing rather than appearance is required, and that this property is exactly what allows a magnet to sort scrap. Classifying metals by magnetic test, explaining the terms ferrous and non-ferrous and recording a fair test are examinable.

## Subtopic: Testing the Tray

Set out the tray and predict first. Many learners guess that all metals are magnetic, because metals are shiny and hard. Then test each object by bringing the pole of the magnet close and lifting. The steel nail jumps to the magnet; so do the washers, the food tin, the galvanised fence wire and most of the spoon. The copper wire does nothing, the aluminium can does nothing, the brass key does nothing, and the lead sinker does nothing. Record each result as attracted or not attracted in the table before moving on, and note anything surprising.

The pattern becomes clear when you ask what the attracted objects are made of. Nails, washers, tins and fence wire are all steel, and steel is mostly iron. The spoon is stainless steel, an alloy of iron with chromium, and many stainless steels are magnetic though a few are not, which is a surprise worth writing down. Iron, nickel and cobalt are the only common metals that a magnet attracts, and because iron is by far the most common of the three, metals containing iron are called ferrous, from the Latin word ferrum for iron. Everything a magnet ignores, copper, aluminium, brass, lead, zinc, gold and silver, is non-ferrous.

The food tin is a trap. It is called a tin can, but tin itself is non-magnetic; the can is steel with a very thin coating of tin to stop rust, and the magnet feels the steel underneath. The galvanised wire is steel coated with zinc for the same reason, and zinc is non-magnetic, yet the wire sticks. Coatings do not change what a magnet feels, because the magnetic force passes straight through a thin layer of non-magnetic metal to the iron inside. Appearance is a poor guide; the test is the only guide.

The questions for this section are with you now: the results of the tray test, the meaning of ferrous and the tin-can trap.

## Subtopic: Why Only Iron

Why does a magnet attract iron and ignore copper, when both are grey, heavy, shiny metals? The answer lies inside the metal. Iron, nickel and cobalt are built so that tiny regions inside them, called domains, each behave like a minute magnet. In an ordinary nail the domains point in all directions and cancel out, so the nail is not a magnet. Bring a magnet near, and the domains swing round to line up with its field; the nail becomes a temporary magnet with its near end opposite in pole to the magnet, and so it is attracted. Take the magnet away and most domains relax back into disorder. Copper and aluminium have no such domains, nothing lines up, and there is nothing for the magnet to pull on.

This explains the chain of clips from last session. Each clip, touching the pole, becomes a temporary magnet and attracts the next, which becomes a magnet in turn, until the field is too weak to hold another. It also explains why a steel needle stroked repeatedly in one direction with a magnet becomes a magnet itself: the stroking lines up the domains and steel, unlike soft iron, is hard enough to hold them in place. Soft iron, which lets go of its magnetism as soon as the magnet is removed, is the material chosen for electromagnet cores, as you will see in two sessions' time.

The size of a piece of metal matters to how strongly it is held but not to whether it is attracted. A steel washer and a steel girder are both ferrous; the magnet simply cannot lift the girder. A tiny speck of copper and a copper roof are both non-ferrous and both ignored. The property belongs to the material, not the object.

For the scrap yard this is the whole trick. A pile of mixed metal contains steel, which is ferrous and magnetic, and copper, aluminium and brass, which are non-ferrous and non-magnetic. Swing an electromagnet over the pile and only the steel comes up. No sorting by eye, no handling sharp edges, and the valuable non-ferrous metal is left behind clean.

The questions for this section are with you now: domains, temporary magnets and why the material, not the size, decides.

## Subtopic: Writing Up a Fair Materials Test

An investigation is only as good as its record. Rule a table with columns for the object, the metal you think it is, your prediction, the result of the test and a note. Test every object the same way: the same pole, brought to the same distance, lifted the same way. Test each object twice, because a weak attraction can be missed on a careless first try. Where the result surprised you, as with the tin can or a non-magnetic stainless steel, write the surprise and the explanation in the note column. This is what examiners mean by evidence.

Then draw a conclusion in two sentences. First, a magnet attracts metals containing iron, nickel or cobalt, which are called ferrous or magnetic metals; steel in all its coated forms is the common example. Second, copper, aluminium, brass, lead and zinc are non-ferrous and are not attracted, so a magnet can be used to separate ferrous scrap from non-ferrous scrap. A conclusion links the result back to the scenario, and that link is what turns a science observation into a technology investigation.

Add one line on limits. A magnet sorts ferrous from non-ferrous but cannot separate copper from aluminium, which are both ignored, and it cannot tell one steel from another. The dealer will still need another method for the non-ferrous pile, perhaps sorting by colour and weight, and knowing what your solution cannot do is as valuable as knowing what it can.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: assuming every metal is magnetic because it is shiny; copper and aluminium are not. Two: trusting the name tin can; it is steel under a tin skin. Three: testing each object a different way and comparing the results; a fair test uses one method. Four: writing results with no conclusion, so the record never connects to the scrap-yard need.

The questions for this section are with you now: the table, the two-sentence conclusion and the limits of magnetic sorting.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tray of nails, cans, wire and keys — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Test Everything on the Tray

Guess first, then test. Bring the magnet to each object and lift. Steel nail: sticks. Washers, food tin, fence wire: stick. Copper wire, cooldrink can, brass key, lead sinker: nothing. Write each result as you go.

Everything that stuck is made of steel, and steel is mostly iron. Only iron, nickel and cobalt are pulled by a magnet. Metals with iron in them are called ferrous. Copper, aluminium, brass, lead, zinc, gold and silver are non-ferrous and a magnet ignores them.

The tin can is a trick. Tin is not magnetic, but the can is steel with a thin tin skin, and the magnet feels the steel underneath. Galvanised wire is steel under zinc, same story. Never trust the name or the look; trust the test.

Keep the tray in mind and try the questions for this part: what stuck, the word ferrous and the tin trick.

## Subtopic: Why Iron and Not Copper

Inside iron are tiny regions called domains, each a mini magnet. In a plain nail they point every way and cancel. Bring a magnet close and they swing to line up, so the nail becomes a temporary magnet and is pulled in. Take the magnet away and they scatter again. Copper has no domains, so nothing happens.

That is why a chain of clips forms: each clip becomes a little magnet and grabs the next. Stroke a steel needle with a magnet, always the same way, and its domains stay lined up; it becomes a magnet. Soft iron lets go at once, which is why it is used in electromagnets.

Big or small makes no difference to whether it is attracted. A washer and a girder are both ferrous. The property belongs to the material. In the scrap pile, steel comes up on the magnet and copper and aluminium stay behind.

Hold the lined-up domains in mind and try this part's questions: temporary magnets and material versus size.

## Subtopic: Write It Up Properly

Table columns: object, metal, prediction, result, note. Test every object the same way, twice. Write surprises in the note column, like the tin can.

Conclusion in two sentences. Metals with iron, nickel or cobalt are ferrous and magnetic, mainly steel. Copper, aluminium, brass, lead and zinc are non-ferrous and not attracted, so a magnet can separate ferrous scrap from non-ferrous scrap.

Add a limit: a magnet cannot separate copper from aluminium, so the dealer needs another method for that pile.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Only iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys, chiefly steel, are magnetic; they are ferrous. Copper, aluminium, brass, lead and zinc are non-ferrous and ignored. Coatings like tin and zinc do not hide steel from a magnet. Domains lining up make iron a temporary magnet. A fair test uses one method, a table and a conclusion that links back to the scenario.

Hold the two-sentence conclusion, and take the final questions of the lesson: the table, the conclusion and the limits.
