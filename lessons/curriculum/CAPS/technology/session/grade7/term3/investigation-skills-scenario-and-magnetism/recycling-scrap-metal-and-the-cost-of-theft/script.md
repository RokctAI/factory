# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the case study of scrap-metal recycling and its social impact. The anchor is a scrap yard in Bellville in the Western Cape where, on one morning, a gleaner arrives with a trolley of cans and old gutters collected from household bins, a contractor delivers a load of off-cuts from a building site, and a stranger offers a bundle of thick copper cable with the insulation burned off. The governing idea is that recycling metal saves ore, energy and landfill space and gives work to collectors, that ferrous and non-ferrous scrap follow different routes to steel mills and non-ferrous smelters, that theft of copper cable, manhole covers and railway fittings is a crime that damages infrastructure and endangers lives, and that dealers are required by law to record sellers and refuse suspicious metal. Explaining the benefits of recycling, distinguishing legitimate collection from theft and describing a dealer's responsibilities are examinable.

## Subtopic: Where the Metal Goes

Metal does not wear out the way paper or plastic does; a steel can melted down makes steel as good as new. That is why scrap is valuable. Ferrous scrap sorted by the dealer is baled, sold to a steel mill and melted in an electric arc furnace to make reinforcing bar, roof sheets and new cans. Non-ferrous scrap goes to separate smelters: copper to be refined into new wire, aluminium to become new cans and window frames, brass to become new taps. South Africa recycles a large share of its steel and most of its aluminium cans, and recycled metal supplies a big part of what the mills need.

Recycling saves three things. It saves ore, because every tonne of scrap steel is a tonne of iron ore that stays in the ground. It saves energy, because melting scrap uses far less energy than smelting ore; recycled aluminium uses only about five percent of the energy of making aluminium from bauxite. And it saves landfill, because metal that is collected is not buried. Those savings are why a dealer can pay for scrap at all: the mill pays less for scrap than for new metal, but far more than nothing, and the difference is shared along the chain back to the collector.

Draw the chain in your record as a flow: household or site, collector, dealer who sorts and bales, mill or smelter, new product, back to the household. Mark where the magnet crane sits in the chain, at the dealer's yard, and note that its job of separating ferrous from non-ferrous is what makes the two routes possible. A clean, sorted load fetches a better price, so the crane earns money as well as saving time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two routes for scrap, the three things recycling saves and where the crane sits in the chain.

## Subtopic: The Gleaners

Across South African towns, people push trolleys and pull carts along the streets on refuse day, taking cans, broken appliances, wire and off-cuts from bins and skips before the municipal truck arrives. They are called gleaners, waste pickers or reclaimers, and there are tens of thousands of them. They sort what they find, carry it to a buy-back centre or a scrap dealer, and are paid by the kilogram. For many it is the only income they have, and together they divert a very large share of the country's recyclable material from landfill at no cost to the municipality.

Gleaning is honest work, and it has dignity and skill. A good gleaner knows which metals pay, can tell aluminium from steel by the magnet trick you learned, strips the valuable copper from a broken motor and knows which dealer pays fairly. Many are organised into cooperatives with identity cards, and some cities now register them and give them protective gloves and trolleys. When you think about the scrap dealer's yard, remember that the metal in the pile mostly arrived on a trolley pushed by a person who walked many kilometres for it.

A technology project should consider the people as well as the product. A crane that sorts faster helps the dealer, but it also helps the gleaner if it means the dealer can pay promptly and fairly for a mixed load instead of refusing it or discounting it. Write a sentence in your investigation about who benefits from your design and how; it shows you have thought about the whole system, not only the machine.

The questions for this section are with you now: who gleaners are, what they do and how the crane affects them.

## Subtopic: Theft and the Responsible Dealer

Now the other load at the gate. Copper is worth so much per kilogram that thieves cut live electricity cables, telephone lines and railway signalling wire, burn off the insulation and sell the bare copper. They lift cast-iron manhole covers, leaving open holes in roads and pavements, and steal brass taps, bronze plaques and even the copper earthing straps from substations. The damage is enormous. A stolen cable worth a few hundred rand as scrap can leave a suburb without power for days, stop trains, and cost many thousands to replace. People have died falling into uncovered manholes and from touching cables that thieves left live.

The law answers this. Dealers must be registered, must record the name, identity number and address of every seller, must keep the metal for a period before melting or selling it on, and must refuse metal that is clearly infrastructure: burned cable, manhole covers, railway fittings. Buying such metal is a crime for the dealer as well as the thief. Some metals, like copper cable, may only be traded under special permits. A responsible dealer turns away the stranger with the burned cable and reports it, even though the copper would fetch a high price.

How do you tell honest gleaning from theft? Ask where the metal came from and whether it had an owner who agreed to part with it. A can from a bin was thrown away; a gutter from a demolition was given by the owner; a bundle of cable with burnt insulation was cut from someone's supply. The burn marks are the clue, because insulation is burned off to hide the cable's origin and to lighten the load. Manhole covers and railway sleepers plates have no honest second-hand source at all.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all scrap is rubbish nobody wants; metal is valuable and recycled almost completely. Two: looking down on gleaners; they do skilled, honest work that keeps metal out of landfill. Three: believing that a dealer who buys stolen metal is innocent; the law holds the buyer responsible too. Four: imagining cable theft is a small crime; it cuts power, stops trains and kills.

The questions for this section are with you now: what is stolen and why it matters, the dealer's legal duties and how to tell gleaning from theft.

## Subtopic: From Bin to New Can

Metal can be melted and used again as good as new, so scrap is worth money. Steel scrap goes to a steel mill and comes back as roof sheets and cans. Copper, aluminium and brass go to separate smelters and come back as wire, cans and taps.

Recycling saves ore in the ground, saves a great deal of energy, and keeps metal out of landfill. Recycled aluminium uses only about one twentieth of the energy of new aluminium. Those savings are why the dealer can pay for scrap.

## Subtopic: The People With the Trolleys

On refuse day, people push trolleys down the street taking cans, wire and broken appliances from bins before the truck comes. They are gleaners, also called waste pickers or reclaimers. There are tens of thousands in South Africa, paid by the kilogram at the dealer.

It is honest, skilled work. A gleaner knows which metal pays, uses a magnet to tell steel from aluminium, strips copper from old motors and knows the fair dealers. Many have cooperatives and identity cards. Most of the metal in the dealer's pile arrived on a trolley.

## Subtopic: Stolen Metal

Copper is so valuable that thieves cut live cables, burn off the plastic and sell the bare wire. They steal manhole covers, railway fittings and brass taps. A cable worth a few hundred rand as scrap can black out a suburb for days, stop trains, and people have died in open manholes.
