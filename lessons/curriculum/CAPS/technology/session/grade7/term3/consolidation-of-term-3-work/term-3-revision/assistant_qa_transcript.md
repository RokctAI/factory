### subtopic_1 — Magnetism and the Metals
**Assistant:** How do I decide whether an examination question wants "ferrous" or "magnetic"? They seem to mean the same thing.

**Tutor:** They overlap so much in Grade 7 that either is usually accepted, but they are not the same word, and knowing the difference lets you choose the one the question is pointing at. Ferrous means containing iron. Steel is ferrous because it is mostly iron; cast iron is ferrous; stainless steel is ferrous even though some grades are barely magnetic. Magnetic, in the everyday sense, means attracted to a magnet. Almost everything that is ferrous is magnetic and almost everything magnetic in a scrap yard is ferrous, which is why the scrap trade talks about ferrous and non-ferrous and sorts them with a magnet. But nickel and cobalt are magnetic and not ferrous, and a few stainless steels are ferrous and hardly magnetic, so the words part company at the edges. For the examination, read what the question is about. If it is about the scrap dealer, recycling, the sorting of metals or the price of copper against steel, the word is ferrous and non-ferrous, because that is the vocabulary of the case study and of the industry. If it is about the physics, what a magnet attracts, the test with a bar magnet, the result of a practical investigation, magnetic and non-magnetic is the natural pair, and the question will often have used those words itself. If it asks you to name metals that are not attracted, list copper, brass, aluminium, lead and zinc, and you are right under either heading. The word that is never right is plain metal: a sentence that says the crane's magnet picks up metal is wrong on both vocabularies and undoes the whole investigation. A safe habit for a sentence you are unsure of is to use both: the electromagnet lifts ferrous metals such as steel because they are magnetic, and leaves non-ferrous metals such as copper and aluminium, which are not. That sentence cannot lose a mark.

### subtopic_2 — Circuits and Electromagnets
**Assistant:** If I am given a picture of a machine in the exam and cannot tell what mechanism it is, what should I do?

**Tutor:** Work from the signatures and the three questions, and write what you can see even if you cannot name the whole machine. Start with the three system questions: what goes in, which is usually a hand, a foot or a motor; what comes out, which is whatever the machine does to its load; and what is in between. Then look at each part in between for its signature. A rigid bar that turns about a fixed point is a lever; find the pivot, then see whether the load or the effort is nearer it to name the class. An arm with a handle sticking out of an axle is a crank, and you can say at once that it is a second-class lever and that a longer handle makes it easier. A grooved wheel with a rope is a pulley; if it is fastened to something fixed it changes direction only, if it hangs on the load it halves the effort, and you count the ropes holding the load for the advantage. Two circles of different sizes turning together are a wheel and axle. Toothed wheels meshing are gears, and the smaller one turns faster. A slope, a point or a thread is an inclined plane, wedge or screw. Write each one you identify, in order from input to output, as a block diagram if the question gives space, and say for each whether it multiplies force, multiplies speed or changes direction. Markers award marks per mechanism identified and per correct statement about it, so a half-identified machine described carefully earns more than a wrong guess at its name. If a calculation is asked, look for the two distances or two forces given and divide the larger distance by the smaller, or the load by the effort, and state the trade: the effort moves further. And if the machine is a crane, which in this year's paper it may well be, you already know the answer, because you built it: crank, drum, string, fixed pulley, electromagnet, a chain with the advantage in the crank.

### subtopic_3 — Cranks, Pulleys and Mechanical Advantage
**Assistant:** Which parts of term three are most likely to be in the end-of-year examination, so I know where to spend my time?

**Tutor:** The examination covers the whole year, so term three will share the paper with the Jaws of Life, structures and the term-four work, and within term three the weight follows the time the syllabus gave each strand and the skills that can be tested on paper. Spend the most time on three things. First, the electromagnet: how to make one, the role of the iron core, the three ways to strengthen it and the comparison with a permanent magnet, because it is the heart of the term's PAT and it is easy to set as a structured question with a diagram. Second, circuit diagrams: drawing one from a description with correct symbols, and reading one to say why a bulb does not light, because every paper has a circuit, and the marks go to a ruler, open switch, correct symbols and labels. Third, mechanical advantage with the crank and pulley: a calculation from given distances or forces, the class of the crank as a lever, fixed versus movable pulleys and the rope count, because these are the kind of short calculation and reasoning questions examiners like. After those, revise magnetism's facts, poles, field, ferrous and non-ferrous, domains, making and destroying a magnet, which appear as quick one- or two-mark questions, and the impact context of recycling and theft, which appears as a short written answer. The drawing skills, oblique and flow charts, are examinable too, often as a small drawing or an ordering task, so know the four flow chart shapes and the 45 degree rule. What is least likely is the detail of making, such as how to glue a mast, because that was marked in the PAT; what is most likely is anything that can be asked with a diagram and a few lines. Revise by doing: draw the circuit, do the calculation, label the magnet, rather than by reading, because the paper will ask you to do exactly those things.
