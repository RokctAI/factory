# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the consolidation of term three for the end-of-year examination and it revisits four strands in sequence, each tied back to the crane PAT that gave them purpose. The anchor is a revision table in a classroom in Mahikeng laid with a bar magnet, a compass, a dish of mixed metals, the class electromagnet on its nail, a cell, switch and bulb, a cotton-reel crank and a bead pulley, which are picked up in turn as each strand is summarised and questioned. The governing idea is that magnetism, electric circuits, electromagnets, cranks and pulleys and mechanical advantage are the term's examinable content, that each has a small set of definitions, facts and skills the paper tests, and that the crane links them: the magnet sorts because only ferrous metal is attracted, the circuit switches the electromagnet, the crank and pulley lift with a mechanical advantage paid for in distance. Each strand is examinable in the end-of-year paper.

## Subtopic: Magnetism and the Metals

A magnet has two poles, north and south, and the poles are where its force is strongest. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract, and a freely suspended magnet turns to point north because the Earth itself is a magnet, which is how a compass works. The region round a magnet where it exerts a force is its magnetic field, shown by field lines running from north to south, closest together where the field is strongest, which iron filings or a plotting compass reveal. Cut a magnet in half and each half has two poles; there is no such thing as a single pole.

Only ferrous metals, iron and steel and a few others such as nickel and cobalt, are attracted to a magnet. Copper, brass, aluminium, lead, zinc, gold and silver are non-ferrous and are not. This is the whole basis of magnetic sorting at the scrap yard and of your crane. Inside iron are tiny regions called domains, each a small magnet; in an unmagnetised nail they point every way and cancel, in a magnet they are lined up. Stroke a nail with a magnet in one direction and the domains line up, so the nail becomes a magnet; hammer it, heat it or drop it repeatedly and they scatter, so it loses its magnetism. Soft iron magnetises and demagnetises easily, which makes it right for an electromagnet core; hard steel keeps its magnetism, which makes it right for a permanent magnet.

Examination questions on this strand ask for the law of poles, for a labelled field diagram, for ferrous and non-ferrous examples with the test that tells them apart, for how to make and destroy a magnet, and for why a scrap dealer uses a magnet to sort. The one-word error that loses marks is "metal" where "ferrous metal" is meant, and the recycling context from the case study, honest gleaning against the theft of copper cable and manhole covers, can appear as a short-answer question on the impact of technology.

The questions for this section are with you now: poles, fields and the compass, ferrous and non-ferrous, domains and making and destroying a magnet.

## Subtopic: Circuits and Electromagnets

A simple electric circuit needs a source of energy, the cell; a conductor, the wire, to carry the current; a control, the switch; and an output such as a bulb or buzzer. Current flows only when the loop is complete, so an open switch anywhere stops it. The circuit diagram uses symbols: a cell as a long thin and a short thick line, the long line positive; a switch as a gap with a slanted line, drawn open; a bulb as a circle with a cross; a buzzer as a half circle; wires as ruled straight lines with right-angle corners. Components in one loop are in series and share the same current, so a second bulb in series makes both dimmer; a branch beside a component is parallel and gets the full cell voltage. Conductors let current through, metals above all; insulators such as plastic, rubber, glass and dry wood do not, which is why wire is coated.

A current in a wire produces a magnetic field around the wire, as Oersted's compass showed. Wind the wire into a coil, a solenoid, and the field concentrates so the coil behaves like a bar magnet with poles that swap when the current reverses. Put a soft iron core inside and the core's domains line up, multiplying the field hundreds of times. That is an electromagnet. It is strengthened by more turns, more current and a soft iron core, and weakened by turns wound in opposite directions, a non-ferrous core or bare patches shorting the turns. Its great advantage over a permanent magnet is that it can be switched off, adjusted and reversed, which is why the crane uses one to release its load; its disadvantage is that it needs a supply and fails if the supply fails.

Examination questions ask you to draw a circuit from a description with correct symbols, to say why a bulb does not light in a given faulty circuit, to describe how to make an electromagnet and three ways to strengthen it, to compare electromagnet and permanent magnet, and to name uses of electromagnets such as bells, relays, scrap cranes, loudspeakers and motors. Draw with a ruler, label every symbol, and show the switch open.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four parts of a circuit and the symbols, series and parallel, the electromagnet and its strengthening.

## Subtopic: Cranks, Pulleys and Mechanical Advantage

Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort, the number of times a mechanism multiplies the force you apply, with no unit. Every mechanism that multiplies force makes the effort move further by the same factor, so force is traded for distance and nothing is gained for free. A chain of mechanisms multiplies its advantages together, and complex machines are chains of simple mechanisms, each a system with input, process and output. Levers come in three classes by the position of pivot, load and effort; a second-class lever has the load between the pivot and the effort and always multiplies force.

A crank is an arm at right angles to an axle with a handle at its end, converting a push into rotation. It is an adaptation of the second-class lever: pivot at the axle, load at the drum close to it, effort at the handle far out, with mechanical advantage equal to handle radius over drum radius. A pulley is a grooved wheel turning on an axle with a rope in the groove, a wheel and axle used to guide a rope with little friction. A fixed pulley changes the direction of a force without changing its size, advantage one; a movable pulley hangs on the load and halves the effort while doubling the distance; the advantage of a pulley system is the number of rope sections supporting the load. Friction makes the real advantage a little less than the calculated one, so it is measured.

In the crane the hand turns the crank, advantage about six, the string runs over a fixed pulley at the arm's tip, advantage one, and the electromagnet lifts the load, a chain with a total of six. The crane is also a frame structure, strengthened by tubes, triangulation, gussets and a weighted base, which the paper may ask about alongside the mechanism. Questions on this strand ask you to identify mechanisms in a picture, name the class of a lever, calculate a mechanical advantage from given distances or forces, explain why a crank is a second-class lever, distinguish fixed from movable pulleys and give the advantage of a drawn pulley system.

The error museum, four exhibits for the whole term. One: "metal" for "ferrous metal". Two: a circuit drawn freehand with the switch closed and a bulb that is a plain circle. Three: a fixed pulley said to halve the effort. Four: a crank called a first-class lever because the handle goes round the axle; the load sits between pivot and effort, so it is second class.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition and trade of mechanical advantage, the crank and the pulley, the crane's chain and the term's error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the revision table, four objects and the crane on the end of it — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Magnets

Two poles, north and south. Like poles push apart, unlike pull together. A hanging magnet points north, which is a compass. The field is drawn as lines from north to south, closest where strongest. Cut a magnet and you get two magnets.

Only ferrous metals, iron and steel, stick to a magnet. Copper, brass, aluminium do not. That is why the crane sorts. Inside iron are domains: lined up, it is a magnet; jumbled, it is not. Stroke to make, hammer or heat to destroy. Soft iron for electromagnets, hard steel for permanent magnets.

Say ferrous. Remember the gleaners and the cable thieves for an impact question.

Keep the compass needle in mind and try the questions for this part: poles, ferrous metals and domains.

## Subtopic: Circuits and the Electromagnet

Cell, wire, switch, bulb in a closed loop. Open the switch anywhere and everything stops. Symbols with a ruler: long and short line, gap with a slant drawn open, circle with a cross. Series shares current; parallel gets full voltage. Metals conduct; plastic insulates.

Current makes a field. Coil it and it is a bar magnet you can switch. Iron core inside multiplies it hundreds of times. Stronger: more turns, more current, soft iron. It switches off, so the crane can drop its load.

Bells, relays, speakers, motors, scrap cranes all use electromagnets.

Hold the switched-off nail dropping its clips in mind and try this part's questions: the loop, the symbols and the three ways to strengthen.

## Subtopic: Cranks, Pulleys and Advantage

Advantage is load over effort. Six means six times the force and six times the distance. Chains multiply.

Crank: pivot at the axle, load at the drum, effort at the handle, so second class; handle over drum. Pulley: a grooved wheel and axle for rope. Fixed changes direction, movable halves effort; count the ropes holding the load.

Crane: crank six, pulley one, total six, on a frame stiffened with tubes, triangles and a weighted base.

Pocket summary of the term. Magnets have two poles and attract only ferrous metals because domains align. A circuit is a closed loop of cell, switch, conductor and output drawn with standard symbols. A coil with an iron core is an electromagnet that can be switched off and strengthened by turns, current and core. A crank is a second-class lever, a pulley a wheel and axle, and mechanical advantage is load over effort, paid for in distance and multiplied along a chain.

Hold the crane on the end of the table, and take the final questions of the term: the crank, the pulley, advantage and the summary.
