# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the crane PAT with the Evaluate and Communicate stages: a class rubric, peer evaluation of models and a team presentation. The anchor is a classroom in Soweto with six finished cranes on the front benches, a dish of mixed metals and a ruler at each, a blank rubric grid on the board and a timer set to four minutes per team. The governing idea is that a rubric converts the specifications into criteria, each with levels of achievement and points, so that evaluation is objective, consistent and fair, that evaluating another team's work by testing against the rubric rather than by opinion is a skill, that feedback names what worked and what to improve with reasons, and that a presentation communicates the design process from brief to tested model with the sketches, working drawing and flow chart as evidence, and explains the science of sorting, strengthening an electromagnet and the value of recycling. Evaluation and communication are marked PAT stages and are examinable.

## Subtopic: Building the Rubric

A rubric is a scoring table. Down the left are the criteria, the things being judged; across the top are the levels of achievement, usually three or four, each with a description and a number of points; and in each cell is what that level looks like for that criterion. Its purpose is fairness: every model is judged against the same statements, by anyone, and gets the same score whoever holds the pen. Without a rubric, evaluation drifts toward liking the tallest crane, the neatest paint or your friends' work.

Take the criteria from the specifications, because the specifications were the promise and the rubric checks whether it was kept. Lifting: lifts ten clips or the washer, three points; five to nine, two; one to four, one; none, zero. Releasing: all dropped within one second, three; most, two; some, one; none, zero. Sorting: no non-ferrous pieces carried over, three; one, two; two, one; more, zero. Reach: lifts to 150 millimetres and moves 200, three; meets one of the two, two; falls short on both, one; cannot move the load, zero. Stability: stands unaided through all three tests, three; lifts at the back but does not fall, two; must be held, one; falls, zero. Indicator: bulb on whenever the magnet is on, two or zero. Add two criteria for the design folder: the working drawing matches the model and is dimensioned, and the flow chart was followed and ticked, each with a three-point scale. Total: twenty-two.

Write the level descriptions so that two evaluators would agree on them without discussion. "Good stability" is an opinion; "back edge of the base does not lift during any of three tests" is an observation. Agree the rubric as a class before anyone tests, write it on the board and copy it onto each evaluation sheet. Agreeing the rules before the game is what makes the game fair, and the PAT rubric that your teacher uses to mark you was written in exactly this way.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a rubric is, criteria from the specifications with point levels, and writing observable descriptions.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Another Team Objectively

Each team moves to another team's bench with the rubric, the dish of mixed metals and a ruler, and runs the tests, not watches them. Objectivity means the evidence decides. Lower the magnet, lift, count the clips, release and count the stragglers; measure the height with the ruler at the top of the lift; mark the bin and measure the move; watch the base's back edge; check what crossed to the bin. Three runs, as the rubric says, and the score for each criterion follows from the observed level, not from how hard the team worked or how the crane looks.

Fairness has rules. Test every crane the same way, with the same dish, from the same height, using the owners' switch as they direct, since a crane handled roughly by a stranger is not being tested, it is being broken. Do not help, and do not hinder; if a string jams, note it, stop, and let the owners clear it, then restart that run. Score what you saw, not what the team says it did yesterday. If two evaluators disagree, go back to the rubric's words and the observation, not to persuasion. Sign the sheet, because an evaluation that someone will stand behind is more careful than an anonymous one.

Feedback goes under the scores, in two parts. What worked, with the reason: "the lattice mast did not sway because every face had a diagonal." What to improve, with the reason and a suggestion: "two clips stayed on after release because the nail is hard steel; a softer nail would drop them." Three of each is enough. Feedback that is only praise teaches nothing and feedback that is only fault-finding is not heard; name the strength first, then the improvement. Hand the sheet to the team, and receive your own with the same grace: read it, ask about anything unclear, and keep it for the written evaluation, because a reflective paragraph on peer feedback is part of the PAT.

When the sheets are in, the class compares. Where every evaluator gave the same score, the rubric worked. Where scores differ, find the cell whose words were vague and sharpen it, which is how a rubric is improved and how examiners calibrate before marking. That discussion is itself the lesson in evaluation.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing rather than watching, the rules of fairness and the two-part feedback.

## Subtopic: The Team Presentation

The presentation is the Communicate stage, four minutes per team, with every member speaking. It follows the design process in order, because that is the story the PAT tells. One: the brief, the need of the scrap dealer and what the crane had to do. Two: the investigation, held up as evidence: the magnet and metals results, the crane pictures and what was borrowed from them. Three: the design, with both sketches shown and the reason the chosen one won, then the working drawing and the circuit diagram. Four: the making, with the flow chart and what took longer than planned. Five: the test results against the specifications, including what failed and what was fixed. Six: a live demonstration, one lift from pile to bin. Finish with what the team would improve.

Three explanations must be in it, because the syllabus names them. How magnetic sorting works: only ferrous metals, iron and steel, are attracted to a magnet, because their atoms form domains that line up in the field, while copper, brass and aluminium are not, so an electromagnet lifts the steel and leaves the rest, and switching it off releases the steel into the bin. How the electromagnet could be strengthened: more turns of wire, more current from an extra cell, a thicker or longer soft iron core, and tidy insulation so no turns short, with the caution that more current means more heat. Why recycling scrap metal matters: it saves the ore, the energy and the water that making new metal needs, aluminium especially, keeps metal out of landfill, gives gleaners an honest living, and must be distinguished from theft of copper cable and manhole covers, which costs lives and services.

Present with evidence in hand. Hold up the sketch when you talk about the sketches; point to the dimension on the drawing when you say 350; show the ticked flow chart; read the test numbers from the table. Speak to the class, not the floor, one member per stage, and rehearse once against the timer. Answer questions briefly and honestly, and if the demonstration fails, say what you think went wrong and what you would check, because a calm diagnosis in front of the class is worth more than a lift that works by luck.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a rubric with opinions in its cells, "good", "neat", "nice", so two evaluators cannot agree. Two: scoring a friend's crane higher than the tests showed, which is unfair to every other team and is noticed. Three: a presentation that shows only the model and skips the brief, sketches and drawing, so the design process earns nothing. Four: saying "the magnet picks up metal" instead of "ferrous metal", undoing a term's investigation in one word.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six-part presentation, the three required explanations and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a scoring table on the board, another team's crane in front of you and four minutes to speak — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Make the Scoring Table

A rubric is a scoring table: criteria down the side, levels and points across the top, and in each box what that level looks like. It makes judging fair, because everyone uses the same words.

Criteria come from the specifications: lifting, releasing, sorting, reach, stability, indicator, plus the drawing matches and the chart was followed. Three points for fully met, down to zero.

Write boxes you can see, not feel: "back edge never lifts" rather than "stable". Agree it as a class before testing starts.

Keep the table on the board in mind and try the questions for this part: what a rubric is, where criteria come from and observable words.

## Subtopic: Judge Fairly

Go to another team's crane and run the tests yourself: count, measure, watch the base, check the bin. Three runs. Score what you saw.

Same dish, same height, owners press their own switch. Do not help or hinder. Disagree? Go back to the rubric's words. Sign the sheet.

Feedback: three things that worked and why, three things to improve and how. Strength first. Take your own sheet with the same grace.

Hold the signed sheet in mind and try this part's questions: testing, fairness and feedback.

## Subtopic: Tell the Story

Four minutes, everyone speaks, in design-process order: brief, investigation, two sketches and the choice, drawing and circuit, flow chart and making, test results, one live lift, what you would improve.

Three explanations: only ferrous metals stick to a magnet, so switching off drops the steel in the bin; more turns, more current, a soft iron core and good insulation make the magnet stronger; recycling saves ore, energy and water and gives gleaners a living, unlike cable theft.

Hold up the evidence as you speak. If the demo fails, diagnose calmly.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A rubric turns specifications into observable criteria with points, agreed before testing. Evaluate by testing, the same way for all, scoring what you see, with strength-first feedback. Present the whole process with evidence, explain sorting, strengthening and recycling, and demonstrate.

Hold the ticked flow chart in your hand, and take the final questions of the lesson: the presentation order, the explanations and the summary.
