# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the first making period of the crane PAT and it produces the electromagnet and its circuit. The anchor is a bench in Kimberley laid out from the flow chart: a 100 millimetre soft iron nail or mild steel bolt, two metres of enamelled or thin insulated copper wire, a cell holder with one or two 1.5 volt cells, a push switch, a bulb in a holder, crocodile-clip leads or thin connecting wire, insulating tape, sandpaper, a dish of paper clips, a 20 gram washer and a results table. The governing idea is that making follows the design documents, the circuit diagram and the specifications, that the coil is wound tightly in one direction with stripped ends, that the circuit is a series loop of cell, switch, coil and bulb or a parallel bulb if the design chose it, that every stage is tested against the specification and recorded, and that cells, blades and hot glue are handled by stated safety rules. Making to a design and testing against specifications are marked PAT skills.

## Subtopic: Preparing the Core and Winding the Coil

Begin with the flow chart open and the circuit diagram beside it, because making is following the plan, not improvising. Check the core. It must be soft iron or mild steel: hold it to a permanent magnet for ten seconds, pull it away, and confirm it will not pick up a clip on its own. If it does, it is hardened steel and will not let go; swap it. Wrap one turn of tape over the nail's head and point so the wire's insulation is not cut by the sharp edges when you pull it tight.

Measure 150 millimetres of free wire and hold it along the nail with your thumb. Wind from near the head toward the point, each turn tight against the last, all in the same direction, counting aloud in tens. Fifty turns makes a first layer on a 100 millimetre nail with thin wire; continue back toward the head for a second layer to reach 100, still in the same direction of rotation, which is the detail most teams get wrong when they reverse at the end of the first layer. Leave 150 millimetres free at the finish, and tape the coil in two places so it cannot unwind. Strip 10 millimetres of insulation from each free end with sandpaper for enamelled wire or by nicking the plastic with the craft knife on the cutting mat and pulling it off with fingers, blade moving away from the hand. Bright bare copper at both ends is the test.

Write on the results table: core material and length, wire type, number of turns, date, team. Those are the variables of your electromagnet, and if the test is weak you will change one of them, so they must be on record. Label the coil with a flag of tape and the team name; by the end of the period the bench will hold six coils that look identical.

The questions for this section are with you now: checking the core, winding tight and one way in two layers, stripping the ends and recording the variables.

## Subtopic: Wiring the Circuit from the Diagram

Lay the components on the bench in the positions of the circuit diagram: cell holder, push switch, coil, bulb holder, in a loop. Connect them in that order with leads, each bare end gripped firmly by a clip or screwed into a terminal, so that the circuit on the bench is the diagram made real. Series means one loop, each component's second terminal joined to the next component's first. Leave the switch for last so nothing is live while you connect. Press the switch. The bulb should light. If the bulb lights, current is flowing through the coil as well, which is the whole point of the indicator.

If the bulb does not light, work round the loop. Is the cell in the holder the right way round and making contact? Are both wire ends bare where the clips grip, not gripping insulation? Is the bulb screwed down? Is the switch's internal contact working, which you can test by bridging it with a lead? A circuit with one break anywhere is dead everywhere, so one fault stops everything. Fix the break, press again.

If your design chose the bulb in parallel across the coil so the magnet gets the full cell voltage, connect it so: the bulb's two terminals joined to the coil's two ends, with the switch between the cell and this pair so that it controls both. Press, and both bulb and coil are live. Whichever you build, it must match the diagram in the design folder; if a change is made on the bench, go back to the folder now and alter the diagram with a dated note explaining why. The PAT marks the match between what was designed and what was made, and an honest annotated change scores better than a silent one.

Safety while wiring. Never connect the coil directly across the cell without the switch, because the coil's resistance is low and the cell will drain and the wire heat quickly. Never hold the switch down for more than ten seconds at a time in testing, and feel the coil between tests; warm is acceptable, hot means stop. Keep the cells away from loose clips and wire scraps that could short them. The craft knife is used only on the mat, blade away from the body, and returned to its sleeve between cuts.

The questions for this section are with you now: building the loop from the diagram, fault-finding round the loop, parallel bulb and the safety rules.

## Subtopic: Testing Against the Specification

The specification says the electromagnet must lift at least ten clips or a 20 gram washer on one cell and release the whole load within one second of the switch opening. Test it exactly so. Lower the nail point into the dish with the switch pressed, lift, and count the clips that come up. Release the switch and count how many remain hanging after one second; the specification wants none. Repeat three times and record each count in the table, because one lucky lift proves nothing and the average is the result. Then test the washer: does the nail hold it flat against the point, and does it drop on release.

If the magnet lifts fewer than ten clips, change one variable at a time and retest, recording each change. Add turns, a third layer of fifty, and expect roughly a proportional gain. Add a second cell in series and expect a further gain with more heat; check the coil temperature. Check that the windings all run one way, since a reversed layer can halve the strength. Check for bare patches on the coil where turns short across each other, especially near the nail head. If the bulb is in series and the magnet remains weak, switch to the parallel bulb and note the change in the diagram. If clips remain hanging after release, the core is too hard; swap it for a softer nail and retest.

Record the final result as the specification is written: clips lifted, average of three; washer lifted, yes or no; release within one second, yes or no; cells used; turns. Write a one-line verdict: meets specification or does not, and if not, what will be changed next period. Then fit the hanging loop: tie a short string round the coil near the head, with a knot that will not slide, leaving a loop to attach to the crane's string, and tape the coil's two leads along the string for the first 100 millimetres so the weight of the wire does not pull on the coil ends.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reversing the winding direction when starting the second layer, so the two layers cancel. Two: clips gripping insulation instead of bare copper, so nothing works and the team blames the cell. Three: testing once, getting twelve clips, and writing it down as proof; test three times. Four: holding the switch for a minute to show the class, which heats the coil and flattens the cell before the real test.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three-trial clip test, changing one variable at a time, recording the verdict and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a nail, a reel of wire and a dish of clips — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Wind the Coil

Open the flow chart and the circuit diagram. Check the nail: hold it on a magnet, pull away, and make sure it does not pick up a clip by itself. Tape the head and point so they do not cut the wire.

Leave a hand-length free. Wind tight turns from head to point, counting in tens, all the same way. Fifty makes a layer; come back for a second layer, still turning the same way, to 100. Tape it. Strip 10 millimetres off each end until the copper shines.

Write down core, wire, turns and team on your table. Flag the coil with your name.

Keep the shining copper ends in mind and try the questions for this part: the core test, winding one way and the record.

## Subtopic: Wire It Up

Lay the parts out like the diagram: cell, switch, coil, bulb, in a loop. Clip bare end to bare end. Switch last. Press: the bulb should light, which means the coil is live too.

No light? Go round the loop: cell the right way, ends bare, bulb tight, switch working. One break kills the whole loop.

Parallel bulb if your design says so, with the switch controlling both. Build what you drew, or change the drawing with a note. Never coil straight across a cell. Ten seconds at a time. Knife on the mat, blade away.

Hold the lit bulb in mind and try this part's questions: the loop, fault-finding and the safety rules.

## Subtopic: Test It Properly

Press, dip, lift, count the clips. Let go, count what still hangs after one second. Three times. Average. Then the washer.

Under ten? Change one thing at a time: more turns, a second cell, check the direction, check for bare patches, move the bulb to parallel. Clips stay hanging? Softer nail.

Write the verdict: meets the specification or not, and what to change. Tie a string loop round the coil and tape the leads along it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Test the core, tape the ends, wind tight and one way to 100 turns, strip the ends bright. Build the loop from the diagram, switch last, bulb as indicator, fix one break at a time. Test three times against ten clips and one-second release, change one variable at a time, record everything, write the verdict.

Hold the results table, and take the final questions of the lesson: the test, the fixes and the summary.
