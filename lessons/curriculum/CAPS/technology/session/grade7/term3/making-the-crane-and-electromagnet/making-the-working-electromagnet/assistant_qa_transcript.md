### subtopic_1 — Preparing the Core and Winding the Coil
**Assistant:** Our coil lifts fifteen clips but two of them stay stuck when we let go. Does it fail the specification, and what do we do?

**Tutor:** As the specification is written, it fails, because the release clause says the whole load within one second, and two clips hanging on is not the whole load. But it is a near miss with a clear cause and a clear fix, which is exactly the kind of result a good evaluation is built from. Clips that stay are held by residual magnetism, the small amount of magnetism left in the core after the current stops. Soft iron loses almost all of it at once; mild steel keeps a little; hardened steel keeps a lot. Your nail is probably mild steel at the harder end, and the stronger you make the coil, the more residue it leaves, which is why the problem appears just as the magnet gets good. Three fixes, in order of ease. First, tap it: a real scrap crane does not rely on perfect release either, and a tiny jolt as the magnet reaches the bin shakes off the stragglers, but for the test that is a workaround, not a fix. Second, swap the core for a softer one, a plain bright wire nail rather than a blued or hardened one, and retest; this is usually the whole answer. Third, if you cannot swap, reduce the current slightly, one cell instead of two, which lowers the residue, and make up the strength with more turns, which does not. Record the whole episode: fifteen clips lifted, two retained, cause identified as a hard core, action taken, retest result. In the PAT this earns more than a coil that happened to work first time, because it shows the test-fix-retest loop in action, and in the written evaluation it becomes a paragraph about how a real crane designer chooses the magnet's core and why a scrap yard still bangs the magnet against the bin.

### subtopic_2 — Wiring the Circuit from the Diagram
**Assistant:** Why can we not just connect the coil straight to the cell to see if it works, and put the switch in afterwards?

**Tutor:** Because a coil of thin wire has very little resistance, and a cell connected straight across a very low resistance is a short circuit in everything but name. Two things happen at once. The current rises to whatever the cell can supply, several amps for a fresh alkaline cell, which heats the thin wire fast enough that the insulation can soften in under a minute and your fingers will not want to hold it; and the cell dumps its stored energy at that rate, so the cell you need for the test and for the crane is a good part of the way to flat before the switch is ever fitted. Neither breaks anything on the first try, which is why it feels harmless, but a team that tests this way all period arrives at the real test with a warm, weakened coil and a tired cell and wonders why it lifts six clips. The switch is not an afterthought; it is the component that makes the circuit usable, because it limits the time the current flows to the seconds you actually need, and in the crane it is what lets the operator drop the load. There is also the indicator bulb to consider. In series it limits the current to a safe value as well as showing the state, so the loop with the switch and bulb is kinder to the coil and the cell than the bare connection. If you want a quick check that the coil is alive before the switch arrives, touch one lead to the cell for one second with the other already connected and watch for a clip to twitch; that is a tap, not a connection. Then build the loop as drawn. The habit you are forming is the one every electrician has: nothing is energised until the circuit is complete and controlled.

### subtopic_3 — Testing Against the Specification
**Assistant:** Does it matter if our 100 turns are a bit untidy, with some crossing over? It was hard to keep them neat.

**Tutor:** It matters less than winding direction and more than you might hope, and the reason is worth understanding. The magnetic field of the coil comes from current circling the core, and a turn that crosses over another still circles the core, so a crossed turn is not lost; the field inside is set mainly by how many turns there are and how much current flows, not by how neatly they lie. In that sense an untidy coil of 100 turns, all wound the same way round, is nearly as strong as a neat one. Three things do go wrong with untidiness, though. First, crossed turns pile up, so the coil becomes fat in one place and the outer turns sit further from the core, where they contribute slightly less field; the loss is small, a few percent. Second, where wires cross at an angle under tension, the insulation is pinched and can crack, especially with enamelled wire, and a cracked spot lets current jump between turns, which cuts out part of the coil and can lose you far more than a few percent; this is the real danger. Third, a lumpy coil is hard to tape and tends to spring loose. So the practical rule is: direction perfect, count accurate, neatness as good as your patience allows, and then test. If your coil lifts ten clips, its untidiness has cost you nothing that matters for the PAT. If it lifts seven, before you add turns, run a finger over the coil feeling for a sharp cross-over near the head, and look for a bright nick in the enamel; if you find one, that turn is probably shorting, and rewinding the top layer neatly will give you more than a third layer would. Then in the evaluation you can write, truthfully, that neat winding matters for insulation rather than for magnetism, which is a more sophisticated sentence than most examiners expect.
