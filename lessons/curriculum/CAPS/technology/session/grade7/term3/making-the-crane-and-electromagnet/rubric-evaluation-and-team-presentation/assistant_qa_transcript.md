### subtopic_1 — Building the Rubric
**Assistant:** Why do we evaluate another team's crane instead of our own? We know ours best.

**Tutor:** Because knowing it best is exactly the problem. You know how hard the mast was to roll, how late you stayed, and how it lifted twelve clips on Tuesday, and all of that leaks into a score that is supposed to measure only what the crane does today against the specifications. Another team knows none of it and sees only the crane, which is what a customer, an examiner or the scrap dealer would see. That is what objective means: the evidence decides, not the history. There is a second reason, which is that evaluating is a skill in its own right, and you learn it by doing it on something you did not make. Running the tests on an unfamiliar crane forces you to read the rubric's words carefully, to notice what the crane actually does rather than what its makers say it does, and to write feedback that a stranger can use. Those are the skills the examiner uses on your work, and seeing the process from the other chair makes your own next design better; most teams, after evaluating another crane, can name three things they would change on their own without being told. The third reason is fairness across the class. If each team marked itself, scores would reflect confidence rather than cranes, and the rubric would be pointless. Cross-evaluation with a shared rubric, three runs and signed sheets gives every team a score produced the same way. You do still evaluate your own crane, in the written evaluation that follows: there you compare your results against the specifications, respond to the peer feedback, and say what you would improve. That self-evaluation is better for having been through the other team's eyes first, because you have just practised being honest about a crane you did not love.

### subtopic_2 — Evaluating Another Team Objectively
**Assistant:** What do we do if the other team's crane is clearly better built than ours but fails one of the tests?

**Tutor:** Score the test, and let the rubric handle the rest. The rubric has separate criteria for separate things precisely so that a crane can be strong in one and weak in another and receive a score that says so. If their mast is a beautifully triangulated lattice that never sways, that earns full points under stability and a sentence of praise in the feedback, and if their magnet carries a piece of foil across to the bin, that costs a point under sorting and earns a sentence of advice, perhaps that the foil was trapped between clips rather than attracted, which is a mechanical problem not a magnetic one, and that lowering the magnet more gently or using a shallower dish would cure it. The two judgements do not cancel or blend; they sit in their own rows. What you must not do is let the general impression of quality push the failed test up to a pass, or let the one failure drag down criteria it has nothing to do with. Both are the drift that rubrics exist to stop. If you find yourself wanting to adjust a score because the crane deserves it, that is the moment to reread the cell's words and ask what you observed. There is one legitimate adjustment: if the failure was caused by the test, not the crane, such as the evaluator knocking the dish or the string catching on the ruler, the run does not count and is repeated, and the rubric says three valid runs. Record that on the sheet. The honest result for a well-built crane with one weak specification is a high total with one low row and clear feedback on that row, and that is a far more useful document for its team than a rounded-up perfect score, because it tells them exactly which afternoon's work would make it perfect.

### subtopic_3 — The Team Presentation
**Assistant:** Our demonstration crane worked all week but we are worried it will fail in front of the class. How do we prepare?

**Tutor:** Prepare the way an engineer prepares a demonstration: rehearse the exact sequence, control the variables, and have a plan for failure. First, rehearse the demonstration itself, not just the talk, twice, with the person who will operate it doing every step: press, lower, lift, swing, release. Use the same dish and clips you will use in front of the class, and put the dish and the bin at marked positions on the bench so nothing is improvised. Second, control the variables that change between Tuesday and today. Fit fresh or freshly tested cells, because a cell that has been pressed all week is tired, and a weak cell is the most common cause of a demonstration that lifts six clips after lifting twelve all week. Check the wire ends in their clips and the bulb in its holder. Wind the string onto the drum in one layer so it cannot jam. Check that the stop peg is in place. Third, decide in advance what you will say if it fails, because the class and the teacher are watching how you respond as much as whether it works. "The magnet is weak; I think the cell is low, we will swap it and try again" is a diagnosis, and the rubric for communication rewards it; silence or blame does not. Bring a spare cell and a spare pre-wound coil if you have one, and keep your results table on the bench so that if the live lift fails you can still show the three recorded tests from the making period, which are the evidence the PAT actually marks. Finally, keep the demonstration short: one lift, pile to bin, is enough, and a second attempt is allowed if the first is unlucky. Teams that rehearse once and bring a spare cell almost always succeed, and the few that do not still score well because they explain what went wrong with the confidence of people who understand their machine.
