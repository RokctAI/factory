### subtopic_1 — Base, Mast and Arm
**Assistant:** Our mast tube is glued but it still bends a little at the top when the load swings. Is that a fail, or can we leave it?

**Tutor:** A little flex at the top of a 350 millimetre card tube under a swinging load is normal and is not a fail on its own; the specification is that the crane lifts 150 millimetres, moves the load and stands, not that nothing moves. The question to ask is whether the flex costs you a specification. Measure the lift height at the moment the load is highest; if the bend drops the magnet below 150 millimetres, or lets the arm sag so the load does not clear the bin's edge, you must stiffen. If the height is still met and the crane stays up, record the flex as an observation, fix it if there is time, and mention it in the evaluation as an improvement. The stiffening options cost little. The most effective is a tie: a string from the arm's tip back over the mast top and down the far side to the back of the base, pulled taut, which turns the arm and mast into a triangle and takes the bending out of the mast altogether; real tower cranes have exactly this tie bar. Second, brace the mast foot higher: the four gussets at the base can be replaced by card triangles reaching 50 or 60 millimetres up the mast, which shortens the free length that bends. Third, slide a second, slightly smaller rolled tube inside the first for the bottom half, which doubles the wall thickness where the bending is greatest. Fourth, if the lattice option is open, a single diagonal skewer from the base to the mast at mid-height on the side away from the load acts as a strut. Do one of these, retest, and write it down. In the PAT the marks go to a team that saw a weakness, understood it as bending in a long member, and applied the right term-two remedy, so a tube that flexed and was then tied is worth more than one that happened to be stiff enough.

### subtopic_2 — Winch, Pulley and Assembly
**Assistant:** The string keeps jumping off the bead at the arm's tip when the magnet swings. What is going wrong?

**Tutor:** The bead is not behaving as a pulley because it has no groove to hold the string in line, and a swinging load pulls the string sideways across the bead's surface until it slides off the edge. A real pulley has a groove deep enough that the string sits in it and cannot climb out even when pulled at an angle, and the fix is to give your tip pulley a groove or a pair of walls. Three ways. First, the quickest: glue a card disc either side of the bead, slightly larger than the bead, so the string runs in the channel between them; those are the cheeks a real sheave has. Second, make a proper grooved pulley from three card discs, two large and one small, glued concentrically and pushed onto the wire, with the small one forming the bottom of the groove. Third, if you have a plastic bead with a flared shape, turn it so the string runs round the narrowest part. While you are there, check two other things that make the string wander. The pulley's axle must be at right angles to the arm and horizontal; if the wire is bent so the bead leans, the string runs downhill off it. And the string must arrive at the pulley in its own plane, which means the guide at the mast top and the drum below must be in line with the arm; a string that comes up the mast off to one side approaches the pulley at an angle and tends to climb off. If the magnet itself swings a lot, hang it on a short piece of stiffer string or add a small swivel so it does not twist, and lower it into the pile gently rather than dropping it. Record the fix: string derailed, cause no groove, cheeks added, retested, held through three lifts. That is a sentence any engineer would recognise.

### subtopic_3 — The Whole-Crane Test
**Assistant:** We are running out of time. Which parts of the crane can we leave rough and which must be right?

**Tutor:** Decide by the specifications, because that is how the model will be judged, and by what cannot be fixed later. Must be right: everything in the path from the hand to the load. The crank must be firmly fixed at right angles and the drum must turn freely between its cheeks, because a stiff or wobbling winch makes every test fail. The string must run straight from the drum, up the mast, along the arm and over a grooved pulley, because a derailed or rubbing string stops the lift. The mast must stand upright on the base with gussets, and the arm must be stiff enough to hold the magnet and load at 150 millimetres; those are structure and they cannot be bolted on at the end. The cells and switch must be at the back and the circuit wired as drawn with the bulb working, because sorting and indicating are specifications. Can be rough: anything that is appearance only. Card edges need not be trimmed square, tape may show, the tubes need not be the same colour, and the base may be marked with pencil crosses, none of which the rubric scores. Can be simplified without penalty if time is short: the stop peg can be a loop of string that catches the handle; the tie from arm to mast can be a single knot rather than an adjustable one; the cheeks round the pulley can be tape flags rather than cut discs. Can be deferred to the evaluation period: the final trimming of string, the label, and the photograph. One more thing cannot be cut: the test itself, three runs recorded against the specifications. A rough crane with three recorded tests scores better than a neat one with none, because the Make stage is marked on function and evidence, and the presentation next week needs the numbers. So if ten minutes remain, stop building, run the tests, and write them down.
