# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the design and communication stage of the crane PAT with a flow chart of the sequence of manufacture. The anchor is a classroom in Nelspruit where a team has its working drawing and a sheet of A4 on which, using a ruler and the standard flow chart shapes, it plans the making of the crane and electromagnet so that the four making periods are used without waiting. The governing idea is that a flow chart is a diagram of a sequence of steps in time, drawn with an oval for start and end, a rectangle for each process step, a diamond for a decision with yes and no exits, and arrows for the order, that steps are written as short instructions, that the sequence is arranged so parts that dry or set are made early and tested parts are checked before assembly, and that the chart is read back as a rehearsal of the build. Flow charts are examinable and the sequence of manufacture is a marked PAT product.

## Subtopic: The Language of Flow Charts

A flow chart shows the order in which things happen. It is different from the block diagram of a machine, which shows how force passes through mechanisms, and from a working drawing, which shows sizes; a flow chart shows time, step after step. It uses a small set of shapes, each with one meaning, so that anyone who knows the shapes can read any flow chart. An oval, sometimes called a terminator, marks the start and the end. A rectangle holds one step of the process, written as a short instruction beginning with a verb: cut the base, roll the mast tube, wind the coil. A diamond holds a decision, written as a question with a yes or no answer: does the magnet lift ten clips? Two arrows leave a diamond, one labelled yes and one labelled no, and only one arrow leaves every other shape. Arrows join the shapes in order and are drawn with a ruler, running down or across the page, never crossing if it can be helped.

A parallelogram is sometimes used for an input or output, such as collect materials or hand in the model, and a small circle with a letter is a connector when a chart runs over two columns or pages. For the crane, ovals, rectangles, diamonds and arrows are enough. Keep every box the same width so the chart reads as a column, and keep the text inside to a few words; detail belongs on the working drawing, not in the flow chart.

Flow charts are used wherever a process must be done the same way every time: a factory assembly line, a recipe, the steps a mechanic follows to diagnose a fault, the safety procedure for a fire alarm, a computer program. In the examination you may be given a jumbled list of steps for making something and asked to draw them as a flow chart in the right order with a decision, or given a flow chart and asked what happens when a test fails. Both depend on knowing the shapes and reading the arrows.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a flow chart shows, the meaning of each shape and the rule for arrows.

## Subtopic: Ordering the Crane's Build

Write the steps on scrap paper first, as a list, before drawing any shape. Begin with what the team collects: card, skewers, string, nail, wire, cells, switch, bulb, glue, tape, tools. Then the parts, grouped by what they depend on. The base must be cut and, if doubled, glued and left to set, so it comes early. The mast tube must be rolled and glued and left to dry, so it comes early too. The arm, whether a tube or a triangulated section, is the same. While glue dries, hands are free, so the electromagnet is wound then: strip the wire ends, wind 100 turns, tape the coil. The winch drum and handle are assembled. The pulley is made and fitted to the arm's tip.

Now the first test, as a diamond: does the electromagnet lift ten clips on one cell with the switch pressed and release them when it opens? If no, the arrow goes back to a rectangle, check connections, add turns or a second cell, and returns to the test. If yes, continue. Then assembly: fix the mast to the base; fit the arm to the mast with gussets or a sleeve; fit the winch at the mast foot; run the string from the drum up the mast and over the pulley; tie the magnet to the string; wire cells, switch, coil and bulb as in the circuit diagram; fix the cells and switch at the back of the base.

The second test, another diamond: does the loaded crane lift the clips 150 millimetres, move them to the bin and release them without tipping? If no, the arrow returns to a rectangle, add counterweight or brace the arm, then to the test again. If yes, finish: trim string and tape, label the model with the team name, and stop at the end oval. Number the steps so the chart can be referred to on making day: step 7, wind the coil, is what the team member with free hands does while step 4, glue the base, dries.

Think about who does what. A flow chart drawn as a single column assumes one pair of hands, but a team of four has four. A good chart for a team draws two or three columns side by side where steps are independent, base and mast in one, electromagnet in another, winch in a third, and brings them together with arrows into a single assembly column. That is how a real factory draws its process, and it is what makes four periods enough.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing before drawing, what comes early and why, and the two tests with their no loops.

## Subtopic: Drawing and Checking the Chart

Draw the chart on A4 in portrait with a ruler. Start oval at the top. Rectangles of the same width down the page, about 15 millimetres apart, each with a number and a short verb instruction. Diamonds for the two tests, wide enough for the question, with yes continuing down and no branching to the side into the fix rectangle and an arrow back up to the step before the test. End oval at the bottom. If the chart is too long for one column, run it into a second column with a connector circle, or use the team layout with parallel columns merging. Give it a title: Sequence of Manufacture, Model Crane with Electromagnet, the team, the date.

Then read it as a rehearsal. Walk through the chart with the team, pointing at each box and asking who does it, with what, and how long it takes. Where two steps need the same tool, the one hot-glue gun, move one earlier or later. Where a step depends on glue drying, check that something useful fills the gap. Where a step needs a part that has not been made yet, the order is wrong; fix it. Add up the minutes and compare with four periods. A chart that comes to six periods is a warning in week seven, which is far better than a discovery in week nine.

Keep the chart in the design folder with the sketches and working drawing, and bring it to the making periods, because it is the team's agenda: at the start of each period the chart shows what is done, what is next and who is free. Tick each box as it is completed. In the evaluation in week nine, a ticked chart with notes on what took longer than planned is evidence for the PAT and for the written reflection.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: steps in rectangles written as nouns, "base", "coil", instead of instructions, "cut the base", "wind the coil". Two: a decision diamond with one exit, so the chart cannot show what happens when the test fails. Three: assembly before parts, such as fitting the arm to a mast that has not yet been rolled. Four: no test until the very end, so a weak electromagnet is discovered after it is wired, strung and glued into the crane.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing the chart neatly, the rehearsal read-through and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a ruler, four shapes and the crane's build laid out like a recipe — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Four Shapes

A flow chart shows the order of steps in time. Oval: start and end. Rectangle: one step, written as an instruction with a verb. Diamond: a yes-or-no question, with a yes arrow and a no arrow. Arrows, ruled, join them in order.

Same-width boxes, a few words each, no crossing arrows. Details live on the working drawing.

Recipes, factory lines, safety procedures and computer programs are all flow charts. In the exam you may have to put jumbled steps in order or say what happens when a test says no.

Keep the four shapes in mind and try the questions for this part: what each shape means and the arrows.

## Subtopic: The Crane as a Recipe

List first. Collect materials. Cut and glue the base. Roll and glue the mast. Make the arm. While glue dries: wind the coil, make the winch, make the pulley.

Test one: does the magnet lift ten clips and let go? No: fix and test again. Yes: carry on. Assemble: mast to base, arm to mast, winch, string over the pulley, magnet on, wire the circuit, cells at the back.

Test two: does the loaded crane lift, move, drop and stay standing? No: add counterweight or brace, test again. Yes: trim, label, end. Draw parallel columns so four pairs of hands work at once.

Hold the glue drying while the coil is wound in mind and try this part's questions: order, the two tests and the columns.

## Subtopic: Draw It, Walk It

Portrait A4, ruler. Start oval at the top, numbered rectangles down the page, diamonds for the tests with no branching sideways and looping back, end oval at the bottom, title and team at the top.

Walk through it with the team: who, with what, how long. Move steps that fight over the glue gun. Fill drying gaps with useful work. Add up the time against four periods.

Bring it to making days and tick the boxes. The ticked chart is evidence for the evaluation.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Oval, rectangle, diamond, arrow. Steps as instructions, in dependency order, glued parts early, tests before assembly, no loops back to a fix. Draw it ruled, rehearse it aloud, tick it as you build.

Hold the ticked chart, and take the final questions of the lesson: layout, the rehearsal and the summary.
