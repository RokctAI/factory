### subtopic_1 — The Language of Flow Charts
**Assistant:** Why do we need a decision diamond at all? If the magnet does not work we will obviously fix it.

**Tutor:** You will, but the chart is not written for the moment when the magnet fails; it is written for the moment before, when the team is deciding what to do next and has three jobs competing for attention. The diamond does two things that an obvious intention does not. First, it fixes where the test happens. Without a diamond, the natural thing on making day is to keep building, because building feels like progress, and the magnet gets tested for the first time after it has been tied to the string and wired to the switch inside the finished crane. If it is weak then, the fix means unwiring, untying and rewinding with the glue already set around the cells, which costs most of a period. The diamond after the coil is wound says: stop here, test now, with the coil loose on the bench, when adding fifty turns takes three minutes. Second, the diamond names the fix. Its no arrow goes to a specific rectangle, check the bare wire ends and connections, add turns or a second cell, which is a decision the team made calmly in week seven rather than in a panic in week eight, and the arrow back to the test says keep going round until it passes. That loop is the heart of every quality system in industry: make, test, fix, test again, and only then move on. There is also an examination reason. A flow chart question will almost always include a decision, because a chart without one is just a list, and the marks are for drawing the diamond with a question inside, two labelled exits and a loop back. So the diamond is not stating the obvious; it is deciding in advance when to stop and look, and what to do when what you see is wrong.

### subtopic_2 — Ordering the Crane's Build
**Assistant:** How detailed should each step be? Is "make the mast" one step or five?

**Tutor:** It depends on who will read the chart and what they already know, and the rule of thumb is one step per action that someone could be assigned and could tick off. For your team's chart, make the mast is too coarse, because it hides decisions and times that matter for planning: cut the card to size, roll it round a pencil, glue the edge, tape it while it dries, and that drying is exactly the gap you want to see on the chart so that another step can fill it. So the mast is probably three steps: cut the mast card, roll and glue the mast tube, set aside to dry, with the arrow from set aside leading to the wind the coil column rather than straight to the next mast step. On the other hand, cut the mast card does not need to become measure the card, mark the card, pick up the scissors, because those are one person's uninterrupted actions and nobody will tick them separately. A good test is the handover: if a step could reasonably pass from one person to another, or stop for drying or for a tool, it is a separate box. A second test is time: a step that takes more than about a quarter of a period is probably hiding several steps, and a step that takes under a minute is probably part of a bigger one. For the whole crane this gives something like twenty to thirty boxes, two diamonds, and a chart that fits on one A4 page in two or three columns. In the examination, where you are given a list of jumbled steps, use exactly the steps given and do not add or merge; the marks are for the order, the shapes and the decision, not for inventing finer detail.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing and Checking the Chart
**Assistant:** Our team has four people. How do we show on the chart who does which step?

**Tutor:** Use swim lanes, which is what industry calls a flow chart divided into parallel columns, one per person or group, and it is the most useful thing you can do with this chart. Divide the page into three or four vertical lanes with a heading at the top of each: Base and mast, Electromagnet and circuit, Winch and pulley, or the names of team members. Every step goes in the lane of the person who does it, at the height on the page that shows when it happens, so that steps side by side are happening at the same time. Arrows run down each lane in order and cross between lanes only where one person's work depends on another's, for example the arrow from test the magnet in the electromagnet lane into assemble the crane in the base lane, because the magnet must pass its test before it is built in. The chart then shows at a glance that while one person waits for the base glue to dry, another is winding the coil and a third is making the drum, and that all three lanes meet at a single assembly column near the bottom. It also exposes problems: if two lanes both need the hot-glue gun at the same height, one must move, and if one lane is twice as long as the others, the work is badly shared and a step should move across. On making day each person follows their own lane and ticks their own boxes, and nobody has to ask what to do next. If the page is too narrow for four lanes of readable boxes, turn it landscape or use two lanes and write the name of the person beside each box. Either way, show the parallel work, because the reason a team of four can finish in four periods is that they do not work in a single line.
