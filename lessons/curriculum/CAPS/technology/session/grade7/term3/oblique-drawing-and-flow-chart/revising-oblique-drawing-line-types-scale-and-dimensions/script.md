# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the graphical communication of term one in preparation for drawing the chosen crane. The anchor is a desk in Rustenburg with squared paper, a 45 degree set square, an HB and a 2B pencil, a ruler and a cardboard box 120 by 80 by 60 millimetres that is drawn at half scale. The governing idea is that oblique projection draws the front face true to shape with receding edges at 45 degrees, usually at half their true length in cabinet oblique, that line types carry meaning, outlines thick and continuous, construction lines thin, hidden detail dashed, centre lines chain, that a scale is a fixed ratio between drawing and object stated on the drawing, and that dimensions are placed on thin dimension lines with arrowheads and extension lines, in millimetres, each size given once. Oblique drawing, line types, scale and dimensioning are examinable.

## Subtopic: Oblique Projection

An oblique drawing is a way of showing three dimensions on flat paper. The front face is drawn first, true to shape and size at the chosen scale, exactly as you would see it looking straight at it: a rectangle for a box, a circle for a wheel. Then every edge that runs away from you is drawn at 45 degrees to the horizontal, up and to the right, using the set square. The back face is drawn parallel to the front and joined up. On squared paper the 45 degree lines run corner to corner through the squares, which is why squared paper is the paper for this job.

There are two kinds. In cavalier oblique the receding edges are drawn at full length, which is simple but makes the box look too deep. In cabinet oblique they are drawn at half their true length, which looks more natural and is what the textbook uses. For the 120 by 80 by 60 box at half scale, the front face is 60 by 40 on the paper, and the depth of 60 becomes 30 at half scale and then 15 along the 45 degree line in cabinet oblique. Draw the front rectangle, draw a 15 millimetre line at 45 degrees from each of the four corners, join the far ends, and you have a box.

Choose the front face wisely. Put the most detailed face, the one with circles or the most information, at the front, where it is drawn true and circles stay circles. A circle on a receding face becomes an ellipse, which is hard to draw and harder to dimension. For the crane that means the side view with the mast, arm and winch at the front, and the base running back at 45 degrees.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the front face and receding edges are drawn, cavalier versus cabinet and choosing the front face.

## Subtopic: Line Types and Scale

Lines on a technical drawing are a language, and each type means one thing. A thick continuous line is a visible outline or edge, drawn with the softer 2B pencil pressed firmly. A thin continuous line is a construction line, a projection line, a dimension line or an extension line, drawn lightly with the HB. A thin dashed line is hidden detail, an edge that exists but is behind a face, such as the dowel inside the crane's mast tube. A thin chain line, long dash, short dash, long dash, is a centre line through a circle or down the axis of a symmetrical part, such as the centre of the drum. Construction lines are drawn first and left faint; outlines are gone over last and dark. A drawing in one weight of grey pencil is marked down because the eye cannot tell edge from guide.

Scale is the ratio between the size on the drawing and the size of the real object. A scale of 1:1 is full size; 1:2 is half size, every real millimetre is half a millimetre on paper; 2:1 is twice size for small parts. The scale is chosen so the drawing fills the page without running off it, and it is written in the title block as, for example, SCALE 1:2. A 350 millimetre crane will not fit on A4 at full size; at 1:2 it is 175 millimetres tall and fits with room for dimensions. Every length in the drawing is then measured off the ruler at half, but every dimension written on the drawing is the real size, never the paper size.

To work at scale without mistakes, make a small table before drawing: real size in one column, drawing size in the other, for every length you will need. Mast 350 becomes 175. Arm 250 becomes 125. Base 297 by 210 becomes 148.5 by 105, and in cabinet oblique the receding 210 becomes 52.5 along the 45 degree line. The table is the difference between a drawing done in one go and a drawing rubbed out three times.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four line types and their meanings, what scale means and the conversion table.

## Subtopic: Dimensioning Rules

A dimensioned drawing lets a maker build the object without asking a single question. The rules make that possible. Dimensions are in millimetres, with the unit stated once in the title block rather than after every number. Each dimension is written once, on the view where it is clearest, never repeated. A dimension line is a thin line parallel to the edge being measured, set about 10 millimetres clear of the outline, ending in small arrowheads that touch thin extension lines projected out from the ends of the edge. The number sits just above the middle of the dimension line, reading from the bottom or from the right of the page, and never touches the line. Smaller dimensions sit nearer the outline, larger ones further out, so dimension lines never cross one another.

Circles are dimensioned by diameter with the symbol for diameter before the number, and drawn with their centre lines crossing at the centre; holes are placed by dimensions to their centre lines, not to their edges. Radii are given with R before the number and an arrow touching the arc. On an oblique drawing, dimensions on the front face follow the ordinary rules; dimensions along a receding edge are written along a dimension line drawn parallel to that edge at 45 degrees, and give the true length, not the shortened paper length.

The title block completes the drawing. It is a rectangle at the bottom of the sheet with the title, the name of the drawer, the date, the scale and the units, and in the PAT it also carries the team name and the project. A drawing without a title block is an unfinished drawing. Check the box: 120 by 80 by 60 at 1:2, three dimensions, each once, arrowheads touching extension lines, numbers above the lines, title block filled in.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a receding edge drawn at 30 or 60 degrees because the set square was turned the wrong way; oblique uses 45. Two: writing the paper size instead of the real size on a dimension, 175 on a mast that is 350. Three: dimension lines crossing or touching the outline, with numbers sitting on the line. Four: no scale and no units in the title block, so the drawing cannot be read.

The questions for this section are with you now: dimension lines, extension lines and arrowheads, circles and receding edges, the title block and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a shoe box, a set square and a sheet of squared paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Box in 3D

Draw the front face flat and true. Then from each corner draw a line up and to the right at 45 degrees with the set square. Join the far ends. That is oblique.

Cabinet oblique draws the receding lines at half length so the box looks right. A 60 millimetre depth at half scale becomes 30, then 15 along the slant.

Put the busiest face at the front, where circles stay round. For the crane, the side with the mast and winch goes in front.

Keep the shoe box in mind and try the questions for this part: the front face, the 45 degree lines and half depth.

## Subtopic: Lines and Scale

Thick continuous: an edge you can see. Thin continuous: construction, dimension, extension. Thin dashed: hidden edge. Chain: centre line. Light first, dark last.

Scale 1:2 means half size on paper. A 350 mast is 175 on the page. Write the real size on the drawing, never the paper size. Put SCALE 1:2 in the title block.

Make a table first: real size, paper size, for every length. Then draw once.

Hold the dashed hidden line in mind and try this part's questions: four line types, what 1:2 means and the table.

## Subtopic: Putting on the Sizes

Millimetres. Each size once. A thin dimension line parallel to the edge, about 10 millimetres away, arrowheads touching thin extension lines. Number above the middle, not on the line. Small sizes near, big sizes far, lines never crossing.

Circles: diameter with the diameter symbol and centre lines. Slanted edges: a slanted dimension line, with the true length written.

Title block at the bottom: title, name, date, scale, units, team.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Oblique: front face true, receding edges at 45 degrees, half length in cabinet oblique, busy face at the front. Lines: thick visible, thin construction, dashed hidden, chain centre. Scale: a stated ratio, real sizes written. Dimensions: once each, thin lines, arrowheads, numbers above, title block filled in.

Hold the arrowheads touching the extension lines, and take the final questions of the lesson: dimension rules, circles and the summary.
