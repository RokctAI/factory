### subtopic_1 — Oblique Projection
**Assistant:** Why do we draw the receding edges at half length in cabinet oblique? Surely a drawing should show true sizes.

**Tutor:** Because oblique projection is a trick of the eye, not a true view, and the half length is a correction for the trick. When you look at a real box from the front, the edges running away from you appear shortened by perspective, and your brain expects that. Cavalier oblique ignores this and draws the depth at full length, so a cube comes out looking like a long block leaning backwards; the eye reads it as too deep because it is seeing more depth than it expects. Cabinet oblique halves the depth, and the result looks much closer to how a box appears, even though neither drawing is a true view and neither has the vanishing points of a real perspective drawing. The convention is a compromise between a drawing that is easy to construct, 45 degree lines with a set square, no vanishing points, and one that reads naturally. True sizes are not lost, because the front face is drawn true at the chosen scale, and any dimension along a receding edge is written as the true length, 60 for the box's depth, even though the pencil line is 15 at half scale and half length. This is why the dimension rule says write the real size, never the paper size; the drawing is a scaled, partly foreshortened picture, and the numbers carry the truth. If the examiner asks you to state the two kinds of oblique, name both and say which looks more realistic and why, and if a question gives you a cube and asks for cabinet oblique, draw the front true and the depth at half: a 40 millimetre cube has a 40 by 40 front and 20 millimetre slanted edges.

### subtopic_2 — Line Types and Scale
**Assistant:** How do I know what scale to choose for a drawing?

**Tutor:** Pick the largest scale that lets the whole object, its dimensions and a title block fit the sheet with a comfortable margin, and choose from the standard set rather than inventing one. The standard reducing scales are 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50 and so on, and the enlarging ones are 2:1, 5:1, 10:1. The reasoning is in three steps. First, measure the object's largest overall sizes; for the crane, roughly 350 high, 297 long including the base, and 210 deep. Second, measure the space you have; an A4 sheet in portrait is 210 by 297, and after a 10 millimetre margin all round and a 30 millimetre title block there is about 190 by 247 of drawing space, and you also need 20 to 30 millimetres beyond each outline for dimension lines. Third, divide and round to a standard scale. At 1:1 the crane does not fit at all. At 1:2 it is 175 high and about 150 long with the oblique depth adding roughly 50 to the right, so around 200 by 230 with dimensions; that fits an A4 sheet in landscape or in portrait with care. At 1:5 it is only 70 high, which fits easily but leaves the detail too small to dimension clearly. So 1:2 is right for the crane, and that is the scale the textbook expects for the PAT working drawing. For a small part you might go the other way: the winch drum at 2:1 so the groove and axle hole can be shown. Two habits save marks. Write the scale in the title block as soon as you decide it, before drawing a line, and make the conversion table at the same time so every measurement you take off the ruler is already converted and every number you write is the real size.

### subtopic_3 — Dimensioning Rules
**Assistant:** Why can we not just write all the dimensions on one view, since it would be quicker?

**Tutor:** You can put them all on one view when the object is simple enough, and the box in this lesson is dimensioned on its oblique view alone, three numbers, done. The rule is not about how many views but about two things: that each dimension appears once, and that it appears where it is clearest. Repeating a dimension seems harmless but it is a trap, because a drawing is revised, and when the mast is changed from 350 to 320 one of the two copies is forgotten and the maker builds from the wrong one. Engineering drawings have caused real factory errors exactly that way, so the convention is strict: once, and only once. Clarity decides where. A hole is dimensioned on the view where it appears as a circle, so its diameter and centre position can be read directly; its depth goes on the view where it appears as a hidden dashed rectangle. On an oblique drawing many dimensions are clear on the front face, and the depth dimensions go along the slanted edges, but a crane has details that are hidden or crowded in one view, such as the position of the drum axle through the mast or the slot the arm pivots in. For those the working drawing in the next lesson adds a separate detail or a second view, and the dimension goes there, once. A third reason for spreading dimensions is simply space: dimension lines must not cross, and the numbers must be readable, so a face with eight sizes crowded onto it becomes a face with four and a second view with the other four. The quickest drawing is not the one with the fewest views; it is the one the maker never has to query.
