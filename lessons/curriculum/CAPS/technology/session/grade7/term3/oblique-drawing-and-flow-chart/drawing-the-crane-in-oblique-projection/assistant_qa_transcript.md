### subtopic_1 — Setting Out the Sheet
**Assistant:** Our crane has a round base. How do we draw a circle in oblique?

**Tutor:** You have two good options, and the choice depends on which face you make the front. If the base is the front face, the circle is drawn true, a plain circle with a compass and chain centre lines, and the mast rises from it; but then the crane's side, with the arm and winch, becomes the receding face and all its detail is foreshortened, which is not what you want for a working drawing. The better option for a crane keeps the side view at the front, in which case the round base lies in the receding plane and appears as an ellipse. Drawing it is a term-one skill revisited: construct the oblique parallelogram that would enclose the circle, a rhombus with sides at 0 and 45 degrees, each side equal to the circle's scaled diameter but the receding sides halved for cabinet oblique, then mark the midpoints of the four sides and sketch a smooth curve through them, touching each side at its midpoint. The result is a convincing ellipse without any compass work. Dimension it on the drawing as a diameter, with the diameter symbol and the real size, placed on a dimension line parallel to one of the rhombus's sides. There is a third, pragmatic option that many draughtsmen choose: add a small plan view, the base seen from above, in a corner of the sheet, where the circle is true and can be dimensioned directly, and leave the oblique view to show the shape only. Whichever you pick, draw the enclosing rhombus faintly first, because an ellipse sketched freehand without it leans the wrong way, and remember that the mast stands at the centre of the ellipse, which is where the rhombus's diagonals cross.

### subtopic_2 — Building the Crane in Oblique
**Assistant:** The real crane will have string, which flexes. How do we draw the string and the hanging magnet when they will move?

**Tutor:** Draw the crane in one defined position, state it, and show the range if it matters. A working drawing is a snapshot, so you choose the position that is most useful for making: usually the arm horizontal, the string vertical from the pulley, the magnet hanging at some stated height, for instance 50 millimetres above the base. Draw the string as a thin continuous line, because it is a visible part with no thickness worth showing, and label it string, since a thin line could otherwise be mistaken for a construction line. Draw the magnet below it as the nail and coil, with its lowest point dimensioned from the base so the maker knows how long to cut the string. If the arm luffs up and down, draw it in the horizontal position and add a thin chain line, or a faint second outline, showing its highest position, with a note such as arm raised, and perhaps the angle. This is standard practice for moving parts: the main outline is one position and alternative positions are shown in thin chain or phantom lines. Do not try to show the swing of the arm round the mast on an oblique view; instead note it, or give the plan view a curved arrow with the angle. One more practical point: the string length is not fixed by the drawing alone, because the drum winds it up, so write it as a note rather than a dimension, for example string: 600 long, wound on drum, and let the maker trim it at the end. The purpose of all this is the same as for every working drawing: a maker who has never seen your sketch should be able to cut, assemble and set up the crane exactly as you intend, including where the magnet hangs when the handle is let go.

### subtopic_3 — Dimensions, Detail and Checking
**Assistant:** How much detail is enough? Do we have to draw every turn of wire on the electromagnet?

**Tutor:** No, and knowing where to stop is part of the skill. A working drawing shows what the maker needs to make the part and nothing that only decorates. For the electromagnet the maker needs the nail's length and diameter, where the coil sits along it, how long the wound section is, and the number of turns, and only the first three of those are drawn; the number of turns is written as a note, 100 turns enamelled copper wire, because drawing a hundred lines would take an hour and tell the maker nothing a number does not. The convention for a coil is a short band of a few diagonal lines, or simply a rectangle with a note, and in the circuit diagram it is a row of loops. The same reasoning applies elsewhere. The card tube of the mast is drawn as a rectangle with its diameter dimensioned, not with the spiral of its rolled edge. The pulley is a circle with a diameter, not a groove drawn in cross-section unless you give it a detail at 2:1 where the groove matters for making. Glue, tape and thread bindings are not drawn at all; they are notes. The test for whether something belongs on the drawing is to ask whether the maker would cut or position anything differently without it. A dimension locating the mast on the base: yes, because the maker would otherwise guess. The texture of the card: no. The position of the push switch: yes, as a labelled rectangle with a locating dimension, because its wires must reach. The brand of cell: no. Spend your pencil on the sizes and positions, your notes on quantities and materials, and your details on the two or three small assemblies, winch and pulley above all, that the maker will struggle with. A clean drawing that answers every making question in ninety minutes beats a beautiful one that answers half of them in three hours.
