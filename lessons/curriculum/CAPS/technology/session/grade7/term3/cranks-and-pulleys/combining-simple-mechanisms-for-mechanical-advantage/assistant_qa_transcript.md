### subtopic_1 — Systems Inside Machines
**Assistant:** Is a block diagram the same thing as the flow chart we are going to draw for making the crane?

**Tutor:** They look alike, boxes joined by arrows, but they answer different questions, and keeping them apart will save you marks. A block diagram, also called a systems diagram, describes how a machine works: the boxes are input, the mechanisms that process it in order, and output, and the arrows show force or movement passing from one stage to the next. For the bicycle the boxes read foot, pedal and crank, chain drive, wheel and axle, road, and the diagram would be the same whether the bicycle was built yesterday or a century ago. A flow chart describes how something is done in sequence over time: the boxes are steps in a process, such as cut the base, roll the mast, wind the coil, test the magnet, and the arrows show which step follows which. Flow charts also use special shapes, an oval for start and stop, a rectangle for a step, a diamond for a decision with yes and no arrows coming out of it, and you will draw one in week seven for the order of making the crane. So the block diagram is about the machine and the flow chart is about the maker. There is a neat overlap, which is probably why you asked: both are ways of thinking in stages, and an engineer does both for the same project, a systems diagram to design the mechanism and a flow chart to plan the build. In the examination, if the question shows a machine and asks for input, process and output, draw a block diagram; if it asks for the sequence of manufacture, draw a flow chart with the proper shapes.

### subtopic_2 — Why Advantages Multiply
**Assistant:** If advantages multiply, why not just add more and more mechanisms to the crane and lift anything?

**Tutor:** In principle you could, and in practice three things stop you, each of which is a real engineering lesson. The first is distance. Every stage that multiplies force divides distance by the same number, so a chain with a combined mechanical advantage of twenty makes your hand move twenty times as far as the load. To lift the magnet 150 millimetres you would wind two tubes of 3 metres of string through the crank, which is slow and tangles. Real cranes accept this: a tower crane's hoist drum turns many times for each metre of lift, but it is driven by a motor that does not get bored. The second is friction. Every pivot, axle and pulley wastes some of the input in rubbing, so the actual mechanical advantage is always less than the one you calculate, and each extra stage loses a little more. With card bearings and string over a skewer, a model crane might lose a third of its effort in friction, and a chain of five stages could lose most of it; you would turn hard and the load would barely move. The third is strength. Multiplying force at the output means the output parts carry that force: the string, the drum, the arm and the mast all have to be strong enough for the load you are now able to lift, and a card arm that is fine for 50 grams will fold at 500. So the designer adds stages until the effort is comfortable for the hand, and not one stage more, and then checks that every part after the multiplication is strong enough. For your crane, a crank with a handle about three times the drum's radius and a single direction-changing pulley is usually right: enough advantage to wind a loaded magnet with one finger, few enough parts to keep friction small.

### subtopic_3 — Reading a Machine You Have Never Seen
**Assistant:** You said the egg beater gears have a mechanical advantage less than one. Does that mean they are a bad design?

**Tutor:** It means they are the right design for the job, because the job is not lifting a load but moving a tool fast. Mechanical advantage is just a number describing the trade a mechanism makes, and a number less than one is a trade in favour of speed and distance at the cost of force. Whisking cream needs the beaters to travel a long way through the liquid quickly; the resistance of the cream is small, so you have force to spare and speed is what is scarce. The large gear on the crank has perhaps forty teeth and each beater gear ten, so one turn of your hand gives four turns of the beaters, a mechanical advantage of a quarter and a speed gain of four. Your hand pushes four times as hard as the cream pushes back, which is barely noticeable, and the beaters race. The same choice is made whenever speed matters more than force: a bicycle in high gear, a fishing reel, a hand drill, a salad spinner. The opposite choice, advantage greater than one, is made when force matters: a car jack, a winch, a crowbar, your crane. A good designer can say for every stage in a machine which way it trades and why. A badly designed egg beater would be one with a mechanical advantage of four, which would whisk at a crawl while your hand turned a tiny handle at great speed. So the lesson is not that advantage should be big, but that it should be chosen. When you present your crane in week nine, you can say that its crank trades distance for force because the magnet and its load are heavy for a finger, and that choice will earn you a mark for understanding, not just for building.
