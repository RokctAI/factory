### subtopic_1 — What a Crank Is
**Assistant:** A bicycle pedal goes round in a circle, so the pivot is in the middle of that circle. Does that not make it a first-class lever, with the pivot in the middle?

**Tutor:** This is the most common confusion with cranks, and it comes from looking at the circle the handle draws instead of at the lever arm itself. A lever is a rigid bar, and its class depends on where pivot, effort and load sit along that bar. The crank's bar is the crank arm, running from the axle out to the pedal or handle. One end of that bar is at the axle, which is the pivot. The other end is at the pedal, which is where you push, the effort. The load is not at the far side of the circle; it acts at the axle itself, where the chainwheel resists turning, or on a winch at the drum's surface a few millimetres from the axle. So along the bar you have pivot at one end, load right beside it, effort at the other end: load between pivot and effort, which is the definition of a second-class lever. The circle is a red herring. It is simply the path the effort end traces because the bar can keep rotating rather than stopping, and if you froze the bicycle at any instant and drew only the crank arm, you would see a short wheelbarrow with the wheel at the axle. The test that settles it is the mechanical advantage. A first-class lever can have an advantage greater or less than one depending on where the pivot sits; a second-class lever always has an advantage greater than one because the effort is always further from the pivot than the load. A crank always makes turning easier, never harder, which is the second-class signature. For the examination, say: the crank is an adaptation of a second-class lever, with the pivot at the axle, the load at the axle or drum and the effort at the handle.

### subtopic_2 — The Crank Is a Second-Class Lever
**Assistant:** If a longer handle is always easier, why do real winches and car window winders have such short handles?

**Tutor:** Because easier is not the only thing a designer wants, and the crank is a trade that has to suit the whole machine and the person using it. A long handle gives a bigger mechanical advantage, but it also makes each turn a bigger movement, so the hand travels further for the same lift, the handle needs more space to swing, and the swinging arm becomes awkward to use in a tight place such as a car door. A window winder has a handle about 60 millimetres long because it must clear your knee, and the designer gets the advantage elsewhere: inside the door a small gear on the winder drives a large toothed quadrant, so the mechanisms are combined and their advantages multiply, exactly as in the previous lesson. A boat-trailer winch has a handle of 200 millimetres because the load is a boat, there is space to swing, and the winder is standing up with a whole arm to use; but it also has gears inside and a ratchet so the boat cannot run back. A fishing reel has a short handle because speed matters more than force and the angler's wrist turns it fast. So the handle length is chosen by asking three questions: how heavy is the load, how much room is there to swing, and which part of the body turns it. For your crane the load is a magnet and a few clips, the room is an A4 base beside a mast, and the turner is a finger and thumb, so 60 to 90 millimetres is the sweet spot. If your tests show the winding is still stiff, do not lengthen the handle beyond that; shrink the drum, reduce friction at the axle with a bead or a drop of oil, or check that the string is not rubbing on the mast.

### subtopic_3 — Designing the Crane's Crank
**Assistant:** Could we use a motor instead of a crank to wind the crane? It would be more like a real crane.

**Tutor:** It would, and the answer is partly about the brief and partly about what the PAT is testing. The brief and constraints for this term's task say a crane with a crank and pulley system, driven by hand, with the only electrical part being the electromagnet circuit, and the PAT marks the making of exactly that: a working crank, a pulley at the arm's tip, a string drive. A motor would replace the mechanism you are being assessed on, so you would lose the marks for it even if the crane worked beautifully. There is also a practical point. A small toy motor spins very fast with very little force, a mechanical advantage far below one, so to wind a loaded string it would need a gearbox with an advantage of fifty or more, which is a term's project on its own, and a second switch, more cells and a way to stop at the top. Real cranes solve exactly that problem with a motor driving a reduction gearbox and a brake, which is the crank-and-gears idea with electricity supplying the turning instead of a hand. So you are right that a real crane uses a motor, and you have also just explained why: the motor is an effort source, and the gears after it are still the second-class-lever mechanism multiplying its small force into a large one. Build the hand crank this term, and if you want the motor idea on record, add a note to your evaluation in week nine saying how the crane could be improved by replacing the crank with a geared motor, and why the gears would still be needed. That earns credit for understanding without breaking the brief.
