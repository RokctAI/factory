# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the second mechanical systems lesson of term three, the crank. The anchor is a bench in Mthatha holding a bicycle crank and pedal cut from an old frame, a toy windlass made from a cotton reel on a dowel with a bent wire handle, a pencil sharpener, a fishing reel and a spring balance, with which the class measures the force needed to wind a load at three handle lengths. The governing idea is that a crank is an arm fixed at right angles to an axle with a handle at its free end, that it converts a reciprocating or circular push into rotation of the axle, that it is an adaptation of the second-class lever because the axle at the centre is the pivot, the handle at the rim is the effort and the load acts at the axle between them, and that its mechanical advantage is the handle radius divided by the drum radius. Identifying cranks, classifying them as second-class levers and reasoning about handle length are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Crank Is

A crank is an arm fixed to an axle at right angles, with a handle at the free end of the arm. Push the handle round in a circle and the axle turns. The bicycle pedal is a crank: the pedal is the handle, the crank arm is the arm, and the chainwheel axle is the axle. The windlass over an old farm well is a crank: a bent iron handle turns a drum and winds up the rope and the bucket. The pencil sharpener, the fishing reel, the hand-operated car window, the old-fashioned meat mincer, the starting handle of a vintage car, the hand winch on a boat trailer: cranks, every one.

The crank converts movement. A hand or foot pushing in a circle, or back and forth, becomes rotation of the axle. Run it backwards and it converts rotation into back-and-forth movement, which is what a crankshaft in a car engine does: the pistons push up and down, the cranks turn that into the rotation of the shaft. Think of it as the mechanism that joins straight pushes to turning wheels, and you will spot it everywhere.

Your crane needs a crank. The string that lifts the magnet must be wound in, and a finger pulling the string directly has no advantage and nowhere to store the string. Wind the string on a drum, a cotton reel or a short card tube on a skewer, fix a card or wire handle to the skewer at right angles, and you have a crank winch. Turn the handle, the drum turns, the string winds in, the magnet rises. That is the crane's hoist, and the rest of this lesson is about making it easy to turn.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a crank, the movement it converts and the crane's winch.

## Subtopic: The Crank Is a Second-Class Lever

Recall the three classes of lever from term one. In a first-class lever the pivot is between the effort and the load: a seesaw, scissors, a crowbar on a stone. In a second-class lever the load is between the pivot and the effort: a wheelbarrow, a bottle opener, a nutcracker. In a third-class lever the effort is between the pivot and the load: tweezers, a fishing rod, your forearm. The class tells you where the mechanical advantage lies: second-class levers always multiply force, because the effort is always further from the pivot than the load.

Now look at the crank through those eyes. Freeze it at one instant. The axle is the pivot, because that is what everything turns about. The handle is where the effort is, out at the end of the arm. The load is the rope on the drum, which pulls at the drum's surface, a short distance from the axle. So reading from the pivot outward: pivot at the axle, load at the drum's rim close to it, effort at the handle far out. Load between pivot and effort: a second-class lever. The crank is a second-class lever that has been bent into a circle so that it can keep turning instead of stopping at the end of a stroke. A wheelbarrow that could go round and round would be a crank.

Because it is a second-class lever, the mechanical advantage is the effort's distance from the pivot divided by the load's distance from the pivot: the handle radius divided by the drum radius. A handle 90 millimetres from the axle winding a drum of 15 millimetres radius gives an advantage of six: a load of 60 grams on the string needs an effort of only 10 grams on the handle. The price is the usual one: the handle travels six times as far round its big circle as the string travels round the small drum, so you turn more to lift the same height. Measure it with the spring balance on the bench. Hook the balance to the handle at three lengths, 30, 60 and 90 millimetres, lift the same load, and read the force each time. It falls as the handle lengthens, in proportion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three classes, why the crank is second class and the handle-over-drum calculation.

## Subtopic: Designing the Crane's Crank

Three choices decide how well the crane's crank works: handle length, drum radius and the handle's grip. Handle length sets the advantage, and the spring balance has shown that longer is easier, but a handle that sticks out 150 millimetres from a 400 millimetre crane is clumsy, hits the mast as it turns and takes a long swing of the arm for each turn. About 60 to 90 millimetres suits a finger. Drum radius sets the other half of the ratio, and a small drum, a short length of pencil or a 6 millimetre dowel, gives a big advantage but winds in only a short length of string per turn, so lifting 150 millimetres takes many turns. A cotton reel of 12 millimetre radius winds 75 millimetres a turn, two turns for the full lift, with an advantage of five to seven from a 60 to 90 millimetre handle. That is a good balance.

The handle must be at right angles to the axle and firmly fixed. A card arm glued to a skewer twists loose after ten turns; push the skewer through two layers of card arm and glue both sides, or bend a wire handle round the skewer and bind it with thread and glue. Add a short tube of card over the wire handle's tip so it turns in your fingers instead of rubbing. Fix a ratchet or a simple stop, a peg that the handle rests against, so that the load does not unwind the moment you let go, because a crane whose hoist runs free drops its load on the pile. Even a loop of string that catches the handle will do.

Mount the drum where the string runs straight. The string leaves the drum, runs up the mast and over the pulley at the arm's tip. If the drum is off to one side the string rubs on the mast, friction eats your advantage and the crane twists. Line it up in the sketch, and when you build, wind the string onto the drum in one neat layer so it does not jam under itself.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling the crank a first-class lever because "the pivot is in the middle of the circle"; the pivot is at one end of the lever arm, the load next to it, the effort at the far end. Two: making the drum large to wind fast and then finding the load too heavy to lift. Three: a handle parallel to the axle instead of at right angles, which cannot be turned. Four: no stop on the winch, so the load falls when the fingers release.

The questions for this section are with you now: handle and drum choices, fixing the handle and the stop, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a cotton reel, a bent wire and a spring balance — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Handle That Turns Things

A crank is an arm on an axle with a handle at the end. Push the handle round and the axle turns. Bicycle pedal, well windlass, pencil sharpener, fishing reel, car window winder: all cranks.

A crank turns a push into a turn, or a turn into a push, which is what the crankshaft in a car engine does.

Your crane winds its string on a drum turned by a crank. Cotton reel on a skewer, wire handle at right angles. Turn, the string winds, the magnet rises.

Keep the well windlass in mind and try the questions for this part: the parts, the conversion and the winch.

## Subtopic: A Lever Bent in a Circle

Three lever classes. First: pivot in the middle, a seesaw. Second: load in the middle, a wheelbarrow. Third: effort in the middle, tweezers.

Freeze the crank. Pivot: the axle. Effort: the handle, far out. Load: the string on the drum, close to the axle. Load between pivot and effort. Second class, bent round so it keeps turning.

Advantage: handle length divided by drum radius. 90 over 15 is six. Hang 60 grams and the handle needs 10. Test it with a spring balance at 30, 60 and 90 millimetres; the force drops as the handle grows.

Hold the wheelbarrow going round and round in mind and try this part's questions: classes, pivot, load, effort and the ratio.

## Subtopic: Build a Good Crank

Handle 60 to 90 millimetres: long enough to be easy, short enough not to hit the mast. Drum about 12 millimetres radius: enough advantage, not too many turns.

Fix the handle at right angles, through two layers of card or bound with thread and glue. Put a card tube on the tip to grip. Add a peg or loop so the load cannot unwind when you let go.

Line up the drum so the string runs straight up the mast, and wind it in one neat layer.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A crank is an arm and handle on an axle that turns pushes into rotation. It is a second-class lever: pivot at the axle, load at the drum, effort at the handle. Advantage is handle length over drum radius. Choose a 60 to 90 millimetre handle, a small drum, a firm right-angle fixing and a stop.

Hold the cotton reel on a skewer, and take the final questions of the lesson: sizes, fixings, the stop and the summary.
