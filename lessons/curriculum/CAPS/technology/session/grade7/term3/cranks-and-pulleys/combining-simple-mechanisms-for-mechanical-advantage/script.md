# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the first lesson on mechanical systems in term three and it sets up the crank and pulley lessons that follow. The anchor is a workbench in East London with a hand-cranked egg beater, a bicycle wheel, a fishing reel and a small hand drill, each of which is turned, opened and traced from the hand that drives it to the part that does the work. The governing idea is that complex machinery is built from combinations of simple mechanisms, that each mechanism is a system with an input, a process and an output, that the output of one stage is the input of the next, that each stage can change the size, direction or speed of a force, and that the mechanical advantages of stages multiply, so that a chain of modest advantages gives a large one. Recognising simple mechanisms in a complex machine, describing input, process and output, and explaining combined mechanical advantage are examinable.

## Subtopic: Systems Inside Machines

Every machine, however complicated, can be described as a system: something goes in, something happens to it, something comes out. The input is the force or movement you supply, usually with a hand or foot. The process is what the mechanism does to it, changing its size, direction, speed or type of movement. The output is the force or movement delivered where the work is done. Turn the handle of an egg beater. The input is your hand turning a crank slowly in a vertical circle. The process is a pair of gears, a large one on the crank driving two small ones on the beaters. The output is the beaters spinning fast, in a horizontal plane, at right angles to your hand. One mechanism has changed the speed, the direction and the axis of the movement.

Now trace a bicycle. Input: a foot pushing a pedal down. The pedal is on a crank, so the push becomes rotation of the chainwheel. The chain carries that rotation to a smaller sprocket on the back wheel, so the wheel turns faster than the pedals. The wheel is a wheel and axle, so the rim moves faster still but with less force than the hub. Output: the tyre pushing on the road and the bicycle moving. Four mechanisms in a row: lever, crank, chain drive, wheel and axle. The output of each is the input of the next. That is what complex machinery means: not one clever part, but a chain of simple parts, each doing one job.

Write a machine as a block diagram: a box for input, an arrow, a box for each mechanism in order, an arrow, a box for output. The egg beater has one mechanism box; the bicycle has four. The block diagram is the first thing an engineer draws when studying an unfamiliar machine, and it is the first thing you will be asked to draw in the examination when given a picture of one.

The questions for this section are with you now: input, process and output, the bicycle chain of mechanisms and the block diagram.

## Subtopic: Why Advantages Multiply

Mechanical advantage, from term one, is the number of times a mechanism multiplies the input force: load divided by effort. A lever with the effort twice as far from the pivot as the load has a mechanical advantage of two. The price of that advantage is always distance: the effort moves twice as far as the load. No mechanism gives something for nothing; it trades distance for force or force for distance.

Now put two mechanisms in a chain. A hand pushes a lever with mechanical advantage two, and the lever's output pulls a rope over a pulley system with mechanical advantage three. The lever doubles the force; the pulleys triple the doubled force; the load feels six times the hand's effort. Mechanical advantages in a chain multiply. The hand moves two times three, six times as far as the load, which is the same trade seen from the other side. A car jack is a crank turning a screw, and the screw is a very long inclined plane wrapped round a rod, so a small hand force on the crank, multiplied by the crank and multiplied again by the screw, lifts a tonne of car. A hand winch on a boat trailer is a crank driving a gear driving a drum, and a child can wind a boat up a ramp.

The same multiplication works the other way to gain speed. The egg beater's gears have a mechanical advantage less than one, so the beaters move faster than the handle with less force, which is what whisking needs. The bicycle's chain and wheel do the same, trading the leg's big slow push for the tyre's small fast push. A designer chooses each stage to trade in the direction the job needs: force for a crane, speed for a beater, and sometimes one stage of each in the same machine.

For the crane you are designing, the chain is: hand turns a crank, the crank winds a drum, the string runs over a pulley at the arm's tip and lifts the magnet. The crank gives a mechanical advantage because the handle moves in a bigger circle than the drum. The single pulley at the tip gives no force advantage, only a change of direction, from a horizontal pull along the arm to a vertical lift. Knowing which stage does what tells you where to improve the crane if the load proves heavy: a longer crank handle or a smaller drum, not a different pulley.

The questions for this section are with you now: the distance trade, multiplying advantages through a chain and the crane's own chain.

## Subtopic: Reading a Machine You Have Never Seen

The examination skill is to be shown a drawing or photograph of an unfamiliar machine and to name its mechanisms, trace its input to its output and say what each stage does to the force. Practise on the fishing reel. The handle is a crank; the crank turns a gear pair that makes the spool turn several times for each turn of the handle; the spool is a wheel and axle winding the line. Input: hand on the crank. Process: crank, gears, wheel and axle. Output: line pulled in fast. The advantage is in speed, because a fish is retrieved quickly, and the angler pays with effort, which is why big fish need big reels with lower gearing.

Practise on a hand drill. Crank handle turns a large bevel gear, which turns a small bevel gear at right angles on the chuck. The drill bit spins fast, at right angles to the crank, with less force but more speed, and the bit's cutting edges are wedges. On a wheelbarrow: a second-class lever with the wheel as pivot, the load in the middle and the effort at the handles, giving a mechanical advantage over two, and the wheel and axle under it turning sliding into rolling. On scissors: two first-class levers sharing a pivot, with wedge blades. Every machine in the room yields to the same three questions: what goes in, what does each part do, what comes out.

Learn to spot the five simple mechanisms by their signatures. A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a pivot. A wheel and axle is two different-sized circles turning together on one centre. A pulley is a grooved wheel with a rope round it. A gear is a wheel with teeth meshing with another. An inclined plane or wedge or screw is a slope, straight, pointed or wrapped. If you can see which signature each part carries, you can describe any machine, including one you will meet for the first time in the examination.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling the whole machine one mechanism, "a bicycle is a wheel and axle", when it is a chain of four. Two: forgetting that a mechanical advantage over one costs distance, so claiming a machine gives more force and more speed at the same time. Three: saying a single fixed pulley multiplies force; it only changes direction. Four: drawing a block diagram with the boxes in the wrong order, output before process.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the reel and the drill, the five signatures and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an egg beater, a bicycle and a fishing reel — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Goes In, What Comes Out

A machine is a system: input, process, output. Turn an egg beater. Input: your hand turning the handle slowly. Process: a big gear turning two small gears. Output: beaters spinning fast at right angles to your hand.

A bicycle is four mechanisms in a row. Foot pushes pedal, a lever. Pedal turns the crank. Chain carries the turn to the back wheel. Wheel and axle rolls on the road. The output of each one is the input of the next.

Draw it as boxes and arrows: input, then one box per mechanism, then output. That is a block diagram, and it is how you read any machine.

Keep the egg beater in mind and try the questions for this part: input, process, output and the boxes.

## Subtopic: Advantages Multiply

Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. A lever with advantage two doubles your push, but you move twice as far. Nothing is free: force is traded for distance.

Chain two mechanisms and the advantages multiply. A lever of two feeding pulleys of three gives six. A car jack is a crank times a screw, and a hand lifts a car. Gears can also go the other way, giving speed instead of force, which is what a beater wants.

Your crane: hand turns a crank, crank winds a drum, string goes over a pulley at the tip and lifts the magnet. The crank gives the advantage. The pulley at the tip only turns the pull from sideways to up.

Hold the car jack in mind and try this part's questions: the trade, multiplying and the crane chain.

## Subtopic: Reading Any Machine

Fishing reel: crank, gears, spool. Hand in, line pulled in fast. Hand drill: crank, two gears at right angles, spinning bit. Wheelbarrow: a lever with the wheel as pivot, and a wheel under it. Scissors: two levers on one pivot with wedge blades.

Spot the five: a bar on a pivot is a lever; two circles on one centre are a wheel and axle; a grooved wheel with a rope is a pulley; a wheel with teeth is a gear; a slope is an inclined plane, wedge or screw.

Ask three questions of any machine: what goes in, what does each part do, what comes out.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Complex machines are simple mechanisms in a chain. Each is a system with input, process and output, and each output feeds the next input. Mechanical advantage is load over effort and costs distance. Advantages in a chain multiply. The crane's chain is crank, drum, pulley, magnet.

Hold the three questions, and take the final questions of the lesson: the reel, the five signatures and the summary.
