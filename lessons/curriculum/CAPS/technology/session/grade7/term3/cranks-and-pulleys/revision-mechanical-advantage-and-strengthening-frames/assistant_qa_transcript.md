### subtopic_1 — Mechanical Advantage Revisited
**Assistant:** If the crane's advantage is six, does that mean the string pulls on the arm with six times the force of my finger? Will the arm not break?

**Tutor:** Yes to the first part, and that is exactly why the structural half of this lesson exists. Mechanical advantage multiplies force, and the multiplied force does not vanish; it appears in the string and in everything the string pulls on. If your finger pushes the handle with 10 grams-force and the crank's advantage is six, the string carries up to 60 grams-force, which is what lifts the 60 gram magnet and load. The tip of the arm feels that 60 grams pulling down, and because the arm is a lever too, with the mast joint as its pivot, the joint feels more than 60: a 250 millimetre arm with a 60 gram load at the tip makes a turning effect at the mast equal to 60 times 250, and a tie from the arm's tip back to the mast top 100 millimetres above the joint would have to carry 60 times 250 over 100, which is 150 grams-force, to hold it. So the forces inside the structure grow as you move inward from the load, and a crane that can lift more must be stronger in proportion. This is the general rule for every machine: whatever force the mechanism delivers, the frame around it has to resist, and often more at the joints than at the load. It is why you check the arm as a tube rather than a strip, brace it back to the mast, and gusset the joint. It is also a useful sanity check for the handle length: there is no point building a crank with an advantage of twenty if the card arm cannot survive the load it would let you lift. Match the mechanism's advantage to the structure's strength, and when you want to lift more, strengthen first, then multiply.

### subtopic_2 — Strengthening Frames Revisited
**Assistant:** We want to make the mast a lattice of four skewers like a real tower crane. How many diagonals does it need?

**Tutor:** More than you might think, and the counting method is worth knowing. A lattice mast of four vertical skewers has four rectangular faces, and each face is a parallelogram that will fold sideways unless it is triangulated. One diagonal per face, running from the bottom of one upright to the top of the next, turns that face into two triangles and stops it folding. That is four diagonals for a mast of one storey. A real tower crane mast is divided into bays, horizontal rungs every so often, and every bay of every face has its own diagonal, which is why the pattern looks like a row of zigzags; the rungs also shorten the uprights between braces so they do not buckle. For a 350 millimetre model mast, two bays of 175 millimetres with a card rung at the middle and the top, and a diagonal in each of the eight face panels, gives a mast that is rigid in every direction and still very light. If skewers run short, there is a cheaper trick: card gussets, small triangles glued across each corner where a rung meets an upright, do the same job as diagonals over a short distance, and a strip of card glued flat across a whole face acts as a shear panel, which is triangulation by another name because a flat sheet cannot change its shape in its own plane. Whatever you choose, test the mast before you fit the arm: hold the base, push the top sideways in both directions and twist it. If it moves, find the face that is still a parallelogram and brace it. A lattice done this way will carry the loaded arm with no visible lean, and the marker will see a team that understood term two.

### subtopic_3 — Applying Both to the Chosen Sketch
**Assistant:** Why does putting the cells at the back of the base help, when they only weigh about 50 grams?

**Tutor:** Because tipping is a contest of turning effects, not of weights, and position counts as much as mass. A crane tips when the turning effect of the load about the front edge of the base is bigger than the turning effect of everything else about the same edge. The load is small, say 60 grams, but it hangs 250 millimetres out along the arm, so if the mast stands 100 millimetres in from the front edge the load's lever arm about that edge is 150 millimetres, and its turning effect is 60 times 150, which is 9 000 in gram-millimetres. The crane's own card weighs perhaps 80 grams but sits close to the mast, say 20 millimetres behind the front edge on average, giving 1 600. Not enough: 9 000 against 1 600, and the back of the base lifts. Now put the two cells and their holder, 50 grams, at the back edge of an A4 base, 197 millimetres from the front edge. Their turning effect is 50 times 197, nearly 10 000, and the total holding the crane down becomes 11 600 against the load's 9 000. It stands. Move the same cells to the mast and their lever arm drops to 100, their contribution to 5 000, and the margin shrinks to almost nothing; mount them up the mast and you have also raised the centre of gravity, so a nudge finishes the job. This is the counterweight principle from the tower lesson, and real tower cranes use it: slabs of concrete at the back of the jib, as far from the mast as possible, so that a modest mass gives a large turning effect. The design rule for your crane is to put every heavy thing you are allowed, cells, switch, a stone if the constraints permit, as far behind the mast as the base allows, and to keep the mast itself a little behind the middle of the base so the load's lever arm is short. Then check by hanging the test load and watching the back edge.
