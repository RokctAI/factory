### subtopic_1 — A Pulley Is a Wheel and Axle
**Assistant:** If a fixed pulley does not make the load any lighter, why do we bother putting one on the crane at all? Could the string not just go over the end of the arm?

**Tutor:** It could, and the crane would work, badly. Three things happen when a string runs over a bare card edge instead of a pulley. The first is friction. A string dragged over a fixed edge rubs along its whole contact length, and on card or wood that can eat a third or more of your effort, so the crank that lifted the magnet easily with a pulley becomes stiff without one, and the mechanical advantage you worked so hard to get from the crank is thrown away at the tip. The second is wear. The string saws into the card edge, the edge cuts the string, and after twenty lifts one of them gives way, usually during the evaluation. The third is control. Over a bare edge the string wanders sideways with every swing of the magnet, so the load drops in a slightly different place each time; a grooved pulley holds the string in one line, which matters when the second bin is only 200 millimetres away. There is a fourth, subtler point about direction. The whole reason the winch can sit on the base, where it is stable and near the operator's hand, rather than out on the arm, is that the pulley turns the pull from horizontal along the arm to vertical down to the magnet. Without something at the tip to do that, the winch would have to hang at the tip itself, which would tip the crane. So the fixed pulley's job is not to multiply force but to let the force arrive from a convenient direction with almost nothing lost on the way, and on a model crane that is the difference between a hoist that works and one that jams. Real cranes have the same answer on a bigger scale: the sheaves at the jib's tip are ball-bearing pulleys precisely because every percent of friction there costs motor power.

### subtopic_2 — Fixed, Movable and the Rope Count
**Assistant:** When we tested the movable pulley the balance read 120 grams, not 100. Did we do it wrong?

**Tutor:** You did it right, and you measured something real that the rule leaves out: friction and the weight of the pulley itself. The rope-count rule gives the ideal mechanical advantage, the value you would get with a weightless pulley spinning on a frictionless axle, and it says a single movable pulley halves the effort, 200 grams to 100. Your cotton reel is not weightless, perhaps 10 grams, and it is part of what the two rope sections are holding up, so half of that, 5 grams, is added to your effort. Its wire axle rubs in the hole, and the string rubs slightly in the groove and against the hook, and that friction has to be overcome by the pulling end, which adds the rest. So 120 is the actual effort, 100 is the ideal, and the ratio of ideal to actual, about 83 percent, is called the efficiency of your pulley system. Engineers report both numbers, and a good Technology investigation does too: ideal advantage two, measured advantage 200 over 120 which is 1.7, efficiency 83 percent. Then the interesting question is how to raise it. Oil the axle and the reading drops. Use a bead with a smooth hole instead of a reel on bent wire and it drops further. Add more pulleys and the ideal advantage rises but the efficiency falls, because each pulley adds its own friction, which is why a four-pulley block of cotton reels may measure barely three. For your crane this is practical knowledge: the tip pulley should spin freely, because every gram of friction there is a gram your crank has to overcome, and if you ever add a movable pulley on the magnet, expect somewhat less than the halving the rule promises and check it with the balance.

### subtopic_3 — A Pulley at the Tip of the Crane
**Assistant:** Is a gear just a pulley with teeth? They both seem to be wheels on axles.

**Tutor:** They are cousins, both members of the wheel and axle family, and the difference is in how they pass motion on. A pulley passes motion by a rope or belt lying in its groove, so the rope can run off to any distance and round any corner, but the connection relies on friction and tension: a rope can slip on a pulley if it is slack, and it can only pull, never push. A gear passes motion by teeth meshing directly with the teeth of another gear, so there is no slipping, the two must be close together, and the motion is positive: turn one and the other must turn, in the opposite direction, by an exact ratio set by the numbers of teeth. A belt drive, which is two pulleys joined by a loop of belt, sits between them: it carries motion over a distance like a rope but keeps it going like gears, and a car's fan belt or a workshop drill's belt are examples. Which one you choose depends on the job. For lifting a load on a string, which must hang down from a tip and wind onto a drum, a pulley is the only choice, because the string has to be free to run and to carry the load in tension. For turning one shaft from another at a fixed ratio, such as the beaters of an egg beater or the hands of a clock, gears are the choice, because they do not slip and they can change speed and force precisely. Your crane uses a crank, a drum and a pulley, no gears, which is one of the reasons it has only a few parts and is quick to make. If you want to see the difference, look at a bicycle: the chain and sprockets are gears joined by a chain, a positive drive that cannot slip, and the brake cable over its little guide wheels is a pulley arrangement that only pulls. Same bicycle, both cousins, each doing the job the other could not.
