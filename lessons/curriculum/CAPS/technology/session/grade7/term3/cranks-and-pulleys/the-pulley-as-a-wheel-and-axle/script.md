# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the third mechanical systems lesson of term three, the pulley. The anchor is a doorway in a Technology room in Upington with a broom handle across the top, two cotton-reel pulleys on bent-wire hooks, a length of string, a 200 gram mass and a spring balance, with which the class measures the effort for a direct lift, a single fixed pulley, a single movable pulley and a two-pulley block. The governing idea is that a pulley is a grooved wheel turning on an axle, and therefore a wheel and axle whose job is to guide a rope with little friction, that a fixed pulley changes the direction of a force without changing its size, that a movable pulley halves the effort at the cost of doubling the distance pulled, and that the mechanical advantage of a pulley system equals the number of rope sections supporting the load. Classifying the pulley, distinguishing fixed from movable and calculating pulley advantage are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Pulley Is a Wheel and Axle

A wheel and axle, from term one, is two circles of different sizes fixed together so that they turn as one on a common centre: a doorknob and its spindle, a steering wheel and its column, a bicycle wheel and its hub. A pulley is a grooved wheel that turns freely on an axle, with a rope lying in the groove. It belongs to the wheel and axle family because it is a wheel turning on an axle, but it is used differently: instead of multiplying force between the wheel and the axle, it carries a rope round a corner with as little friction as possible. The rope cannot slip sideways because of the groove, and it does not rub because the wheel turns with it. Without the wheel, a rope dragged over a fixed bar loses much of its pull in friction and wears through; over a pulley it loses almost none.

Pulleys are everywhere a rope changes direction. The flagpole at school has a pulley at the top so that the flag is raised by pulling down. The washing line on a pulley runs out over a yard. A window blind, a sailing boat's rigging, a lift shaft, a tow truck, a gym machine, a crane: all pulleys. The crane you are designing has one at the tip of its arm, where the string turns from running along the arm to dropping down to the magnet.

Make a model pulley from a cotton reel on a wire axle, the wire bent into a hook at each end so the reel spins freely. Hang it on the broom handle, pass the string over it, hang the 200 gram mass on one end and the spring balance on the other, and pull down. Read the balance: 200 grams, the same as lifting the mass directly. The pulley has changed the direction of your pull from up to down but has not changed its size. That is a single fixed pulley, and its mechanical advantage is one.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a pulley is a wheel and axle, where pulleys are used and the single fixed pulley test.

## Subtopic: Fixed, Movable and the Rope Count

A fixed pulley is fastened to something that does not move, the broom handle, the flagpole top, the crane's arm. It changes direction only. So why use it? Because pulling down with your weight behind you is easier on the body than lifting up, because the pull can be moved to a convenient place, as at the base of a flagpole, and because a rope round a corner is the only way to lift from the tip of an arm when the winch is at the base. The crane's tip pulley is a fixed pulley doing exactly that job.

Now hang the second cotton reel differently. Tie one end of the string to the broom handle, run it down and under the second reel, then back up over the first, fixed reel and down to the spring balance. Hang the mass from the hook of the lower, movable reel. Pull, and read the balance: about 100 grams. The movable pulley has halved the effort. Look at why: two sections of rope now hold up the lower reel and its load, one rising from the load to the handle and one rising from the load over the fixed pulley, and each carries half the load. Pull the balance down 200 millimetres and the load rises only 100; you have traded distance for force as always.

The rule that comes out of this is the rope count. The mechanical advantage of a pulley system equals the number of rope sections directly supporting the load, counting the sections that run up from the movable pulley and ignoring the one you pull on if it comes off a fixed pulley. One rope section: advantage one, a fixed pulley. Two sections: advantage two, one movable pulley. Add another movable pulley and a second fixed one, which is a block and tackle, and four sections give an advantage of four: a 200 gram mass lifted with 50 grams of pull, moving a quarter as far. Ship's rigging, engine hoists and the tackle on a tow truck use blocks of three or four pulleys for exactly this reason.

Friction spoils the arithmetic slightly, so the balance reads a little more than the rule predicts, more for cotton reels on wire than for ball-bearing pulleys, and more with every extra pulley. A model with four cotton reels may give an advantage of three rather than four. Measure, do not assume, and record the measured value beside the calculated one in your table.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a fixed pulley is for, the movable pulley test and the rope-count rule.

## Subtopic: A Pulley at the Tip of the Crane

The crane's arm needs a pulley at its tip, and it has to be built so that the string runs over it freely without jumping off. A cotton reel is too heavy for the end of a card arm and shifts the centre of gravity outward, so use something small: a bead with a hole, a short length of plastic tube, or a disc of thick card with a groove cut round its edge, turning on a wire or a straightened paper clip pushed through the arm's tip. The groove is essential; a plain disc lets the string slide off sideways and then it rubs on the arm. If there is no way to groove a disc, glue two larger card discs either side of a smaller one to make a reel.

The pulley's axle must be at right angles to the arm and held on both sides, so bend the wire ends down and tape them to the arm's sides or pass the wire through two small card cheeks glued to the tip. Test by hanging the magnet and winding the crank: the string should run smoothly with the pulley turning, not dragging. If it drags, the hole is too tight or the wire is bent; a drop of oil or a slightly larger hole fixes it.

Now consider whether the crane should also use a movable pulley. The tip pulley gives no advantage, so the whole mechanical advantage comes from the crank. If your tests show the magnet and load too heavy to wind comfortably, you can add a movable pulley: tie the string to the arm's tip, run it down and under a small pulley on the magnet's hook and back up over the tip pulley to the drum. The effort halves, the magnet rises half as far per turn, and the design stage gets an extra line in your evaluation explaining why. Most model cranes do not need it, but the option is the kind of improvement the rubric rewards when it is reasoned.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a single fixed pulley halves the effort; it only changes direction. Two: counting the rope you pull as a supporting section when it comes off a fixed pulley, so getting the advantage one too high. Three: a tip pulley without a groove, so the string slides off and rubs. Four: a heavy cotton reel on the arm's tip, shifting the centre of gravity until the crane tips with its load.

The questions for this section are with you now: building the tip pulley, the optional movable pulley and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a broom handle across a doorway, two cotton reels and a bag of sand — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Wheel for a Rope

A wheel and axle is two circles turning together on one centre. A pulley is a grooved wheel on an axle with a rope in the groove: a wheel and axle whose job is to carry a rope round a corner without rubbing.

Flagpoles, washing lines, blinds, sailing boats, lifts, cranes: wherever a rope changes direction there is a pulley.

Hang a cotton reel on a broom handle, string over it, 200 grams on one end, a spring balance on the other. Pull down. The balance reads 200. Direction changed, force not. A single fixed pulley has an advantage of one.

Keep the flagpole in mind and try the questions for this part: wheel and axle, where pulleys live and the fixed pulley test.

## Subtopic: Halving the Pull

A fixed pulley is for direction: pull down to lift up, pull from the base to lift at the tip. The crane's tip pulley is a fixed pulley.

Tie the string to the handle, run it under a second reel that hangs on the load, back up over the fixed reel and down to your hand. Pull: the balance reads 100. Two ropes hold the load, each carrying half. You pull twice as far.

Count the ropes holding the load and that is the advantage. One: fixed pulley. Two: one movable pulley. Four: a block and tackle. Friction makes the real number a bit worse, so measure it.

Hold the two ropes sharing the load in mind and try this part's questions: direction, halving and counting.

## Subtopic: Fit the Crane's Pulley

At the tip of the arm use something light with a groove: a bead, a bit of tube or a card disc with a groove, on a wire through the arm, held both sides. No groove and the string slides off and rubs.

Wind the crank with the magnet hanging. The pulley should turn, not drag. Oil or a bigger hole if it drags.

Too heavy to wind? Add a movable pulley on the magnet's hook: effort halves, lift per turn halves, and write down why.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A pulley is a wheel and axle that guides a rope. A fixed pulley changes direction only, advantage one. A movable pulley halves the effort and doubles the distance. Advantage equals the number of ropes holding the load. The crane needs a light, grooved fixed pulley at its tip.

Hold the bead on a wire, and take the final questions of the lesson: the tip pulley, the option and the summary.
