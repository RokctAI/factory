# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the making of a simple working model of a Jaws of Life rescue device from plastic tubing, syringes and cardboard, the make stage of PAT 1, together with the evaluation and communication that complete the process. The anchor is a Technology bench in a school in East London over two making lessons: cutting mats, craft knives and steel rulers laid out, two syringes and a metre of clear tubing, sheets of corrugated card, split pins and glue, and a learner's own working drawing and parts list taped to the wall above the bench. The governing idea is that making is a planned sequence, mark, cut, shape, join, assemble, test, carried out safely and accurately to the drawing, with problems recorded and solved rather than hidden, and that the finished model is then judged honestly against the design brief. Planning a sequence of manufacture, using tools safely, building to the drawing, testing and evaluating against specifications and presenting the work are examinable and carry the largest share of PAT marks.

## Subtopic: Planning the Sequence and Working Safely

Before the first cut, write the sequence of manufacture: a numbered list of steps in the order they must happen. A sensible sequence for the rescue device runs: one, mark out all parts on the card from the working drawing; two, cut the base, arms, pivot block and saddles; three, laminate the arms and base by gluing two layers together and leave them to dry under books; four, punch the pivot and rod holes in the arms; five, glue the pivot block and saddles to the base; six, fit the arms to the block with a split pin; seven, make the rod and pin it to the arms and the plunger; eight, fill the syringes and tube with water and connect them; nine, mount the syringe in its saddles; ten, test, adjust and finish. Writing the sequence prevents the classic mistakes of gluing a part before a hole is punched, or assembling before glue has dried.

Safety rules are not decoration. A craft knife is used only on a cutting mat, with a steel ruler as a guide, cutting away from the body with the free hand kept behind the blade, in several light passes rather than one heavy one; the blade is retracted or capped whenever it is put down. Scissors are carried closed and points down. Syringes are never pointed at anyone and never used as water pistols. Glue is used in a ventilated space and kept off skin. The bench is cleared of offcuts as you go so that nothing slides under the ruler. A learner who works safely works accurately, because both come from the same slow, deliberate habits.

Marking out comes straight from the working drawing. Measure twice from a straight edge of the card, mark lightly with a pencil and a square, and label each part with its name so that it does not get confused with an offcut. For the corrugated card base, cut with the corrugations running along the length for stiffness. Lay out the parts to use the card economically, with straight edges shared where possible, because wasting material is a cost and an environmental impact in the sense of the fitness-for-purpose considerations.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing the sequence of manufacture, the safety rules for knives and syringes, and marking out from the drawing.

## Subtopic: Cutting, Joining and the Hydraulic System

Cutting corrugated card cleanly needs a sharp blade and patience. Score the top layer along the ruler with light pressure, then cut through with two or three more passes, keeping the blade upright so the edge is square. Arms are laminated: glue two strips face to face with the corrugations crossed at right angles, press them flat under a heavy book for ten minutes, and the result is far stiffer than either strip alone, which stops the force leaking into bending as the linkage investigation warned. The base is laminated the same way.

Holes for the split pins are punched, not cut, so the card is not torn: use a sharp pencil or a hole punch on the marked centre, through both layers, and test that the pin turns freely but without wobble. The pivot block raises the arms above the base so they can swing; it is a stack of card glued and clamped, with the pivot hole punched before it is glued down. Saddles are two card arches sized to the syringe diameter, glued to the base at the marked positions.

Joints are where models fail. The pivot pin passes through both arms and the block, with the arms crossed so that pushing their back ends apart closes the jaws; a small card washer between the arms reduces friction. The rod is a drinking straw or a strip of laminated card, cut to the length on the drawing, with a split pin at each arm end and a loop of card or tape fastening it to the plunger so that the push is transferred with no slack. If a joint is loose, the plunger moves and the jaws do not; if it is too tight, the plunger will not move at all. Adjust until the arms swing freely with no play.

The hydraulic system is filled exactly as in the investigation: both syringes and the tube under water in a basin, the hand syringe drawn full, the jaw syringe pushed in, the tube connected under the surface, bubbles tapped out and expelled, and the outside dried. The jaw syringe is then seated in its saddles and held with a band of tape. Push the hand syringe gently and watch the jaws; a crisp immediate movement means the system is bubble-free, a soft delayed one means air is trapped and the filling must be repeated.

The questions for this section are with you now: cutting and laminating card, making joints without slack and filling the hydraulic system.

## Subtopic: Testing, Evaluating and Communicating

Testing happens against the specifications in the brief, one by one, with a ruler and a load. Measure the jaw opening and record it. Place the 50 gram wooden block between the jaws, push the hand syringe and see whether the block is held without slipping. Push from 30 centimetres away through the tube. Check that the device stands unaided, that the arms do not visibly bend and that the mechanism works ten times without loosening. Write the result for each specification: met, partly met or not met, with the measurement.

Faults found in testing are normal and are fixed in the making time: a loose rod loop is retaped, a jaw that slips gets a strip of sandpaper glued as a gripping pad, an arm that flexes gets a straw rib glued along its back, an opening that is too small has the specification revised or the pivot moved. Each fix is written in the record with the date. Making is the step where the design meets reality, and the record of how problems were solved is strong evidence of understanding.

Evaluation is the honest judgement at the end. Did the model meet the brief: a working model of a hydraulic rescue device, syringe-driven, linked-lever jaws, built from the given materials, demonstrating hydraulics and levers producing a controlled force? Which specifications were met, which were not and why? What would be changed in a second version: a larger drive syringe for more grip, a lower pivot block for less wobble, stiffer arms? Evaluation also covers the process: was the sequence followed, was the time used well, were the safety rules kept? Communication finishes the project: the design record with brief, investigations, sketches, drawings, parts list, sequence, test results and evaluation, presented neatly, and a short explanation to the class of how the model shows the principle behind the real Jaws of Life.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: gluing before punching holes, so a drilled part cannot be drilled; the sequence exists to prevent this. Two: leaving slack in the rod joints and blaming the syringe. Three: filling the syringes in the air and wondering why the jaws are sluggish. Four: evaluating by saying the model is nice; evaluation lists each specification with a measured result and a proposed improvement.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing against each specification, fixing faults, honest evaluation and presenting the record.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a cutting mat, a knife, two syringes and a basin of water — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Plan First, Cut Safely

Before you cut anything, write down the order of work. Mark out all the parts. Cut them. Glue two layers together for the arms and base and let them dry. Punch the holes. Glue the block and saddles to the base. Fit the arms with a split pin. Make the rod and pin it on. Fill the syringes with water and connect them. Mount the syringe. Test. Writing the order stops you gluing a part before you have punched its hole.

Safety: a craft knife only on a cutting mat, along a steel ruler, cutting away from your body, free hand behind the blade, several light cuts instead of one hard one, cap it whenever you put it down. Never point a syringe at anyone. Clear the offcuts as you go.

Marking out: measure twice from a straight edge, mark lightly, label each part with its name. Cut the base with the ridges running along its length so it is stiff. Fit the parts together on the sheet so you waste as little card as possible.

Keep the numbered list in mind and try the questions for this part: the order of work and the safety rules.

## Subtopic: Cut, Glue, Pin, Fill

Cut card with light passes, blade upright. Make the arms stiff by gluing two strips together with the ridges crossed, pressed under a book for ten minutes. Do the same for the base.

Punch the holes with a sharp pencil or punch, never cut them, so the card does not tear. The pin should turn freely but not wobble. Build the pivot block from a stack of card, punch its hole, then glue it down. Make two card arches for the syringe to sit in.

Joints make or break the model. The pin goes through both arms and the block, arms crossed so pushing the back ends apart closes the jaws. The rod is a straw, pinned to each arm and looped to the plunger with no slack. Too loose: the plunger moves and the jaws do not. Too tight: nothing moves.

Fill under water, exactly like the investigation: no bubbles, dry the outside, seat the syringe in its arches with tape. Push gently. Crisp movement means no air. Soft and late means refill.

Hold the crossed arms and the straw rod in mind and try this part's questions: stiff card, good joints and a bubble-free fill.

## Subtopic: Test It, Judge It, Show It

Test against your specifications one at a time. Measure the opening. Put the 50 gram block in the jaws and see if it holds. Push from 30 centimetres away. Does it stand on its own? Do the arms bend? Does it work ten times? Write met, partly met or not met for each, with the number.

Fix what fails: retape a loose rod loop, glue sandpaper on slipping jaws, glue a straw along a bending arm. Write each fix down. Problems solved are good evidence.

Evaluate honestly: did it meet the brief, which specifications were missed and why, what would you change next time? Did you follow your order of work and keep the safety rules? Then communicate: a neat record with brief, tests, sketches, drawings, parts list, order of work, results and evaluation, and a short talk to the class on how your model shows the idea behind the real Jaws of Life.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Write the order of work first. Cut safely on a mat with light passes. Laminate the card, punch holes, make joints with no slack. Fill the syringes under water. Test each specification with a measurement, fix faults and record them. Evaluate against the brief and present the whole record.

Hold the test table, and take the final questions of the lesson: testing, evaluating and communicating.
