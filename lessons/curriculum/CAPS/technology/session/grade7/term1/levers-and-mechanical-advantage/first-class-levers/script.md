# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies first-class levers, in which the pivot lies between the effort and the load. The anchor is a seesaw in a park in Soweto, a crowbar lifting a loose paving slab outside a school in Mthatha, and a pair of kitchen scissors, three tools that look nothing alike yet share the same arrangement. The governing idea is that a first-class lever always reverses the direction of the force, pushing down to lift up, and that its mechanical advantage can be greater than 1, equal to 1 or less than 1 depending on whether the pivot is nearer the load, in the centre or nearer the effort. Identifying a first-class lever from the position of its pivot, predicting its mechanical advantage from the pivot position and giving everyday examples are examinable.

## Subtopic: Pivot in the Middle

The three classes of lever are distinguished by which of the three parts, pivot, load or effort, sits in the middle. In a first-class lever the pivot is in the middle, with the effort on one side and the load on the other. Because the two forces are on opposite sides of the pivot, a first-class lever always changes the direction of the force: you push down on one end and the other end goes up. The seesaw is the clearest case, with the pivot bar in the centre and a child on each end.

The crowbar shows the same arrangement at work. One end is slipped under the edge of a loose paving slab, a brick is placed under the bar near the slab to act as the pivot, and the worker presses down on the long handle. The slab lifts. Effort down, load up, pivot between them. A claw hammer pulling a nail is another: the head of the hammer rests on the wood as the pivot, the nail is the load gripped by the claw, and the hand pulls the handle on the other side.

Scissors are a first-class lever too, in fact two of them joined. The screw where the blades cross is the pivot. The handles, where the fingers push, are the effort. The blades, where the paper or cloth is cut, are the load. Each blade is a bar with the pivot between the handle and the cutting edge. Pliers and tin snips work the same way.

To identify a first-class lever, find the pivot and ask whether the effort and the load are on opposite sides of it. If they are, it is first class. Everything else about the lever, its mechanical advantage and how far the load moves, depends on exactly where along the bar that pivot sits.

## Subtopic: Three Possible Advantages

A first-class lever is the only class whose mechanical advantage can be any of three things. Where the pivot sits decides which.

Pivot nearer the load. The effort arm is longer than the load arm. A small effort moves a large load, and the mechanical advantage is greater than 1. The crowbar with the brick placed close to the slab is this case, and so is the worker's bar under the boulder from the last session. Rescue spreaders put the pivot of each arm close to the tips for the same reason: a modest push from the hydraulic cylinder becomes an enormous force at the jaws.

Pivot in the centre. The effort arm and the load arm are equal. The effort needed equals the load, so the mechanical advantage is exactly 1. The lever gives no extra strength; it only changes the direction of the force and lets you work at a distance. A seesaw with the bar fixed at its midpoint and two equal children is the example, and so is a balance scale, where equal arms let you compare two masses directly.

Pivot nearer the effort. The effort arm is shorter than the load arm, so the effort must be larger than the load and the mechanical advantage is less than 1. This looks like a bad design until you watch the load end: it moves much further and faster than the effort end. A pair of long-bladed scissors cutting far from the pivot works like this, and so does a child sitting close to the middle of a seesaw lifting a bigger child sitting far out, which needs a hard push but swings the far child high.

The pattern to remember is simple: the side with the longer arm has the smaller force. Lengthen the effort arm and the effort shrinks; lengthen the load arm and the effort grows. Only a first-class lever lets you slide the pivot to either side and switch between all three outcomes.

## Subtopic: First-Class Levers in Design

When a designer uses a first-class lever, the first decision is the position of the pivot, because that single choice fixes the mechanical advantage and the direction of movement. For the rescue device the aim is a strong grip, so the pivot of each jaw goes close to the tip that grips and far from the point where the syringe pushes. If the jaws must open wide, however, the pivot moves back towards the push and the grip weakens. Every design is a compromise between force and movement, and the pivot is where the compromise is set.

Scissors show how designers tune this. Kitchen scissors have short blades and long handles, giving a mechanical advantage above 1 for cutting tough packaging. Dressmaking shears have long blades for long straight cuts in cloth, and the cutting force is strongest near the pivot where the arm is shortest, which is why thick cloth is cut at the back of the blades. Tin snips have very short blades and very long handles so that a hand can cut sheet metal.

First-class levers also solve the problem of direction. On a building site a worker who needs to lift a beam from below cannot stand under it, but with a bar over a pivot can push down from a safe position and lift the beam up. Pedal-operated bins use a first-class lever so that a foot pressing down lifts a lid up. Reversing direction is often the real reason a first-class lever is chosen, with the mechanical advantage a secondary matter.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all first-class levers make you stronger; only those with the pivot nearer the load do. Two: identifying the class by what the lever looks like; identify it by what is in the middle. Three: forgetting that scissors contain two levers sharing one pivot. Four: thinking the pivot position is fixed by nature; the designer chooses it to trade force against movement.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a seesaw, a crowbar and a pair of scissors — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Pivot Sits in the Middle

There are three kinds of lever. To tell them apart, look at what sits in the middle. In a first-class lever, the pivot sits in the middle, with your push on one side and the load on the other.

A seesaw is a first-class lever. The bar in the middle is the pivot. Push one end down and the other end goes up. That is the trick of every first-class lever: it turns your push the other way round.

A crowbar lifting a paving slab is one too. Brick under the bar as the pivot, slab on the short end, your hands on the long end. Push down, slab goes up.

Scissors are two first-class levers joined at the screw. Fingers on the handles, paper between the blades, screw in the middle.

## Subtopic: Stronger, Same or Weaker

Here is what makes first-class levers special. Depending on where the pivot sits, the lever can make you stronger, leave you the same, or make you weaker.

Pivot close to the load: your side is long, the load's side is short. You get stronger. The crowbar with the brick near the slab is like this. Mechanical advantage more than 1.

Pivot exactly in the middle: both sides equal. You are not stronger or weaker, you just push in the opposite direction. A seesaw with two equal children. Mechanical advantage of 1.

Pivot close to you: your side is short, the load's side is long. You have to push harder, but the far end moves fast and far. Mechanical advantage less than 1. Cutting with the tips of long scissors is like this.

The rule: the longer side carries the smaller force.

## Subtopic: Choosing Where the Pivot Goes

A designer chooses where to put the pivot, and that choice decides everything. In your rescue device, you want the jaws to grip hard, so you put the pivot close to the tips and let the syringe push far from it. Small push in, big grip out.

Scissors are designed this way too. Kitchen scissors have short blades and long handles, for cutting tough things. Tin snips have tiny blades and very long handles so that a hand can cut metal. Thick cloth is cut at the back of the blades, near the pivot, where the grip is strongest.

Sometimes the reason for a first-class lever is simply to change direction. A pedal bin: foot down, lid up. A bar lifting a beam: push down from a safe place, beam goes up.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A first-class lever has the pivot in the middle and reverses the direction of the force. Pivot near the load gives a mechanical advantage above 1, pivot in the middle gives 1, pivot near the effort gives less than 1. Seesaws, crowbars, scissors and pliers are all first class. The designer chooses the pivot position to trade force against movement.
