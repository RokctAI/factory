### subtopic_1 — Investigating Pairs of Levers
**Assistant:** Why do scissors for cutting hair or paper have long blades, if cutting far from the pivot is harder? Wouldn't short blades always be better?

**Tutor:** Short blades would be stronger, but strength is not the main thing a hairdresser or a paper cutter needs, so the designer trades it away for something more useful: a long, smooth, straight cut. Paper and hair offer very little resistance, so even at the tips of long blades the force a hand provides is far more than enough. What matters instead is that one closing stroke travels a long way through the material, giving a clean straight line without the little steps that many short snips leave. Long blades also let the user see where the cut is going and keep the hand away from the work. Dressmaking shears are longer still for exactly this reason: a seamstress cuts a metre of fabric with a few strokes, and the back of the blades near the pivot is kept for the occasional thick seam. Compare this with tin snips or bolt cutters, where the material fights back hard; there the designer shortens the jaws, lengthens the handles and sometimes adds a second pivot stage, giving up all reach to win force. So blade length is a design decision set by the material. When you analyse any tool, ask first what the material demands, and the shape of the lever will usually make sense.

### subtopic_2 — Linkages With More Than One Pivot
**Assistant:** Why does a bending cardboard arm lose force? The force still has to go somewhere, doesn't it?

**Tutor:** The force does go somewhere, but into deforming the arm instead of into the load, and the energy of the push is spent before it arrives at the tips. Think of pushing a door with a floppy piece of rubber. Your hand moves, the rubber bends, and the door hardly feels anything until the rubber has flexed as far as it can. A lever works only because it is rigid: the whole bar turns as one piece, so the movement at the effort end is faithfully transferred to the load end in the fixed ratio of the arms. If the arm bends, part of the movement at the effort end is used up in curving the arm, so the load end moves less than the geometry promises and the force it can exert on the load falls accordingly. In a cardboard model this shows up as jaws that flex outwards instead of gripping, especially with the pivot placed near the tips where the arm behind the pivot is long and the forces on it are large. Engineers call this stiffness, and it is why real levers are made of steel and shaped with deep sections. For the model you can fight it cheaply: fold the card into an L or a channel, glue two layers together with the grain crossed, or glue a drinking straw along the arm as a rib. Next term's structures work explains why folding and tubing stiffen materials so effectively; for now, build the mock-up and watch what happens to a flat strip when you load the tips.

### subtopic_3 — Designing the Rescue Jaws as Linked Levers
**Assistant:** Is the Jaws of Life spreader really just a pair of first-class levers? It seems too simple for such a powerful tool.

**Tutor:** It is a pair of first-class levers at the business end, and the simplicity is the point, but the whole tool is a chain of three force multipliers working in series. The first stage is the hydraulic pump, where an engine or an electric motor pushes oil at very high pressure through a hose; even before the oil reaches the tool, the pump has turned a modest motor torque into a very large pressure. The second stage is the cylinder in the tool: the high-pressure oil presses on a piston, and because the pressure acts over the whole area of the piston face, a wide piston produces a push of many thousands of newtons. The third stage is the pair of arms. The piston rod drives the back ends of the two arms, each arm pivots on a hardened steel pin in the tool body, and the tips beyond the pin either spread apart or close together. The arm behind the pin is longer than the arm in front of it, so the force at the tips is multiplied again by that ratio while the tip movement is reduced. Three stages multiply together, which is how a tool one person can carry reaches forces that bend car pillars. The arms are first class, with the pivot between the piston push and the tips, which also gives the reversal that makes the tips open when the piston pushes the back ends together. Your model has the same chain, scaled down: two syringes as the hydraulic stage, and two cardboard arms on a pin as the lever stage.
