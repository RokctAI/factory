### subtopic_1 — Second-Class Levers: Load in the Middle
**Assistant:** If the body is full of third-class levers that make muscles work harder, why didn't the body evolve second-class levers instead?

**Tutor:** Because an animal's body needs speed and range of movement far more than it needs raw force at the hand or foot, and the third-class arrangement delivers exactly that. Muscles are built to contract through a short distance, a few centimetres for the biceps, and they can do so very quickly and powerfully. If the muscle attached far from the elbow, near the hand, the forearm would gain mechanical advantage but the hand would move only as far as the muscle shortened, a few centimetres, and much too slowly to throw a stone, catch a ball or pull a hand away from a fire. By attaching close to the joint, the body multiplies the small, fast muscle movement into a large, fast movement at the hand. The cost is that muscles must be strong, and they are: the biceps can pull with a force equivalent to several hundred newtons. There is also a packaging reason. A second-class limb would need the muscle to attach beyond the load, which would put bulky muscle out at the hands and feet, making them heavy and clumsy. Keeping the heavy muscles close to the body and the ends of the limbs light makes them quicker to swing, which is why your forearm is slim and your upper arm is thick. A few animals do use second-class arrangements for particular jobs, and the human foot standing on tiptoe acts as a second-class lever with the ball of the foot as pivot, the body weight as load and the calf muscle as effort, which is why calf muscles can lift the whole body. But for limbs that must move fast, the third class wins.

### subtopic_2 — Third-Class Levers: Effort in the Middle
**Assistant:** Is a door a lever? It does not look like any of the examples.

**Tutor:** A door is a lever, and which class it is depends on where you push. The hinges are the pivot. The resistance you must overcome, the door's own mass and the friction in the hinges, acts roughly through the middle of the door, so it plays the role of the load. When you push at the handle, which is deliberately placed at the far edge from the hinges, the effort is further from the pivot than the load, so the load is in the middle and the door is working as a second-class lever with a mechanical advantage above 1. That is why a heavy door swings open with a fingertip. Now try pushing the door near the hinges instead. The effort is now between the pivot and the load, the door becomes a third-class lever, and you have to shove hard to move it even though it is the same door. Anyone who has tried to close a door by pushing right beside the hinge has felt this. Designers put handles as far from the hinges as possible precisely to make the lever as favourable as they can, and the same thinking places the handle of a tap at the end of a lever arm and the pedals of a bicycle as far from the axle as a leg can comfortably reach. Levers are hiding in most objects that turn about a fixed point; the trick is to find the pivot first and then ask where the pushes and resistances are.

### subtopic_3 — Sorting Levers by What Is in the Middle
**Assistant:** Why is a stapler a third-class lever when it clearly pushes a staple through paper with a lot of force?

**Tutor:** Because the force a stapler needs is small enough that the designer could afford to choose the third class for a different benefit: a long, smooth travel of the head with a short movement of the hand, and a compact shape. Look at where the parts are. The hinge at the back of the stapler is the pivot. Your hand presses on the top, somewhere in the middle of the length. The staple is driven at the front, furthest from the hinge. Effort between pivot and load means third class, so the hand actually presses harder than the force arriving at the staple. A staple through a few sheets of paper needs only a modest force, well within what a hand can supply, so the loss is acceptable, and in return the front of the stapler moves through a larger distance than the hand does, which lets the mechanism fold the staple legs underneath. Heavy-duty staplers that must go through fifty sheets abandon this; they are long-handled and have the hinge arranged so that the hand is far from the pivot and the staple is close to it, turning them into second-class levers with a real mechanical advantage. The same product, the stapler, appears in two classes depending on how much force the designer needs, which is a good reminder that the class is a design decision made for a purpose, not a property of the name of the tool.
