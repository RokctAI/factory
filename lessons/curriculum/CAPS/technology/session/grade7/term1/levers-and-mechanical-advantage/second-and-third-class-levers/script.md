# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the classification of levers with the second and third classes. The anchor is a Saturday at a building site in Mamelodi, where a wheelbarrow carries bricks, a worker cracks nuts for lunch in a nutcracker, a friend fishes from the dam wall afterwards with a long rod, and every brick lifted by hand uses the third-class lever of the human forearm. The governing idea is that the class of a lever is fixed by which part is in the middle: load in the middle gives a second-class lever, which always has a mechanical advantage greater than 1; effort in the middle gives a third-class lever, which always has a mechanical advantage less than 1 but moves the load further and faster than the effort. Identifying the class, stating whether an advantage is gained and explaining why a tool with no mechanical advantage is still useful are examinable.

## Subtopic: Second-Class Levers: Load in the Middle

In a second-class lever the load lies between the pivot and the effort. The wheelbarrow is the classic example: the wheel is the pivot, the heavy bricks in the tray are the load, and the worker lifting the handles at the far end provides the effort. The load is nearer the pivot than the effort is, so the effort arm is always longer than the load arm, and the effort is always smaller than the load. Every second-class lever gives a mechanical advantage greater than 1.

Because the load and effort are on the same side of the pivot, a second-class lever does not reverse direction. Lift the handles up and the load goes up too. That is convenient for carrying, which is why wheelbarrows, hand trucks and the lifting levers on some car jacks are second class.

The nutcracker is a pair of second-class levers hinged together. The hinge is the pivot, the nut sits in the jaws near the hinge as the load, and the hands squeeze the far ends of the handles as the effort. The office punch and the bottle opener work the same way: with a bottle opener the lip hooked under the cap is the pivot, the cap being lifted is the load, and the hand at the end of the handle is the effort. Even a door is a second-class lever when you push it near the handle, with the hinges as pivot and the door's own weight and stiffness as load.

How much advantage a second-class lever gives depends on how close the load is to the pivot. Put the bricks at the front of the wheelbarrow tray, right over the wheel, and the handles feel light. Pile them at the back near the handles and the same bricks feel almost as heavy as lifting them directly. The lever is still second class, but the load arm has grown and the advantage has shrunk.

The questions for this section are with you now: the arrangement of a second-class lever, why it always gives an advantage, and where to load a wheelbarrow.

## Subtopic: Third-Class Levers: Effort in the Middle

In a third-class lever the effort lies between the pivot and the load. The fishing rod is the example to picture: the hand at the butt of the rod is the pivot, the other hand a little further up pushes the rod forward as the effort, and the fish on the line at the tip is the load. The effort is nearer the pivot than the load, so the effort arm is always shorter than the load arm. The effort must always be larger than the load, and the mechanical advantage is always less than 1.

That sounds like a bad bargain until you watch the tip. The hand near the pivot moves a few centimetres and the tip of the rod sweeps through a metre or more at high speed. A third-class lever trades force for distance and speed in the opposite direction to the other two classes: you supply more force and gain more movement. Fishing rods, brooms, cricket bats, hockey sticks and tweezers all choose this trade on purpose. Tweezers need gentleness and fine control, not strength, so the fingers press in the middle and the tips move delicately.

The human body is full of third-class levers. When you lift a brick with a bent arm, the elbow is the pivot, the biceps muscle pulls on the forearm bone just a few centimetres in front of the elbow as the effort, and the brick in the hand is the load, far out at the end. The muscle must pull with a force several times the weight of the brick, which is why arms tire quickly, but the hand moves fast and far, which is what a body needs for throwing, reaching and running. Legs, jaws and fingers work the same way.

Like the second class, a third-class lever does not reverse direction. Pull the forearm up and the brick comes up. Load and effort move together on the same side of the pivot.

The questions for this section are with you now: the arrangement of a third-class lever, why its advantage is always below 1 and why it is still useful.

## Subtopic: Sorting Levers by What Is in the Middle

All three classes can now be put side by side. Ask one question: what is in the middle? Pivot in the middle is first class, which reverses direction and may have any mechanical advantage. Load in the middle is second class, which keeps direction and always has an advantage above 1. Effort in the middle is third class, which keeps direction and always has an advantage below 1. Some learners remember the middle elements in order as P, L, E, or with the phrase pivot, load, effort for classes one, two, three.

Pairs of linked levers can be sorted the same way. Scissors, pliers and shears are first class because the pivot screw is between the handles and the jaws. Nutcrackers and office punches are second class because the thing being squeezed or punched sits between the hinge and the hands. Tweezers, kitchen tongs and staplers are third class because the fingers press between the hinge and the working tips.

In design, the class is chosen to suit the job. If a large force is the goal and movement is not important, use a second-class lever or a first-class lever with the pivot near the load. If speed, reach or delicate control is the goal, use a third-class lever. If the direction must be reversed, only a first-class lever will do. In the rescue device the jaws are first class, chosen for a strong grip and the reversal that lets a push at the back close the tips at the front, but the syringe plunger you press is a straight push, and the arm you press it with is a third-class lever of your own body.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: sorting levers by their shape or size; sort them by what is in the middle. Two: saying a third-class lever is useless because its advantage is below 1; it gives speed and reach. Three: thinking second-class levers reverse direction; only first-class levers do. Four: believing the advantage of a wheelbarrow is fixed; it rises as the load moves towards the wheel.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying levers and linked tools, and choosing a class for a design goal.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a wheelbarrow, a fishing rod and your own arm — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Load in the Middle: Second Class

Picture a wheelbarrow full of bricks. The wheel is the pivot. The bricks are the load, in the middle. Your hands on the handles are the effort, at the far end. Load in the middle means second class.

Because your hands are far from the wheel and the bricks are close to it, you always get stronger. Every second-class lever makes you stronger. Nutcrackers, bottle openers, office punches and wheelbarrows are all second class.

One tip: pile the bricks at the front, over the wheel. The closer the load is to the pivot, the lighter the handles feel. Pile them at the back and you may as well carry them.

And notice the direction. Lift the handles up, the bricks go up. Second-class levers do not flip your push the other way.

Keep the wheelbarrow in mind and try the questions for this part: where the load sits and why you always get stronger.

## Subtopic: Effort in the Middle: Third Class

Now a fishing rod. One hand at the bottom holds the rod still: that is the pivot. The other hand a bit higher pushes the rod: that is the effort, in the middle. The fish on the tip is the load, far out. Effort in the middle means third class.

You have to push harder than the weight of the fish, so the lever never makes you stronger. But the tip swings far and fast while your hand barely moves. Third-class levers give you speed and reach.

Your own arm is a third-class lever. Elbow is the pivot, muscle pulls just in front of it, brick in your hand is the load. Your muscle works hard, but your hand moves quickly. Brooms, cricket bats, tweezers and tongs are third class too.

Hold the fishing rod in mind and try this part's questions: where the effort sits and what you gain instead of strength.

## Subtopic: One Question Sorts Them All

To sort any lever, ask: what is in the middle? Pivot in the middle: first class, like scissors and seesaws. Load in the middle: second class, like wheelbarrows and nutcrackers. Effort in the middle: third class, like fishing rods and tweezers.

First class flips the direction and can give any advantage. Second class always makes you stronger. Third class never makes you stronger but gives speed and reach.

Designers pick the class for the job. Need force? Second class, or first class with the pivot near the load. Need speed or a gentle touch? Third class. Need to push down to lift up? First class only. Our rescue jaws are first class, for grip and for the flip.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Second class: load in the middle, always an advantage above 1, no reversal. Third class: effort in the middle, always an advantage below 1, no reversal, but speed and reach. Sort every lever by what is in the middle.

Hold the one question, and take the final questions of the lesson: sorting tools into their classes.
