# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a practical investigation of linked levers and of linkages with more than one pivot. The anchor is a kitchen drawer in a home in Pietermaritzburg emptied onto the table: scissors, pliers, garden shears, an office punch, a nutcracker, a stapler and a pair of tweezers, followed by a walk outside to a folding clothes airer and a car jack. The governing idea is that most useful tools are not single levers but two or more levers sharing a pivot, and that linking levers with several pivots allows a designer to multiply force in stages, reverse direction, and convert a push into a grip or a lift. Classifying linked tools, describing how force and movement pass through a linkage, and investigating practically where a tool cuts or grips best are examinable, and the linked-lever jaws of the rescue device are designed directly from this work.

## Subtopic: Investigating Pairs of Levers

A pair of linked levers is two levers joined at a common pivot so that one hand movement drives both. The investigation is simple: hold each tool, find the pivot, find where the hand applies effort, find where the work is done on the load, and then test where the tool is strongest. Record the class and the observation for each.

Scissors, pliers and shears. The pivot is the screw or rivet where the two arms cross. Hands on the handles, load at the jaws or blades, pivot between them: first class. Test with a strip of cardboard. Cutting close to the pivot takes little effort; cutting at the tips takes more, because the load arm is longest there. With pliers, gripping a bolt deep in the jaws near the pivot holds far more firmly than gripping it at the tips. Garden shears have long handles and short blades so that the effort arm greatly exceeds the load arm, which is why they cut thick stems that scissors cannot.

Office punch and nutcracker. The hinge is at one end, the paper or nut sits just in front of the hinge, and the hand presses the far end. Load between pivot and effort: second class. Test with the nutcracker: a walnut placed near the hinge cracks easily, the same walnut near the handle ends barely dents. The punch cuts clean holes in several sheets because its handle is long and the cutting pins are close to the hinge.

Stapler and tweezers. The hinge is at the back, the fingers press in the middle, and the working tips are at the far end. Effort between pivot and load: third class. Test with the tweezers: you squeeze harder than the tiny force the tips exert, but the tips move further than your fingers, which gives fine, gentle control for picking up a splinter or a bead. The stapler's head travels further than the hand presses, which is what folds the staple.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying each tool from the position of its pivot, and where each one is strongest.

## Subtopic: Linkages With More Than One Pivot

A linkage is a set of levers connected by two or more pivots so that movement at one point is passed along and changed on the way. The simplest is the folding clothes airer or a pair of folding gates: several bars crossed and pinned in a lazy-tong pattern. Push the two ends together and the whole structure extends; pull them apart and it folds. Each crossing is a pivot, and a small movement at the handle becomes a large movement at the far end. Extendable mirrors and scissor lifts that raise workers to fix streetlights use the same pattern.

A second common linkage reverses direction and transfers force around a corner. Two first-class levers connected by a rod will turn a push into a pull and back into a push, which is how old mechanical bells, some bicycle brakes and the pedals of a sewing machine work. A bell crank is a single bent lever with the pivot at the bend, turning a horizontal pull into a vertical lift, and it appears in car handbrakes and aircraft controls.

The most important linkage for us is one that multiplies force in stages. A scissor car jack has a threaded rod that pulls two pivots together; the four arms of the diamond then lever the car upward. The small turning force on the handle is multiplied first by the screw and then again by the lever arms, so one person can raise a corner of a one tonne car. The Jaws of Life spreader is the same idea: the hydraulic cylinder pushes the back ends of two first-class arms apart, the arms pivot on a strong central pin, and the tips close or spread with the force multiplied by the arm ratio.

In every linkage, force and movement travel together but in opposite senses. Where the movement is magnified, as at the far end of the lazy tongs, the force is reduced; where the movement is reduced, as at the tips of the spreader, the force is magnified. Tracing a linkage means following the movement from the input to the output and asking at each pivot whether the arm has grown or shrunk.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a linkage is, examples with several pivots, and how force and movement change through it.

## Subtopic: Designing the Rescue Jaws as Linked Levers

The rescue device you will design uses a syringe as its input and a pair of jaws as its output, and the link between them is a pair of first-class levers. The syringe plunger, pushed by water from a second syringe, moves a rod forward a few centimetres. The rod pushes the back ends of the two jaw arms apart or together. The arms pivot on a pin through the body of the device, and the tips at the front open or close.

Three decisions make or break the design. First, the pivot position: placing the pin close to the tips gives a strong grip but a small opening; placing it towards the back gives a wide opening but a weak grip. Second, the connection between the rod and the arms: a loose join wastes movement, a stiff join transfers it. Third, the stiffness of the arms themselves: a cardboard arm that bends under load loses the force before it reaches the tips, so arms are often doubled or folded for rigidity, an idea that returns in the structures work next term.

Testing a linkage is part of designing it. Make a quick mock-up of the two arms from strips of card and a split pin, push the back ends with a finger and watch the tips. Move the pin and feel the change. This is investigation feeding design, exactly as IDMEC describes, and the observations belong in your project record.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a tool with two handles is two different machines; it is one pair of linked levers sharing a pivot. Two: expecting force and movement both to increase through a linkage; one grows as the other shrinks. Three: placing the jaw pivot at the back for a wide opening and then wondering why the grip is weak. Four: ignoring the stiffness of the arms; a bending arm leaks force before it reaches the load.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the syringe drives the jaws, the three design decisions and why a mock-up is worth making.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a drawer full of tools and a folding clothes airer — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Tools That Come in Pairs

Empty the kitchen drawer. Most of the tools are two levers joined at one pivot, so that one squeeze moves both. Pick each one up and find the pivot.

Scissors, pliers, shears: the pivot is the screw in the middle, between your hand and the cutting part. First class. Cut a strip of card near the screw and it is easy; cut at the tips and it is harder.

Nutcracker and office punch: the hinge is at one end, the nut or paper sits right next to it, and your hand is at the far end. Load in the middle, second class. A nut near the hinge cracks easily; near the handles it barely dents.

Stapler and tweezers: the hinge is at the back, your fingers press in the middle, and the working tips are at the front. Effort in the middle, third class. Your fingers squeeze harder than the tips push, but the tips move further, which gives you a gentle, careful grip.

Keep the drawer in mind and try the questions for this part: the class of each tool and where it works best.

## Subtopic: Chains of Levers

Now join more than two levers with more than one pivot. That is a linkage.

A folding clothes airer is a chain of crossed bars pinned where they cross. Squeeze the ends together and the whole thing stretches out long. Each pin is a pivot. A small movement at your end becomes a big movement at the far end. Scissor lifts that raise workers to streetlights work the same way.

A scissor car jack does the opposite. You turn a handle, a screw pulls two pivots together, and four arms lever the car upward. Small turning force, big lift. Force multiplied in two stages.

The Jaws of Life is a linkage too. A hydraulic piston pushes the back ends of two arms, the arms pivot on a strong pin, and the tips open with huge force. Remember the rule: where movement gets bigger, force gets smaller, and where movement gets smaller, force gets bigger.

Hold the airer and the car jack in mind and try this part's questions: what a linkage is and how force and movement change along it.

## Subtopic: Building the Jaws

Your rescue device will be a small linkage. Water pushed from one syringe moves the plunger of a second syringe. The plunger pushes a rod. The rod pushes the back ends of two jaw arms. The arms pivot on a pin, and the tips open and close.

Three choices matter. Where the pin goes: near the tips for a strong grip, near the back for a wide opening. How the rod joins the arms: tight joins pass on the movement, loose joins waste it. How stiff the arms are: a bending cardboard arm loses the force before it reaches the tips, so double it or fold it.

Try it before you build it properly. Two strips of card and a split pin make a mock-up in two minutes. Push the back ends and watch the tips. Move the pin and feel the difference. Write down what you see; that is investigation feeding your design.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Linked levers are two levers on one pivot: scissors and pliers are first class, nutcrackers and punches are second class, staplers and tweezers are third class. A linkage joins levers with several pivots to magnify movement or force, never both at once. The rescue jaws are two first-class levers driven by a syringe, and the pivot position sets the trade between grip and opening.

Hold the two strips of card and the pin, and take the final questions of the lesson: the jaw linkage and its design choices.
