# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the lever as a simple machine and the relationship between load, effort and their distances from the pivot. The anchor is a familiar South African scene: a farm worker near Polokwane prising a boulder out of a field with a steel bar and a stone, and a child seesaw in a township park where a small child balances a bigger one by sitting further out. The governing idea is that a lever trades distance for force: when the effort is applied further from the pivot than the load, a small effort moves a large load, and the ratio of load to effort is called the mechanical advantage. The CAPS curriculum asks for a simple quantitative treatment, comparing distances and forces, without calculations using moments. Defining load, effort and pivot, explaining mechanical advantage as load divided by effort, and predicting how moving the pivot changes the effort are examinable, and these ideas return in every mechanism studied in Grades 8 and 9.

## Subtopic: Load, Effort and Pivot

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point. The fixed point is called the pivot or fulcrum. The force you apply to the bar is the effort. The force the bar must overcome, usually the weight of the thing you are moving, is the load. Every lever, from a seesaw to a crowbar to the arm of a rescue spreader, can be described with these three words, and the first skill in this topic is to point at a tool and name where each one is.

Take the worker and the boulder. The steel bar is the lever. The small stone pushed under the bar close to the boulder is the pivot. The boulder pressing down on the short end of the bar is the load. The worker's hands pressing down on the long end provide the effort. When the worker pushes down, the bar turns on the stone, and the short end rises, lifting the boulder.

Forces are measured in newtons, and a mass of one kilogram weighs about ten newtons on Earth. A boulder of 80 kilograms therefore presses down with a load of about 800 newtons. No adult can lift 800 newtons by hand. With a two metre bar and the stone placed 20 centimetres from the boulder, the worker finds that a push of about 100 newtons, roughly the weight of a ten kilogram bag of maize meal, is enough to lift it. The lever has multiplied the effort eight times.

The distance from the pivot to the point where the effort is applied is the effort arm. The distance from the pivot to the load is the load arm. These two distances, not the length of the bar by itself, decide what the lever does.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining load, effort and pivot, and naming them on a bar lifting a boulder.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Advantage

Mechanical advantage, written MA, tells us how many times a machine multiplies the effort. It is the load divided by the effort. If an 800 newton boulder is lifted with 100 newtons of effort, the mechanical advantage is 800 divided by 100, which is 8. The lever makes the worker eight times stronger. A mechanical advantage greater than 1 means the machine multiplies force. A mechanical advantage equal to 1 means the machine changes only the direction of the force, as a seesaw does when two equal children sit at equal distances. A mechanical advantage less than 1 means the effort is larger than the load, which sounds useless but is chosen deliberately when speed or distance matters more than force.

Why does the lever multiply force? Because of the distances. The effort arm of the worker's bar is 1.8 metres and the load arm is 0.2 metres, so the effort arm is nine times longer. Roughly speaking, the force is multiplied by about the same number of times as the effort arm is longer than the load arm. Make the effort arm longer, or the load arm shorter, and the lever helps you more. This is why a long spanner loosens a tight nut that a short spanner cannot, and why a door is easiest to push far from its hinges.

The seesaw shows the same rule with weights instead of forces. A 30 kilogram child sitting two metres from the middle balances a 60 kilogram adult sitting one metre from the middle. The lighter person sits further out. Load times its distance balances effort times its distance, but in Grade 7 the point is the pattern, not the arithmetic: further out means more effect.

In Grade 7 we describe mechanical advantage in words and with simple comparisons of forces and distances. The full calculation using moments, force multiplied by distance, is Grade 8 and 9 work. For now, be able to say whether a lever gives an advantage, how big it is from the forces, and how moving the pivot would change it.

The questions for this section are with you now: calculating MA from load and effort, and predicting what a longer effort arm does.

## Subtopic: Nothing for Nothing

A lever that multiplies force always pays for it with distance. The worker's hands move the long end down about 40 centimetres, but the boulder rises only about 5 centimetres. The effort is eight times smaller, and the effort moves about eight times further. The amount of work done, force times distance moved, is the same at both ends. No machine creates energy; a lever only lets you apply a small force over a long distance to produce a large force over a short distance. Engineers call this the trade-off of force against distance, and it holds for every simple machine, including the hydraulic systems coming later in the term.

This trade-off explains why some tools are deliberately designed with a mechanical advantage below 1. A broom, a fishing rod and a cricket bat all have the effort closer to the pivot than the load. The effort is larger than the load, but the far end moves much further and faster than the hands do. When speed or reach matters, a lever with a low mechanical advantage is the right choice.

Moving the pivot changes everything. Slide the stone closer to the boulder and the load arm shrinks while the effort arm grows, so the effort needed drops and the mechanical advantage rises, but the boulder lifts a smaller height for each push. Slide the stone towards the worker and the opposite happens: the push gets harder, the advantage falls, and the boulder rises further per stroke. Designers choose the pivot position to suit the job, and in the rescue device you will place the pivot of each jaw to turn the small push of a syringe into a strong grip.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling the load the effort; the load is what you move, the effort is what you apply. Two: thinking a longer bar always helps; only a longer effort arm compared with the load arm helps. Three: believing a lever gives more energy than you put in; it multiplies force and divides distance, never energy. Four: thinking a mechanical advantage below 1 is a design mistake; it is chosen when speed and reach matter.

The questions for this section are with you now: the force-distance trade-off, why some tools have MA below 1, and the effect of moving the pivot.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a steel bar, a stone and a boulder — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Words for Every Lever

A lever is just a stiff bar that turns on a fixed point. We need three words.

The pivot is the point the bar turns on. For the worker lifting a boulder, it is the stone under the bar.

The load is the thing you are trying to move. Here it is the boulder, pressing down on the short end of the bar.

The effort is the push or pull you make. Here it is the worker's hands pressing down on the long end.

That is it. Pivot, load, effort. A seesaw has them too: the middle is the pivot, and each child is a load or an effort depending on who is being lifted.

Keep the bar and the stone in mind and try the questions for this part: pointing to the pivot, load and effort.

## Subtopic: How Much Stronger the Lever Makes You

The boulder weighs about 800 newtons. The worker only pushes with 100 newtons and it lifts. So the lever made the worker eight times stronger. We call that number the mechanical advantage. Load divided by effort: 800 divided by 100 is 8.

Where does the extra strength come from? From distance. The worker's hands are far from the stone, the boulder is close to it. The further your effort is from the pivot, compared with the load, the more the lever helps. That is why a long spanner beats a short one, and why you push a door far from the hinges.

If the mechanical advantage is more than 1, the lever makes you stronger. If it is exactly 1, like two equal children on a seesaw, it only changes direction. If it is less than 1, you push harder than the load, which is sometimes exactly what you want.

Hold the long spanner in mind and try this part's questions: working out MA from the two forces, and what happens when the effort moves further out.

## Subtopic: You Pay With Distance

There is a catch. The worker's hands move down a long way, about 40 centimetres, and the boulder only rises about 5 centimetres. You push with less force, but you push through more distance. A lever never gives you free energy. It swaps force for distance.

That is why some tools have a mechanical advantage of less than 1 on purpose. A broom, a fishing rod, a cricket bat: your hands are near the pivot and the far end swings fast and far. Less force, more speed.

Moving the pivot changes the deal. Stone closer to the boulder: easier push, but the boulder lifts less each time. Stone closer to the worker: harder push, but the boulder rises more. Designers put the pivot where the job needs it. In your rescue device you will put the pivot of each jaw where a small syringe push becomes a firm grip.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A lever is a bar turning on a pivot. The load is what you move, the effort is what you apply. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. A longer effort arm compared with the load arm gives a bigger advantage. You pay for the extra force with extra distance, and moving the pivot changes both.

Hold the swap of force for distance, and take the final questions of the lesson: the trade-off and the effect of the pivot.
