### subtopic_1 — The 2D Working Drawing of One Face
**Assistant:** Why is the depth drawn at half length in oblique? Surely a drawing should show the true size.

**Tutor:** Because our eyes judge depth by the way parallel lines appear to converge in real life, and when a drawing shows depth lines that are parallel and full length, the brain reads the object as deeper than it really is. A cube drawn in cavalier oblique, with full-length depth, looks like a box that is noticeably longer from front to back than it is wide, even though every line is correct. Reducing the depth to half, which is cabinet oblique, compensates for that illusion and makes the drawing look right to a viewer, which is the whole purpose of a pictorial drawing. This is a reminder that the oblique drawing is a picture, not a measuring document. Its front face is true, which is why oblique is so convenient for objects with important shapes or circles on one face, but its depth lines are a convention for showing form and are never used for taking sizes. The sizes live on the working drawing, where every line is true length. Some teachers and textbooks accept either cavalier or cabinet oblique, and some workplaces prefer other pictorial systems such as isometric drawing, which you will meet in later grades, where all three axes are foreshortened equally. The key discipline is the same in all of them: state what you are doing, keep every receding line parallel and the same proportion, and never dimension a line that is not true length.

### subtopic_2 — 3D Oblique Projection at 45 Degrees
**Assistant:** Why are all the depth lines drawn from the corners parallel? In a photograph of a box they would get closer together towards the back.

**Tutor:** You are describing perspective, which is how a camera and the human eye see the world, and oblique projection deliberately does not use it. In a photograph, lines that are parallel on the real object converge towards a vanishing point, and the back of the box appears smaller than the front. That looks realistic, but it makes the drawing hard to construct and impossible to measure, because every line has a different scale depending on how far back it is. Artists and architects use perspective when realism matters, for a painting or a picture of a proposed building in its street. Designers and engineers mostly use parallel projections, of which oblique is one, because they are quick to draw on a grid, every depth line has the same scale, and the back face of a box comes out the same size as the front, which is true to the object even if not to the eye. The small loss of realism is accepted in exchange for a drawing anyone can construct in a minute with a pencil and squared paper, and read without confusion. For a Grade 7 design, where the purpose is to show the teacher and the class how the parts of the rescue device fit, parallel depth lines are exactly right, and the convention of 45 degrees on the grid keeps everyone's drawings consistent so that they can be compared fairly.

### subtopic_3 — Which Drawing for Which Job
**Assistant:** How do I decide which face to make the front in an oblique drawing of my rescue device?

**Tutor:** Choose the face that carries the most important shape and the most circles, because that face is the one drawn true and undistorted. For the rescue device the obvious candidate is the side view, where you can see the two arms crossing at the pivot, the round pivot pin, the round holes at the arm ends, the syringe lying along the base and the jaws reaching forward. All of those are shapes and circles that would become awkward ovals and slanted lines on a receding face. Drawing the side as the front face makes the mechanism readable at a glance, and the width of the device, which is just the thickness of the base and the arms, becomes the depth going back at 45 degrees, where its exact appearance matters less. A second rule of thumb is to choose the face that a user would naturally look at when the device is in use, because that is what the drawing is meant to explain. A third is practical: pick the face whose details you have already drawn as your working drawing, since the oblique drawing then starts with lines you have already worked out. If two faces are both important, designers simply make two oblique drawings or add a second view; there is no rule that says one picture must do everything. The test is whether a classmate who has never seen your device can look at the drawing and explain how the syringe moves the jaws. If they can, you chose the right front.
