### subtopic_1 — Why Designers Draw
**Assistant:** Why do drawings use millimetres rather than centimetres? Centimetres seem more natural for something the size of a desk.

**Tutor:** Because millimetres remove decimal points and the mistakes that come with them, and that matters more than feeling natural. A desk top might be 1200 millimetres by 600 millimetres with a thickness of 18 millimetres. In centimetres those become 120, 60 and 1.8, and a smudged or missed decimal point turns 1.8 into 18, a tenfold error. On a busy drawing with dozens of dimensions, a convention that uses whole numbers everywhere is far safer, and the people who build things learned this through costly mistakes. Using one unit throughout also means the unit never has to be written, which keeps the drawing clean and prevents the other classic error of mixing units on one sheet. Engineering drawings around the world follow this rule, so a South African drawing can be read in a German or Indian workshop without conversion. There is a precision argument too: a maker working to a drawing usually needs to cut within a millimetre or so, and dimensioning in the same unit as the tolerance makes it obvious how accurate to be. Centimetres are fine for everyday talk, and you will still think of a 30 centimetre ruler, but when you put a number on a drawing, write 300. Your teacher and any future workshop will read it instantly, and nobody will have to wonder whether you meant 30 or 300.

### subtopic_2 — Line Types, Scale and Dimensions
**Assistant:** Why is hidden detail drawn at all? If you cannot see the edge from that view, why not leave it out?

**Tutor:** Because the maker needs to know the edge is there, and the view that shows it best may not exist or may already be crowded. Imagine the base of your rescue device has a slot cut into its underside so that the syringe can lie flat. From the top, the slot is invisible, yet the person building the base must cut it. A dashed line in the top view says, in effect, there is an edge under here that you cannot see from this side, and its position and length are these. Without it, the maker would have to find another view or guess. Hidden lines also let a single drawing carry more information, which saves paper and reading time; a drawing with three views and sensible hidden detail can describe an object that would otherwise need six. The convention is strict so that nobody confuses hidden lines with anything else: short, even dashes with small gaps, never so dense that they look solid. Designers do use judgement. If a view has so many hidden lines that it becomes a tangle, they add a section view instead, cutting the object open on paper to show the inside directly. For your Grade 7 drawings the rule is simpler: if an important edge is hidden in the view you are drawing and the maker needs to know about it, show it dashed; if it adds nothing, leave it out. The test is always whether the drawing tells the maker everything needed and nothing confusing.

### subtopic_3 — Free-Hand Sketching
**Assistant:** My free-hand lines are wobbly. Is there a way to get better quickly, or is it just talent?

**Tutor:** It is overwhelmingly practice, and a few changes of habit produce visible improvement within a week. Most wobbly lines come from drawing with the fingers and wrist, the way we write, which gives tight control over a few centimetres and none beyond. For a line longer than your thumb, lock the wrist and fingers and move the whole forearm from the elbow or the shoulder, keeping your eye on the point where the line should end rather than on the pencil tip. The line will be straighter because the big joints move more smoothly. Second, ghost the line first: move the pencil along the path once or twice just above the paper, then draw it in one stroke at a steady speed. Hesitation causes wobble; a confident medium-fast stroke is straighter than a slow careful one. Third, hold the pencil further back than for writing, lightly, so it can glide. Fourth, use squared paper and let the grid guide your eye. Finally, accept the first few lines as construction lines, drawn feint so that errors cost nothing, and darken only the lines that turn out right. Professional designers fill pages with practice strokes, circles and boxes, the way musicians play scales, and their sketches look effortless only because of that drill. Five minutes a day of straight lines and circles on squared paper will do more for your drawing than any amount of talent you think you lack.
