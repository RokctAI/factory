# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces graphical communication: its purpose in developing and communicating ideas, the drawing conventions of line types, scale and dimensioning, and the skill of free-hand sketching. The anchor is a carpenter in Port Elizabeth who receives a drawing of a school desk from a designer in Johannesburg and builds it exactly, never having met the designer, alongside a learner's own first sketches of the rescue device. The governing idea is that drawings are a language with agreed rules, so that a dark outline, a feint construction line, a dashed hidden line, a stated scale and a dimension in millimetres mean the same thing to every reader, and that free-hand sketching is the fast, rough form of that language used to think and to try ideas. Stating the purposes of graphics, using the line conventions correctly, reading and writing scale and dimensions, and producing clear free-hand sketches are examinable, and the Practical Assessment Task awards design marks for them.

## Subtopic: Why Designers Draw

Graphics serve two purposes in Technology. The first is to develop ideas. When you draw a design, you discover what you have not yet decided: where the pivot goes, how long the arms are, how the syringe attaches. The drawing asks questions that the idea in your head could ignore. Designers say they think with a pencil, and they mean it literally; sketches are a way of testing ideas cheaply before any material is cut.

The second purpose is to communicate ideas. A product is seldom made by the person who designed it. The desk designer in Johannesburg sends drawings to a carpenter in Port Elizabeth, who has to cut every piece to the right size and join them in the right places without ever asking a question. That only works if both of them follow the same rules for what each line, number and symbol means. Those rules are the drawing conventions, and they are the same in a Soweto classroom and a shipyard in Korea.

Words alone cannot do this job. Try describing the shape of a pair of pliers in a paragraph and then ask a friend to make one from the description; the result will not match. A drawing shows shape, proportion, position and size at once, and a good one leaves nothing to be guessed. In the Practical Assessment Task, your drawings are the main evidence of the design step, and a teacher must be able to understand your device from the drawing alone.

Drawings in Technology come in grades of care. A free-hand sketch is quick and rough, for developing and sharing early ideas. A working drawing is precise, drawn with instruments to scale and fully dimensioned, so that the object can be made from it. Both use the same conventions; the difference is accuracy.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two purposes of graphics and why words are not enough.

## Subtopic: Line Types, Scale and Dimensions

Line types carry meaning. Outlines, the visible edges of the object, are drawn as dark, continuous lines, firm and even, so the shape stands out. Construction lines are the feint, light lines you draw first to set out positions and proportions; they stay on the drawing but are so light that the eye ignores them. Hidden detail, an edge that exists but is behind another surface so that you cannot see it from this view, is drawn as a dashed line of short, even dashes. Centre lines, used for the middle of circles and symmetrical parts, are drawn as a chain of long and short dashes. A reader who knows these four can tell what is solid, what is guide work, what is hidden and where the centre is, without a single label.

Scale states how the size on paper relates to the real size. Scale 1 to 1 means full size. Scale 1 to 2 means every length on the drawing is half the real length, so a 200 millimetre arm is drawn 100 millimetres long. Scale 2 to 1 means the drawing is twice full size, used for small parts. The scale is written on the drawing, and the dimensions always give the real sizes, never the measured paper sizes, so a reader never has to multiply.

Dimensioning tells the maker exactly how big each part is. Dimensions in Technology are in millimetres, and the unit is not written after each number because the whole drawing is understood to be in millimetres. Each dimension sits on a thin dimension line with arrowheads at each end, placed a little away from the outline, with thin extension lines showing which edges it refers to. The number is written above the line, in the middle, reading from the bottom or from the right of the page. Dimensions are not repeated and not crowded; each size appears once, where it is clearest.

A drawing also carries a title block or a label: the name of the object, the scale, the designer's name and the date. For the rescue device, every drawing should say what view it shows and what part it is, so the set of drawings tells a complete story.

## Subtopic: Free-Hand Sketching

Free-hand sketching is drawing without a ruler or compass, using the hand and eye alone. Its purpose is speed: to get an idea onto paper in a minute while it is fresh, to compare three ideas side by side, or to explain a detail to a partner. It is not meant to be exact, but it is meant to be clear, and the conventions still apply: outlines dark, construction lines feint, hidden detail dashed where it matters.

Technique makes sketching easier. Hold the pencil loosely and further from the point than for writing. Draw long lines in one confident stroke from the shoulder, not in many short nervous ones; if the line is slightly wrong, leave it and draw the correct one beside it. Start with feint construction lines to block in the overall shape as simple boxes and circles, then put in the dark outlines once the proportions look right. Use squared paper when you can; the grid keeps verticals vertical and helps you judge proportion. Label parts with short notes and arrows, because a sketch with labels communicates far more than a sketch alone.

Sketch the ideas for the rescue device in this way: the base as a rectangle, the two arms as long thin strips crossing at a pivot dot, the syringe as a tube with a plunger, the connecting rod as a line. Three such sketches, each labelled with how it works, are the raw material for choosing a design. Sketch quickly and often; the twentieth sketch is always better than the first.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing every line equally dark so that construction and outline cannot be told apart. Two: writing mm after every dimension or giving the paper size instead of the real size. Three: using a ruler for a free-hand sketch, which slows it down and defeats its purpose. Four: sketching without labels, leaving the reader to guess which part is which.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose and technique of free-hand sketching and the conventions that still apply.

## Subtopic: Drawing Is How Designers Talk

Designers draw for two reasons. First, to think. When you draw your rescue device, you find out what you have not decided yet: where the pivot goes, how long the arm is. The drawing asks you questions.

Second, to tell someone else. A designer in Johannesburg draws a desk. A carpenter in Port Elizabeth builds it exactly, without ever phoning to ask. That only works because they both follow the same drawing rules.

Words cannot do this. Try describing a pair of pliers in a paragraph and get a friend to make one. It will not match. A drawing shows shape, size and position all at once.

Quick rough drawings are sketches. Careful exact drawings with measurements are working drawings. Both use the same rules.

Keep the carpenter in mind and try the questions for this part: the two reasons to draw.

## Subtopic: The Rules of the Lines

Lines mean different things. Outlines, the edges you can see, are dark and solid. Construction lines, the guide lines you draw first, are feint and light. Hidden lines, edges that are behind something, are dashed. Centre lines, through the middle of circles, are long dash, short dash.

Scale says how the paper size relates to the real size. 1 to 1 is full size. 1 to 2 means half size: a 200 millimetre arm is drawn 100 millimetres long. Write the scale on the drawing, and always write the real sizes, not the paper sizes.

Dimensions are the sizes. They are in millimetres, and you do not write mm after each one. Each size sits on a thin line with arrowheads, a little away from the shape, written once, above the line.

Add a label: what the object is, the scale, your name, the date.

Hold the four line types in mind and try this part's questions: which line is which, and how scale and dimensions work.

## Subtopic: Quick Sketches by Hand

A free-hand sketch is drawn with no ruler. It is fast and a bit rough, but it must be clear. Hold the pencil loosely. Draw long lines in one go. If a line is wrong, leave it and draw another beside it. Start feint, then go dark over the right lines.

Use squared paper. The grid keeps your lines straight and your sizes sensible. Label the parts with short notes and arrows.

For the rescue device: a rectangle for the base, two long strips crossing at a dot for the arms and pivot, a tube with a plunger for the syringe, a line for the rod. Three quick sketches like that, each labelled, and you are ready to choose.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Drawings develop ideas and communicate them. Outlines dark, construction feint, hidden dashed, centre lines chain. Scale on the drawing, real sizes in millimetres, each dimension once. Free-hand sketches are quick, loose, labelled and still follow the rules.
