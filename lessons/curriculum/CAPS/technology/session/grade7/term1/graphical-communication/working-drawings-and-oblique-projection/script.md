# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches two formal drawing methods: the 2D working drawing of one face of an object with line conventions, dimensions and scale, and the 3D oblique projection in which the front face is drawn true to shape and depth lines recede at 45 degrees on squared paper. The anchor is a wooden tool box in a Technology workshop in Bloemfontein, first drawn flat so that a learner can cut its front panel to size, then drawn in oblique so that a visitor can see what the finished box looks like, followed by the same two drawings of a lever arm from the rescue device. The governing idea is that a working drawing gives the exact information needed to make a part while an oblique drawing gives an understandable picture of the whole, and that both depend on the same conventions of line, scale and dimension. Producing a dimensioned 2D working drawing of one face and a 3D oblique drawing on squared paper are examinable and are assessed directly in the Practical Assessment Task.

## Subtopic: The 2D Working Drawing of One Face

A working drawing shows an object so exactly that it can be made from the drawing alone. In Grade 7 we draw one face, usually the front, looking straight at it so that there is no perspective and every line is its true length. The result is flat, two-dimensional, and it is the drawing a maker uses to cut a panel or an arm to size.

Begin with the tool box front panel, which is 300 millimetres long, 150 millimetres high, with a rectangular hand hole 80 by 30 millimetres cut in the centre near the top. Choose a scale that fits the paper: at 1 to 2, the panel is drawn 150 by 75 millimetres, which fits comfortably on A4. Write the scale in the title block. Draw feint construction lines first: the outer rectangle, then the centre lines to locate the hand hole, then the hole itself. Check the proportions, then darken the outlines with firm, even strokes.

Now dimension it. Draw thin extension lines out from the edges and thin dimension lines between them, a little away from the outline, with arrowheads touching the extension lines. Write 300 above the length and 150 beside the height, in real millimetres, not the 150 and 75 measured on the paper. Dimension the hand hole, 80 and 30, and locate it, for example 20 from the top edge, and use the centre line so that the maker knows it is central. Every size the maker needs appears once; nothing is left to be measured off the drawing with a ruler.

Repeat for a lever arm of the rescue device: a strip 200 millimetres long and 25 wide with a pivot hole 6 millimetres in diameter, 60 millimetres from one end, and a smaller hole at each end for the rod and the jaw. The holes are circles, so draw their centre lines and dimension the diameter with the diameter symbol or the word dia, and locate each centre from an end. A maker with this drawing can mark, cut and punch the arm without asking anything.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of a working drawing, choosing a scale and dimensioning correctly.

## Subtopic: 3D Oblique Projection at 45 Degrees

A working drawing of one face tells the maker everything about that face and nothing about the depth. To show what the whole object looks like, designers use a pictorial drawing, and the simplest is oblique projection. In oblique projection the front face is drawn exactly as in the working drawing, true shape and true size to scale, and then the depth is shown by lines going back from each corner at 45 degrees, all parallel, all the same length. The back face is drawn parallel to the front, and the hidden edges are left out or dashed.

Squared paper makes this easy. The front face uses the horizontal and vertical lines of the grid. The 45 degree lines run exactly through the corners of the squares, one square across for one square up, so they are always parallel and always at the right angle without a set square. Draw the front face first, feint; from each corner that will be visible, draw a 45 degree line back; mark the depth along each of these lines; join the ends to make the back edges; then darken the visible outlines.

Depth is usually drawn at half its true length, which is called cabinet oblique, because full-length depth makes objects look stretched; the tool box, 300 long, 150 high and 200 deep, would have its depth lines drawn 100 millimetres long at 1 to 1, or 50 at 1 to 2. Some classes draw depth at full length, called cavalier oblique, and either is acceptable as long as it is stated and consistent. Circles on the front face stay as true circles, which is the great advantage of oblique over other methods; circles on the receding faces become squashed ovals, so designers place any round features on the front face where they can.

Draw the tool box in oblique: a 300 by 150 front rectangle with its hand hole, 45 degree lines from the top two corners and the right-hand bottom corner, depth marked, back edges joined. Then the lever arm: its long face at the front with the three holes as true circles, the thickness receding at 45 degrees as a thin slab. Add the scale and a title. The drawing now shows anyone what the part looks like in space.

The questions for this section are with you now: the method for an oblique drawing, why 45 degrees on squared paper, and where circles go.

## Subtopic: Which Drawing for Which Job

The two drawings answer different questions, and a complete design uses both. The working drawing answers how big is each part and where does each feature go, so it is the drawing for making. The oblique drawing answers what does the object look like and how do the parts fit together, so it is the drawing for explaining and for checking that the idea makes sense as a whole. A maker who only has an oblique drawing has to guess sizes; a client who only has a working drawing of one face has to imagine the rest.

Both share the conventions from the last session. Outlines dark, construction lines feint and left in place, hidden detail dashed where useful, centre lines on circles, scale stated, dimensions in millimetres given once on the working drawing. An oblique drawing is not normally fully dimensioned, because the receding lines are not true length; its job is to show, and a few overall sizes are enough.

For the rescue device, the Practical Assessment Task asks for exactly this pair: a 3D oblique drawing of the whole device using dark and feint lines, and a 2D working drawing of one view with dimensions to scale. The oblique drawing shows the base, the two arms crossing at the pivot, the syringe mounted behind, and the jaws in front, so that the teacher can see the mechanism. The working drawing shows the side or top view with every size needed to cut the base and arms. Practise now on the tool box and the arm so that drawing the whole device next week is a matter of applying known steps.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: dimensioning the oblique drawing along its 45 degree lines; those lines are not true length, so sizes belong on the working drawing. Two: writing the paper measurement instead of the real size on a scaled working drawing. Three: drawing depth lines at different angles or different lengths so the object looks twisted; all depth lines are parallel and equal. Four: putting circular holes on a receding face where they become ovals and are hard to draw; put them on the front face.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of each drawing, which conventions each uses, and the pair the PAT requires.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tool box, a lever arm and a sheet of squared paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Flat and Exact: The Working Drawing

A working drawing is a flat picture of one face, usually the front, with every size written on, so somebody can make the part from it.

Take the tool box front: 300 millimetres long, 150 high, with a hand hole 80 by 30 near the top. Pick a scale so it fits the page. At 1 to 2 you draw it 150 by 75. Write the scale on the drawing.

Draw feint lines first: the rectangle, the centre lines, the hole. Check it looks right. Then go over the edges dark.

Now the sizes. Thin lines out from the edges, a thin line with arrowheads between them, and the real size written above: 300, 150, 80, 30, and 20 from the top for the hole. Real sizes, not paper sizes. Each size once.

Do the same for a lever arm: 200 long, 25 wide, a pivot hole 6 across, 60 from one end. Mark the circle centres and write the diameter.

Keep the tool box front in mind and try the questions for this part: steps, scale and sizes on a working drawing.

## Subtopic: Solid and Real: The Oblique Drawing

A working drawing shows one face only. To show the whole object, draw it in oblique.

Draw the front face exactly as before, true shape, on the grid. Then from each visible corner draw a line going back at 45 degrees. On squared paper that is one square across for one square up, so every line is parallel and the angle is always right. Make all the depth lines the same length, usually half the real depth so the box does not look stretched. Join the ends for the back edges. Darken the visible edges.

Circles on the front face stay as proper circles. Circles on the slanted faces turn into ovals, so put holes on the front face where you can.

The tool box becomes a box you can see into space. The lever arm becomes a thin slab with three round holes on its front face. Add the scale and a title.

Hold the 45 degree lines on the grid in mind and try this part's questions: the oblique method and where circles go.

## Subtopic: One to Make It, One to Show It

Which drawing when? The working drawing is for making: it says exactly how big and exactly where. The oblique drawing is for showing: it says what the thing looks like and how the parts fit. You need both.

Both follow the same rules: dark outlines, feint construction, dashed hidden lines, centre lines on circles, scale written on. Sizes go on the working drawing, because its lines are true length. The oblique drawing just needs a few overall sizes at most.

For the rescue device, the PAT asks for exactly these two: an oblique drawing of the whole device, dark and feint lines, and a working drawing of one view with sizes to scale. Practise on the tool box and the arm now, and next week the device is just the same steps on a bigger object.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A working drawing shows one face flat, to scale, with every real size in millimetres written once. An oblique drawing shows the front face true and the depth at 45 degrees on squared paper, all depth lines parallel and equal. Working drawings are for making, oblique drawings for showing, and both use the line conventions.

Hold the pair of drawings, and take the final questions of the lesson: which drawing does which job.
