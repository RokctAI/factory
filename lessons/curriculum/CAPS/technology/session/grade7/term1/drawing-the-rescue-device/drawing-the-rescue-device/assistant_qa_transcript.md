### subtopic_1 — Developing the Idea Into Sizes
**Assistant:** How do I work out how far the jaws will open before I have built anything?

**Tutor:** You can estimate it from the plunger travel and the arm ratio, and the estimate is good enough to make design decisions. Start at the input: the 20 millilitre syringe's plunger moves about 70 millimetres from fully in to fully out, so the rod it pushes moves about the same, and the back ends of the two arms are spread by roughly that amount between them, about 35 millimetres each if the rod pushes a yoke symmetrically. Now use the lever ratio. If the pivot is 140 millimetres from the back end and 60 from the jaw tip, the tip moves 60 divided by 140 of the back end's movement, which is about 0.43. Each tip therefore moves about 15 millimetres, so the gap between the jaws changes by about 30 millimetres. Move the pivot to 80 from the tip and 120 from the back and the ratio becomes 0.67, giving a change of about 47 millimetres but with the grip force reduced in the same proportion. Real devices lose a little to slack in the pins and flex in the card, so take about ten percent off your estimate. Doing this sum on paper is exactly what the develop step is for: it tells you before you cut anything whether your opening specification will be met, and if not, whether you would rather move the pivot, use a longer-travel syringe or revise the specification. Write the estimate and the decision in your design record; examiners reward the reasoning even when the final measured opening turns out slightly different.

### subtopic_2 — The Oblique Drawing of the Device
**Assistant:** Why should the oblique drawing and the working drawing be at the same scale? It seems like extra effort to match them.

**Tutor:** They do not have to be at the same scale, but keeping them the same makes life easier for you and for anyone reading them, which is why it is good practice. When both drawings are at 1 to 2, the front face of the oblique drawing is simply the working drawing's view with depth added, so you can lay out both from the same measurements, and the reader can glance from one to the other and recognise every part at the same size. Mismatched scales invite errors: a part that looks large in the picture but is dimensioned small in the working drawing makes the reader doubt one of them. The same logic is why professional drawing sets state one scale in the title block and use it for all views unless a detail needs enlarging, in which case the detail is clearly marked with its own scale. There is a practical limit, of course. If your device were a metre long, 1 to 2 would not fit on A4 and you would use 1 to 5 for the overall drawings and perhaps 1 to 1 for a detail of the pivot. For a 300 millimetre device, 1 to 2 fits both drawings comfortably on A4 or A3 and leaves room for dimensions and the parts list. Choose the scale once, write it on both sheets, and the pair of drawings reads as one coherent document rather than two separate pictures.

### subtopic_3 — The Working Drawing of One View
**Assistant:** What if my design changes while I am making it? Do I have to redraw everything?

**Tutor:** You do not redraw everything, but you do record every change, because the drawings must end up describing the device that was actually built. Designers call this marking up: when a size changes during making, you neatly cross out the old dimension on the working drawing, write the new one beside it, and add a short dated note explaining why, such as arm shortened to 180 because the jaws fouled the syringe. If a part is added or removed, you amend the parts list the same way. On the oblique drawing, small changes usually need nothing more than a note, since its job is to show the general arrangement; a major change, such as moving the syringe from the base to a vertical mount, deserves a fresh sketch alongside the original. The reason for keeping the original rather than replacing it is that your design record should tell the honest story of the project, including the places where making taught you something the drawing had not anticipated. That story is what the evaluation step feeds on, and it is where many of the marks for understanding the design process are earned. Professional workshops do exactly this with as-built drawings, which show what was finally constructed as opposed to what was first proposed. So keep a pencil at the bench, change the drawing when you change the device, and your final drawings will be both accurate and a record of good design thinking.
