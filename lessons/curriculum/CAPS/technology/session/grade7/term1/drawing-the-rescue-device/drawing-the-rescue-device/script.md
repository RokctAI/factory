# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops the chosen idea for the rescue device into a buildable design and produces the two drawings the Practical Assessment Task requires: a 3D oblique drawing using dark and feint lines, and a 2D working drawing of one view with dimensions to scale. The anchor is a design desk in a classroom in Polokwane where a learner has an approved sketch, a sheet of squared paper, a pencil, a ruler and the real syringes and cardboard in front of her, so that every size on the drawing is checked against the actual parts. The governing idea is that developing a design means fixing every dimension and connection with reasons drawn from the investigation, that the oblique drawing communicates the mechanism as a whole, and that the working drawing is the instruction sheet for making. Developing an idea into specified sizes, drawing it in oblique projection and producing a dimensioned working drawing are examinable and are the design marks of PAT 1.

## Subtopic: Developing the Idea Into Sizes

The chosen sketch shows a base, two first-class lever arms crossing at a pivot, a syringe mounted on the base pushing a rod that spreads the back ends of the arms, and jaws at the front. Developing the idea means turning each of those parts into a size and a position, and giving a reason for each from what the investigations showed.

Start with the parts you cannot change: the syringes. Measure them. A 20 millilitre syringe is about 110 millimetres long with the plunger pushed in, about 20 millimetres in diameter, and its plunger travels about 70 millimetres. The base must be long enough to hold the syringe lying flat plus the arms in front of it, so a base of 300 by 120 millimetres of double-thickness corrugated card is a sensible start; it also fits the A4 constraint from the brief when stood on its long edge.

Now the arms. Each arm is a strip of doubled card, 200 millimetres long and 25 wide, chosen so that the jaw end can reach forward of the base and the back end can meet the rod. The pivot position sets the mechanical advantage: placing the pivot 60 millimetres from the jaw tip and 140 from the back end gives a load arm of 60 and an effort arm of 140, so the grip force is more than double the push and the jaw tips move less than half the distance the back ends do. With a plunger travel of 70 millimetres and the rod spreading the back ends, the jaws open roughly 30 millimetres, which meets a specification of at least 25 millimetres but not one of 40; the designer records this and either accepts a revised specification or moves the pivot to 80 millimetres from the tip, trading some grip for opening. Decisions like this, written down with reasons, are what examiners look for.

Finally the connections. The pivot is a split pin through both arms and a raised block on the base so the arms can swing clear. The rod is a strip of card or a drinking straw, pinned to the plunger end with a loop and to each arm with a split pin, so that the push is transferred without slack. The syringe is held in a cradle of two card saddles glued to the base. Each connection is sketched in a small detail drawing with a note on how it is made.

The questions for this section are with you now: fixing sizes from the real parts, choosing the pivot position with reasons and specifying the connections.

## Subtopic: The Oblique Drawing of the Device

The oblique drawing shows the whole device as it will look, so that the mechanism can be understood at a glance. Choose the side of the device as the front face, because that is where the arms, the pivot, the syringe and the jaws are all visible in true shape, and the round pivot pin and arm holes stay as true circles.

Work on squared paper at a scale of 1 to 2, so the 300 millimetre base is drawn 150 millimetres long. Begin with feint construction lines: the base as a long rectangle, the syringe as a tube lying on it, the raised pivot block, and then the two arms as long thin rectangles crossing at the pivot, tilted so that the jaws at the front are slightly open and the back ends are spread by the rod. Add the rod from the plunger to the arm ends and the two saddles under the syringe. Check the proportions against your sizes before darkening anything.

Then add depth. From each visible corner of the base, the block, the saddles and the arm ends, draw feint lines back at 45 degrees along the grid diagonals, all the same length, at half the real depth, so the 120 millimetre width of the base becomes a 30 millimetre depth line on the paper. Join the ends to form the back edges. Where a depth line would be hidden behind another part, leave it out or draw it dashed if it helps understanding. Now darken the visible outlines with firm even strokes and leave the construction lines feint.

Finish with labels: base, arm, pivot, syringe, rod, jaw, saddle, each with a short leader line. Add a title, your name, the date and the scale. A reader should be able to follow the movement from the plunger, through the rod, to the arms, to the jaws, without a word of explanation.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing the front face, building the oblique drawing feint to dark and labelling it.

## Subtopic: The Working Drawing of One View

The working drawing is the cutting list in picture form. Draw the top view of the base and arms, or the side view, whichever shows the sizes that the maker needs most, at a scale of 1 to 2, and dimension it completely in real millimetres.

For the top view: the base rectangle dimensioned 300 by 120; the position of the pivot block from the front edge, say 110, and its size; the two arms shown as 200 by 25 strips with their centre lines, the pivot hole 6 millimetres in diameter located 60 from the jaw tip, and the rod holes 8 from each back end; the syringe saddles located from the back edge. Dimension lines sit outside the outline with arrowheads on thin extension lines, each size given once, the diameter of holes shown with the diameter symbol or the word dia. Hidden edges, such as the part of the base under the arms, are dashed where needed.

Add a parts list beside the drawing: part name, how many, material, size. Base, 1, double corrugated card, 300 by 120. Arm, 2, double card, 200 by 25. Pivot block, 1, card, 40 by 30 by 20. Rod, 1, straw or card strip, 90 long. Saddle, 2, card, 40 by 30. Syringe, 20 millilitre, 1; syringe, 10 millilitre, 1; tubing, 1 metre; split pins, 4. The parts list and the working drawing together are the instruction sheet for the making lessons, and a classmate should be able to build your device from them without you present.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing the arms a length that looks good without checking the plunger travel; the opening the jaws achieve depends on it. Two: placing the pivot without a reason; the position sets the trade between grip and opening and must be justified. Three: dimensioning the oblique drawing instead of the working drawing; sizes belong where lines are true length. Four: a working drawing with no parts list, leaving the maker to count and guess materials.

The questions for this section are with you now: dimensioning the working drawing completely and writing the parts list.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with real syringes on the desk, squared paper and two drawings — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: From Sketch to Sizes

Your sketch shows a base, two arms crossing at a pivot, a syringe pushing a rod that spreads the arm ends, and jaws at the front. Now every part needs a size and a reason.

Start with what you cannot change: measure the syringe. About 110 millimetres long, 20 across, plunger moves about 70. So the base must hold the syringe plus the arms: 300 by 120 of double cardboard works and fits the A4 rule.

Arms: 200 long, 25 wide, doubled so they do not bend. The pivot 60 millimetres from the jaw tip gives a strong grip but a smaller opening; 80 from the tip opens wider but grips less. Pick one and write why.

Joints: a split pin through both arms and a raised block for the pivot. A straw or card strip for the rod, pinned at both ends so there is no slack. Two card saddles to hold the syringe.

Keep the measured syringe in mind and try the questions for this part: sizes, the pivot choice and the joints.

## Subtopic: Drawing It Solid

Now draw the whole device in oblique so everyone can see how it works. Use the side as the front face, because that is where the arms, pivot, syringe and jaws all show, and the round holes stay round.

Squared paper, scale 1 to 2. Feint lines first: base, syringe tube, pivot block, two crossed arms, rod, saddles. Check it looks right. Then from each visible corner draw a line back at 45 degrees, one square across and one up, all the same length, about half the real width. Join the ends. Go dark over the edges you can see. Leave the feint lines feint.

Label every part with a short line: base, arm, pivot, syringe, rod, jaw. Title, name, date, scale. Someone should be able to follow the push from the plunger to the jaws just by looking.

Hold the side view in mind and try this part's questions: front face, feint to dark, labels.

## Subtopic: Drawing It to Cut

The working drawing is the picture you cut from. Draw one view, top or side, at 1 to 2, and put every real size on it in millimetres: base 300 by 120, arms 200 by 25, pivot hole 6 across and 60 from the tip, rod holes 8 from the ends, block and saddles located from the edges. Thin lines, arrowheads, each size once. Dashed lines for edges that are hidden.

Next to it, a parts list: what, how many, what material, what size. Base, arms, block, rod, saddles, two syringes, tubing, split pins. With the drawing and the list, a friend could build your device without you.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Develop the idea by measuring the real parts and fixing every size with a reason, especially the pivot position. Draw the whole device in oblique, side view as the front face, feint first, 45 degree depth, dark outlines, labels. Draw one view as a working drawing with every size in millimetres and a parts list. These two drawings are the design marks of the PAT.

Hold the parts list, and take the final questions of the lesson: dimensioning the working drawing.
