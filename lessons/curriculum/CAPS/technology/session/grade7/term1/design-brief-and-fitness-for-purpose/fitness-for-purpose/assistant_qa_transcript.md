### subtopic_1 — Users, Purpose, Function and Cost
**Assistant:** If the paraffin stove is so dangerous, why is it still sold? Surely designers could just make a safe one.

**Tutor:** Safer ones exist, and the story of why the dangerous ones persist is a lesson in how the considerations trade against each other in the real world. The classic cheap wick stove has a small fuel tank directly under the burner, a light pressed-steel frame and no automatic shut-off. It costs very little, which is the whole reason families on tight budgets buy it. Designers have produced stoves with wide stable bases, fuel tanks set to one side, flame guards and valves that close if the stove tips. Every one of those features adds cost and weight, and a stove that costs three or four times as much is simply not bought by the families who need it most, so the safe design fails on cost-effectiveness for its real users even though it wins on safety. South Africa responded with a compulsory safety standard for paraffin stoves and banned the worst designs, and some organisations subsidise safer stoves so that the price falls to what households can pay. That is society acting on a design problem the market alone did not solve. Meanwhile electrification and gas have reduced paraffin use in many areas, which is a change in the users' situation rather than in the stove. For a designer the lesson is that fitness for purpose is judged for the actual users in their actual circumstances. A beautifully safe stove that stays in the shop protects nobody; the real design challenge is a stove that is both safe and affordable, and that is much harder than either alone.

### subtopic_2 — Safety, Ergonomics and Aesthetics
**Assistant:** Why does aesthetics count at all in Technology? It seems like something for art class.

**Tutor:** Because people are not machines, and a product that people dislike is used badly, neglected or abandoned, which defeats its purpose as surely as a broken part would. Hospitals discovered that medical equipment in friendlier colours and shapes made patients less anxious and staff less likely to make errors, so aesthetics became a safety matter. Cities found that public benches people find attractive are respected, while ugly ones are vandalised, so aesthetics became a cost and durability matter. Companies learned that a well-designed phone or kettle sells, and that keeps workers employed, so aesthetics became an economic matter. Aesthetics also carries information. The bright colours of rescue tools, the yellow and black stripes on machinery and the red of fire equipment are visual language that tells users what a thing is and how to treat it, and that language works faster than a written label. In Technology the rule is that appearance must serve the purpose rather than fight it. A rescue spreader with chrome plating would be slippery and would glare in a torch beam; its matt red paint is the right aesthetic choice because it is also the safer one. On your model, neat edges, clear labels and a sensible colour scheme are not decoration added at the end; they show that you understood who would look at the device and what they needed to see, and examiners reward that understanding.

### subtopic_3 — Impact on Society and the Environment
**Assistant:** How can one designer possibly know the impact a product will have on society or the environment years later?

**Tutor:** No designer knows for certain, which is exactly why the curriculum asks you to think about it on purpose rather than hoping for the best. Many of the worst impacts in history came from products whose designers simply did not ask the question: lead was added to petrol for decades before anyone checked what it did to children's brains, and plastic packaging was celebrated as hygienic and modern long before oceans filled with it. Designers today use a set of habits to do better. They think about the whole life of the product, from where the raw materials come from, through how it is made and used, to where it goes when it is thrown away, and they ask at each stage who is affected and what is released. They look at what happened with similar products in the past. They talk to the people who will live near a factory or use a tool, not only to the people who will buy it. And they design for the end: choosing materials that can be recycled, making products that can be repaired, and avoiding mixtures of materials that cannot be separated. In South Africa the impact questions are often about fairness as well as pollution, because a technology that only serves cities or only the wealthy leaves most people out. You cannot predict everything, but a designer who writes down the likely impacts, good and bad, and makes choices to improve them has done the job honestly. That written thinking is what the Practical Assessment Task asks for, and it is the same thinking an engineer does before a mine or a dam is approved.
