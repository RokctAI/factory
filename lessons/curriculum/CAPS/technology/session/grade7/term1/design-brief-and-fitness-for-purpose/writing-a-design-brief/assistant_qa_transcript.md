### subtopic_1 — What a Design Brief Says
**Assistant:** Who actually writes design briefs in the real world? Is it the designer or the person who wants the product?

**Tutor:** Usually both, and the back-and-forth between them is one of the most important parts of a professional project. In most industries the client, the person or company that needs something, writes a first version that says what they want and why: a municipality might brief an engineering firm to design a footbridge over a stream that floods every summer. The designers then rewrite it with the client, turning wishes into testable specifications and uncovering constraints the client had not mentioned, such as the budget, the maximum slope for wheelchair users and the fact that the stream is a protected wetland. The final brief is signed by both sides and becomes part of the contract, so that when the bridge is finished everyone agrees on what counts as success. Designers take this seriously because a vague brief is the commonest cause of disputes: the client imagined something, the designer built something else, and there was nothing written to settle the argument. In your project you are both client and designer, which is why the teacher asks you to write the brief yourself and to make the specifications measurable. You are learning to be the person who turns a loose wish into a clear target, and that skill is valued in engineering, architecture, software, advertising and film, all of which begin every job with a brief.

### subtopic_2 — Specifications and Constraints
**Assistant:** Can a specification change once I have started building? My arm design did not work and I want to change the opening width.

**Tutor:** A specification can change, but the change must be deliberate, written down and justified, rather than quietly adjusted to match whatever the model happens to do. Designers call this a revision, and professional projects keep a record of every one with the date and the reason. Suppose your brief says the jaws must open 40 millimetres, and you find that with the pivot placed for a strong grip the jaws open only 30. You have three honest options. You can change the design, moving the pivot or using a longer syringe stroke, to meet the 40 millimetre target. You can keep the design and revise the specification to 30 millimetres, explaining that the strong grip mattered more and that 30 millimetres still demonstrates the opening action. Or you can keep the specification and record at evaluation that it was not met, with a suggestion for how a second version would meet it. All three can earn good marks, because all three show the design process working. What loses marks is the fourth option, rubbing out 40 and writing 30 without a word, because then the brief no longer tells the truth about what was intended and the evaluation becomes meaningless. Keep your first brief, add a dated note of any change and the reason, and you are doing exactly what engineers do when a bridge design meets an unexpected rock.

### subtopic_3 — From Brief to Ideas and Materials
**Assistant:** Why does the curriculum care about cost as a constraint for a cardboard model? It costs almost nothing.

**Tutor:** Because cost is a constraint on every real design, and the habit of thinking about it has to be learned on small projects before it matters on large ones. A model that costs almost nothing is the perfect place to practise, since the arithmetic is easy and the stakes are low. When you write that extras may not exceed twenty rand, you are forced to ask whether a wider syringe or a stronger glue is worth the money, and that question is identical in shape to the one an engineer asks when deciding whether a bridge should use steel or concrete. Cost also connects to several other things the curriculum wants you to notice. Fitness for purpose, which comes up in the next session, includes cost-effectiveness: a rescue tool so expensive that no fire station can afford it rescues nobody. Impact on society includes who can afford the technology; a cheap water purifier changes more lives than a perfect one that costs a fortune. And impact on the environment often tracks cost, because materials that are cheap in rands are sometimes expensive in pollution. In the Practical Assessment Task you may simply be asked to list the materials and estimate what they would cost if bought, which shows that you understand a design has a price and that choices have consequences. On your own project, the cheap and honest answer is often that the free cardboard is also the best material, and being able to say why is a mark of a thoughtful designer.
