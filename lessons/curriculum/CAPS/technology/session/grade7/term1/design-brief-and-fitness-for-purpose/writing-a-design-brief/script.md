# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches how to write a design brief with specifications and constraints, and how to move from the brief to initial idea sketches, a chosen design and a list of materials. The anchor is the rescue device project itself, now at week 5 of the term, with a class in Kimberley sitting down to write the brief that will guide their syringe-and-cardboard model, and the comparison case of a learner who skipped the brief and built a device nobody could assess. The governing idea is that a design brief states the problem, the product, the user and the purpose in a few sentences, that specifications describe measurable features the product must have, that constraints describe the limits the designer must work within, and that ideas are then generated and judged against this written standard. Writing a brief, distinguishing specifications from constraints, producing and comparing initial sketches and choosing materials with reasons are examinable and carry marks in the Practical Assessment Task.

## Subtopic: What a Design Brief Says

A design brief is a short paragraph, usually three to five sentences, that states the problem to be solved, what will be designed and made, who it is for and what it must achieve. It is written before any sketching begins, and every later decision is checked against it. It is not a description of the finished product, because the product does not exist yet; it is a statement of intent.

A good brief for our project might read: Emergency workers need a tool to prise open crushed vehicle doors and free trapped people. I am going to design and make a working model of a hydraulic rescue device for the Technology class. The model will use water-filled syringes to drive a pair of linked-lever jaws that open and close, so that it demonstrates how hydraulics and levers combine to produce a large controlled force. It must be built from the cardboard, syringes and tubing provided and be finished in two weeks.

Each sentence does a job. The first names the problem and the people affected. The second names the product and its user, in this case the class and the teacher who will assess it. The third says what the product will do and why, linking it to the principle it must show. The fourth begins to set limits. Anyone reading the brief knows what is being attempted without seeing a single drawing.

Compare a weak brief: I am going to make a rescue machine out of cardboard. It names no problem, no user, no function and no limits. When that learner's model was finished, the teacher could not tell whether it had succeeded, because nothing had been promised. A brief is the yardstick against which evaluation happens; without it, evaluation is only opinion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four things a brief must say and what makes a brief weak.

## Subtopic: Specifications and Constraints

Below the brief come two lists. Specifications describe what the product must be and do, in terms that can be checked. Constraints describe the limits the designer must stay within. Learners often confuse them, so a test helps: a specification is about the product's performance and features; a constraint is about the conditions of the project, such as materials, time, cost, size and safety rules.

Specifications for the rescue device could include: the jaws must open at least 40 millimetres and close fully; the jaws must grip and hold a wooden block of 50 grams without slipping; the device must be operated by pushing one syringe from at least 30 centimetres away through the tube; the device must stand on its own base without being held; the lever arms must not bend visibly when gripping; the moving parts must work at least ten times without coming apart. Each one can be tested with a ruler, a block or a count, so each can be ticked or crossed at evaluation.

Constraints could include: the device must be built only from the corrugated cardboard, two syringes, one metre of tubing, split pins, glue and string provided; it must fit on an A4 sheet when standing; it must be completed in the two making lessons and finished at home only with permission; it must not use sharp metal parts or anything that could injure the user; the total cost of any extra materials must not exceed twenty rand. Constraints are the fence around the design; specifications are the targets inside the fence.

Writing specifications well means using numbers and tests where you can. The jaws must be strong is hard to check; the jaws must hold a 50 gram block is easy. The device must be a good size is vague; the device must fit on an A4 sheet is clear. The sharper the specification, the fairer and easier the evaluation.

The questions for this section are with you now: telling a specification from a constraint and writing specifications that can be tested.

## Subtopic: From Brief to Ideas and Materials

With the brief and lists written, idea generation begins. The rule is at least two, preferably three, genuinely different initial sketches. For the rescue device the differences might lie in how the syringe drives the jaws: a syringe pushing a rod that spreads the back ends of two first-class arms; a syringe pulling a string over a pivot to close the jaws; a syringe pushing one jaw directly while the other is fixed. Each is sketched free-hand, labelled, and given a few words about how it works.

Choosing the best design means scoring the sketches against the specifications and constraints, not against taste. Make a simple table with the ideas across the top and the specifications down the side, and put a tick, a cross or a question mark in each cell. The idea with the most ticks and the fewest question marks is chosen, and the reasons are written down in a sentence or two. A string-pulled design might score well on simplicity but badly on holding a load; a direct-push design might be strong but open too little. The chosen idea may borrow a feature from a rejected one, which is good design practice.

Selecting materials is the final step before drawing. For each part of the chosen design, state the material and the reason: corrugated cardboard for the base because it is stiff and free; two layers of card for the arms because a single layer bends; split pins for the pivots because they allow turning; a 20 millilitre syringe for the jaws and a 10 millilitre syringe for the hand so that the push is doubled. The reason is as important as the choice, because it shows the specifications being applied.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing the brief after the model is finished so that it describes what happened; the brief is written first and the model is judged against it. Two: listing the jaws must be strong as a specification; it cannot be tested, so it must become a number. Three: sketching one idea three times with different colours; the ideas must differ in how they work. Four: choosing materials for looks alone; every material needs a reason linked to a specification.

The questions for this section are with you now: generating different ideas, choosing with a table and giving reasons for materials.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a short paragraph, two lists and three sketches — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Brief: What, Who and Why

Before you draw or build anything, you write a design brief. It is a short paragraph, three to five sentences, that says what the problem is, what you are going to make, who it is for and what it must do.

For our project: Emergency workers need a tool to open crushed car doors. I am going to make a working model of a hydraulic rescue device. It will use water-filled syringes to drive a pair of lever jaws that open and close, to show how hydraulics and levers give a big, controlled force. It will be made from the cardboard, syringes and tubing we are given, in two weeks.

Now a weak one: I am going to make a rescue machine out of cardboard. No problem, no user, no job, no limits. Nobody can tell at the end whether it worked. A brief is the ruler you measure your finished model against.

Keep the four jobs of the brief in mind and try the questions for this part: what, who, why, and what is missing from a weak brief.

## Subtopic: Two Lists: Must Do and Must Not Break

Under the brief go two lists.

Specifications say what the product must do, in ways you can check. The jaws must open at least 40 millimetres. The jaws must hold a 50 gram block. One syringe push from 30 centimetres away must work the jaws. The device must stand on its own. The arms must not bend.

Constraints are the limits you must stay inside. Only the materials given. Fits on an A4 sheet. Finished in two lessons. No sharp metal. No more than twenty rand on extras.

Easy way to tell them apart: specifications are about the product doing its job; constraints are about the rules of the project. And always use numbers where you can. Strong is vague; holds 50 grams is clear.

Hold the two lists in mind and try this part's questions: which list each statement belongs to, and how to make a vague one clear.

## Subtopic: Three Sketches, One Choice, Good Reasons

Now ideas. Draw at least two, better three, that work differently. A syringe pushing a rod that spreads two arms. A syringe pulling a string to close the jaws. A syringe pushing one jaw while the other stays still. Label each one.

To choose, make a table: ideas across the top, specifications down the side, tick or cross in each box. The idea with the most ticks wins, and you write down why. You can borrow a good part from a losing idea.

Then pick materials, and give a reason for each. Corrugated cardboard for the base, because it is stiff and free. Two layers of card for the arms, because one bends. Split pins for pivots, because they turn. A big syringe at the jaws and a small one in your hand, because that doubles the push.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A brief says the problem, the product, the user and the purpose, in a few sentences, written first. Specifications are testable targets for the product; constraints are the limits of the project. Draw several different ideas, choose with a table against the specifications, and give a reason for every material.

Hold the table of ticks and crosses, and take the final questions of the lesson: choosing ideas and materials with reasons.
