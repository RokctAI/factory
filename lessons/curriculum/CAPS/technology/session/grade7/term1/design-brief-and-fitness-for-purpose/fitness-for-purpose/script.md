# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the design considerations that together decide whether a product is fit for purpose: users, purpose, function, cost-effectiveness, safety, ergonomics, aesthetics and impact on society and the environment. The anchor is three objects examined side by side in a classroom in Nelspruit, a photograph of a hydraulic rescue spreader, the plastic chair the learners are sitting on, and a paraffin stove of the kind used in many South African homes, which cooks food well yet causes thousands of burns and fires each year. The governing idea is that fitness for purpose is judged on all of these considerations at once, not on function alone, and that a designer must balance them, since improving one often costs another. Naming the considerations, applying them to an existing product and using them to refine a design brief are examinable, and the Practical Assessment Task expects the rescue device brief to show this thinking.

## Subtopic: Users, Purpose, Function and Cost

The first consideration is the users: who will use the product, and what are they like? A rescue spreader is used by trained firefighters, often wearing thick gloves, in the dark, under stress. A school chair is used by children of many sizes for five hours a day. A paraffin stove is used by families, sometimes by children, in small rooms. Knowing the user shapes every other decision, because a product fit for one user may be dangerous for another.

Purpose is why the product exists, the need it answers. The spreader exists to free trapped people quickly; the chair exists to let a learner sit and work comfortably; the stove exists to cook food cheaply where there is no electricity. Function is what the product does and how well it does it, judged against the purpose. A spreader that opens a door in two minutes has good function; one that takes twenty does not. The chair that supports a learner without tipping has good function; one that wobbles does not.

Cost-effectiveness asks whether the product gives good value for what it costs to make, buy and run. It is not the same as cheapness. A rescue spreader costs more than a small car, yet every fire station buys one because the value of a saved life is beyond price and the tool lasts for decades. A plastic chair costs little, which lets a school equip a thousand learners. A paraffin stove is cheap to buy and the fuel is cheap to run, which is exactly why millions of homes use it despite its dangers. Cost-effectiveness always weighs what you get against what you pay, and for whom.

These four, users, purpose, function and cost, are the questions a designer asks first, because a product that does not do its job for its users at a price they can pay fails before any other consideration matters.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining users, purpose, function and cost-effectiveness, and applying them to the three objects.

## Subtopic: Safety, Ergonomics and Aesthetics

Safety asks whether the product can harm its user or anyone nearby, in normal use and when things go wrong. The spreader is designed so that nothing can pinch the operator's hands, the hydraulic hose is rated far above its working pressure, and the controls return to neutral when released. The plastic chair must not collapse, must not have sharp edges and must not tip when a learner leans back. The paraffin stove is where safety fails badly: an open flame, a tank of flammable fuel and a light frame that tips easily have caused shack fires across the country, and poor designs have been banned. A product that works but injures is not fit for purpose.

Ergonomics is the fit between the product and the human body: its size, weight, shape, grip and the effort needed to use it. The spreader has balanced handles so that its weight is carried in two hands, and a control that a gloved thumb can operate. A school chair has a seat height that lets feet rest on the floor and a back shaped to the spine. A stove with controls that need bending low to reach is poor ergonomics. Good ergonomics reduces tiredness, mistakes and injuries, and it overlaps with safety.

Aesthetics is how a product looks, feels and sounds, and whether people find it pleasing. It is sometimes dismissed as decoration, but it affects whether people want to use a thing and how they treat it. A rescue tool painted bright red or yellow is easier to find in the dark and signals its purpose. A chair in a pleasant colour is looked after; an ugly one is kicked. Aesthetics should serve the purpose, not fight it, and should never be bought at the expense of safety or function.

Designers often find that these considerations pull against each other. A safer stove with a wide heavy base costs more and is harder to carry. A chair with perfect ergonomics for a tall learner is wrong for a small one. Fitness for purpose is the balance that serves the users best, and the designer must be able to say what was traded for what.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining safety, ergonomics and aesthetics, and giving an example where they conflict.

## Subtopic: Impact on Society and the Environment

The last consideration looks beyond the user. Impact on society asks how the product affects communities, jobs, fairness and daily life. The rescue spreader saves lives and shortens road closures, but it is expensive, so rural districts may wait longer for rescue than cities, which is an unfairness designers and governments both have to face. Cheap plastic chairs let every school seat its learners, but their arrival put furniture makers who used wood out of work in some towns. The paraffin stove makes cooking possible for millions without electricity, yet the burns and fires it causes fill hospital wards and destroy homes, and alternatives such as safer stove designs and electrification are social questions as much as technical ones.

Impact on the environment asks what the product costs the natural world over its whole life: the materials dug or grown to make it, the energy used in making and using it, the pollution it releases, and what happens when it is thrown away. The spreader is made of steel and aluminium that can be recycled and runs on a small petrol engine. The plastic chair is made from oil, lasts a few years and then usually goes to a landfill where it stays for centuries. The paraffin stove burns a fossil fuel and releases smoke into the room, harming lungs as well as the air outside. A designer who ignores this creates problems for people who never bought the product.

Apply all eight considerations to the rescue device model and the brief from last session grows. Users: Grade 7 learners and the teacher; so the syringes must be easy to push with one hand. Purpose and function: to show hydraulics and levers producing a controlled force; so the jaws must open and grip visibly. Cost: only the free materials and a small budget. Safety: no sharp edges, water not oil, syringes never pointed at anyone. Ergonomics: the hand syringe fits a child's grip and the base sits flat on a desk. Aesthetics: neat, labelled, painted in rescue colours if time allows. Impact: cardboard and card recycled afterwards, syringes reused, and a note on how the real tool serves society. The brief now describes a product fit for its purpose, not just a working mechanism.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: judging fitness for purpose on function alone; a working stove that burns children is not fit. Two: confusing cost-effectiveness with cheapness; the expensive spreader is excellent value. Three: treating aesthetics as the opposite of seriousness; colour helps a rescuer find a tool in the dark. Four: forgetting the people who never use the product; environment and society count too.

The questions for this section are with you now: impact on society and environment for the three objects, and applying all eight considerations to the rescue device.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a rescue tool, a plastic chair and a paraffin stove — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Does It Do the Job, and for Whom?

Fit for purpose means the product suits the job and the people. We judge it with eight questions. Here are the first four.

Users: who uses it? Firefighters in gloves use the spreader. Children of all sizes use the school chair. Whole families, in small rooms, use the paraffin stove.

Purpose: why does it exist? To free trapped people. To let a learner sit and work. To cook without electricity.

Function: does it actually do that job well? A spreader that opens a door in two minutes, yes. A chair that wobbles, no.

Cost-effectiveness: is it good value for the money? Not the same as cheap. The spreader costs more than a car, but it saves lives for thirty years, so it is good value. The chair is cheap and seats a thousand learners. The stove is cheap to buy and run, which is why so many homes have one.

Keep the three objects in mind and try the questions for this part: users, purpose, function and value.

## Subtopic: Safe, Comfortable and Good to Look At

Safety: can it hurt anyone? The spreader has guards and a control that stops the moment you let go. The chair must not collapse or tip. The paraffin stove fails here: open flame, fuel tank, easy to knock over. That is why so many shack fires start in the kitchen. Working but dangerous means not fit for purpose.

Ergonomics: does it fit the human body? Handles you can hold in gloves. A seat where your feet touch the floor. Controls you do not have to bend down to reach. Good ergonomics means less tiredness and fewer accidents.

Aesthetics: does it look and feel good? This is not just decoration. A bright red tool is easy to find in the dark. A chair in a nice colour gets looked after. Looks should help the job, never replace safety.

These often pull against each other. A safer stove with a heavy base costs more and is harder to carry. The designer has to balance them and say what was traded.

Hold the dangerous stove in mind and try this part's questions: safety, ergonomics and aesthetics, and how they clash.

## Subtopic: What About Everyone Else?

The last question looks past the user. Impact on society: how does it affect other people? The spreader saves lives, but it is costly, so rural areas may wait longer for rescue. Cheap chairs seat every learner but cost some wood furniture makers their jobs. The stove lets millions cook, but fills hospital wards with burns.

Impact on the environment: what does it cost nature, from the materials to the rubbish at the end? Steel can be recycled. A plastic chair sits in a landfill for centuries. A paraffin stove burns fossil fuel and fills the room with smoke.

Now our rescue device. Users: learners and the teacher, so the syringe must suit a child's hand. Function: jaws that open and grip clearly. Cost: free materials. Safety: no sharp edges, water not oil. Ergonomics: a flat base, an easy push. Aesthetics: neat and labelled. Impact: recycle the card, reuse the syringes. Add these to the brief and it describes a product, not just a mechanism.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Fit for purpose is judged on users, purpose, function, cost-effectiveness, safety, ergonomics, aesthetics and impact on society and the environment. A product can work and still fail on safety or impact. Cost-effectiveness means value, not cheapness. Designers balance the eight and say what they traded.

Hold the eight questions, and take the final questions of the lesson: impact, and applying all eight to the model.
