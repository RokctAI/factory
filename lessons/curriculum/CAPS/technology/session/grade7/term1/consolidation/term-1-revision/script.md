# Part 1 — Expert

This session consolidates the work of Term 1 by following the Jaws of Life project backwards from the finished model to the problem it solved, revising each concept where it was used. The anchor is a learner's completed design record laid open on a desk in a classroom in Welkom, page by page: the scenario, the IDMEC plan, the lever investigations, the syringe results table, the brief with its two lists, the eight fitness-for-purpose considerations, the oblique and working drawings and the test results. The governing idea is that the term's content forms one connected argument, a need, a method, two force-multiplying systems, a written target, a set of drawings and a tested product, and that revision means being able to state each idea precisely and to use it on a new example. Every concept listed is examinable in the formal assessment, and the most common errors are confusions between neighbouring ideas, which this session separates deliberately.

## Subtopic: The Process and the Problem

The term began with a problem scenario: people trapped in crushed vehicles need to be freed quickly and safely with a tool that gives a large, controlled force. Technology starts with a need, and the first skill is to turn a story into a problem statement that names the people, the need and the conditions. From the problem came requirements, and from the requirements, later, the brief.

The method for solving the problem was the design process, IDMEC: investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate. Investigate means finding out about the problem, existing solutions and materials, ending in a brief. Design means producing several ideas, choosing with reasons and drawing the chosen one. Make means building safely to the drawing in a planned sequence. Evaluate means testing against the specifications and judging honestly. Communicate runs throughout as notes, labelled sketches, drawings and a final presentation. The process is a loop, because evaluation sends the designer back to earlier steps, and the term followed it: weeks 1 to 4 investigation, 5 to 7 design, 8 and 9 making, then evaluation and communication.

A design brief states the problem, the product, the user and the purpose in a few sentences, written before any sketching. Specifications are testable features of the product, such as jaws that open 40 millimetres and hold a 50 gram block. Constraints are the limits of the project, such as the materials provided, an A4 footprint and two lessons. Fitness for purpose is judged on eight considerations together: users, purpose, function, cost-effectiveness, safety, ergonomics, aesthetics, and impact on society and the environment; a product that works but is unsafe or harmful is not fit, and cost-effectiveness means value, not cheapness.

Test yourself on this section by writing, from memory, a problem statement for a new scenario, the five IDMEC steps with one sentence each, two specifications and two constraints for any product, and the eight considerations.

The questions for this section are with you now: problem statements, IDMEC, briefs, specifications versus constraints and fitness for purpose.

## Subtopic: Levers and Fluids

A lever is a rigid bar turning on a pivot. The load is the force to be overcome, the effort is the force applied, and the effort arm and load arm are their distances from the pivot. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort; an 800 newton load lifted by 100 newtons gives an MA of 8. The longer the effort arm compared with the load arm, the greater the advantage, and the price is distance: the effort moves further than the load, because no machine creates energy.

The three classes are sorted by what is in the middle. First class, pivot in the middle: seesaw, crowbar, scissors, pliers; it reverses direction and its MA can be greater than, equal to or less than 1 depending on where the pivot sits. Second class, load in the middle: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, office punch, bottle opener; always MA greater than 1, no reversal. Third class, effort in the middle: fishing rod, tweezers, stapler, the forearm; always MA less than 1, no reversal, but speed and reach. Linked levers share a pivot, and linkages with several pivots pass movement along, magnifying movement where they reduce force and the reverse.

Pneumatics uses a gas, hydraulics uses a liquid, and both transmit pressure, force spread over area, through a sealed system. Unequal pistons give a mechanical advantage in proportion to their areas: a piston of four times the area gives four times the force and one quarter of the movement. Air compresses, so a pneumatic system lags and feels springy; water does not, so a hydraulic system responds at once and holds under load. That is why the rescue tool is hydraulic and the bus door is pneumatic. The syringe investigation showed equal syringes giving equal force and distance with water, unequal syringes trading force for distance, and air giving late, soft movement in every case.

Test yourself here by classifying ten tools, calculating MA from two forces, predicting what moving a pivot does, and explaining the difference between air and water in the syringes.

The questions for this section are with you now: lever parts and classes, mechanical advantage and hydraulic and pneumatic systems.

## Subtopic: Drawing and the Confusions That Cost Marks

Graphics develop ideas and communicate them. The conventions: outlines dark and continuous, construction lines feint, hidden detail dashed, centre lines chain. Scale states drawn size to real size, and dimensions give real sizes in millimetres, each once, on thin lines with arrowheads. Free-hand sketches are quick and labelled. A 2D working drawing of one face is for making and carries all the dimensions. A 3D oblique drawing has a true front face and depth at 45 degrees on squared paper, all depth lines parallel and equal, usually half length, circles kept on the front face; it is for showing and is not dimensioned along its receding lines.

Now the confusions. Respiration is not the subject here, but the same kind of trap appears everywhere in this term. Load and effort: the load is what you move, the effort is what you apply. Specification and constraint: a specification is about the product and can be tested, a constraint is a limit on the project. Cost-effectiveness and cheapness: value for money, not the lowest price. Mechanical advantage less than 1 and a design mistake: a low MA is chosen for speed and reach. Hydraulics and pneumatics: liquid and gas, incompressible and compressible. Working drawing and oblique drawing: for making with every size, and for showing with depth at 45 degrees. Evaluation and praise: a measured result against each specification, not a compliment.

A model exam question might read: a learner designs a cardboard device in which a 10 millilitre syringe drives a 20 millilitre syringe, which pushes the back ends of two arms pivoted 60 millimetres from their tips and 140 from their back ends. Name the two systems that multiply the force, state the class of lever and give a reason, explain why water rather than air is used, and state one specification and one constraint the design must meet. A full answer names hydraulics and levers, identifies first-class levers because the pivot lies between the effort at the back and the load at the tips, explains that water is incompressible so the jaws respond at once and hold, and gives, for example, a testable opening of 30 millimetres and the constraint of the materials provided.

The error museum, four exhibits for the whole term. One: writing mm after dimensions or giving paper sizes on a scaled drawing. Two: judging a lever's class by its shape instead of by what is in the middle. Three: saying hydraulics creates force; it transmits and redistributes it. Four: evaluating without reference to the brief.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing conventions, the paired confusions and the model question.

## Subtopic: The Story of the Term

We started with a crashed car and people trapped inside. The need: free them fast and safely with a big, controlled force. Technology always starts with a need, and the first job is to write it down as a problem in one sentence.

We solved it with IDMEC. Investigate: find out. Design: draw ideas, choose one, draw it properly. Make: build it in order, safely. Evaluate: test it against what you promised. Communicate: write and explain all the way through. It is a loop, and the whole term was one trip around it.

The promise was the design brief: problem, product, user, purpose, in a few sentences. Under it, specifications, things the product must do that you can test, and constraints, the limits of the project. Fit for purpose means passing eight checks: users, purpose, function, value, safety, ergonomics, looks, and impact on people and the planet.

Keep the story in mind and try the questions for this part: the problem, the five steps and the brief.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Multiply a Push

The first way is a lever: a bar on a pivot. Load is what you move, effort is your push. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. Push far from the pivot and you get stronger, but your hand moves further. Nothing is free.

Sort levers by what is in the middle. Pivot in the middle: first class, like scissors, flips direction, can give any advantage. Load in the middle: second class, like a wheelbarrow, always makes you stronger. Effort in the middle: third class, like tweezers or your arm, never stronger but fast and far.

The second way is a fluid in a tube. Air is pneumatics, liquid is hydraulics. Push a small piston and the pressure spreads to a big piston: bigger face, bigger force, shorter move. Air squashes and feels spongy; water does not and feels firm. That is why the Jaws of Life and our model use liquid.

Hold the two ways in mind and try this part's questions: lever classes, MA and air versus water.

## Subtopic: Drawing It and Not Getting Mixed Up

Drawings follow rules: dark outlines, feint construction, dashed hidden lines, chain centre lines. Scale on the drawing, real sizes in millimetres, each once. Working drawing: one flat face with all the sizes, for making. Oblique drawing: true front face, depth back at 45 degrees on the grid, for showing.

Now the mix-ups that lose marks. Load versus effort. Specification versus constraint. Cheap versus good value. Low MA versus a mistake. Air versus liquid. Working drawing versus oblique drawing. Praise versus evaluation. For each pair, know the difference in one sentence.

Pocket summary of the term. A need becomes a problem statement. IDMEC turns it into a tested product. A brief with specifications and constraints is the target, and fitness for purpose is judged on eight considerations. Levers and fluid systems multiply force by trading distance. Levers are sorted by what is in the middle. Hydraulics is firm, pneumatics is springy. Drawings use agreed line types, scale and dimensions, with working drawings to make and oblique drawings to show.

Hold the pocket summary, and take the final questions of the lesson: the drawing rules and the pairs.
