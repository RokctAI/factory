### subtopic_1 — The Process and the Problem
**Assistant:** What is the best way to revise Technology? It does not feel like a subject you can just memorise.

**Tutor:** You are right that memorising alone does not work, because most Technology questions ask you to apply an idea to a new object rather than repeat a definition, so the best revision mixes three activities. First, know the definitions and lists precisely: the five IDMEC steps, the three classes with their middle element, the formula for mechanical advantage, the eight fitness considerations, the four line types. Write them from memory, check, and repeat until they are automatic, because an answer that fumbles the basic terms loses marks before it starts. Second, practise applying each idea to objects you have never been asked about: pick up ten things in your kitchen and classify any that are levers, look at a bicycle pump and a car jack and say which fluid each uses and why, write a brief with two specifications and two constraints for a school bag. This is the part most learners skip, and it is where the marks are. Third, go back through your own design record, because the examiner's model question is essentially your project described in words: the systems that multiplied the force, the class of the arms, the reason for water, the specifications you set. Explaining your own device aloud to a family member, while pointing at the parts, is excellent revision. Spread this over several short sessions rather than one long night, and finish each session by writing the pocket summary from memory. If you can do that and classify a stranger's tools, you are ready.

### subtopic_2 — Levers and Fluids
**Assistant:** Why does the test ask about the same ideas in so many different disguises, like asking about a wheelbarrow when we studied the rescue device?

**Tutor:** Because the subject is testing whether you understand the idea, not whether you remember the example, and the only way to tell the difference is to change the example. If every question used the rescue device, a learner could score well by memorising the device without understanding levers at all. By asking about a wheelbarrow, a bottle opener or a bus door, the examiner finds out whether you can see the pivot, the load and the effort in something new, which is the actual skill a designer needs. This is also how the real world works: the engineer who understands first-class levers from scissors can design a brake pedal, a crane arm or a prosthetic hand, because the principle travels even though the object changes. The good news is that the disguises are shallow. Behind every lever question is the same procedure: find the pivot, locate the load and the effort, say what is in the middle, state the class, reason about the arm lengths. Behind every fluid question is the same pair of ideas: pressure spreads through the fluid and acts on area, and liquids do not compress while gases do. Behind every design question is the brief, the specifications, the constraints and the eight considerations. When you meet an unfamiliar object in a test, do not panic at the newness; run the procedure you know, and the disguise falls away.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing and the Confusions That Cost Marks
**Assistant:** How much detail do I need in an evaluation answer to get full marks?

**Tutor:** Enough to show that you compared the product with the brief using evidence, and proposed a specific improvement, which usually means three distinct moves in a few sentences. The first move is to refer to the specifications: name them and say for each whether it was met, partly met or not met, with the figure you measured, such as the jaws opened 32 millimetres against a target of 40, so this was not met. The second move is to explain why, in terms of the design: the pivot was placed 60 millimetres from the tips to give a strong grip, which limited the opening. The third move is to propose an improvement tied to that explanation: move the pivot to 80 millimetres from the tips, or use a syringe with a longer stroke, and note what that would cost, in this case some grip force. If the question allows, add one sentence on the process, such as whether the sequence of manufacture was followed and the safety rules kept. What loses marks is an answer that only describes the product, or praises it, or lists faults without explaining them, or proposes vague improvements like make it better. What earns full marks is the chain of specification, result, reason, improvement, written plainly. Practise this chain on your own model now, because the examiner's evaluation question will almost certainly describe a device like yours and ask you to judge it.
