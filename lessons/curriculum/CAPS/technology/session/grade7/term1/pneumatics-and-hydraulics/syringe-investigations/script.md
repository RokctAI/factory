# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the practical investigation of force transfer between syringes, first between two equal syringes and then between two unequal ones, each filled with air and then with water. The anchor is a classroom bench in a school in Rustenburg, with two 10 millilitre syringes, one 20 millilitre syringe, a metre of clear aquarium tubing, a basin of water, a ruler and a results table. The governing idea is that a sealed fluid system transfers movement and force from one piston to the other, that a liquid transfers them immediately and completely while a gas absorbs part of the push by compressing, and that unequal pistons change the force and the distance in opposite directions. Carrying out the investigation safely, recording observations in a table, explaining them using pressure and compressibility, and drawing conclusions for the rescue device are examinable, and this investigation is assessed in the Practical Assessment Task.

## Subtopic: Equal Syringes: Air and Then Water

Set up the first investigation. Take two syringes of the same size, 10 millilitres each. Pull the plunger of one fully out and push the plunger of the other fully in. Connect their nozzles with a short length of tubing. The system now contains air. Push the plunger of the full syringe in slowly and watch the other.

Observe carefully and record. The second plunger moves out, but it lags behind the first: at the start of the push it barely moves while the air inside is squeezed, then it catches up. If you stop pushing and hold the first plunger still, the second drifts slightly. If a partner presses gently on the second plunger to act as a load, you feel a spongy resistance, and the first plunger can be pushed in a little further without the second moving at all. The air in the system is compressing. Measure how far each plunger moves: push the first in by 4 centimetres and the second typically moves out less than 4 centimetres at first and only reaches the full distance after a pause.

Now repeat with water. Hold both syringes and the tube under water in the basin, draw water into one syringe, push the other fully in, and connect the tube under the surface so that no air is trapped. Tilt the system and tap it to chase any bubbles to a nozzle and expel them. Dry the outside and push the first plunger slowly.

The difference is immediate. The second plunger moves the instant the first does, and by the same distance: push 4 centimetres in, 4 centimetres out. Stop and it stops. Have your partner press on the second plunger as a load and the first plunger presses back on your thumb with the same force; you cannot push it further without the second plunger moving. With equal syringes the force transferred is equal to the force applied, the distance is equal, and the mechanical advantage is 1. The water has transferred the push perfectly, with none lost to compression.

The questions for this section are with you now: the procedure for the equal-syringe test and the difference observed between air and water.

## Subtopic: Unequal Syringes: Air and Then Water

For the second investigation replace one 10 millilitre syringe with a 20 millilitre syringe. The large syringe has a plunger face with about twice the area of the small one. Connect them with air first, with the small syringe full and the large one empty. Push the small plunger in fully and record how far the large plunger moves. Because air compresses, the movement is again late and reduced, and the larger volume of air in the system makes the lag more noticeable than before.

Then repeat with water, filling and connecting under the surface as before. Push the small plunger in by 4 centimetres. The large plunger moves out by only about 2 centimetres, half the distance, because the volume of water leaving the small syringe spreads over twice the area in the large one. Now have a partner press on the large plunger as a load while you push the small plunger. You will find you can hold the large plunger against a push roughly twice as strong as your own. Push with your thumb on the small plunger and the force at the large plunger is about doubled, while its movement is halved. That is a mechanical advantage of about 2, read directly from the ratio of the areas.

Reverse the direction. Fill the large syringe and push its plunger in by 2 centimetres: the small plunger shoots out about 4 centimetres but can be stopped with a lighter touch. Movement is doubled, force is halved. Record both directions in the table. A tidy results table has columns for the fluid, the syringes used, the distance pushed in, the distance moved out, and a description of the force felt, with a final column for the conclusion.

A few points of technique. Push slowly, because a fast shove can pop a tube off and spray water or make the air test look better than it is. Keep the tube short, because long tubing stretches slightly and adds its own softness. Make sure the plungers move freely before connecting; a sticky plunger feels like a load and spoils the comparison. And never point a syringe at anyone, even full of water.

The questions for this section are with you now: the unequal-syringe results, how distance and force change in each direction, and good technique.

## Subtopic: Explaining and Using the Results

The explanation runs on two ideas from the last session. First, pressure is transferred through the fluid, so the force at the output piston equals the pressure multiplied by that piston's area; equal areas give equal forces, and a larger area gives a larger force. Second, liquids are incompressible and gases are not, so water passes on both force and movement at once while air absorbs part of the push by squeezing and gives some of it back late as the air expands. These two ideas account for every row of the results table.

The conclusions for the rescue device follow. The working fluid must be water, not air, so that the jaws respond immediately and hold under load. The syringes must be filled and connected under water to exclude bubbles, and the tube should be as short as the design allows. If a strong grip is wanted, the operator should push a small syringe and the jaws should be driven by a large syringe, accepting that the jaws will move a shorter distance. If a wide, fast opening is wanted, the arrangement is reversed. Combined with the lever arms, the designer now has two places to set the trade between force and movement: the ratio of the syringe areas and the position of the jaw pivot.

Writing up matters as much as doing. The Practical Assessment Task awards investigation marks for a clear aim, a labelled diagram of the setup, a completed table, observations written in full sentences and a conclusion that answers the aim. Record what actually happened, including a lagging air plunger or a bubble that spoiled a run, and say how you fixed it. Honest results that are explained earn more than tidy results that are invented.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: connecting the water syringes in the air and trapping a bubble, then concluding that water compresses. Two: reading the air test as a failure; it is the comparison that shows why liquids are chosen. Three: expecting the large plunger to move as far as the small one; it moves less in proportion to its area. Four: leaving the results in your head; unrecorded observations earn no investigation marks.

The questions for this section are with you now: explaining the results with pressure and compressibility, the conclusions for the design and how to write up.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two syringes, a tube and a basin of water — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Same Size, Air Then Water

Take two syringes the same size. Fill one with air, push the other all the way in, and join them with a tube. Push the full one slowly. The other plunger comes out, but late and lazy: it waits while the air inside gets squeezed, then catches up. Press on it with a finger and it feels spongy, like pushing a pillow.

Now do it with water. Fill under water in the basin so no air gets in, join the tube under water, and chase out any bubbles. Push one plunger in 4 centimetres and the other comes out exactly 4 centimetres, at exactly the same moment. Press on it and the push comes straight back to your thumb. Firm, instant, equal.

Same size syringes: same force out as in, same distance. The water just carries your push along the tube like a solid rod would.

Keep the lazy air plunger and the instant water plunger in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Small Into Big, Big Into Small

Swap one syringe for a bigger one, with a plunger face about twice the size. Fill with water again. Push the small plunger in 4 centimetres. The big plunger only comes out about 2 centimetres. Half the distance.

But now get a partner to push back on the big plunger. You can hold it against a push about twice as strong as yours. Half the distance, twice the force. That is a mechanical advantage of 2, just from the sizes of the two syringes.

Now push the big one in instead. The small plunger shoots out twice as far, but a light finger can stop it. Twice the distance, half the force.

Do the same with air and everything is late and spongy again. Write every result in a table: fluid, syringes, distance in, distance out, how the force felt.

Hold the half-distance, double-force result in mind and try this part's questions: what the big and small syringes do.

## Subtopic: What It Means for Our Rescue Device

Two ideas explain everything. The push spreads through the fluid as pressure, and a bigger plunger face collects more force from the same pressure. And water does not squash, so it passes on the push at once, while air squashes first and passes it on late.

So for the rescue device: use water, not air. Fill under water so there are no bubbles. Keep the tube short. If you want a strong grip, push a small syringe and let a big syringe drive the jaws. If you want a wide, quick opening, swap them around.

Write it all up. Aim, labelled drawing, table, what you saw in full sentences, and a conclusion. If a bubble spoiled a run, say so and say how you fixed it. Real results explained well earn the marks.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Equal syringes transfer equal force and equal distance. Air lags and feels spongy because it compresses; water is instant and firm because it does not. A small syringe pushing a large one doubles the force and halves the distance, and the reverse halves force and doubles distance. Use water, exclude bubbles, and choose the syringe sizes for grip or for opening.

Hold the two ideas, pressure and no squashing, and take the final questions of the lesson: conclusions and write-up.
