# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces pneumatic and hydraulic systems as a second way, after levers, of obtaining a mechanical advantage. The anchor is a taxi rank in Durban: the doors of a bus open with a hiss of compressed air, a tyre is inflated by a pneumatic compressor, and in the workshop behind the rank a mechanic lifts a loaded minibus taxi with a trolley jack using one arm. The governing idea is that a fluid confined in a sealed system transmits pressure equally in all directions, so a force applied to a small piston appears as a larger force at a large piston, and that gases can be compressed while liquids cannot, which decides when each is used. Defining pneumatics and hydraulics, explaining how they transfer and multiply force, and giving reasons for choosing one over the other are examinable, and the water-filled syringes of the rescue device apply this directly.

## Subtopic: Fluids That Carry a Push

A fluid is anything that flows: both liquids and gases are fluids. Pneumatics is the use of gases, nearly always air, to transmit force and movement. Hydraulics is the use of liquids, usually oil or water, to do the same. Both work because a fluid in a closed container pushes on every wall of the container, and when you push on the fluid at one place, that push is felt everywhere else in the system.

The push spread over an area is called pressure. If you press a drawing pin into a board, the same force from your thumb is spread over the wide head on one side and concentrated onto the tiny point on the other. The point has a very high pressure and goes into the wood. Pressure is force divided by the area it acts on. In a fluid system, pressure is what travels along the pipe: push a piston into a cylinder full of fluid and the pressure rises throughout the fluid, all the way to the far end of the hose.

The bus door at the taxi rank is pneumatic. A compressor driven by the engine squeezes air into a tank. When the driver presses a button, a valve lets the compressed air into a cylinder beside the door, the air pushes a piston, and the piston swings the door open. When the air is released, you hear the hiss. The tyre pump is pneumatic too, and so are the air brakes on trucks, dentists' drills and the nail guns on a building site.

The trolley jack is hydraulic. The mechanic pumps a long handle, which pushes a small piston into a reservoir of oil. The oil is forced into a large cylinder under the lifting arm, and the large piston rises slowly, carrying the minibus with it. A valve holds the oil in place until the mechanic opens it to lower the vehicle. Car brakes, excavator arms, dentist chairs and the Jaws of Life are all hydraulic.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining pneumatics, hydraulics and pressure, and giving examples of each system.

## Subtopic: Why a Small Push Becomes a Big Push

The mechanical advantage of a fluid system comes from the two pistons having different areas. Imagine two syringes joined by a tube and filled with water with no air in it. Push the plunger of the small syringe with a certain force. That force is spread over the small face of the plunger, giving a certain pressure in the water. The water carries that same pressure to the large syringe, where it acts over the whole, larger face of the big plunger. Same pressure, bigger area, bigger force. The large plunger pushes outward with a force greater than the one you applied.

The multiplication is in proportion to the areas. If the large piston has a face four times the area of the small one, the force at the large piston is four times the force at the small one, and the mechanical advantage is 4. The trolley jack's lifting piston has an area perhaps fifty times that of the pump piston, so a push of 200 newtons on the handle, already multiplied by the handle lever, becomes a lift of thousands of newtons at the saddle.

As with levers, the gain in force is paid for with distance. Water cannot be squashed, so the volume pushed out of the small syringe must equal the volume pushed into the large one. The small plunger moves a long way to make the large plunger move a short way. If the large piston has four times the area, it moves one quarter of the distance. The mechanic pumps the jack handle many times to raise the car a few centimetres.

The reverse arrangement is useful too. Push the large plunger and the small one shoots out a long way with less force. That is how a hydraulic system can magnify movement for a job that needs reach rather than strength, just as a third-class lever does.

The questions for this section are with you now: why unequal pistons multiply force, how the areas set the advantage and what is traded for it.

## Subtopic: Air Is Springy, Water Is Not

Gases and liquids behave differently when squeezed, and that decides which fluid a designer chooses. Air can be compressed: its particles are far apart and push closer together under pressure. A syringe full of air with the nozzle blocked can be pushed in a long way, and it springs back when released. Water cannot be compressed in any noticeable way: its particles are already touching, so the plunger of a blocked, water-filled syringe will not move at all, however hard you push.

For transmitting force precisely, this makes liquid the better choice. Push the small syringe of a water-filled pair and the large plunger moves at once, by a predictable amount, and holds its position under load. Do the same with air and part of your push goes into squeezing the air; the far plunger moves late, moves less and bounces back when the load pushes on it. A hydraulic rescue tool must hold a car door open while rescuers work, so it must not spring; it uses oil. A hydraulic car brake must respond instantly and firmly; it uses brake fluid and must have no air bubbles in it, which is why mechanics bleed the brakes.

Pneumatics has its own advantages. Air is free and does not leak dangerously onto the floor, it is light, and its springiness can be useful: a pneumatic door closes gently, a pneumatic hammer bounces, and air-filled tyres and air suspension absorb bumps. Compressed air can be stored in a tank and released fast, which suits tools that need quick, light, repeated actions. The weaknesses are that air compresses, so the movement is less precise, and that to get a large force you need high pressure and a large piston, so very heavy lifting is almost always hydraulic.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking hydraulics and pneumatics are the same because both use tubes; one uses liquid, the other gas, and they behave differently. Two: believing the fluid creates force; it only carries and redistributes the force you apply. Three: expecting the large piston to move as far as the small one; it moves less in proportion to its area. Four: assuming air is always the worse choice; where springiness, lightness and speed matter, pneumatics wins.

The questions for this section are with you now: why gases compress and liquids do not, and when a designer chooses each.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a hissing bus door, a trolley jack and two syringes — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pushing Through a Pipe

Levers are not the only way to move a push from one place to another. You can also push through a pipe full of fluid. Fluid means anything that flows: air is a fluid, water is a fluid.

If you use air, it is called pneumatics. The bus door at the taxi rank hisses because compressed air pushes a piston that swings the door. Tyre pumps, truck brakes and nail guns are pneumatic.

If you use a liquid, it is called hydraulics. The mechanic pumps a handle, oil is pushed into a big cylinder, and a minibus rises slowly off the ground. Car brakes, diggers and the Jaws of Life are hydraulic.

The thing that travels along the pipe is pressure: a push spread over an area. Push on one end and the whole fluid feels it, right to the other end.

Keep the hissing door and the rising minibus in mind and try the questions for this part: air or liquid, and what travels along the pipe.

## Subtopic: Small Piston In, Big Piston Out

Here is the magic. Join a small syringe and a big syringe with a tube and fill them with water. Push the small plunger. The big plunger moves out, and it pushes harder than you pushed.

Why? The pressure is the same everywhere in the water. The big plunger has a bigger face, so the same pressure over a bigger face means a bigger force. If the big face is four times the size, the force is four times bigger. That is a mechanical advantage of 4, with no lever in sight.

The catch is the same as always. The big plunger moves less. Four times the force, one quarter of the distance. The mechanic pumps the handle twenty times to lift the car a hand's width.

Push the big one instead and the small one shoots out far and fast with less force. Same trade, other way round.

Hold the two syringes in mind and try this part's questions: why the big plunger pushes harder and why it moves less.

## Subtopic: Air Squashes, Water Does Not

Block the nozzle of a syringe full of air and push. The plunger goes in a long way and springs back. Air squashes. Now do it with water. The plunger will not budge. Water does not squash.

That decides the choice. If you need a firm, exact push that holds under a load, use liquid. The Jaws of Life must hold a car door open without bouncing, so it uses oil. Car brakes must grab instantly, so they use brake fluid with no air bubbles.

If you need something light, fast and a bit springy, use air. Bus doors close gently on air. Tyres ride smoothly on air. Air is free and does not make a mess if it leaks. But air is not good for lifting heavy things precisely.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Pneumatics uses air, hydraulics uses liquid, both carry pressure along a pipe. A small piston pushing a bigger piston multiplies force in proportion to the areas, and the big piston moves less. Air compresses and springs; liquid does not, so heavy, precise jobs are hydraulic. Our rescue device uses water in syringes for the same reason.

Hold the two blocked syringes, and take the final questions of the lesson: squashy air, firm water and the choice between them.
