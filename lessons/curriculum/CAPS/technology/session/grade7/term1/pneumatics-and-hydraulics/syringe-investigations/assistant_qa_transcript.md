### subtopic_1 — Equal Syringes: Air and Then Water
**Assistant:** Why does the air-filled system catch up eventually? If air absorbs the push, shouldn't the far plunger just move less and stay there?

**Tutor:** It catches up because the compressed air is storing your push, not destroying it, and stored pressure keeps working after your hand has stopped. When you push the first plunger in, the air in the syringes and the tube is squeezed into a smaller volume and its pressure rises. That raised pressure presses on the far plunger, but the plunger has friction in its barrel and will not start to move until the pressure is high enough to overcome it. During that first part of the push you see almost nothing happen at the far end while the pressure builds. Once the far plunger starts sliding, the compressed air expands behind it and continues pushing it out, so it goes on moving for a moment after you stop, which is the drift you notice. If the far plunger is held by a load, the air simply stays compressed, like a spring held down, and the first plunger can be pushed further in because there is more room to squeeze the gas. With water none of this happens because the volume cannot change: the far plunger must move the instant the near one does, by exactly the volume displaced, and stops the instant you stop. This springy storage is a nuisance for a rescue tool but a gift for a bus door or a pneumatic hammer, where a stored cushion of air gives a gentle, bouncy action. Watching the lag closely in your own experiment is the best way to understand why engineers call air compressible and water incompressible.

### subtopic_2 — Unequal Syringes: Air and Then Water
**Assistant:** My large plunger did not move exactly half as far. Does that mean the theory is wrong?

**Tutor:** No, it means you have met the difference between an ideal system and a real one, which is a valuable thing to record. The theory says the volume leaving the small syringe equals the volume entering the large one, so the distances are in inverse proportion to the areas. Several real-world effects nudge the result. The nominal sizes printed on syringes are volumes, not face areas, and the barrel diameters are not in an exact ratio; a 20 millilitre syringe does not have precisely twice the face area of a 10 millilitre one, so the ideal ratio may be 1.8 or 2.2 rather than 2. The clear tubing swells very slightly under pressure, absorbing a little volume. A small bubble you did not spot absorbs more, and makes the large plunger move noticeably less than expected. Friction in the barrels means you have to push harder at the start, and the rubber plunger tips deform slightly. Finally, reading a ruler against a moving plunger to the nearest millimetre is hard. Good scientific practice is to repeat the push three times, record all three distances, and comment on the spread. If your results come out at 1.9 or 2.1 centimetres for a 4 centimetre push, the theory is well supported. If one run gives 1.2, look for a bubble, note it in the table, fix it and run again. Writing the discrepancy down, and explaining it, shows exactly the understanding the investigation marks are looking for.

### subtopic_3 — Explaining and Using the Results
**Assistant:** Why can't we just use oil like the real Jaws of Life instead of water?

**Tutor:** You could, and in a professional tool oil is the right choice, but for a classroom model water wins on every practical count. Oil is used in real hydraulic systems for three main reasons: it lubricates the pump and the piston seals so they last for years, it does not rust the steel parts, and it can be formulated to stay liquid in freezing weather and not boil at the high temperatures that heavy use produces. None of those matters for a cardboard device that will be used for a few lessons and then kept as a record. Water, on the other hand, is free, clean, easy to see through a clear tube so that bubbles can be spotted and chased out, safe if it splashes onto skin or a desk, and simple to wipe up. Oil would stain the cardboard, weaken the glue, make the syringe plungers slippery to handle and be unpleasant to clean off hands and clothes, and if it leaked onto the floor someone could slip. Both liquids are effectively incompressible, so the physics of the model is identical: the force and distance ratios depend only on the piston areas, not on whether the liquid is water or oil. Some learners add a drop of food colouring to the water so the movement in the tube is easy to see and to photograph for the project record, which is a sensible touch. The lesson for a designer is to choose materials for the actual situation, and the situation here is a school bench, not a motorway crash.
