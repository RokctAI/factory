# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the method of the design process, which the CAPS Technology curriculum summarises as IDMEC: investigate, design, make, evaluate and communicate. The anchor is two ordinary problems that have nothing to do with rescue vehicles, a Grade 7 learner whose sandwiches arrive at school squashed, and a spaza shop owner in Khayelitsha whose stock is ruined every time it rains, because the process is easiest to see when the problem is small. The governing idea is that the design process is a way of thinking that turns a need into a tested solution, and that each step produces something, notes, drawings, a product, a judgement or a report, which can be assessed. Naming the five steps, explaining what happens in each and recognising them in a project are examinable, and the Practical Assessment Task is marked step by step against them.

## Subtopic: The Five Steps of IDMEC

Investigate is the first step. The designer finds out everything useful about the problem: who has it, what causes it, how others have solved similar problems, and what materials and tools are available. Investigation means asking questions, looking at existing products, doing simple experiments and reading. It ends with a clear understanding of the need and usually with a design brief, a short statement of what must be made and for whom.

Design is the step where ideas are produced and the best is chosen. The designer sketches several possible solutions, not just one, and compares them against the requirements. Drawing is the main tool here, from quick free-hand sketches to careful working drawings with measurements. The design step ends with a chosen idea, drawn clearly enough for someone to make it, and a list of the materials and tools needed.

Make is the step where the product is built. The designer plans the order of work, measures and marks materials, cuts, shapes, joins and finishes, working safely and accurately. Making often reveals problems the drawing hid, so small changes are normal and should be recorded.

Evaluate is the step where the product is tested against the design brief and the specifications. Does it work? Does it meet every requirement? What would make it better? Evaluation is honest judgement, not praise, and it applies to the process as well as the product: was the investigation thorough, was the time well used?

Communicate runs through the whole process. The designer keeps notes, labels sketches, writes the brief, and at the end presents the solution to others with drawings, a report or a talk. In the CAPS curriculum, communication is listed last, but a designer communicates at every step, because a design that cannot be explained cannot be made by anyone else.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming the five steps in order and saying what each step produces.

## Subtopic: Two Everyday Examples

First example: the squashed sandwich. A Grade 7 learner, Thabo, finds that his sandwiches are flattened by his textbooks every day. Investigate: he notices the problem happens only on days with heavy books, he looks at shop-bought lunch boxes and finds them too big for his bag, and he measures the space he has. Design: he sketches three ideas, a rigid plastic box, a folded cardboard sleeve and a small tin with a lid, and chooses the tin because it is strong, cheap and fits. Make: he cleans an empty biscuit tin, lines it with a cloth and adds a rubber band to hold the lid. Evaluate: after a week the sandwiches are intact, but the tin rattles, so he adds a paper pad; the rattling was a specification he had not thought of. Communicate: he shows the class his sketches and explains why the tin beat the cardboard sleeve.

Second example: the leaking roof. Mrs Dlamini's spaza shop has a corrugated iron roof, and rain drips onto the shelf of maize meal. Investigate: she watches where the water enters and finds a rusted nail hole, she asks a neighbour who has fixed roofs, and she checks the price of sealant at the hardware store. Design: she considers replacing the sheet, patching with a tin lid and bitumen, or using a silicone sealant, and chooses the patch because a whole sheet is too expensive. Make: on a dry day she cleans the area, screws the patch over the hole and seals the edges. Evaluate: the next rain shows no drip, but she notices two other nails starting to rust, so she treats them too. Communicate: she tells the neighbour what worked, so the solution spreads.

In both examples the steps are the same, the people are not engineers, and the solutions are modest. That is the point. The design process is not reserved for factories; it is a disciplined way of solving problems that anybody can use, and the rescue device project simply applies it to a harder problem.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying which IDMEC step a described action belongs to in the two examples.

## Subtopic: A Loop, Not a Line

Although IDMEC is written as a list, the process is not a straight line. Evaluation sends the designer back to design when a test fails. Making sends the designer back to investigate when a material behaves unexpectedly. Thabo's rattling tin was discovered at the evaluate step and fixed by returning to design and make. Mrs Dlamini's rusting nails were discovered at evaluate and sent her back to investigate how widespread the rust was. Designers draw the process as a cycle with arrows between the steps, and the word iterate, meaning to repeat with improvements, describes this looping.

Communication is the step that is easiest to leave out and the one examiners look for most carefully. A sketch without labels, a model without a design brief, or a test without written results shows that the process was not followed, however good the product looks. In the Practical Assessment Task the marks for investigate, design and make are awarded from the written and drawn record, not from memory of what you did.

Map the term onto IDMEC. Weeks 1 to 4 are investigation: the scenario, levers, mechanical advantage, pneumatics and hydraulics, with practical tests using syringes. Weeks 5 to 7 are design: the design brief, specifications, sketches and drawings of the rescue device. Weeks 8 and 9 are make: the working cardboard and syringe model. Evaluate and communicate happen at the end, when the model is tested against the brief and presented. The structure of the term is the structure of the process.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking design means drawing a single pretty picture; design means producing several ideas and choosing with reasons. Two: treating evaluate as saying the product is nice; evaluation tests against the brief and records faults. Three: leaving communication to the end; notes and labelled sketches are communication throughout. Four: believing the process is a straight line; it loops back whenever a test or a material shows a problem.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the process loops, what happens at evaluate, and matching the term's weeks to the five steps.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a squashed sandwich, a leaking roof and five letters — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Five Letters: I, D, M, E, C

Every Technology project follows five steps. We remember them as IDMEC.

I is investigate. Find out about the problem. Ask questions, look at what already exists, test materials.

D is design. Draw more than one idea, then pick the best one and say why.

M is make. Build it, carefully and safely, following your drawing.

E is evaluate. Test it. Does it do the job? What would make it better?

C is communicate. Write things down, label your sketches and explain your solution to others. You do this all the way through, not just at the end.

Keep the five letters in mind and try the questions for this part: what each letter stands for and what you do in that step.

## Subtopic: The Sandwich and the Roof

Thabo's sandwiches get squashed by his books. He investigates: the problem happens on heavy-book days, and shop lunch boxes are too big. He designs: a plastic box, a cardboard sleeve or a biscuit tin. He picks the tin. He makes it: cleans the tin, adds a cloth and a rubber band. He evaluates: the sandwiches are fine, but the tin rattles, so he adds a paper pad. He communicates: he shows his sketches to the class.

Mrs Dlamini's shop roof leaks onto the maize meal. She investigates: the water comes through a rusted nail hole, and a neighbour gives advice. She designs: new sheet, a patch, or sealant. The patch is cheapest. She makes: she screws on a patch and seals it. She evaluates: no more drips, but two other nails are rusting, so she fixes them too. She communicates: she tells the neighbour.

Two small problems, two ordinary people, the same five steps. The design process is for everyone.

Hold the tin and the patched roof in mind and try this part's questions: spotting the step in each action.

## Subtopic: Going Round Again

The five steps are not a straight line. When Thabo's tin rattled, the evaluate step sent him back to design and make. When Mrs Dlamini found more rust, evaluate sent her back to investigate. Designers draw IDMEC as a circle. Going round again to improve is normal, and it is called iterating.

Our term follows the circle too. Weeks 1 to 4, we investigate: the crash scenario, levers, syringes and water. Weeks 5 to 7, we design: the brief, the sketches, the drawings. Weeks 8 and 9, we make the model. Then we evaluate it against the brief and communicate it to the class.

Pocket summary of the lesson. IDMEC is investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate. Investigate means finding out; design means drawing several ideas and choosing; make means building; evaluate means testing against the brief; communicate means recording and explaining throughout. The steps loop back when a test shows a problem. The whole term is one trip around the loop.

Hold the circle of five steps, and take the final questions of the lesson: why the process loops and how the term fits it.
