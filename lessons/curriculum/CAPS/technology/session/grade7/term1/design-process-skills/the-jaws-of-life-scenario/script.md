# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the problem scenario that drives the whole of Term 1: emergency workers using a hydraulic rescue tool, known by the trade name Jaws of Life, to free people trapped in crashed vehicles. The anchor is a road accident on the N1 outside Bloemfontein, where paramedics cannot reach an injured passenger because the door frame has folded around her. The governing idea is that technology begins with a human need or problem, and that a designer must understand the problem fully before trying to solve it. In Technology, a problem scenario is the starting point of the design process, and describing a need, identifying the people affected and listing what a solution must do are examinable skills that lead directly to the Practical Assessment Task.

## Subtopic: The Scenario: Trapped After a Crash

Every year South African roads see thousands of serious collisions, and in many of them the people inside are not killed by the impact but are trapped by it. A car body is designed to crumple so that it absorbs energy and protects the passengers, but the same crumpling can jam doors, push the dashboard onto legs and bend the roof pillars so that nobody can get in or out. Paramedics call this entrapment. Time matters enormously, because a badly injured person needs to reach a hospital quickly, and rescue workers often speak of the golden hour, the first hour after an injury, when treatment makes the biggest difference.

Picture the scene on the N1. It is raining. A bakkie and a small car have collided and the car has rolled onto its side. The driver has climbed out, but the passenger is pinned, conscious and in pain. The ambulance crew arrives first, followed by the fire and rescue service. They try the doors: jammed. They try to lift the roof by hand: impossible. The metal of a car body is thin, but it is steel, and once it is folded it is far too strong for human muscles to bend back. This is the moment when a technological problem becomes clear: people need to open crushed steel quickly, safely and without hurting the person inside.

Notice what the rescuers cannot use. A hammer would shake the car and injure the passenger further. A hacksaw is slow and the vibrations are dangerous. Flame cutting is out of the question with petrol leaking. Whatever tool is used must produce a very large force but apply it slowly and under control. That combination, large force and careful control, is what the Jaws of Life provides.

The questions for this section are with you now: what entrapment means, why time matters in a rescue and why ordinary hand tools are unsuitable.

## Subtopic: How the Jaws of Life Works

The Jaws of Life is a family of hydraulic rescue tools: spreaders, cutters and rams. A spreader has two arms shaped like a bird's beak. The closed tips are pushed into the gap between a door and its frame, and when the operator presses a control the arms open slowly with enormous force, prising the door away from the body. A cutter works the other way, closing hardened steel blades to snip through a roof pillar. A ram is a straight pushing tool that forces the dashboard off a trapped person's legs. All three use the same principle: a liquid under pressure.

Hydraulics means using a liquid, usually a special oil, to transfer force. A petrol engine or an electric motor drives a pump that pushes oil along a flexible hose into a cylinder inside the tool. The oil presses on a piston, and because the piston is wide and the oil cannot be squashed, a small pump produces a huge push at the tool. The arms of the spreader are levers, so the force is multiplied again. A typical rescue spreader can push with a force of more than a hundred thousand newtons, which is roughly the weight of ten family cars, and yet the operator controls it with a thumb.

The tool was developed in the early 1970s by an American engineer, George Hurst, who had watched racing drivers trapped in wrecks while rescuers struggled with saws. Today every major fire station in South Africa carries a set. The important thing for us is not the brand but the technology: a hydraulic system and a lever system working together. Those two systems are exactly what we study in the next few weeks, and they are the two systems our own model will use.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three types of rescue tool, what hydraulics means and the two systems the tool combines.

## Subtopic: From Scenario to Problem Statement

A designer never jumps straight from a scenario to a solution. The first job is to turn the story into a clear statement of the problem and the need. From the N1 scene we can write: people trapped in crushed vehicles need to be freed quickly, safely and without further injury, using a tool that produces a large controlled force. That sentence names the people affected, the need and the conditions a solution must meet.

Next the designer lists the requirements that the scenario forces on any solution. The tool must be strong enough to bend or cut steel. It must be portable, because the crash can happen far from a workshop. It must be controllable, so that it opens a few millimetres at a time. It must be safe for the operator and the victim, with no sparks near fuel. It must work in rain and darkness. Requirements like these later become the specifications in a design brief.

In this term you will be asked to design and make a working model of a rescue device, powered by syringes and water and built from cardboard, that opens a pair of jaws. Nobody expects a model to cut steel. What is expected is that you understand the real problem, that you can explain how hydraulics and levers solve it, and that your model shows the same principle on a small scale. Keep the N1 scene in mind through the whole term; every lesson until the model is finished connects back to it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Jaws of Life is one tool; it is a set of spreaders, cutters and rams. Two: thinking the tool is fast because it is powerful; it is powerful and slow, by design, to protect the victim. Three: starting to design before the problem is understood; the scenario must be turned into a problem statement first. Four: thinking the model is meant to cut metal; the model shows the principle, not the full-size force.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing a problem statement from a scenario, listing requirements and what the term's model must show.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a crashed car, a rescue tool and a problem written in one sentence — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Stuck in a Crushed Car

Imagine a car crash on a wet highway. The car has rolled over. The doors are bent shut. Someone inside is hurt and cannot get out. The rescuers are there within minutes, but they cannot open the doors by pulling, because bent steel is far too strong for human arms.

They also cannot bash the car with a hammer, because that would hurt the person inside. They cannot use a flame, because petrol is leaking. They need a tool that pushes with a huge force, but slowly and gently, a little at a time.

That is a technology problem. A problem is simply a need that people have and cannot meet yet. Here the need is clear: free trapped people quickly and safely.

Keep the crashed car in mind and try the questions for this part: what the rescuers need and why normal tools fail.

## Subtopic: A Tool Powered by Liquid

The Jaws of Life is the tool the rescuers use. The spreader looks like a giant beak. Its closed tips go into the crack of the door, and then the beak opens, pushing the door off the car.

What gives it the power? A liquid. A pump pushes oil down a hose into the tool. The oil presses on a wide piston, and because you cannot squash a liquid, a small pump turns into a giant push. The arms of the beak are levers, which make the push even bigger. The operator just presses a button with a thumb.

So the tool uses two ideas: liquid under pressure, which we call hydraulics, and levers. Those are the two ideas we will learn this term, and our model will use both.

Hold the beak-shaped spreader in mind and try this part's questions: what makes it strong and what two systems it uses.

## Subtopic: Writing the Problem Down

Before anyone designs anything, they write the problem down in one clear sentence. Ours could be: people trapped in crashed cars need to be freed quickly and safely with a tool that gives a big, controlled force.

Then we list what any solution must do. It must be strong. It must be carried to the crash. It must open a little at a time. It must be safe, with no sparks. It must work in rain and in the dark. Later, this list becomes our design brief.

This term you will design and make a small working model of a rescue device using syringes, water tubing and cardboard. It will not cut steel, and it is not supposed to. It is supposed to show the same idea: liquid pushing levers to open a pair of jaws.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Technology starts with a real human need. Trapped crash victims need to be freed fast and safely. The Jaws of Life does this with hydraulics and levers. A designer first writes the problem down and lists what a solution must do. Our term project is a model that shows the same principle.

Hold the one-sentence problem and the list of needs, and take the final questions of the lesson: the problem statement and what the model must show.
