### subtopic_1 — The Five Steps of IDMEC
**Assistant:** Why does the curriculum insist on sketching several ideas in the design step? If I already know the best idea, drawing others seems like a waste.

**Tutor:** Because the idea you already have is almost always the first one that came to mind, and first ideas are usually the most obvious rather than the best. Designers have learned this the hard way across many fields. When a team produces only one idea, every later decision bends to protect it, and faults are explained away instead of fixed. When the same team sketches three or four ideas, something useful happens: the ideas start to compete, and the comparison itself reveals requirements nobody had written down. Thabo only realised that size mattered when he compared the shop-bought box with the tin; the comparison exposed a hidden specification. There is a second benefit. Sketching is fast and cheap, while making is slow and expensive. A bad idea rejected on paper costs ten minutes; a bad idea rejected after it is built costs the materials and a week. Professional engineers put it bluntly: fail on paper, not in the workshop. Finally, the Practical Assessment Task gives marks for the range of ideas and for the reasons behind the choice, because the reasoning is what shows understanding. A single idea, even a good one, offers no evidence that you understood the requirements well enough to judge between options. So sketch the obvious idea first, by all means, and then force yourself to draw two more that are genuinely different; the exercise sharpens the one you finally choose.

### subtopic_2 — Two Everyday Examples
**Assistant:** Is the design process really the same for a lunch box and for something like a bridge or a cell phone? The scale seems completely different.

**Tutor:** The scale is wildly different, the steps are the same, and that is one of the most useful facts in the subject. An engineering firm designing a bridge across the Umgeni River investigates the ground, the traffic, the floods and the budget, and the investigation may take years and fill shelves of reports. It designs by producing many concepts, from arches to suspension cables, and chooses with calculations rather than a quick look. It makes with cranes and concrete over several seasons. It evaluates with load tests and inspections for decades afterwards, and it communicates through thousands of drawings and documents so that every worker builds the same bridge. A cell phone company runs the same loop with teams of thousands, iterating through hundreds of prototypes before one model is sold. Thabo did the same five things at his kitchen table in an afternoon. What changes with scale is the depth of each step, the number of people involved and the cost of a mistake, not the sequence of thinking. This is why the Senior Phase teaches the process on small projects: you can go round the whole loop in a term and feel how the steps connect, which is impossible to feel on a bridge. When you later meet engineering, architecture or software, you will recognise the same shape, just with more at each station.

### subtopic_3 — A Loop, Not a Line
**Assistant:** What exactly is the difference between evaluating and testing? My teacher uses both words.

**Tutor:** Testing is one of the tools of evaluation, and evaluation is the wider judgement that uses the test results. When Mrs Dlamini waits for the next rain and looks for drips, she is testing: she sets up a situation and observes a result. When she then decides that the patch worked but that two further nails are a risk and must be treated, she is evaluating: she compares what she observed with what the solution was meant to achieve and judges what to do next. A test gives you facts; an evaluation gives you a verdict and a decision. Good evaluation in Technology always refers back to the design brief and the specifications. If the brief said the rescue model must lift a 200 gram load, the test is loading it with 200 grams and seeing whether the jaws hold; the evaluation is stating whether the specification was met, explaining why or why not, and recommending a change such as a stiffer lever arm. Evaluation also covers things a test cannot measure directly, such as whether the materials were used economically, whether the process stayed on time and whether safety rules were followed. Examiners look for this difference. An answer that says only that the model worked well is a weak evaluation; an answer that lists each specification, states whether the test met it and proposes one improvement is a strong one.
