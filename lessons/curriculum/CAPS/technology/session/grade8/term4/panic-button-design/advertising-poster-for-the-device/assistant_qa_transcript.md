### subtopic_1 — What a Poster Must Do: Audience, Message and the Ten-Second Test
**Assistant:** Why can we not say 'keeps you safe'? That is what the product is for.

**Tutor:** Because it is not what the product does, and the gap between what a thing is for and what it does is exactly where advertising becomes dishonest. The panic button makes a loud noise and a bright light when a button is pressed. Whether that keeps anyone safe depends on a neighbour being home, hearing it, choosing to come, and arriving in time, and on the emergency being one that a neighbour can help with; none of that is in the device. "Keeps you safe" promises an outcome the designer cannot deliver, and a grandmother who buys the device on that promise and is not kept safe has been misled at the worst possible moment. The advertising code calls this an unsubstantiated claim, and the Advertising Regulatory Board has ordered many such claims withdrawn. The honest version is more specific and, as it happens, more persuasive: "Press any button and a siren and light tell your neighbour you need help, even when the power is off." It says what happens, it implies the benefit, and every word of it was tested on the bench and across the fence. The habit this builds is the one that matters in every field: describe what you have tested, let the reader draw the conclusion, and never let the purpose you hoped for stand in for the result you measured.

### subtopic_2 — Designing the Poster: Headline, Image, Benefits, Layout
**Assistant:** The clinic board is full of posters. How do we make ours stand out without shouting?

**Tutor:** By doing the opposite of what the crowded board does, because on a board full of noise the quiet thing is the one the eye rests on. Most notices on a community board are dense, small-typed, multi-coloured and photocopied, so a poster with a single very large headline, one strong picture and a third of its area left empty is immediately different; the space around it acts as a frame and the eye is drawn to the calm. Second, use the picture to carry the story: a large, clear image of a hand on a big button, or a grandmother in a doorway with a lamp glowing above it, can be read from across the room before any words, which is more than any amount of shouting type achieves. Third, speak to the reader's situation rather than about yourself: a headline like "Living alone? Help is one press away" stops the person it describes, where "New alarm system available" stops nobody. Fourth, colour used sparingly stands out more than colour used everywhere: a single accent colour on the product name and the button, against a mostly black-and-white design, reads as deliberate and professional. Finally, position: ask the clinic to pin it at eye height near the queue, since the best poster on the top corner of the board behind the door goes unread. Standing out is not about volume; it is about being the one thing on the wall that is easy to read.

### subtopic_3 — Honesty, Accessibility and Evaluating the Finished Poster
**Assistant:** If the poster is in two languages, does that not double the text and break the rule about space?

**Tutor:** It would if you simply translated everything, which is why the bilingual poster is a design problem and not a translation problem. The trick is to decide which elements need both languages and which do not. The headline does, because it is the message, so it appears twice, in two large lines, perhaps in two weights or two colours so the pairing is obvious. The benefits do, but they are short lines, and the translations can sit directly beneath each English line in a slightly smaller or lighter type, so that each benefit becomes a two-line pair rather than two separate lists. The picture needs no translation at all, which is one of the reasons the picture is worth the space it takes; it is the one element every reader shares. The call to action, "Ask at the school office" with a number and price, is short in any language, and numbers are universal. The product name stays as it is. Done this way the bilingual poster carries perhaps forty percent more words than an English-only one, which is absorbed by using slightly fewer benefits, four rather than five, and keeping the headline to six words. The rule about space is really a rule about reading: a third of the sheet empty so that the eye can find the hierarchy; two languages arranged in clear pairs respect that just as well as one. The reward is a poster that half the queue could not otherwise read, and that, for a device meant for grandmothers, is the whole point.
