# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers designing an advertising poster for the panic button device. The anchor is the notice board outside the Mamelodi clinic, where Gogo Mahlangu's granddaughter will pin the class's poster among the notices about immunisation days and funeral policies, to be read by people queueing in the sun who have perhaps ten seconds to spare.

## Subtopic: What a Poster Must Do: Audience, Message and the Ten-Second Test

A poster is a piece of graphic communication, and like every piece of communication it has an audience, a message and a purpose, and it fails if any of the three is unclear. The audience here is the people who would buy or ask for a panic button for themselves or a relative: older people, their adult children, carers, and neighbours in a community where the clinic notice board is read while waiting. They are standing, in sunlight, at a distance of one to three metres, for a few seconds, and many of them read English as a second or third language or have failing eyesight. Every design decision follows from that picture.

The message is one idea, stated so that the reader takes it away without effort. For the panic button it is something like: help is one press away, from any room, even when the power is off. Everything else on the poster supports that idea; anything that distracts from it is removed. A poster that tries to say five things says nothing, because the reader does not stop long enough to sort them out. The purpose is action: the reader should know what to do next, phone a number, visit the school, ask at the clinic, and that instruction must be on the poster in a form that can be photographed or torn off.

The ten-second test governs everything. Pin the poster up, walk away three metres, turn and look at it for ten seconds, then turn back and say what the product is, who it is for, why they would want it, and what to do about it. If any of those is missing, the poster has failed, however pretty it is. Professional advertisers run this test with strangers, because the designer already knows the answers and cannot judge; you will run it with another group.

The questions for this section are with you now: the audience and their reading conditions, the single message, the purpose, and the ten-second test.

## Subtopic: Designing the Poster: Headline, Image, Benefits, Layout

Design in stages, like any other product. Start with thumbnails: six quick sketches, each the size of a matchbox, trying different arrangements of the same four elements, headline, image, benefits and call to action; this costs ten minutes and saves hours, because the layout that works is obvious at matchbox size and a bad one is cheap to throw away. Choose one, enlarge it to a rough A4 pencil layout, and only then begin the finished A3 sheet.

The elements. The headline is the message in the fewest words, five to eight, in the largest type on the page, readable from three metres, which means letters at least fifty millimetres high; it speaks to the reader's need, "Help is one press away", not to the product's features, "Three-switch parallel alarm". The image is one strong picture that shows the product in use, a hand pressing a large button, a grandmother in a doorway, a neighbour looking up at a light; a drawing or a photograph, large, with the product clearly visible and the people shown with dignity. The benefits are three to five short lines, each beginning with what the reader gets, "Works when the power is off", "Buttons by the bed, stove and toilet", "Loud enough for the neighbour to hear", "No monthly fee", in type readable from two metres; features are translated into benefits, so "battery powered" becomes "works during load shedding". The call to action is the instruction and the contact: "Ask at the school office" with a phone number and a price, in a box at the bottom, large enough to read and perhaps with tear-off strips.

Layout principles that work. Hierarchy: the eye should go headline, image, benefits, action, in that order, by size and position; the top left and centre are read first. Alignment: elements line up on a few invisible lines, not scattered; a grid of three columns and four rows helps. Contrast: dark type on a light background or the reverse, never dark on dark or pale on pale; a product name in a single accent colour. Space: at least a third of the sheet empty, because space is what makes the rest readable; a crowded poster is skipped. Consistency: one or two typefaces, bold sans-serif for headlines, one palette of three colours. A logo or name for the product, something short and memorable, "Gogo's Button", appears once, large, and is repeated small near the contact.

The questions for this section are with you now: the thumbnail and rough stages, the four elements and how each is written or drawn, and the layout principles.

## Subtopic: Honesty, Accessibility and Evaluating the Finished Poster

A poster also has duties. The first is honesty. The Advertising Regulatory Board's code, which South African advertisers follow, requires that claims be truthful and substantiated, that the product not be presented as doing what it cannot do, and that fear not be used irresponsibly to sell. For the panic button this means: say "loud enough for the neighbour to hear", which you have tested, not "calls the police", which it does not; say "works during load shedding", which the battery guarantees, not "keeps you safe", which no device can promise; show a grandmother reaching a button with calm competence, not a terrified victim, because frightening elderly readers into buying is the kind of advertising the code forbids and the kind a school should not produce. Every claim on the poster should trace back to a specification that was tested, which is why the design process matters: the test record is the evidence behind the advertisement.

The second duty is accessibility, which here overlaps with good design. Large type for readers with weak eyes; a headline and benefits in English with a second language of the community alongside, isiZulu or Sepedi or whatever the clinic's readers speak, which doubles the readers for little cost; a picture that tells the story without words; strong contrast for readers in bright sun and for colour-blind readers, who should not have to tell red from green to understand anything; a price and contact in plain numbers. These are not extras but the point, since the people who most need the device are the ones most likely to be excluded by small, clever, English-only design.

Evaluate the finished poster against a checklist before it goes up: the ten-second test with another group, scored on product, audience, benefit and action; a check of every claim against the specification and test record; a readability check from three metres and two metres for headline and benefits; a bilingual check with a speaker of the second language; and a look at the picture for dignity. Record what passed and what did not, change what you can, and write a line of evaluation in the project file. The poster, the diagram, the truth table, the brief and the evaluation together are the project the term is marked on.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a paragraph of text instead of a headline and benefits, unreadable from a metre away. Two: a claim the device cannot meet, "calls for help automatically". Three: fear imagery, a cowering figure, to sell to the elderly. Four: tiny type, pale colours and English only, excluding exactly the people the device is for.

The questions for this section are with you now: honest claims and the advertising code, accessibility choices, and the evaluation checklist.
