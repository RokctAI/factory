### subtopic_1 — The Scenario: Who Needs a Panic Button and Why
**Assistant:** Why can the brief not just say 'three switches in parallel with a buzzer'? That is obviously what we are going to build.

**Tutor:** Because the moment the brief names the solution it stops being a brief and becomes an instruction, and it closes off every better idea before anyone has had it. A brief describes the need: any one of three points must raise an alarm that the neighbour can hear and see. Several designs satisfy that. Three push switches in parallel with a buzzer is one. Three switches in parallel with a buzzer and a flashing LED is another, and better, because of the hard-of-hearing neighbour. A design with a latching relay so that the alarm keeps sounding after Gogo lets go of the button, which she will if she has fallen, is better still, and the brief that said "three switches in parallel with a buzzer" would have ruled it out. A wireless pendant she wears round her neck satisfies the brief and might beat all of them. The brief's job is to hold the door open for those ideas while making sure every one of them is judged by the same standard, the need. There is also an examination reason: the marker is checking that you can separate a problem from a solution, which is the core skill of design, and a brief that is really a solution shows you cannot. Write the need; let the circuit earn its place next lesson by being the design that best meets it.

### subtopic_2 — Writing the Design Brief: Problem, Users, Purpose
**Assistant:** How do I know whether something is a specification or a constraint? Some things seem like both.

**Tutor:** Ask whether it describes what the device must do for the user or what the designer must work within, and if it is honestly both, put it where it helps most and do not lose sleep. A specification is a property of the finished product that you can test on the product itself: it must sound from any one button, it must be audible across the fence, it must run on batteries. A constraint is a boundary on the design process or the resources: it must be built from the class kit, it must cost under two hundred rand, it must be finished in two sessions, it must not use mains. Cost is the usual case that seems to be both, because "under two hundred rand" is a property of the product and a limit on the designer; the convention is to treat money, time, available materials and legal or safety rules as constraints, since they are imposed from outside the need, and to treat everything the user will experience as a specification. The reason the distinction matters is what you do when you fail one. A failed specification means the product does not meet the need and must be redesigned. A failed constraint means the product may be excellent but cannot be built here and now, and the fix may be to argue for a bigger budget or a different kit rather than to change the design. Keeping the two lists apart tells you, at evaluation time, which kind of problem you have.

### subtopic_3 — Specifications and Constraints: What the Device Must Do and Must Not Be
**Assistant:** Should the brief include the daughter twenty minutes away? A real panic button would phone her.

**Tutor:** It should include her as part of the investigation and probably exclude her from this brief's purpose, and the reasoning is a good example of scoping. The investigation records everyone who could help, and the daughter is one of them; a complete system would indeed send her a message, and commercial panic buttons do exactly that through a phone network. But a brief must be achievable within its constraints, and the class kit has cells, switches, buzzers and LEDs, not a mobile modem; a purpose that includes "and sends a text to her daughter" sets a goal the project cannot reach, and the design would fail its own brief on day one. The honest approach is to write the brief around what can be built, the local siren and light, and to add a sentence in the investigation or the evaluation that says a future version should alert a remote contact by phone, which is a recommendation for development rather than a requirement for this design. This is how real engineering briefs work: they name a scope, deliver inside it, and list what the next version should add. The daughter is also served indirectly by the design you can build, because the neighbour who hears the siren is the one who will phone her, which is worth saying in the brief's purpose: the alarm calls the neighbour, and the neighbour calls the family.
