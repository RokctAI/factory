### subtopic_1 — Revising Term 4: Circuits, Energy and Generation
**Assistant:** Which Term 4 topics are most likely to be on the paper, so I know where to spend my time?

**Tutor:** The paper is set from the whole term, so nothing can be skipped, but the weighting follows the time the curriculum gave each topic and the skills that are easiest to examine on paper, and that points to three clusters. First, circuits: drawing and reading circuit diagrams with standard symbols, series and parallel behaviour, and the Ohm's law investigation, because these can be tested with a diagram and a few lines and they carry the term's core skill. Second, logic and the panic button: a truth table for AND or OR, naming the gate from a table or a description, and the design process items, a brief from a scenario, a specification list, a circuit diagram with switches in parallel and an eight-row table, because the project is the term's assessment task and the paper will echo it. Third, generation and the grid: advantages and disadvantages of coal, nuclear, solar and wind, how a thermal station works, why the grid uses AC and transformers, and the settlement energy and illegal connection questions in the form of a short balanced paragraph. Cells in series and parallel and the solar cell usually appear as a few marks each. A sensible plan gives a third of your revision to circuits and Ohm's law, a third to logic and the project, and a third to generation, energy and Term 3, with the Term 3 gear ratio calculation practised until it is automatic because it is the one place numbers appear.

### subtopic_2 — Revising Term 4: Ohm's Law, Logic Gates and the Panic Button
**Assistant:** I understand the work but I lose marks on explain questions. What am I doing wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly you are writing the answer and leaving out the reason, which is what the word "explain" is asking for. An examiner who writes "Explain why two lamps in series are dimmer than one" has a marking scheme with two or three points: that the lamps share the supply voltage, so each has less voltage across it; that less voltage means less current through the loop; and that a lamp with less current glows less brightly. A learner who writes "because there are two lamps" has restated the question and earns nothing; one who writes "they are dimmer because they share the voltage" earns one mark; one who writes all three steps earns full marks. The fix is mechanical and works every time: for an explain question, write the fact, then write "because", then write the cause, then write "so" or "which means" and the consequence, and check that your answer has at least as many reasons as the question has marks. The same applies to "why" and "account for". Two other habits help. Use the technical words the question uses, voltage, current, resistance, series, because the marking scheme is written in them. And resist the urge to write everything you know about the topic; a focused answer that follows the chain from cause to effect scores higher than a paragraph that mentions the right idea somewhere in the middle. Practise by taking any fact from this lesson and writing the three-step explanation for it in under a minute.

### subtopic_3 — Revising Term 3 and Preparing for the End-of-Year Examination
**Assistant:** How do I remember which gate is which under pressure? I keep swapping AND and OR.

**Tutor:** Anchor each gate to one picture you cannot confuse, and to one physical fact about switches. The physical fact is this: switches in series must both be closed for current to pass, because there is one path, and that is AND; switches in parallel let current pass if either is closed, because there are two paths, and that is OR. If you can see the two circuits in your mind, the gates follow; you do not need to memorise the rules, only to picture the paths. The picture for AND is the microwave or the two-handed guillotine: both conditions, both hands, nothing happens with one alone, so the output column has a single 1 at the very bottom where everything is on. The picture for OR is a burglar alarm or your own panic button: any door, any window, any button, so the output column has a single 0 at the very top where nothing is on. Under pressure, draw the two-switch circuit first, trace the current with your finger for each row, and write what the lamp does; the table builds itself and you will never swap them. A final check that costs five seconds: the AND symbol is a D, and the word AND has a D in it; the OR symbol is a pointed shield, which has nothing to do with a D. Silly, but it has rescued many learners in the last minute of a paper.
