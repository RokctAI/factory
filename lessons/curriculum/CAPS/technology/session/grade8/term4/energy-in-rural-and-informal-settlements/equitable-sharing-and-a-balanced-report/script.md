# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers equitable sharing of power resources and writing a balanced report. The anchor is a Grade 8 learner in Tembisa with a notebook of interviews: a grandmother on a grant with a prepaid meter, a shop owner whose fridges fail in load shedding, a councillor, a municipal electrician, and a neighbour with an illegal connection, all answering the same question: what would be fair?

## Subtopic: Equitable Sharing of Power: What Fair Access to Electricity Means

Equitable does not mean equal. Equal sharing would give every household the same number of units regardless of need; equitable sharing gives people what they need to live decently, weighted by their circumstances, which is why South Africa's policy includes Free Basic Electricity, a free monthly allocation of about fifty kilowatt-hours to poor households, enough for lights, a radio and a phone, and inclining block tariffs, where the first units each month are cheapest and heavy users pay more per unit. Both are attempts at equity: those with the least get a floor; those who use most pay most.

The resource being shared is finite in several ways. Generation capacity is finite, which is why load shedding exists: when demand exceeds what the power stations can supply, the grid operator switches off areas in turn to prevent the whole system collapsing, and the schedule by which it does so is itself a question of fairness, since hospitals, water pumps and some industries are protected while residential areas rotate. Network capacity is finite in each place: a transformer serves a fixed load, and connecting a settlement to it means either upgrading it or sharing less with everyone on it. Money is finite: the electrification grant, the Free Basic Electricity subsidy and the maintenance budget all compete, so connecting one settlement may mean a slower upgrade elsewhere. And the country's generation is mostly coal, so sharing more electricity more widely also shares more pollution and more climate damage, which raises the question of fairness to future generations.

The parties with a claim are many: households that pay and depend on supply for work and school; households that cannot pay and depend on it for safety and dignity; industry and mines that employ people and need reliable power; farms that pump water; hospitals, schools and clinics; the utility that must recover its costs to keep the lights on; and the environment and the future. Equitable sharing is the attempt to weigh these claims openly. Any policy that pretends there is no trade-off is hiding one.

The questions for this section are with you now: equitable versus equal, the ways the resource is finite, and the parties with a claim.

## Subtopic: Gathering Evidence: Facts, Figures and Voices From Both Sides

A balanced report rests on evidence, and evidence comes in two kinds. Facts and figures: how many households in the area are connected, how much Free Basic Electricity is, what a unit costs on the local tariff, how often the transformer has failed, how many fires the fire service recorded, what the municipality's electrification plan says, what the Constitution and the Electricity Regulation Act require. These come from Statistics South Africa's household surveys, municipal documents and tariff schedules, Eskom and NERSA publications, newspaper reports and the fire department, and each must be cited: say where the number came from and when. A number without a source is an opinion wearing a disguise.

Voices: the second kind of evidence is what people say, gathered by interview or survey, and here balance means hearing more than one side on purpose. Interview someone who pays and someone who cannot; someone who has supply and someone who has not; an official and a resident; a person harmed by illegal connections, such as a shop owner whose fridge stock spoiled when the transformer failed, and a person who depends on one. Ask the same questions of each so that answers can be compared: what do you use electricity for, what does it cost you, what happens when it fails, what would be fair? Record what they say accurately, in their words where possible, and note who they are without exposing them: "a grandmother on a pension in Extension 9", not a name and address, especially when someone admits to an illegal connection, since your report could put them at risk.

Then test the evidence. Do the figures and the voices agree? If the councillor says the settlement will be electrified next year and residents say they were told the same thing three years ago, both are evidence, and the gap between them is a finding. Is a figure recent? Is a source likely to be biased, and if so, is the bias stated? Have you looked for evidence that contradicts your own first opinion, which is the single discipline that separates a balanced report from a persuasive one?

The questions for this section are with you now: the two kinds of evidence, how to gather voices fairly, and how evidence is tested.

## Subtopic: Writing the Balanced Report: Structure, Tone and a Reasoned Conclusion

The report itself has a structure that readers expect. A title that names the question: "How should electricity be shared fairly in Tembisa Extension 9?" An introduction of a paragraph that states the question, why it matters and how you gathered evidence. A background section that gives the facts and figures with their sources. A section presenting the views, organised by side or by theme, each side given comparable space and stated in its strongest form, with quotations where they are vivid. A discussion section that weighs the sides against criteria you make explicit, need, cost, safety, fairness, sustainability, and that admits where the evidence is thin. A conclusion that states what you think is the fairest course and why, as a reasoned judgement that follows from the discussion, with recommendations that are specific and possible: "upgrade the Extension 9 transformer and install pole-mounted ready boards within eighteen months, with Free Basic Electricity applied automatically to grant recipients", not "the government must fix it". A list of sources and interviews at the end.

Tone matters as much as structure. Balanced writing uses neutral verbs, "residents report" and "the municipality states" rather than "residents complain" and "the municipality claims"; attributes every opinion to its holder, "the councillor argues that" rather than presenting one side as fact; keeps your own view out of the evidence sections and puts it only in the conclusion, clearly marked as yours; and avoids loaded words, "criminals", "victims", "corrupt", unless quoting someone. Balanced does not mean spineless: the conclusion must take a position, but it earns the right to by having given every side its due first.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a report that is a persuasive essay with the opposing side mentioned in one dismissive sentence. Two: figures with no sources, or sources with no dates. Three: a conclusion that appears from nowhere, unconnected to the evidence presented. Four: naming an interviewee who admitted to an illegal connection, exposing them to harm.

The questions for this section are with you now: the report's sections, the rules of balanced tone, and what a conclusion must do.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a notebook of five interviews that all asked, what would be fair? — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Fair Looks Like

Fair does not mean the same for everyone. Equal: every home gets the same units. Equitable: people get what they need to live decently, allowing for their situation. That is why poor homes get Free Basic Electricity, about fifty units a month free, enough for lights, radio and a phone, and why the first units each month are cheapest and heavy users pay more per unit.

The power is limited in four ways. Power stations: when demand beats supply, load shedding switches areas off in turn so the whole grid does not fall over, and who gets protected, hospitals, water pumps, is itself a fairness question. Local network: a transformer carries a fixed load; connect a settlement and you upgrade it or everyone gets less. Money: grants for connections, subsidies and repairs all compete. Pollution: our power is mostly coal, so more power is more smoke and climate harm, which is unfair to the future.

Who has a claim? Homes that pay, homes that cannot, factories and mines with jobs, farms with pumps, hospitals and schools, the utility that must cover costs, and the environment. Fair sharing weighs all of these openly. Anyone who says there is no trade-off is hiding one.

Hold the fifty free units in mind and try the questions for this part: what fair looks like.

## Subtopic: Evidence From Every Side

Two kinds of evidence. Numbers: how many homes are connected, what a unit costs, how often the transformer failed, how many fires, what the plan says, what the law requires. Sources: Stats SA, the municipality, Eskom and NERSA, newspapers, the fire service. Always say where a number came from and when. A number with no source is just an opinion in disguise.

Voices: what people say, from interviews. Balance means choosing on purpose: one who pays and one who cannot; one with power and one without; an official and a resident; someone hurt by illegal connections, the shop owner whose stock spoiled, and someone who depends on one. Ask everyone the same questions so answers compare: what do you use it for, what does it cost, what happens when it fails, what would be fair? Write their words down accurately. Describe them without exposing them: "a grandmother on a pension in Extension 9", no name, especially if they admitted an illegal connection.

Then test it. Do numbers and voices agree? The councillor says next year; residents say they heard that three years ago; the gap is a finding. Is the number recent? Is the source biased, and did you say so? Did you look for evidence against your own first opinion? That last one is what makes it balanced.

Hold the same five questions for every person in mind and try the questions for this part: evidence from every side.

## Subtopic: Writing It Up

Structure. Title as a question. Introduction: the question, why it matters, how you gathered evidence. Background: facts and figures with sources. Views: each side, equal space, strongest form, quotes where they bite. Discussion: weigh the sides against criteria you name, need, cost, safety, fairness, sustainability, and admit where evidence is thin. Conclusion: your reasoned judgement, following from the discussion, with specific, possible recommendations: "upgrade the transformer and fit ready boards within eighteen months", not "government must fix it". Sources and interviews at the end.

Tone. Neutral verbs: "residents report", "the municipality states", not "complain" and "claims". Attribute every opinion: "the councillor argues". Keep your view out until the conclusion, and mark it as yours. No loaded words like criminals or corrupt unless you are quoting. Balanced is not spineless: take a position in the end, having earned it by giving every side its due.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Equitable is need-based, not equal; the resource is limited in generation, network, money and pollution; many parties have claims. Evidence is sourced figures and fairly chosen voices, tested against each other. The report runs title, intro, background, views, discussion, conclusion, sources, in neutral attributed language, ending in a reasoned, specific position.

Hold the conclusion that follows from the evidence in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: writing it up.
