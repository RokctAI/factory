# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers illegal connections, their ethical issues and their safety. The anchor is a bundle of a dozen cables tied to a municipal pole at the edge of a settlement near Johannesburg, running at head height across a dirt road, each one split and spliced with insulation tape, feeding shacks that have no meter, no earth and no circuit breaker.

## Subtopic: What an Illegal Connection Is and Why People Make Them

Define the thing first. An illegal connection, called izinyoka, the snakes, in many townships, is a connection to the electricity network that the utility has not installed, metered or approved. It takes several forms: a cable clamped onto the bare conductors of an overhead line or the terminals of a streetlight; a cable run from a legally connected house's meter box, with or without the occupant's agreement, to houses that have none; a tampered or bypassed prepaid meter so that the home's own consumption is not recorded; and, in some areas, a connection to a mini-substation or a kiosk whose door has been forced. The common feature is that electricity flows without being measured or paid for, through wiring that no one qualified has installed or inspected.

Why do people make them? The reasons are layered. First, absence: many informal settlements have no legal supply because the land is not proclaimed for housing, because the municipality has not reached it, or because a court case over ownership has frozen development, and in those conditions the only electricity available is what can be taken from a line that passes nearby. Second, cost: a legal connection to a house carries a connection fee and then a monthly cost per unit that a household on a grant or casual income may not afford for cooking and heating, and a free illegal supply makes a heater in winter possible. Third, time: electrification programmes work through waiting lists that can run for years, and a family lives in the present. Fourth, normalisation: when a whole street is connected illegally and nothing has happened, the connection seems ordinary, and a local "connector" who does the work for a fee becomes a small business. Fifth, in some places, politics: communities use connections and protests as leverage to force attention from authorities.

Put these together and the person connecting is usually not a criminal mastermind but a parent who wants light for homework and a stove for supper, in a place that has been waiting years for a promise to be kept. Hold that picture, because the next part will show that the same connection can kill a child, and both pictures are true.

The questions for this section are with you now: the forms an illegal connection takes, and the layered reasons people make them.

## Subtopic: The Dangers: Shock, Fire, Overload and Who Gets Hurt

The dangers follow from the physics and from the absence of protection. A legal supply has a meter, an earth connection, a main circuit breaker and earth-leakage protection; those devices exist to cut the current the instant a fault appears. An illegal connection has none of them, so every fault runs to completion. Shock and electrocution first: cables lie on the ground, hang at head height across roads, run through puddles and are spliced with tape that peels; a child who touches an exposed joint, a person who walks into a sagging cable in the dark, a worker who cuts through a buried one while digging, receives the full supply with nothing to interrupt it. Deaths of children in settlements from contact with illegal cables are reported every year, and the municipal workers who remove connections have been electrocuted too. Fire second: thin cable carrying a heater's current heats like an element, insulation melts, tape ignites, and in a shack of wood and plastic the fire spreads to neighbours in minutes; many settlement fires begin at an illegal joint. Overload third: a transformer sized for fifty legal houses is suddenly carrying three hundred shacks with heaters, so it overheats and fails, and when it fails the legal customers lose supply too, often for days, and the municipality's repair budget is spent on the same transformer again and again; in some townships transformers burn out several times a winter.

The harm spreads outward. Streetlights wired into go dark, and dark streets are more dangerous. The legal household whose meter is shared pays for a neighbour's heater or has its own supply disconnected for tampering. The utility loses billions of rand a year in unpaid electricity, which raises tariffs for everyone and starves maintenance. Workers sent to remove connections are threatened and sometimes attacked. And the people with the connection live under a cable that can kill them, with no recourse when it does, because the connection they depend on is the thing that cannot be reported.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the absence of protection makes every fault lethal, the three main dangers, and who beyond the user is harmed.

## Subtopic: The Ethics: Need, Theft, Fairness and Responsibility

Now the ethics, which must be argued, not asserted. An ethical question is one where values conflict and reasonable people weigh them differently. Here the values in tension are need, the claim that people have a right to the basic energy that a dignified life requires; property and law, the claim that taking something unpaid is theft regardless of need and that laws must apply to all; fairness, which cuts both ways, since it is unfair that some have waited years for a service others enjoy, and unfair that paying customers subsidise those who do not pay and lose supply when the shared transformer burns out; safety, the claim that an act which endangers children and neighbours cannot be justified by the actor's need alone; and responsibility, the question of who is obliged to fix the situation, the household, the connector who profits, the municipality that has not delivered, the utility, or the state.

A balanced argument states each claim fairly. In favour of the household: the Constitution promises access to basic services and progressive realisation of socio-economic rights; electricity is now essential for safety, schooling and health; the household did not choose to be unserviced; and condemning the act without offering an alternative is empty. Against: theft is theft and the losses fall on everyone including other poor households; the connection endangers people who did not consent, especially children; the connector profits from others' desperation; and tolerating it undermines the systems that could deliver legal supply. Between: most people hold that the household's need is real and the connection is still wrong because of the danger to others, that the greater fault lies with those who left the settlement unserved, and that the right response is both to remove dangerous connections and to deliver safe legal supply quickly, with free basic allocations and prepaid meters that fit small budgets. Eskom and municipalities have in places done exactly this, electrifying informal settlements with pole-mounted ready boards, which has reduced illegal connections where it was done.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling everyone with an illegal connection a thief and stopping there. Two: calling the connection justified by need and ignoring the dead child two shacks down. Three: arguing only one side when asked for a balanced view. Four: proposing "educate the community" as the whole solution, when the community knows the danger and lacks the alternative.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five values in tension, the arguments on each side, and the balanced position most people reach.

## Subtopic: Why the Wires Go Up

An illegal connection, izinyoka, is electricity taken without the utility installing, measuring or approving it. Forms: a cable clamped onto a bare overhead line or a streetlight; a cable run from a legal house's meter box to houses with none; a bypassed prepaid meter; a forced-open substation. Common thread: power flows unmeasured and unpaid through wiring no qualified person checked.

Why? Layers. No supply: the land is not proclaimed, or the municipality has not arrived, or a court case froze everything. Cost: connection fees and monthly units a grant cannot stretch to for a heater. Time: waiting lists that run for years. Normal: when the whole street is connected, it feels ordinary, and a local connector does it for a fee. Sometimes politics: connections and protests to force attention.

## Subtopic: What the Wires Do

A legal supply has a meter, an earth, a main breaker and earth-leakage protection, devices that cut the power the instant something goes wrong. An illegal connection has none, so every fault runs all the way.

Shock: cables on the ground, across roads at head height, through puddles, joined with peeling tape. A child touches a joint, someone walks into a sagging wire in the dark, a digger cuts a buried one; full supply, nothing to stop it. Children die this way every year; so do workers removing connections. Fire: thin cable carrying a heater heats like an element, tape catches, a shack burns and the next one a metre away catches too. Overload: a transformer built for fifty houses carries three hundred shacks with heaters, overheats, fails, and the legal customers go dark for days while the municipality pays to fix it again and again.

Harm spreads. Streetlights wired into go dark. The legal neighbour pays for others' heaters or gets cut off for tampering. The utility loses billions, so tariffs rise for everyone. Workers get attacked. And the family under the cable cannot report it when it hurts them.

## Subtopic: Right, Wrong and In Between

The ethics must be argued both ways. Five values clash: need, a dignified life needs basic energy; law and property, taking unpaid is theft whoever you are; fairness, both ways, unfair to wait years for what others have, unfair that payers carry non-payers and lose power when the transformer burns; safety, your need does not justify endangering a neighbour's child; responsibility, who must fix this, the household, the connector, the municipality, the utility, the state?

For the household: the Constitution promises basic services; electricity is essential now; they did not choose to be unserved; condemning without an alternative is empty. Against: theft hurts everyone including other poor homes; the danger falls on people who did not agree; the connector profits from desperation; tolerating it weakens the system that could deliver. In between, where most land: the need is real, the connection is still wrong because of the danger to others, the bigger fault lies with those who left the settlement unserved, and the answer is both: remove dangerous wires and deliver safe legal supply fast, with free basic units and prepaid meters. Where settlements got pole-mounted ready boards, illegal connections fell.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Izinyoka: unmeasured, unpaid, uninspected power, made because of absence, cost, waiting and normality. No meter, earth or breaker means shock, fire and burnt transformers, harming users, neighbours, payers and workers. Ethics: need against law, fairness both ways, safety, responsibility; argue both sides and land on a balanced response that fixes causes.
