# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers energy for heating, lighting and cooking in rural and informal settlements. The anchor is a two-room home in an informal settlement outside Cape Town in winter: a paraffin stove for cooking and warmth, a candle on a saucer for light, a car battery charged at a spaza shop for a phone and a radio, and a grid connection two streets away that this household cannot afford to join.

## Subtopic: Energy Needs in Rural and Informal Settlements: Heating, Lighting and Cooking

Begin with the three needs, because they are the same for every household in the country and only the means differ. Heating is the need to keep people warm in winter, and in South Africa it is concentrated in a few cold months on the Highveld, in the Cape and in the mountains, but in a home with thin walls and no ceiling, it is the need that drives the most dangerous choices. Lighting is the need to see after dark, to cook, to do homework, to walk safely to a toilet that may be a hundred metres away. Cooking is the daily need to turn raw food into meals and to boil water for drinking and washing, and it uses more energy than lighting by a wide margin. A fourth need has grown fast: charging phones and running a radio or television, small amounts of electricity that nevertheless connect a household to work, news and family.

Now the settings. A rural household in the former homelands, in Limpopo or the Eastern Cape or KwaZulu-Natal, often has a grid connection today, because the electrification programme since 1994 has connected more than seven million homes, but the household may use electricity only for light and a phone because the monthly cost of cooking and heating on it is beyond its income, so it continues to cook on wood collected from the veld and to heat with the same fire. An informal settlement household near a city may have no legal connection at all, because the land is not formally proclaimed and the utility will not install meters on it, or because the settlement grew faster than the network; here paraffin, candles and illegal connections fill the gap. In both settings the household's energy decisions are made under pressure of money, distance and danger.

The scale of the matter: Statistics South Africa's household surveys find that roughly one in eight households still uses wood as its main cooking fuel, that paraffin remains common in the Western Cape and Gauteng informal settlements, and that candle and paraffin fires are a leading cause of shack fires, which destroy thousands of homes a year. A paraffin stove knocked over in a shack of wood and zinc, with the next shack a metre away, becomes a fire that spreads through a block in minutes. The energy question is therefore a safety question and a justice question, not only a technical one.

## Subtopic: The Energy Options: Wood, Paraffin, Gas, Candles, Solar and Grid Electricity

Now the options, each with its character. Firewood, collected or bought, is the oldest; it is free where it can be gathered, it cooks and heats at once, and it is familiar; but gathering takes hours of mostly women's and children's time, it strips the veld of trees, it produces smoke that fills the room and damages lungs, and an open fire indoors is a burn and fire risk. Paraffin, sold by the litre at spaza shops, burns in a wick or pressure stove and in lamps; it is cheap per meal, portable and quick, but it is poisonous if drunk, which happens to children when it is stored in cold-drink bottles, its fumes cause coughs and headaches, its stoves tip over and flare, and poor-quality stoves have caused many fires; a national standard for paraffin stoves exists because of this. Candles give light for a few rand each but give almost no light to work by, and a candle left burning on a wooden box near a curtain is the single most common start of a shack fire.

Liquefied petroleum gas, LPG, in a cylinder with a two-plate stove, cooks fast and clean, with no smoke and good heat control; the cylinder costs several hundred rand to buy and refill, which is a large lump sum for a household living week to week, and a leak in a closed room is an explosion risk, so the cylinder belongs outside or by an open door. Solar: a small solar lantern or a solar home system with a panel, a battery and LED lights gives clean, safe light and phone charging for years after a once-off cost of a few hundred to a few thousand rand, and government and charities have distributed many; but a panel cannot cook or heat a room affordably, because cooking needs far more energy than lighting. Grid electricity, where it is legal and affordable, does everything: a two-plate stove, a kettle, lights, a heater, a television; Free Basic Electricity gives poor households a small free monthly allocation, enough for lights and a radio, not for cooking every day; prepaid meters let households buy as they can; and the cost of a hot bath or a heated room on a winter evening is still more than many can pay.

Notice the pattern: each household uses several sources at once, a practice called fuel stacking, choosing the cheapest safe-enough option for each task. Electricity for light and phone, paraffin or gas for cooking, wood or paraffin for heat, candles when the prepaid credit runs out. No single source wins everything.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Options: Cost, Safety, Health and Convenience

The comparison must be fair, so we judge every option on the same criteria. Cost, in two parts: the once-off cost to get started, high for gas and solar and a grid connection, zero for candles and wood, and the running cost per week, lowest for gathered wood and solar, moderate for paraffin and electricity, highest for cooking on electricity in a heater-hungry winter. Safety: fire risk, highest for candles, paraffin and open fires, lower for gas if the cylinder is outside, lowest for solar and a properly wired grid connection; poisoning risk, specific to paraffin; shock risk, specific to badly wired electricity. Health: indoor smoke from wood and paraffin causes respiratory disease, especially in children and the elderly, while gas, solar and electricity are clean at the point of use; electricity's pollution happens at the coal power station, far away but real. Convenience: how long it takes, how much carrying, how easy to control; grid and gas are most convenient, wood the least. Environment: wood use strips vegetation; paraffin and gas are fossil fuels; grid electricity in South Africa is mostly coal-generated; solar is clean in use.

A matrix, with options across the top and criteria down the side, scored or described in each cell, is the tool. Done honestly it shows that no option is best on every row, that the dangerous options are often the cheapest to start, and that the safest options need lump sums that poor households do not have. This is why safety campaigns alone fail: telling a family not to use a candle does not light their room. Real solutions change the economics, through cheaper solar lanterns, subsidised gas cylinders, more Free Basic Electricity, legal connections in settlements, or safer stoves that meet the national standard.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: judging a household's choices as foolish, when the choice is rational under its constraints. Two: comparing options on one criterion only, usually cost or usually safety. Three: treating "electricity" as free of pollution, forgetting the coal station. Four: assuming a rural household has no grid connection; most do, and the problem is affordability, not wires.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five criteria, the comparison matrix, and why safety campaigns fail without economic change.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a two-room home, a paraffin stove and a candle on a saucer — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What People Need Energy For

Every home needs three things from energy: warmth in winter, light after dark, and heat to cook and boil water. Add a fourth now: charging a phone. The needs are the same everywhere; the ways of meeting them differ.

Two settings. A rural village home, in Limpopo or the Eastern Cape: often it has electricity now, since more than seven million homes were connected after 1994, but it uses it only for lights and the phone, because cooking and heating on electricity costs too much; so it still cooks on wood. An informal settlement home near a city: often no legal connection, because the land is not official or the settlement grew too fast; so paraffin, candles and illegal wires fill the gap.

Why it matters: about one in eight homes still cooks mainly on wood; paraffin and candle fires burn thousands of shacks a year, and a fire jumps from shack to shack in minutes. Energy here is about safety and fairness, not just technology.

## Subtopic: Six Ways to Get It

Six options. Wood: free if gathered, cooks and heats at once; but hours of collecting, smoke in the lungs, trees stripped, burns and fires. Paraffin: cheap per meal, quick, portable; but poisonous when a child drinks it from a cold-drink bottle, fumes, stoves that tip and flare; there is a national safety standard for stoves because of this. Candles: a few rand, but barely enough light for homework, and the top cause of shack fires.

Gas in a cylinder: fast, clean, good control; but hundreds of rand up front, and a leak indoors can explode, so the cylinder stays outside. Solar lantern or small solar system: clean, safe light and phone charging for years after one payment; but it cannot cook or heat a room, because cooking needs far more energy than light. Grid electricity: does everything; Free Basic Electricity gives a small free amount for lights and radio; prepaid lets you buy as you can; but a heater and a stove in winter cost more than many can pay.

So homes stack fuels: electricity for light and phone, paraffin or gas to cook, wood or paraffin to warm, candles when the prepaid runs out. Nothing wins everything.

## Subtopic: Weighing Them Up

Compare fairly, same questions for every option. Cost to start: high for gas, solar and a connection; nothing for candles and wood. Cost to run each week: lowest for gathered wood and solar, highest for cooking and heating on electricity. Safety: fire worst for candles, paraffin and open fires; poisoning only paraffin; shock only bad wiring. Health: wood and paraffin smoke harm lungs; gas, solar and electricity are clean in the room, though electricity pollutes at the coal station. Convenience: grid and gas easiest, wood hardest. Environment: wood strips trees; paraffin, gas and coal electricity are fossil fuels; solar is clean.

Put it in a table, options across, criteria down. You will see the cheap ones are the dangerous ones and the safe ones need money up front that poor homes do not have. That is why telling people "do not use candles" fails: it does not light the room. Real fixes change the money: cheaper solar lanterns, subsidised gas, more free electricity, legal connections, safer stoves.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Three needs, heat, light, cooking, plus phones. Rural homes often have the grid but cannot afford to cook on it; settlement homes often have no legal connection. Six options, each with strengths and dangers; homes stack several. Judge on cost, safety, health, convenience and environment together, and remember the choice is rational under its limits.

Hold the table with no perfect column in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: weighing them up.
