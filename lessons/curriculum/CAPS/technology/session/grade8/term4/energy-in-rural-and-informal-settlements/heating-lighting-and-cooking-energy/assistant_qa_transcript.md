### subtopic_1 — Energy Needs in Rural and Informal Settlements: Heating, Lighting and Cooking
**Assistant:** If solar is clean and safe and cheap to run, why does the government not just give every household a solar system instead of connecting them to the grid?

**Tutor:** Because a solar system that can do what the grid does would cost far more than the connection, and a system that is affordable cannot cook. Lighting a room with LEDs and charging a phone needs a few watts for a few hours, which a small panel and battery provide for a few hundred rand. Cooking a pot of pap needs a kilowatt or two for half an hour, and heating a room on a winter night needs that for hours; to supply it from solar, the household would need a panel many square metres in size and a battery bank costing tens of thousands of rand, and it would still fail on cloudy days and in the evening when cooking happens. The grid, by contrast, delivers large amounts of energy on demand through a cable that costs a few thousand rand to run to a house already near the network. So the sensible policy, and roughly what has happened, is to use solar home systems where houses are too far from the grid to connect affordably and to connect everyone else, while attacking the real barrier to using the grid for cooking and heating, which is the monthly cost. Solar lanterns and small systems are a bridge and a complement, not a substitute for the grid in dense settlements.

### subtopic_2 — The Energy Options: Wood, Paraffin, Gas, Candles, Solar and Grid Electricity
**Assistant:** Why do people keep buying paraffin when everyone knows it is dangerous?

**Tutor:** Because knowing something is dangerous does not pay for the alternative, and because paraffin is dangerous in ways that are rare per use even if common across millions of homes. On any given evening the paraffin stove almost always cooks the meal and goes out; the fire happens to one shack in thousands, and a household rationally weighs that small chance against the certainty of a hot meal tonight. Paraffin is also sold in small quantities, a litre or half a litre at the corner shop, which fits a household that earns and spends in small daily amounts; gas requires a cylinder deposit and refill of several hundred rand at once, and electricity for cooking requires both a legal connection and the credit to run a stove, neither of which may exist. Add that paraffin stoves are cheap, portable and familiar, and the choice is not ignorance but constraint. This is why the most effective interventions have targeted the product rather than the person: a national standard that bans unsafe stove designs, child-proof containers, and programmes that bring safer options down to the small daily spending that paraffin already fits. Blaming the user is easy and changes nothing.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing the Options: Cost, Safety, Health and Convenience
**Assistant:** Does cooking on wood really harm health if people have done it for thousands of years?

**Tutor:** Yes, and the long history is not evidence of safety but of the absence of alternatives. Burning wood indoors produces fine particles, carbon monoxide and other gases, and when the fire is in a small room with little ventilation, as it is in a cold winter when doors are shut, the people around it breathe concentrations far above what is safe. The World Health Organization attributes millions of early deaths a year worldwide to household air pollution from solid fuels, mostly through pneumonia in children, chronic lung disease and heart disease in adults, and low birth weight in babies whose mothers cook over smoke. Clinics in rural South Africa see the pattern in chest infections every winter. For most of human history people had no choice and died younger partly for it; the fact that something is traditional tells us it was available, not that it was harmless. What has changed is that cleaner options now exist, so the question becomes how to make them accessible, and in the meantime how to reduce harm with ventilation, improved stoves that burn more completely with a chimney, and cooking outside when the weather allows.
