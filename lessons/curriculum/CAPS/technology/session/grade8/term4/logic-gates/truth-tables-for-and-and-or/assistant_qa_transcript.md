### subtopic_1 — What a Truth Table Is and How to Set One Up
**Assistant:** Why do we need a table at all if I can just say 'both switches on lights the lamp'? That is shorter.

**Tutor:** For a two-input AND the sentence is enough, and the table earns its place as soon as the circuit gets any bigger or the stakes get any higher. Try describing in words a three-input circuit that lights when A and B are on, or when C is on alone, but not when all three are on; the sentence becomes a paragraph, two people will read it differently, and nobody can be sure every case is covered. The eight-row table states it without ambiguity, and anyone can check any case in a second. That is the first reason: completeness and precision. The second is testing. A sentence cannot be ticked off; a table can, row by row, and a mismatch on row six tells you exactly which combination of inputs your circuit gets wrong, which is where the fault is. The third is communication between people who never meet. A designer in one city writes the table, a builder in another builds to it, an inspector tests against it, and all three are working from the same object. Engineers describe control systems, from microwave interlocks to aircraft landing gear, with tables and the diagrams built from them precisely because sentences are where misunderstandings hide. Learning to write the four-row table for AND is learning the habit on a case small enough to see all of; the habit is what matters.

### subtopic_2 — The AND Truth Table, Tested on the Bench
**Assistant:** What happens to the table if I wire the two switches so one is in series and one is in parallel with the lamp?

**Tutor:** You get a table that is neither AND nor OR, and working it out is a good test of understanding. Call the series switch A, in the main line from the cell, and the parallel switch B, connected across the lamp. When A is open, no current can flow anywhere, so the lamp is dark whatever B does: rows one and two are 0. When A is closed and B is open, current flows through the lamp: row three is 1. When A is closed and B is closed, B is a short circuit across the lamp, so almost all the current takes the easy path through B and the lamp goes dark, while the cell is being shorted and getting hot: row four is 0. The output column reads 0, 0, 1, 0, which says the lamp is on only when A is on and B is off, a gate that Grade 9 will call A AND NOT B. The lesson in it is twofold. First, any arrangement of switches has a truth table, and the table is found by thinking through each row exactly as you would on the bench. Second, the table exposed a dangerous circuit, the shorted cell in row four, before you built it, which is one of the reasons designers write the table first. If you do build this one to check, close both switches for only a moment.

### subtopic_3 — The OR Truth Table, Comparing the Two, and Reading a Table Back Into a Circuit
**Assistant:** If AND and OR give the same output for both-off and both-on, why does anyone care which gate a machine uses? Most of the time the inputs are both off or both on anyway.

**Tutor:** Because the cases where they differ are exactly the cases where safety and function live, even if they are rare. Consider the microwave. Most of the time the door is closed and start is not pressed, both inputs mixed, or the door is open and start is not pressed, both off; the cooking case has both on. The row that matters for safety is door open with start pressed, a mixed row, and there AND says do not heat while OR says heat with the door open and cook the person standing in front of it. Consider the burglar alarm. Most nights every door and window is shut, all inputs off, and both gates agree the alarm is silent. The night that matters is the one when a single window opens, a mixed row, and there OR says sound the alarm while AND stays silent until the burglar obligingly opens a second window. In both cases the gates agree on the ordinary rows and disagree on the critical ones, which is the general pattern: a control system earns its keep in the unusual case, not the usual one. That is also why a tester must deliberately create the mixed rows rather than only switching everything on and off together, and why the examiner's question about the panic button will ask what happens when one button is pressed and the others are not.
