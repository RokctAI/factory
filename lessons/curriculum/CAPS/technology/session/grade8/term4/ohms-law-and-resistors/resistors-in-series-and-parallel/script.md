# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing and connecting circuits with resistors in series and in parallel. The anchor is a bench in Kimberley with two cells, a lamp, an ammeter and a handful of 100 ohm resistors, and two diagrams on a card: three resistors in a line, and the same three side by side, with the challenge to predict which lets the lamp glow brighter before touching a wire.

## Subtopic: Resistors: What They Are, Their Values and Their Symbol

A resistor is a component made to have a definite resistance. The common type is a small cylinder of carbon film or metal film on a ceramic rod, with a wire leg at each end and coloured bands around its body that state its value; power resistors for heavy currents are larger and sometimes wire-wound in a ceramic case. A resistor has no polarity, so it can be connected either way round. Its job in a circuit is to limit current, to divide voltage, or to convert electrical energy to heat deliberately, as in a heater element, which is a resistor built to run hot.

The colour code: the first two bands give digits, the third gives the number of zeros, and a fourth, gold or silver, gives the tolerance. Black is zero, brown one, red two, orange three, yellow four, green five, blue six, violet seven, grey eight, white nine. So brown, black, brown is one, zero, one zero: 100 ohms; red, red, red is two, two, two zeros: 2200 ohms, written 2.2 kilohms; orange, orange, brown is three, three, one zero: 330 ohms, the usual LED resistor; brown, black, red is 1000 ohms, 1 kilohm. Gold means the real value is within five percent of the stated one. A multimeter on its ohms range, touched across the two legs with the resistor out of the circuit, confirms the value.

The symbol in the standard most South African textbooks use is a plain rectangle with a lead from each end; the older zigzag line means the same thing and you should recognise both. A variable resistor, or rheostat, is a rectangle with an arrow through it; a light-dependent resistor is a rectangle with two arrows pointing in, and a thermistor a rectangle with a bent line through it, but the plain fixed resistor is what this lesson draws. Beside the symbol goes its value, such as 100 Ω or 2.2 kΩ, so that a diagram can be built.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a resistor is and does, how to read the colour code, and the resistor symbols.

## Subtopic: Resistors in Series: Resistances Add, Current Falls

Connect resistors in series, one after another in a single line, and the current must pass through all of them in turn, so each adds its squeeze to the rest. The total resistance of resistors in series is the sum of their resistances: two 100 ohm resistors in series make 200 ohms; three make 300. It does not matter in what order they sit, and the total is always more than the largest single resistor. The current, by Ohm's law, falls as the total rises: with 3 volts across one 100 ohm resistor the current is 30 milliamps; across two in series it is 15; across three it is 10. The current is the same through every resistor in the chain, since there is one path, and the cell's voltage is shared between them in proportion to their values, so three equal resistors in series each have a third of the supply voltage across them, a voltage divider.

On the bench: two cells, the lamp, the ammeter and one 100 ohm resistor in a loop; note the current and brightness; add a second resistor in series and both drop; add a third and they drop further. Series resistors are how you tame a current, which is why the LED's resistor sits in series with it, and how you share a voltage, which is how a volume control or a sensor circuit works.

The drawing: a rectangle for the cells, a rectangle for the lamp in a circle, the ammeter as a circle with an A, and the resistors as rectangles end to end along one side of the loop, each labelled 100 Ω, the switch drawn open, lines straight and corners square. Trace the loop with a finger: supply, switch, resistor, resistor, resistor, lamp, ammeter, back to supply, one path, no branches, no dots.

The questions for this section are with you now: how series resistances add, what happens to current and voltage, and how the series circuit is drawn.

## Subtopic: Resistors in Parallel: More Paths, Lower Resistance, and Drawing Both

Connect resistors in parallel, side by side with both ends joined, and the current reaches a junction and divides, part through each resistor, rejoining at the far junction. More paths means an easier passage, so the total resistance of resistors in parallel is less than the smallest single one: two equal 100 ohm resistors in parallel behave like 50 ohms, three like about 33, because each path carries a share and the combined opening is wider. The rule for equal resistors is the value divided by how many there are; for unequal ones Grade 9 will give a formula, and Grade 8 states the principle: adding a parallel path always lowers the total resistance and raises the total current drawn from the supply. Every parallel resistor has the full supply voltage across it, since each is connected directly between the two supply lines, and the current through each depends on its own value, with the branch currents adding up to the total.

On the bench: two cells, the lamp, the ammeter and one 100 ohm resistor; add a second resistor in parallel with the first, both ends joined, and the ammeter rises and the lamp brightens; add a third in parallel and they rise again, the opposite of the series result. Parallel is how a house is wired: every appliance has the full 230 volts, each draws its own current, and the more you switch on the more the total current, until the breaker says enough.

The drawing: the resistors are rectangles stacked between two vertical lines, a dot at each junction where the lines meet, the ammeter in the main line before the junction to read the total, the lamp and switch in the main line, the whole loop closed with straight lines and square corners. Compare the two drawings side by side: the series one has one path and no dots; the parallel one has branches and a dot wherever three lines meet. Build each from its drawing, predict before closing the switch which gives the brighter lamp, and write the measured currents beside the drawings.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding resistances in parallel as if they were in series, so that two 100 ohm resistors "make 200" in parallel. Two: a parallel drawing with the resistors joined at one end only, which is not parallel and not series but a dead end. Three: a dot drawn where lines merely cross on the parallel diagram. Four: reading the colour bands from the wrong end, so 100 ohms becomes 10 ohms.

The questions for this section are with you now: how parallel paths lower resistance and raise current, the voltage and current in each branch, and how the parallel circuit is drawn and compared.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a handful of 100 ohm resistors, two diagrams on a card and a bet on which lamp glows brighter — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Striped Little Tubes

A resistor is a part built to have a set resistance: a little cylinder of carbon or metal film on a ceramic rod, a wire leg each end, coloured stripes for its value; big ones for heavy current are wire-wound in ceramic. No plus or minus; either way round. Jobs: limit current, share out voltage, or make heat on purpose, like a heater element, which is a resistor built to run hot.

Colour code: first two stripes are digits, third is the number of zeros, fourth gold or silver is tolerance. Black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9. Brown black brown: 1, 0, one zero: 100 ohms. Red red red: 2, 2, two zeros: 2200, or 2.2 kilohms. Orange orange brown: 330 ohms, the LED's friend. Brown black red: 1000, 1 kilohm. Gold means within five percent. A multimeter on ohms across the legs, resistor out of the circuit, checks it.

Symbol: a plain rectangle with a lead each end; the old zigzag means the same, know both. Variable resistor: rectangle with an arrow through. Light-dependent: rectangle with two arrows in. Thermistor: rectangle with a bent line. Today we draw the plain one, and write its value next to it, 100 Ω, so someone can build it.

Hold brown black brown in mind and try the questions for this part: striped little tubes.

## Subtopic: In a Line

Series: resistors one after another in a line, current through each in turn, each adding its squeeze. Total resistance adds: two 100 ohm make 200, three make 300, any order, always more than the biggest one. Current falls as total rises: 3 volts across 100 ohms, 30 milliamps; across 200, 15; across 300, 10. Same current through every resistor, one path; the volts are shared between them by size, so three equal resistors each get a third of the supply: a voltage divider.

Bench: two cells, lamp, ammeter, one 100 ohm resistor in a loop; read it. Add a second resistor in line: current and brightness drop. Add a third: drop again. Series resistors tame current, which is why the LED's resistor sits in line with it, and share voltage, which is how a volume knob or a sensor circuit works.

Drawing: rectangle for the cells, lamp in a circle, ammeter as a circle with A, resistors as rectangles end to end along one side, each marked 100 Ω, switch open, straight lines, square corners. Finger round: supply, switch, resistor, resistor, resistor, lamp, ammeter, back. One path, no branches, no dots.

Hold the three rectangles in a row in mind and try the questions for this part: in a line.

## Subtopic: Side by Side, and the Drawings

Parallel: resistors side by side, both ends joined; current hits a junction, splits, some through each, joins again. More paths, easier passage, so total resistance is less than the smallest one: two equal 100 ohm in parallel act like 50; three like about 33; for equal ones, divide by how many. Unequal ones get a formula in Grade 9; Grade 8 says: every extra parallel path lowers the total resistance and raises the total current. Each parallel resistor has the full supply volts across it, because each sits straight between the two supply lines; each draws its own current by its value; the branch currents add to the total.

Bench: two cells, lamp, ammeter, one 100 ohm resistor; add a second in parallel, both ends joined; the ammeter climbs and the lamp brightens. Add a third: up again. The opposite of series. A house is parallel: every appliance gets 230 volts, each draws its own current, switch on more and the total climbs until the breaker says stop.

Drawing: resistors stacked between two vertical lines, a dot at every junction, ammeter in the main line before the split to read the total, lamp and switch in the main line, loop closed, straight and square. Side by side: series has one path and no dots; parallel has branches and a dot wherever three lines meet. Build both, bet on the brighter lamp before closing the switch, write the currents by the drawings.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Resistor: striped tube, no polarity, rectangle symbol, value from the colour code. Series: resistances add, current falls, volts shared, one path, no dots. Parallel: more paths, resistance below the smallest, current rises, full volts on each, dots at junctions. Build from the drawing and check your prediction.

Hold the dot where three lines meet in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: side by side, and the drawings.
