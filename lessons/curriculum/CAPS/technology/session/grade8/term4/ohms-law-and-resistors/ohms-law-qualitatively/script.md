# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers Ohm's law stated qualitatively: more cells in series increase the current if the resistance is unchanged. The anchor is a Grade 8 investigation table in Mthatha: a lamp in a holder, a tray of 1.5 volt cells, three identical resistors, an ammeter, and a results table with two columns, number of cells and brightness, waiting to be filled.

## Subtopic: Voltage, Current and Resistance: Three Quantities in One Circuit

Three quantities describe what is happening in a circuit, and each has a name, a unit and a meaning. Voltage, measured in volts, is the push that the cell or supply provides, the energy given to each unit of charge; a 1.5 volt cell pushes less hard than a 9 volt battery. Current, measured in amperes or amps, is the flow of charge, how many coulombs pass a point in the wire each second; a torch bulb takes around a quarter of an amp, a kettle around ten. Resistance, measured in ohms, is the opposition a component offers to the flow; a thick copper wire has almost none, a lamp filament a few ohms when hot, a resistor whatever its stripes say, and an open switch effectively infinite.

The water picture helps as long as it is held loosely. Voltage is like the height of a water tank or the pressure of a pump: the push. Current is like the litres per second flowing through the pipe. Resistance is like the narrowness of the pipe or a kink in the hose: the squeeze. A taller tank through the same pipe gives more flow; the same tank through a narrower pipe gives less. In a circuit, more voltage across the same resistance gives more current; the same voltage across more resistance gives less current. The water picture fails in one way, which is that water can leak out while charge cannot: the current that leaves the cell's negative terminal is exactly the current that returns to its positive, all the way round, which is why an ammeter reads the same anywhere in a single loop.

Measuring: a voltmeter is connected across a component, in parallel with it, to read the push between its two ends; an ammeter is connected into the loop, in series, so that the current flows through it, and must never be connected across a cell, because its resistance is tiny and it would short the cell. In this lesson we often use the lamp itself as a rough current meter, since a filament's brightness rises with the current through it, which is why the results table has a column for brightness.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three quantities with their units and meanings, the water picture and where it fails, and how voltmeters and ammeters are connected.

## Subtopic: More Cells in Series, More Current: The Investigation

Now the investigation itself, laid out as a fair test. The question: what happens to the current when more cells are added in series and the resistance is kept the same? The variable we change, the independent variable, is the number of cells in series, one, two, three. The variable we measure, the dependent variable, is the current, read on the ammeter in amps and judged from the brightness of the lamp. The variables we keep the same, the controls, are the lamp, the wires, the connections and the temperature of the room, so that the only thing changing is the push. We use a lamp rated for the highest voltage we will reach, say a 6 volt lamp with up to three 1.5 volt cells, so that it survives.

Procedure: build the circuit with one cell, the lamp, the ammeter and a switch, all in series; close the switch, read the ammeter, note the brightness as dim, normal or bright, and open the switch so the cell does not run down; add a second cell in series, positive to negative, and repeat; add a third and repeat. A typical set of results: one cell, 1.5 volts, 0.10 amps, dim orange glow; two cells, 3 volts, 0.18 amps, yellow and clearly brighter; three cells, 4.5 volts, 0.25 amps, bright white. The pattern is unmistakable: as the voltage rises, with the same lamp, the current rises and the lamp brightens. It does not quite double when the voltage doubles, because a filament's resistance rises as it heats, which is an honest finding to record rather than hide.

State the conclusion in words, which is Ohm's law qualitatively: for a conductor whose resistance stays the same, the current through it increases as the voltage across it increases, in proportion. Then the fair-test questions an examiner asks: what did you change, what did you measure, what did you keep constant, how did you make the test safe, and what would you do to make the result more reliable, which is to repeat each reading and average, to use cells of the same age, and to read the ammeter at eye level.

The questions for this section are with you now: the independent, dependent and controlled variables, the procedure and typical results, and the conclusion stated in words.

## Subtopic: Changing the Resistance and Stating Ohm's Law in Words

The second half of the law comes from the second investigation: keep the voltage the same and change the resistance. Use two cells throughout, and in place of the lamp alone, add identical resistors in series one at a time: lamp alone; lamp plus one resistor; lamp plus two. Each added resistor increases the total resistance of the loop, and the ammeter shows the current falling with each one, 0.18 amps, then perhaps 0.09, then 0.06, while the lamp fades from yellow to dull to barely glowing. With the same push and more squeeze, less flows. Stated in words: for a fixed voltage, the current decreases as the resistance increases.

The two halves together are Ohm's law. For a given resistance, current is proportional to voltage; for a given voltage, current is inversely proportional to resistance. In symbols, which Grade 9 will use, voltage equals current times resistance, V equals I times R, and from it current equals voltage over resistance, which is exactly what the two investigations show. The practical uses are everywhere: a resistor in series with an LED limits the current to the few milliamps the LED can take from a supply that would otherwise destroy it; a dimmer increases resistance to reduce the current through a lamp; a longer, thinner wire has more resistance, which is why extension leads run warm and the grid uses thick conductors and high voltage; a short circuit is a path of almost no resistance, so the current rises enormously and heats the wire, which is what a fuse or breaker is there to interrupt.

Ohm's law has limits worth naming. It holds for metal conductors at steady temperature, called ohmic conductors; a lamp filament heats as current rises and its resistance climbs, so it bends away from a straight line; a diode or LED hardly conducts until a threshold voltage and then conducts freely, so it does not follow the law at all. Grade 8 states the law in words for the ordinary case and notes the exceptions honestly.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: connecting the ammeter across the cell and shorting it. Two: changing two things at once, more cells and a different lamp. Three: concluding "more volts, more current" from one reading rather than a series. Four: forcing the data to show exact doubling and hiding the filament's rising resistance.

The questions for this section are with you now: the second investigation and its conclusion, the law stated both ways with its uses, and its limits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a lamp, a tray of cells, three resistors and an empty results table — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Push, Flow and Squeeze

Three things describe a circuit. Voltage, in volts: the push from the cell, how much energy each bit of charge gets; a 9 volt battery pushes harder than a 1.5 volt cell. Current, in amps: the flow, how much charge passes a point each second; a torch bulb takes about a quarter of an amp, a kettle about ten. Resistance, in ohms: the squeeze, how much a part fights the flow; thick copper wire almost none, a hot lamp filament a few, a resistor whatever its stripes say, an open switch infinite.

Water picture, held loosely. Voltage is the height of the tank, the pressure. Current is the litres a second through the pipe. Resistance is the narrowness of the pipe or a kink in the hose. Taller tank, same pipe, more flow: more volts, same resistance, more current. Same tank, narrower pipe, less flow: same volts, more resistance, less current. Where water fails: water can leak out, charge cannot; the current leaving the cell's minus is exactly what comes back to its plus, all the way round, so an ammeter reads the same anywhere in one loop.

Measuring: voltmeter across a part, in parallel, reads the push between its ends. Ammeter in the loop, in series, so the current flows through it; never across a cell, it would short it. We also use the lamp itself as a rough meter: brighter means more current, hence the brightness column.

Hold the tank, the pipe and the kink in mind and try the questions for this part: push, flow and squeeze.

## Subtopic: Add a Cell, Watch the Lamp

The investigation, as a fair test. Question: what happens to current when we add cells in series and keep the resistance the same? What we change: the number of cells, one, two, three. What we measure: the current, on the ammeter and from the lamp's brightness. What we keep the same: the lamp, the wires, the joints, the room. Use a lamp rated for the top voltage, a 6 volt lamp for three cells, so it survives.

Steps: one cell, lamp, ammeter, switch, all in a loop. Close, read, note dim or normal or bright, open so the cell lasts. Add a second cell plus to minus, repeat. Add a third, repeat. Typical results: one cell, 1.5 volts, 0.10 amps, dim orange; two cells, 3 volts, 0.18 amps, yellow and clearly brighter; three cells, 4.5 volts, 0.25 amps, bright white. More volts, same lamp, more current, brighter. Not quite doubling when the volts double, because a filament's resistance rises as it heats; write that down honestly, do not hide it.

Conclusion in words, which is Ohm's law qualitatively: if the resistance stays the same, the current rises as the voltage rises, in step. Then the examiner's questions: what did you change, measure, keep the same; how was it safe; how would you make it more reliable, repeat and average, same-age cells, read the meter at eye level.

Hold the dim, yellow, white lamp in mind and try the questions for this part: add a cell, watch the lamp.

## Subtopic: Add a Resistor, Watch Again

Second investigation: keep the volts the same, change the resistance. Two cells all the way. Lamp alone; lamp plus one resistor in series; lamp plus two. Each resistor adds squeeze, and the ammeter falls, 0.18, then about 0.09, then 0.06, while the lamp fades from yellow to dull to barely there. Same push, more squeeze, less flow. In words: for a fixed voltage, the current falls as the resistance rises.

Both halves together are Ohm's law: same resistance, current goes with voltage; same voltage, current goes against resistance. Grade 9 will write V equals I times R, so I equals V over R, which is exactly what the two tables show. Uses: a resistor in series with an LED keeps the current down to the few milliamps it can take; a dimmer adds resistance to lower a lamp's current; a long thin wire has more resistance, so extension leads run warm and the grid uses thick wire and high volts; a short circuit is almost no resistance, so current shoots up and heats the wire, which is why fuses and breakers exist.

Limits: the law holds for metal wires at steady temperature, ohmic conductors. A filament heats and its resistance climbs, so it bends off the straight line. A diode or LED barely conducts until a threshold and then floods, so it does not obey at all. Grade 8 states the law in words for the ordinary case and names the exceptions.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Volts push, amps flow, ohms squeeze. Fair test one: more cells in series, same lamp, more current, brighter. Fair test two: same cells, more resistors, less current, dimmer. Ohm's law in words both ways; ammeter in series, voltmeter across; filaments and LEDs are the honest exceptions.

Hold the lamp fading as resistors are added in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: add a resistor, watch again.
