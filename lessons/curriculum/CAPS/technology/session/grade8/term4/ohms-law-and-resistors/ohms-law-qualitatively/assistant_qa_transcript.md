### subtopic_1 — Voltage, Current and Resistance: Three Quantities in One Circuit
**Assistant:** If the current is the same everywhere in a loop, how does the lamp 'use up' anything? Does the current not get smaller after the lamp?

**Tutor:** The current is the same before and after the lamp, and what the lamp uses up is not current but energy. Think of the charge as a conveyor belt of buckets going round the loop. The cell loads each bucket with energy, the voltage being how much energy each bucket receives, and the lamp empties the buckets, turning that energy into light and heat; the buckets themselves keep moving at the same rate, so the number passing any point per second, the current, is identical everywhere. A voltmeter shows this: across the cell it reads the energy given per bucket, and across the lamp it reads the same amount taken per bucket, so the voltage across the lamp equals the cell's voltage in a one-component loop. Where there are two lamps in series, the buckets give up part of their energy in each lamp, so the voltages across the two lamps add up to the cell's voltage, while the current is still the same through both. This is why two series lamps are dimmer: each receives only a share of the push, so less current flows through the whole loop, and each lamp gets less energy per second. The water picture breaks down here because people imagine the water being consumed; the better picture is a chain of buckets, or a bicycle chain that moves at one speed everywhere while delivering force from the pedals to the wheel.

### subtopic_2 — More Cells in Series, More Current: The Investigation
**Assistant:** Why did the current not double when we doubled the cells? Does that mean Ohm's law is wrong?

**Tutor:** It means the resistance did not stay the same, so the condition of the law was not met, and the law itself is fine. Ohm's law says current is proportional to voltage for a conductor whose resistance is constant. A lamp filament is made of tungsten, a metal whose resistance rises sharply with temperature, and the whole point of a filament is to get hot enough to glow. With one cell the filament is dull orange and perhaps 15 ohms; with two cells it is yellow and perhaps 17; with three it is white-hot and perhaps 18. So as you add cells you are raising the voltage and the resistance at once, and the current rises less than proportionally. If you did the same experiment with a fixed carbon resistor instead of a lamp, keeping it cool, the current would track the voltage almost exactly, and that is the honest demonstration of the law. The examiner is pleased, not troubled, when a learner writes "the current increased with voltage but less than doubled, because the filament's resistance rose as it heated"; it shows that you understand both the law and its condition. What the examiner does not want is a table in which the numbers have been quietly adjusted to show perfect doubling, because that is not an investigation but a story.

### subtopic_3 — Changing the Resistance and Stating Ohm's Law in Words
**Assistant:** If a short circuit has almost no resistance, why does it not just let a huge current flow harmlessly? Why does anything get hot?

**Tutor:** Because a huge current is exactly what gets things hot, and the heat appears wherever there is any resistance at all, which in a short circuit means the wires and the cell itself. Ohm's law says that with the cell's voltage pushing across almost no resistance, the current becomes very large, limited only by the small resistance of the wire and the cell's own internal resistance. Heating in a wire depends on the current squared times the wire's resistance, so even a wire of a tenth of an ohm carrying twenty amps is dissipating forty watts, enough to melt its insulation in seconds. The cell's internal resistance dissipates similar power inside the cell, which is why a shorted cell gets hot, swells and can leak or, for lithium, catch fire. In a house, a short across the mains could draw hundreds or thousands of amps for the fraction of a second before the circuit breaker trips, and the wire would glow and the insulation burn if it did not trip; this is the whole purpose of fuses and breakers, which are deliberately the weakest link, melting or tripping at a chosen current before the wiring reaches a dangerous temperature. The idea that a short is harmless because nothing is "in the way" is backwards: a lamp or a resistor in the way is what keeps the current small enough to be safe.
