### subtopic_1 — Resistors: What They Are, Their Values and Their Symbol
**Assistant:** How can adding a resistor make the total resistance smaller? A resistor resists; surely adding one always adds resistance.

**Tutor:** It adds resistance to its own path, but in parallel it also adds a path, and the second effect wins. Resistance is opposition to flow through the whole arrangement, and the whole arrangement now has two doors instead of one. Imagine a hall emptying through one doorway; opening a second doorway beside it, even a narrower one, lets more people out per minute, so the hall as a whole is easier to empty, which is lower total resistance. The new resistor does not reduce the current through the first; the first still carries exactly what it did, because it still has the full supply voltage across it. What changes is that the second resistor now carries its own current in addition, so the total leaving the supply is larger, and a larger current for the same voltage is, by Ohm's law, a smaller resistance. This is why the parallel total is always below the smallest branch: the smallest branch alone would pass a certain current, and the other branches can only add to it. The confusion comes from thinking of a resistor as something you put "in the way"; in series it is in the way, in parallel it is beside the way, offering a detour, and detours make traffic flow more easily, not less.

### subtopic_2 — Resistors in Series: Resistances Add, Current Falls
**Assistant:** If every appliance in a house is in parallel and each gets the full 230 volts, why does the house have a main breaker at all? Each appliance is safe on its own.

**Tutor:** Because each appliance is safe on its own, but their currents add, and the wires feeding all of them have a limit. In a parallel arrangement the branch currents sum to the total drawn through the main supply cable, so a kettle at ten amps, a heater at eight, an oven at twelve and a geyser at sixteen, all on at once, pull nearly fifty amps through the cable from the street and the main switch, and the house's supply cable and meter are rated for sixty or eighty amps. Switch on a few more things and the total exceeds the rating; the main cable heats, and without protection its insulation would eventually soften and fail inside a wall where nobody can see it. The main breaker trips at the cable's rated current to prevent that, exactly as each smaller breaker on the distribution board protects the thinner cable of its own circuit, the twenty-amp breaker for the plug circuit and the ten-amp for the lights. This is parallel resistance in daily life: adding appliances adds paths, lowers the house's total resistance, raises the total current, and the breakers are the limit on how low the resistance may go. It is also why running a heater and a kettle from the same multiplug adaptor is dangerous: the adaptor's cable is the shared main line for those branches and may be rated for far less than their sum.

### subtopic_3 — Resistors in Parallel: More Paths, Lower Resistance, and Drawing Both
**Assistant:** Why do the colour bands matter if I can just measure the resistor with a multimeter?

**Tutor:** Because the bands tell you what the resistor is supposed to be and the meter tells you what it is, and you need both. When you are building from a diagram that says 330 ohms, you reach into a drawer of a hundred resistors, and the bands let you find the right one in seconds, orange, orange, brown, without measuring each; a meter check of the one you picked then confirms it is within tolerance and has not been damaged. When a resistor is already soldered into a circuit, the meter cannot read it accurately because other components are connected across it and share the current, so the bands are the only way to know its value without removing it. When a resistor has overheated, its bands may be scorched but still legible while the meter shows it has drifted far from its value, which is itself a diagnosis. And in an examination there is no meter, only a drawing or a photograph with coloured bands, and the question expects the code. Think of the bands as the label on a tin and the meter as opening the tin: you use the label to choose, and you open it to check. Learning the ten colours is an hour's work that pays off every time you sit at a bench for the rest of your life in electronics.
