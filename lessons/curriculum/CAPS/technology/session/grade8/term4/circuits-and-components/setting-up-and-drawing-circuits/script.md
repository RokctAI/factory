# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers setting up and drawing circuits with a range of components. The anchor is a Grade 8 bench in Polokwane with a battery holder for two cells, a board of screw terminals, crocodile leads, two bulbs, a buzzer, a toy motor, an LED with a 330 ohm resistor, a push switch and a toggle switch, and a card of four circuit diagrams, each to be built, tested, and ticked.

## Subtopic: From Diagram to Bench: Building a Circuit Safely

Building from a diagram is a sequence that prevents most faults. First read the diagram completely: find the supply and note its voltage, count the components, identify any with polarity, trace the loop or loops with a finger, and check that no line bypasses a component. Second gather the components the diagram names and check each against its rating: a 3 volt lamp for a two-cell battery, a 330 ohm resistor for the LED, a buzzer marked for the voltage in use. Third lay the components out on the bench in the same arrangement as the diagram, supply on the left, so that the wiring follows the drawing and can be checked against it. Fourth connect, with the switch open and, if the supply has a switch of its own, that open too, one lead at a time, following the loop from the positive terminal round to the negative, observing polarity for cells, LED and buzzer. Fifth check: trace the built circuit against the diagram once more, look for bare ends that could touch, and only then close the switch. Sixth observe and record: what happened, for how long, and anything unexpected. If nothing happens, open the switch and troubleshoot before trying again.

Troubleshooting follows the same order every time. Is the path complete: every clip on metal, every terminal tight, the lamp screwed in, the switch actually closing? Is the supply alive: test the battery on a known good lamp? Is polarity right: LED and buzzer the correct way round, cells not reversed in the holder? Is the component sound: swap the lamp for another? Is there a short: a lead across a component or the supply making something warm? The order runs from most common to least, and ninety percent of bench faults are a loose connection or a dead cell.

Bench safety for low-voltage circuits is simple. Cells cannot shock you, but they can burn: a shorted cell heats quickly, so leads are connected with the switch open and bare ends kept apart, and anything that warms is disconnected at once. Motors have moving parts, so hair and loose sleeves are kept clear and fingers off the shaft. Leads are not left trailing where they can be pulled. Components are disconnected at the end and cells removed from holders if the kit is stored, so that a stray lead does not drain them overnight. Mains electricity is never used on the bench.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six steps of building from a diagram, the order of troubleshooting, and the bench safety rules.

## Subtopic: Circuits With a Range of Components: Series and Parallel Branches

The card's four circuits add components and paths one at a time, and each teaches something. Circuit one: battery, toggle switch, one lamp, in a single loop; the lamp lights when the switch is closed, the basic circuit of a torch. Circuit two: battery, push switch, buzzer; the buzzer sounds while the switch is pressed and stops when released, which is the heart of a doorbell and of the panic button; swap the buzzer's leads and it is silent, showing polarity. Circuit three: battery, toggle switch, motor; the motor runs; reverse its two leads and it runs the other way, which is how a winder lifts and lowers; add a second switch arrangement later and a single-pole double-throw switch can do the reversing. Circuit four: battery, switch, resistor, LED, in series; the LED lights; remove the resistor and the LED is at risk, so this is shown as a diagram and not tried; reverse the LED and it stays dark.

Then the paths multiply. Two lamps in series, one after the other in the same loop: both light, both dim, because the battery's voltage is shared between them, and unscrewing one puts out both, because the loop is broken; old Christmas lights behaved this way. Two lamps in parallel, each on its own branch across the battery: both light at full brightness, because each has the full battery voltage, and unscrewing one leaves the other lit, because its branch is still complete; house lighting is wired this way. The battery runs down faster with two lamps in parallel than with one, because it is supplying two full currents. A switch in the main line controls both parallel lamps; a switch in one branch controls only that lamp, which is how each room's light has its own switch while the main switch kills everything. Week 5 will measure these effects and name them with Ohm's law; today the point is to build both and see the difference.

Mixed circuits combine them: a lamp and a buzzer in parallel branches, each with its own switch, so that one battery runs an indicator light and an alarm independently, which is almost exactly the panic button's output stage. Each addition is built with the switch open, checked against the diagram and tested, and the result recorded in a table: circuit, what was predicted, what happened.

The questions for this section are with you now: what each of the four card circuits shows, the differences between two lamps in series and in parallel, and how switches in the main line and in branches behave.

## Subtopic: From Bench to Diagram: Drawing What You Built

Drawing a diagram from a built circuit reverses the first skill and is tested just as often. The method is to follow the wire, not the layout. Start at the positive terminal of the supply and draw the cell or battery symbol on the left of a rectangle with its positive at the top. Follow the first lead from the positive terminal to whatever it reaches, a switch, a lamp, a junction, and draw that component's symbol on the next side of the rectangle. Continue component by component round the loop until the lead returns to the negative terminal. Where a lead reaches a point from which two leads leave, that is a junction; draw a dot, and follow each branch in turn, drawing it as a side path that rejoins the main loop at another dot. Components that physically sit beside each other on the bench may be far apart on the diagram, and components far apart on the bench may be side by side on the diagram; only the connections matter.

Then check the drawing against the circuit by tracing it in the other direction, from negative back to positive, and count: the same number of components, the same number of junctions, every component in the correct loop or branch, polarity marked for the cell and the LED, values labelled where known, the switch drawn open. A final test is to hand the diagram to a partner and ask them to build it; if their circuit behaves like yours, the diagram is right.

Neatness rules apply with the ruler: straight horizontal and vertical lines, right-angle corners, symbols of standard size and not touching, dots only at true junctions, no dots where lines merely cross. The circuit's name and the date go beneath, and a line of observation, "both lamps lit at full brightness; removing one left the other lit", turns the diagram into a record. The examination gives a photograph or drawing of a wired circuit and asks for its diagram, and gives a diagram and asks what happens when a switch is opened or a component removed; both are answered by tracing paths.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing the layout of the bench instead of the connections, with leads shown as curves wandering between components. Two: a dot drawn where two wires merely cross, inventing a junction and a short. Three: closing the switch before checking the circuit against the diagram, so a short or reversed LED is discovered the expensive way. Four: declaring a circuit broken without troubleshooting in order, when a crocodile clip is gripping insulation.

The questions for this section are with you now: the follow-the-wire method, how junctions and branches are drawn, and how a drawn diagram is checked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a card of four diagrams and a bench of parts to build them — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Build What the Drawing Says

Building from a diagram, six steps. Read it all: find the supply and its volts, count the parts, spot the ones with a right way round, finger round the loop, check no line skips a part. Gather the parts and check each rating: 3 volt lamp for two cells, 330 ohm resistor for the LED, buzzer marked for your volts. Lay them out like the drawing, supply on the left, so wiring follows the picture. Connect with the switch open, one lead at a time, plus round to minus, cell, LED and buzzer the right way. Check: trace the built circuit against the drawing, look for bare ends that could touch, then close the switch. Watch and write down what happened. Nothing? Open the switch and troubleshoot before trying again.

Troubleshoot in order, most common first. Loop complete: every clip on metal, every screw tight, bulb screwed in, switch really closing? Supply alive: test the battery on a bulb you know works? Right way round: LED, buzzer, cells in the holder? Part dead: swap the bulb? Short: a lead across something, anything warm? Nine faults in ten are a loose clip or a flat cell.

Safety at low voltage: cells cannot shock you but a shorted one burns, so switch open while connecting, bare ends apart, unplug anything warm. Motors spin: hair, sleeves and fingers clear. No trailing leads. Unplug at the end and take cells out of holders for storage. Never mains on the bench.

Hold the switch held open in mind and try the questions for this part: build what the drawing says.

## Subtopic: More Parts, More Paths

Four circuits on the card, one lesson each. One: battery, toggle switch, lamp, one loop; closes, lights; a torch. Two: battery, push switch, buzzer; sounds while pressed, stops when let go; a doorbell, and the heart of the panic button; swap the buzzer's leads and it is silent. Three: battery, switch, motor; runs; swap its leads, runs backwards, how a winder lowers; a double-throw switch can do the swap. Four: battery, switch, resistor, LED in series; lights; take the resistor out and the LED is in danger, so look at that one on paper only; reverse the LED, dark.

Now more paths. Two lamps in series, one after the other in one loop: both light, both dim, because they share the battery's volts; unscrew one, both go out, because the loop is broken; old Christmas lights. Two lamps in parallel, each on its own branch: both full bright, each getting the full volts; unscrew one, the other stays lit, its branch is still whole; house lights. The battery runs down faster with two in parallel, because it feeds two full currents. A switch in the main line controls both; a switch in a branch controls only that lamp: each room has its switch, the main switch kills all.

Mix them: a lamp and a buzzer on separate branches, each with its own switch, one battery running an indicator and an alarm on their own. That is nearly the panic button's output. Build each with the switch open, check against the drawing, test, and record: circuit, what you expected, what happened.

Hold the one dim lamp and the two bright ones in mind and try this part's questions: more parts, more paths.

## Subtopic: Draw What You Built

Drawing from a built circuit: follow the wire, not where things sit. Start at the battery's plus; draw the battery on the left of a rectangle, plus up. Follow the first lead to whatever it reaches, switch, lamp, junction, and draw that symbol on the next side. Keep going part by part round the loop until you are back at minus. Where one lead arrives and two leave, that is a junction: draw a dot, then follow each branch and draw it as a side path rejoining the loop at another dot. Things next to each other on the bench may be far apart on paper and the other way round; only connections count.

Check by tracing the drawing backwards, minus to plus, and count: same number of parts, same number of junctions, each part in the right loop or branch, plus marked on the cell and the LED, values written, switch open. Best test: hand it to a partner to build; if theirs behaves like yours, it is right.

Ruler, straight lines, right angles, standard symbols not touching, dots only at real junctions, none where lines just cross. Name and date underneath, and one line of what you saw: "both lamps full bright; removing one left the other lit." The exam shows a wired circuit and asks for the diagram, or shows a diagram and asks what happens when a switch opens or a part is removed; both are answered by tracing paths.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Build from a diagram in six steps with the switch open, troubleshoot in order from loose clip to short, keep cells from shorting and fingers from motors. Series shares volts and dies together; parallel gives full volts and survives alone; switches control what they sit in line with. Draw from a circuit by following the wire, dots at junctions, checked by a partner's build.

Hold the dot at the junction, and take the final questions of the lesson: draw what you built.
