### subtopic_1 — Electrical Systems: Input Devices
**Assistant:** Is a battery an input device even though it is just storing energy that came from somewhere else?

**Tutor:** Yes, within the circuit it is the input, and the question you are asking is the right one for Week 2 and Week 4, where the term follows the energy further back. An input device is defined by what it does at the boundary of the circuit you are looking at: it is the component that pushes charge around the loop and supplies the electrical energy the rest of the circuit uses. A cell does that by a chemical reaction, and the chemical energy was put into it when it was manufactured, or when a rechargeable cell was charged from the mains or a solar panel. So the cell is an input device for the torch circuit, and at the same time it is an energy store in the larger system that includes the factory or the charger. Systems thinking handles this by drawing the boundary deliberately. For the torch, the boundary is the torch: input, cell; process, switch; output, lamp. For a solar home system, the boundary is wider: input, solar panel converting light; process, charge controller and battery storing it; output, lamps and a phone charger at night. The battery has moved from the input box to the process box because the boundary moved, and both diagrams are correct for their purpose. The same thing happens with a generator: in a bicycle dynamo circuit it is the input, but in Eskom's system the generator is the process that converts the energy of steam or falling water, which is the input. When you are asked to classify a component, say what circuit you are looking at; when the exam gives a simple circuit with a cell, a switch and a lamp, the cell is the input device, and that is the answer it wants.

### subtopic_2 — Output Devices and What They Convert Energy Into
**Assistant:** Why does a solar panel need a battery? If it is an input device it should be able to run things on its own.

**Tutor:** It can run things on its own, in daylight, and that is exactly the limitation. A photovoltaic panel converts light into electrical energy at the moment the light falls on it, with no storage inside; its output rises and falls with the light, from a full rated output in bright midday sun to a fraction under cloud and to nothing after sunset. A solar-powered calculator or a garden light with its own small panel runs directly off the panel while the sun shines and stops when it does not, which for a calculator is fine and for a house is not. A home needs light precisely when the panel gives none, so a solar home system stores the day's energy in a battery, charges it through a controller that stops overcharging, and draws from the battery at night; the panel is the input, the battery the store and the lamps the output. This also explains the differences you will meet in Week 3, where the advantages and disadvantages of solar cells are weighed: no fuel, no emissions, no moving parts and nothing to run out, against intermittency, a modest output per square metre, a high initial cost mostly in the panels and batteries, and the fact that batteries wear out and must be replaced and recycled. Grid-scale solar farms face the same issue, which is why South Africa pairs solar with coal, gas, pumped-storage hydro and increasingly large battery banks to keep the lights on after dark, the national-grid topic of Week 4. For the panic button in Week 7, this is a design point: a panic alarm must work at three in the morning, so if it is to be solar-powered it needs a battery, and if it uses a battery it needs a way of knowing the battery is not flat.

### subtopic_3 — Control Devices: Switches of Many Kinds
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a switch and a resistor if both can reduce the current?

**Tutor:** They act on current in completely different ways, and the difference is the one between control and process. A switch has two states: closed, in which it is a conductor with almost no resistance and the full current flows, and open, in which it is a gap with effectively infinite resistance and no current flows at all. It does not reduce current; it allows it or stops it. Its job is control, deciding when, and in a systems diagram it sits in the process box as the thing that gates the flow, or as a control input if it is operated by a sensor. A resistor has one state: it is always in the circuit, always conducting, and always opposing the flow by a fixed amount measured in ohms, so that for a given voltage a smaller current flows than would flow without it. It does not decide anything; it sets the level. Its job is to protect a component from too much current, to divide a voltage, or to set how bright a lamp or how fast a motor runs, and in an output circuit it is listed with the output devices because it converts a little energy into heat while doing so. There is a component that sits between the two: the variable resistor, or potentiometer, which is the volume knob on a radio or the dimmer on a light; turn it and the resistance changes smoothly from low to high, so the current can be set anywhere between full and almost nothing. It is still a resistor, because it never breaks the circuit, but it is used for control. The distinction matters for Week 7: the panic button needs a switch, because it must go from nothing to full alarm the instant it is pressed; a resistor in that line would make the siren quieter, not later.
