# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers correct connections, short circuits and accepted component symbols. The anchor is a Grade 8 bench in Polokwane where a learner has connected a cell, a switch and a bulb with crocodile-clip leads, the bulb lit, and a second lead lying loose that, if clipped across the bulb's terminals, would make the wire warm within seconds, with a chart of circuit symbols pinned above.

## Subtopic: Correct Connections: Complete Paths and Polarity

A circuit conducts only when it is complete. Charge leaves one terminal of the supply, travels through conductors and components, and returns to the other terminal; break the path anywhere, a loose clip, an open switch, a snapped wire, a blown lamp filament, and the whole circuit stops, not just the part after the break. Correct connection therefore begins with tracing the path with a finger: from the positive terminal, through each component in turn, back to the negative, with every joint firm. Crocodile clips must grip metal, not insulation; wires must be stripped so copper meets copper; terminals must be tightened; and the lamp must be screwed home in its holder. A circuit that does not work is first checked for a complete path before anything else is suspected.

Some components care about direction. A cell has a positive terminal, marked with a raised button and a plus sign, and a negative, the flat end; in a battery of cells the positive of one meets the negative of the next so that their voltages add, and a single cell inserted backwards cancels the one beside it. A light-emitting diode passes current only from its anode, the longer leg, to its cathode, the shorter leg beside the flat edge of the case, and simply does not light the wrong way round. A buzzer usually has a red lead for positive and a black for negative. A motor turns either way, and reversing its connections reverses its rotation, which is how the Term 3 winder was made to lower as well as lift. Lamps, resistors and switches do not care. Correct connection means knowing which components have polarity and observing it, and knowing which do not.

Connection also means putting components where they do their job. A switch goes in the path so that opening it breaks the whole loop, which is anywhere in a single-loop circuit; a resistor that protects an LED goes in series with the LED, in the same path, so that all the current through the LED passes through the resistor too. Components can be connected in series, one after another in a single loop, or in parallel, side by side in separate branches, and Week 5 will show how that changes current and voltage; today's rule is simply that each component must be in a complete path back to the supply.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a break anywhere stops a circuit, which components have polarity and how it is identified, and where a protective resistor goes.

## Subtopic: Short Circuits: What They Are and Why They Are Dangerous

A short circuit is a path of very low resistance connecting the two terminals of the supply directly, or bypassing a component, so that current flows around the components instead of through them. A bare wire laid across the two terminals of a cell, a lead clipped across both terminals of a lamp, two bare wires touching where their insulation has worn, or a nail driven through a cable all make shorts. Because almost nothing resists the current, it becomes as large as the supply can deliver; the wire and the supply heat rapidly, the insulation melts, the cell drains in minutes and may leak or burst, and in a house circuit the wiring can ignite what is near it. Shorts are the commonest cause of electrical fires and of the burnt-out components on a school bench.

In the bench circuit the lead clipped across the lamp's terminals makes the lamp go out, because the current takes the easy path, and the lead gets warm; that warmth is the energy that should have been light, now released in the wire. Removing the lead restores the lamp. The demonstration teaches the rule: current divides between parallel paths in inverse proportion to their resistance, so a path of nearly zero resistance takes nearly all of it.

Protection against shorts is built into every mains installation and into the panic-button circuit you will design. A fuse is a thin wire in a holder, chosen to melt at a stated current; a short pulls far more than that current, the fuse wire melts in a fraction of a second, the circuit is broken and the fault is isolated before the cable overheats. A circuit breaker does the same job with a switch tripped by an electromagnet or a heated strip, and can be reset instead of replaced; the distribution board in a South African home holds the main switch, the earth-leakage unit and a row of breakers, each protecting one circuit, lights at 10 amps, plugs at 20, the stove and geyser at 30 or more. The earth-leakage unit protects people rather than wiring: it trips when current leaks to earth through a person or a damp appliance, and the Week 2 discussion of illegal connections turns largely on what happens when none of this protection exists. On the bench, shorts are avoided by connecting one lead at a time with the switch open, by keeping bare ends apart, by checking the path before closing the switch, and by disconnecting at once if anything warms.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a short circuit is and why it is dangerous, why the bypassed lamp goes out, and how fuses, breakers and earth-leakage units protect.

## Subtopic: Accepted Component Symbols and Reading a Circuit Diagram

A circuit diagram shows a circuit as symbols joined by straight lines, with no attempt to show what the components look like or where they physically sit; it shows how they are connected, which is the only thing that determines how the circuit works. Accepted symbols are standardised so that any reader anywhere understands them, and the curriculum uses the common international set. The cell is a long thin line, the positive terminal, beside a short thick line, the negative, and a battery is several cells in a row, or two cells with a dashed line between for a battery of many. A switch, open, is a line with a hinged gap; a push switch is a line with a bar pressed down onto two contacts. A lamp is a circle with a cross inside. A resistor is a rectangle, or in older books a zigzag; a variable resistor adds an arrow across it. A light-emitting diode is a triangle with a bar at its point and two small arrows leaving it, the triangle pointing in the direction current flows. A buzzer is a half-circle on a base; a bell is a circle on a base. A motor is a circle with an M inside; a generator a circle with a G. A fuse is a rectangle with a line through its length. A heater is a rectangle with diagonal lines. A voltmeter and an ammeter are circles with V and A. Wires are straight lines, joined at a dot where they connect and crossing without a dot where they do not; connections are drawn with right-angle corners.

Drawing a diagram follows rules of neatness that make it readable. Components are placed on the sides of a rectangle with the supply at the top or left and the positive terminal to the left or top by convention; lines are horizontal and vertical, drawn with a ruler; symbols are the standard size and not crowded; each component is labelled with its value or name where that matters, "3 V", "330 ohms", "LED"; the switch is drawn open unless the diagram shows the circuit in operation. A diagram of the bench circuit is a rectangle with a cell on the left, a switch on the top line, a lamp on the right, all in one loop; adding a second lamp in parallel is a branch across the first.

Reading a diagram reverses the process: find the supply, trace the loop with a finger, note each component in order, check polarity for cells and LEDs, and look for any line that would bypass a component, which on paper is a short as surely as on the bench. The ability to draw a circuit from its description and to build a circuit from its diagram is tested in the examination and used every week of the term, and the panic-button design is submitted as a diagram.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: an LED drawn or connected backwards. Two: a lead across a component's terminals, a short on the bench or on paper. Three: a diagram drawn free-hand with curved wires and non-standard symbols that nobody else can read. Four: a switch placed in a branch so that opening it leaves the rest of the circuit live.

The questions for this section are with you now: the symbols for cell, battery, switch, lamp, resistor, LED, buzzer, motor and fuse, the rules for drawing a neat diagram, and how a diagram is read for shorts and polarity.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a lit bulb, a loose lead and a symbol chart on the wall — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Making the Loop

A circuit only works when it is a complete loop: out of one end of the supply, through the parts, back into the other end. Break it anywhere, a loose clip, an open switch, a snapped wire, a dead bulb, and everything stops, not just the bit after the gap. So first always trace the loop with your finger: from plus, through each part, back to minus, every joint tight. Clips on metal, not plastic; wires stripped so copper meets copper; screws tight; bulb screwed home. Circuit dead? Check the loop before you blame anything else.

Some parts care which way round. A cell has a plus end, the button, and a minus end, the flat; in a battery, plus of one meets minus of the next so the volts add, and one cell backwards cancels its neighbour. An LED only passes current from its long leg, the anode, to its short leg by the flat edge, the cathode; wrong way, it just stays dark. A buzzer: red to plus, black to minus. A motor spins either way, and swapping its wires swaps the direction, which is how the winder lowered. Lamps, resistors and switches do not care.

Put parts where they do their job. A switch anywhere in a single loop breaks the whole loop. A resistor protecting an LED goes in the same path as the LED, so all the LED's current goes through it. Parts can be in series, one after another in one loop, or parallel, side by side in branches; Week 5 explains what that does. Today: every part needs a complete path back to the supply.

Hold the finger tracing the loop in mind and try the questions for this part: making the loop.

## Subtopic: The Shortcut That Burns

A short circuit is a shortcut: a path with almost no resistance straight between the supply's two ends, or around a part, so current skips the parts. A bare wire across a cell. A lead across both ends of a bulb. Two worn wires touching. A nail through a cable. With nothing to resist it, the current grows as big as the supply can push; the wire and the cell get hot fast, insulation melts, the cell drains in minutes and may leak or burst, and in a house the cable can set fire to what is next to it. Shorts are the top cause of electrical fires and of dead parts on a school bench.

Clip the loose lead across the bulb and the bulb goes out, because the current takes the easy way, and the lead goes warm; that warmth is the light's energy, dumped in the wire. Unclip it and the bulb comes back. Rule: current splits between parallel paths with most going the easiest way, so a near-zero path takes nearly all.

Protection. A fuse is a thin wire made to melt at a set current; a short pulls far more, it melts in a blink, and the circuit is cut before the cable cooks. A circuit breaker does the same with a switch that trips, and you reset it instead of replacing it; your home's board has the main switch, the earth-leakage, and a breaker per circuit, 10 amps lights, 20 plugs, 30 or more stove and geyser. The earth-leakage protects people: it trips when current leaks to earth through a person or a wet appliance. Week 2's illegal connections have none of this. On the bench: one lead at a time with the switch open, bare ends apart, check the loop before closing, unplug the moment anything warms.

Hold the warm lead in mind and try this part's questions: the shortcut that burns.

## Subtopic: A Language of Lines

A circuit diagram shows how parts are connected, not what they look like or where they sit, using symbols everyone reads. Cell: a long thin line, plus, next to a short thick line, minus. Battery: several in a row, or two with a dashed gap for many. Switch: a line with a hinged gap. Push switch: a bar pressed onto two contacts. Lamp: a circle with a cross. Resistor: a rectangle, zigzag in old books; variable resistor adds an arrow. LED: a triangle with a bar at its tip and two little arrows flying off, triangle pointing the way current goes. Buzzer: a half circle on a base. Bell: a circle on a base. Motor: circle with M. Generator: circle with G. Fuse: a rectangle with a line through it. Heater: a rectangle with diagonals. Meters: circles with V or A. Wires: straight lines, a dot where they join, no dot where they cross without joining, right-angle corners.

Draw it neatly so anyone can read it. Parts on the sides of a rectangle, supply at top or left, plus to the left or top. Ruler, horizontal and vertical lines. Standard-size symbols, not crowded. Label values that matter: 3 V, 330 ohms, LED. Switch drawn open unless you are showing it working. The bench circuit: a rectangle, cell on the left, switch along the top, lamp on the right, one loop. A second lamp in parallel is a branch across the first.

Reading one: find the supply, finger round the loop, name each part in order, check cell and LED direction, and look for any line that skips a part, which is a short on paper exactly as on the bench. You will draw circuits from words and build them from diagrams in the exam and every week, and the panic button is handed in as a diagram.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A circuit needs a complete loop with tight joints; cells, LEDs and buzzers have a right way round. A short is a near-zero path that bypasses parts, overheats and starts fires; fuses and breakers cut it, earth-leakage protects people. Diagrams use standard symbols on ruled rectangles, plus to the left, switch open, dots at joins, and are read by tracing the loop.

Hold the triangle with the bar, and take the final questions of the lesson: a language of lines.
