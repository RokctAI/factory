# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers revision of input devices, cells, generators and solar panels; output devices, resistors, lamps, heaters, buzzers and motors; and control devices, switches. The anchor is a Grade 8 bench in Polokwane laid out with a 1.5 volt cell, a hand-cranked dynamo torch, a small solar panel, a bulb in a holder, a buzzer, a toy motor, a resistor and a row of different switches, each with a card label waiting to be sorted into three boxes.

## Subtopic: Electrical Systems: Input Devices

An electrical circuit is a complete conducting path around which charge flows, and the components in it are grouped by what they do to the energy. Input devices supply the electrical energy, converting it from some other form. Output devices use the electrical energy, converting it into the form the user wants. Control devices decide whether and when the energy flows. This is the input-process-output pattern of the systems diagrams of Term 3 applied to electricity, and drawing a circuit as a systems diagram, input: cell, process: switch and wiring, output: lamp, is a useful first step before the circuit diagram.

Three input devices are revised. The cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy: two different metals, the electrodes, sit in a chemical paste or liquid, the electrolyte, and the chemical reaction pushes charge around the external circuit; a single cell gives about 1.5 volts, and several connected end to end form a battery, which is the correct word for a group of cells, though everyday speech uses it for one. Cells run down as the chemicals are used up; rechargeable cells reverse the reaction when current is pushed back through them. The generator converts movement, kinetic energy, into electrical energy by moving a coil of wire in a magnetic field, or a magnet past a coil; the dynamo on a hand-cranked torch or a bicycle is a small one, and the alternators in Eskom's power stations are very large ones turned by steam or water, which Week 4 will explore. The solar panel, made of photovoltaic cells, converts light energy directly into electrical energy: light falling on a thin slice of treated silicon frees charge, which flows when a circuit is connected; a panel gives current only while light falls on it, more in bright sun, little under cloud, none at night, which is why solar systems store energy in batteries.

Each input device has a voltage, the push it gives the charge, and a capacity or power, how much energy it can supply and how fast. A 1.5 volt cell and a 1.5 volt solar cell give the same push but the cell can supply it in the dark and the solar cell cannot; a car battery and a torch cell both give a steady voltage but one can run a starter motor and the other cannot. These differences will matter when the panic button must work at night and when the Week 2 report compares energy sources for homes.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three groups of components, how a cell, a generator and a solar panel each convert energy, and the difference between voltage and capacity.

## Subtopic: Output Devices and What They Convert Energy Into

Output devices convert electrical energy into something useful, and each is named by what it produces. The lamp gives light: a filament lamp heats a thin tungsten wire until it glows, wasting most of the energy as heat, which is why they are being replaced; a light-emitting diode gives light directly from a semiconductor with far less heat and is the lamp in nearly every new torch, phone and streetlight, and it passes current in one direction only, so its two legs must be connected the right way round. The heater gives heat: a coil of resistance wire, nichrome, through which current flows, used in kettles, stoves, geysers, irons and bar heaters; heating is the most energy-hungry use of electricity in a South African home, which is the subject of Week 2. The buzzer gives sound: a small electromagnet or a piezoelectric crystal makes a diaphragm vibrate; the buzzer in a panic alarm, a microwave and a reversing truck is this, and most buzzers also pass current in one direction only. The motor gives movement: current through coils in a magnetic field produces a turning force on the shaft, in fans, pumps, drills, the Term 3 winder and the compressor of a fridge; a motor turned backwards is a generator, which is the same device used in the opposite direction.

The resistor is listed with the output devices in the curriculum because it is a component that uses energy, converting it into a little heat, but its job in a circuit is to limit current. Every material resists the flow of charge to some degree, and a resistor is made with a chosen resistance so that the current through a sensitive component, such as a light-emitting diode, is held at a safe value; without the resistor the diode passes too much current and burns out at once. Resistance is measured in ohms, and a resistor's value is shown by coloured bands. Week 5 will connect resistors in series and parallel and meet Ohm's law.

Every output device has a rating, the voltage it is designed for and the power it uses, and matching the rating to the supply is the first rule of connecting one: a 3 volt bulb on a 9 volt battery flashes and dies, a 12 volt motor on a 1.5 volt cell barely turns. Output devices also tell the designer which energy conversion the system is for, which is how the panic button's output, a buzzer and a lamp, is chosen before any circuit is drawn.

The questions for this section are with you now: what each of lamp, heater, buzzer and motor converts electrical energy into, why the resistor is a current limiter, and why ratings must match.

## Subtopic: Control Devices: Switches of Many Kinds

Control devices decide when current flows, and the switch is the simplest. A switch makes or breaks the conducting path: closed, the circuit is complete and current flows; open, there is a gap and nothing flows. Switches differ in how they are operated, how they stay, and how many paths they control. By operation: a toggle switch, flipped by hand, as on a wall; a push switch, pressed, which may be push-to-make, closing the circuit while pressed, as on a doorbell or a panic button, or push-to-break, opening it while pressed, as on a fridge door light; a slide switch, as on a torch; a rotary switch, turned, as on a stove plate control; a key switch, operated by a key, as on a car ignition or an alarm panel; a rocker switch, as on a power strip. By how it stays: a latching switch stays in the position it is put, as a wall light switch does, while a momentary switch returns when released, as a doorbell does; the panic button is momentary, a latching alarm circuit will be the Week 7 problem.

By what operates it: switches need not be worked by a hand. A reed switch closes when a magnet comes near, which is how a door or window sensor in an alarm knows the door is open; a tilt switch closes when tipped; a pressure mat closes when stood on; a thermostat is a switch operated by temperature, bending a strip of two metals to open a contact when a geyser or iron is hot enough; a float switch is operated by water level in a tank or a washing machine; a light-dependent resistor with a transistor acts as a switch operated by darkness, turning on streetlights at dusk. These are input sensors and control devices at once, and they let a system respond to the world without a person.

By paths: a single-pole single-throw switch controls one path, on or off; a single-pole double-throw switch connects one wire to either of two others, as a two-way light switch at the top and bottom of a staircase does; a double-pole switch breaks two wires at once, as the main switch on a distribution board does for safety. A relay is a switch operated by an electromagnet, so a small current in one circuit controls a large current in another, which is how a car's key switch starts a motor that draws hundreds of amps, and how the panic button's small circuit can switch a loud siren.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a single cell a battery and then wondering why the "battery" gives 1.5 volts. Two: connecting a light-emitting diode without a resistor, or backwards. Three: choosing a latching switch for a panic button, so it cannot be pressed and released. Four: putting a 3 volt lamp across a 9 volt supply because "more is better".

The questions for this section are with you now: how switches are classified by operation, by latching and by paths, which switches are operated by the environment, and what a relay does.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Where the Energy Comes From

A circuit is a complete loop that charge can flow round. Sort its parts by what they do to the energy. Input devices put electrical energy in, made from some other kind. Output devices use it, turning it into what you want. Control devices decide when it flows. Same input-process-output as Term 3's systems diagrams, and drawing a circuit that way first, cell, switch, lamp, helps before the proper diagram.

Three inputs. The cell: chemical energy to electrical. Two different metals in a chemical paste; the reaction pushes charge round the loop; about 1.5 volts each; several end to end make a battery, which is the correct word for a group, though people say battery for one. It runs down as the chemicals are used; a rechargeable one reverses when you push current back. The generator: movement to electrical, a coil moving in a magnetic field, the dynamo on a wind-up torch or a bicycle, and the giant alternators in power stations spun by steam or water. The solar panel: light to electrical, straight from treated silicon; current only while light falls on it, lots in sun, little in cloud, none at night, so solar systems store in batteries.

Each input has a voltage, how hard it pushes, and a capacity, how much energy and how fast. A cell and a solar cell can both give 1.5 volts, but only the cell works in the dark. A car battery and a torch cell both hold steady volts, but only one starts a car.

## Subtopic: Where the Energy Goes

Outputs turn electrical energy into something you want. Lamp: light. The old filament kind heats a thin wire until it glows and wastes most of the energy as heat; the LED makes light straight from a chip with little heat, and it only passes current one way, so connect its two legs the right way round. Heater: heat, a coil of resistance wire, nichrome, in kettles, stoves, geysers, irons; heating eats more electricity than anything else in a South African home, which is Week 2. Buzzer: sound, a tiny electromagnet or crystal shaking a disc; alarms, microwaves, reversing trucks; usually one-way too. Motor: movement, current in coils in a magnetic field twisting the shaft; fans, pumps, drills, the Term 3 winder, your fridge; turn a motor by hand and it becomes a generator.

The resistor sits with the outputs because it uses energy, as a little heat, but its job is to limit current. Everything resists charge a bit; a resistor has a chosen resistance so a delicate part like an LED gets a safe current. No resistor and the LED burns out instantly. Ohms, shown by coloured bands. Week 5 wires them in series and parallel.

Every output has a rating: the volts it is made for and the power it uses. Match it to the supply. A 3 volt bulb on 9 volts flashes and dies; a 12 volt motor on 1.5 volts barely twitches. The output also tells you what the system is for: the panic button's outputs, a buzzer and a lamp, are chosen before any circuit is drawn.

## Subtopic: Who Decides When

Control devices decide when. A switch makes or breaks the loop: closed, complete, current flows; open, a gap, nothing. Sort switches three ways.

How you work it: toggle, flipped, your wall switch; push, pressed, push-to-make closes while pressed, doorbell, panic button, push-to-break opens while pressed, fridge door light; slide, torch; rotary, stove knob; key, car ignition, alarm panel; rocker, power strip. How it stays: latching stays where you put it, the wall switch; momentary springs back, the doorbell. A panic button is momentary; making the alarm stay on afterwards is Week 7's problem.

What works it, when no hand does: a reed switch closes when a magnet comes near, how an alarm knows a door opened; a tilt switch when tipped; a pressure mat when stood on; a thermostat when hot, a strip of two metals bending to open a contact in a geyser or iron; a float switch at a water level; a light sensor with a transistor at dusk for streetlights. These sense the world and control the circuit at once.

How many paths: single-pole single-throw, one wire on or off; single-pole double-throw, one wire to either of two, the two-way switch at the top and bottom of stairs; double-pole, two wires broken together, the main switch on a distribution board. A relay is a switch worked by an electromagnet, so a tiny current in one loop controls a big current in another: a car key starting a motor that draws hundreds of amps, or a small panic circuit firing a loud siren.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Inputs supply energy: cells from chemistry, generators from movement, solar panels from light. Outputs use it: lamps light, heaters heat, buzzers sound, motors move, resistors limit current. Controls decide: switches sorted by operation, by latching, by what works them and by paths; relays let small control big. Match ratings to the supply.

Hold the reed switch and its magnet, and take the final questions of the lesson: who decides when.
