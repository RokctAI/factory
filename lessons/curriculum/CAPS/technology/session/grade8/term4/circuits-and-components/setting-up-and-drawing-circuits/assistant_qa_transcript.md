### subtopic_1 — From Diagram to Bench: Building a Circuit Safely
**Assistant:** Why do we lay the components out on the bench in the same arrangement as the diagram? It does not change how the circuit works.

**Tutor:** It does not change how the circuit works, and that is exactly why it is worth doing: it changes how easily you can see whether you built what was drawn. A circuit's behaviour depends only on its connections, so a lamp could sit anywhere on the bench and the circuit would be the same. But a human checking a circuit against a diagram is comparing two pictures, and if the pictures have the same shape the comparison takes seconds and catches mistakes; if the bench is a jumble, the comparison needs the wire to be followed clip by clip, which is slow and error-prone, and most learners skip it and close the switch hoping. Laying the supply on the left, the switch along the top and the lamp on the right, as the diagram does, means that a lead running from the switch to the lamp on the diagram is a lead running from the switch to the lamp on the bench, and a missing or extra lead is obvious at a glance. It also makes troubleshooting faster, since a fault found on the diagram can be put a finger on in the circuit. Professional electronics takes this further: a printed circuit board is laid out from the circuit diagram, with components grouped as they are on the schematic, and a breadboard layout drawn before building the panic button in Week 7 will save you an hour of hunting for a misplaced wire. The habit also works in reverse when drawing a circuit you have been handed: if you tidy the bench circuit into a rectangle before drawing, the diagram almost draws itself. None of this is necessary for the electrons; all of it is necessary for the person.

### subtopic_2 — Circuits With a Range of Components: Series and Parallel Branches
**Assistant:** In the series circuit both lamps were dim, but in parallel they were bright and the battery ran down faster. Where does the extra energy come from?

**Tutor:** From the battery, which is working harder in the parallel case, and the two observations are the same fact seen from two sides. A battery supplies energy at a rate set by the voltage it holds across the circuit and the current it pushes through it; more current at the same voltage means more energy per second delivered and more chemical energy used up per second inside the cells. In series, the two lamps form one long path with twice the resistance of one lamp, so the battery pushes about half the current it would through a single lamp, and that current flows through both; each lamp has about half the voltage and a quarter of its normal power, so both are dim, and the battery is delivering about half the energy per second it would to one lamp, so it lasts roughly twice as long. In parallel, each lamp is its own path with the full battery voltage across it, so each draws its full normal current, the battery supplies the sum of the two, twice the current of a single lamp, both lamps are at full brightness, and the battery delivers twice the energy per second and runs down in about half the time. So nothing comes from nowhere: the brightness you see in the parallel circuit is paid for in chemical energy at exactly the rate the lamps consume it. The same accounting runs the national grid in Week 4: every extra kettle switched on in Johannesburg at seven in the evening is another parallel branch across the supply, drawing its full current, and somewhere a power station must burn coal or let water fall a little faster to meet it. Week 5 puts numbers to today's observation with Ohm's law, and you will find that the series and parallel rules for resistance predict exactly the brightness and battery life you saw.

### subtopic_3 — From Bench to Diagram: Drawing What You Built
**Assistant:** My circuit worked, then stopped after a minute. Nothing is loose. What happened?

**Tutor:** Something in the circuit changed with time, and the three likely culprits are the battery, a heated component and a slowly failing joint. The battery first: cells that are old or have been shorted before can hold enough voltage to light a lamp for a minute and then sag as the chemical reaction slows, especially if the circuit draws a heavy current such as a motor or two parallel lamps; test the battery on a known good single lamp and, if it is dim, replace the cells. Heat second: a lamp filament that was already thin from age can light and then burn through, which you see as a dark break inside the glass; a buzzer or LED run without its resistor can work briefly and then fail; and a motor that is stalled, held by hand or jammed, draws a very large current and heats until something gives, so check whether the motor shaft was free. A joint third: a crocodile clip gripping the very edge of a terminal can make contact when first placed and lose it as the lead settles, and a wire whose strands are mostly broken inside the insulation can conduct until a slight movement separates the last strands; wiggle each lead gently with the switch closed and see whether the circuit flickers, which locates the fault. There is a fourth possibility worth knowing for Week 7: a thermal fuse or a protective component in a kit module may open when it warms and close again when it cools, giving a circuit that works for a minute, stops, and works again later. Whichever it is, record what you observed, lit for about a minute, then out, no warmth, battery tests dim, and the fix, because this is exactly the kind of evaluation note the term's design task will ask for.
