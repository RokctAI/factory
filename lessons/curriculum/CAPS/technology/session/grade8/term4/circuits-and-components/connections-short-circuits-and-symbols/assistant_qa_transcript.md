### subtopic_1 — Correct Connections: Complete Paths and Polarity
**Assistant:** Why does the bulb go completely out when a lead is clipped across it, rather than just getting dimmer?

**Tutor:** Because the lead's resistance is so much smaller than the bulb's that almost no current is left for the bulb, and a filament needs a good fraction of its normal current before it glows at all. When two paths connect the same two points, the current divides between them in inverse proportion to their resistances: twice the resistance, half the current. A small torch bulb might have a resistance of a few ohms when hot; a short crocodile lead has a resistance of a few hundredths of an ohm. The ratio is hundreds to one, so for every hundred or more units of current through the lead, one unit goes through the bulb, and the bulb, now carrying perhaps one percent of its normal current, does not reach the temperature at which the filament glows visibly. Meanwhile the total current drawn from the cell has risen sharply, because the pair of paths together has far less resistance than the bulb alone had; that is why the lead warms and the cell runs down fast. If you replaced the lead with something of resistance comparable to the bulb, a second identical bulb for instance, the current would divide equally, both would glow, and each would be a little dimmer than one alone because the cell's voltage drops under the heavier load; that is the parallel case Week 5 will measure. The lesson for the bench is that "a bit of a short" does not exist: any conductor across a component steals nearly all of its current, and any conductor across the supply pulls the maximum the supply can give. Treat every bare wire as if it were already touching something.

### subtopic_2 — Short Circuits: What They Are and Why They Are Dangerous
**Assistant:** If a fuse protects the circuit, why do homes also need an earth-leakage unit? Is one not enough?

**Tutor:** They protect against different things, and a home needs both because the two kinds of fault harm different victims. A fuse or circuit breaker protects the wiring. It watches the current in the live wire and cuts the circuit when that current exceeds the cable's safe rating, which happens in a short circuit or when too many heavy appliances share one circuit; its job is to stop the cable overheating and starting a fire. It does nothing if the current is below its rating, and a current far below the rating of a 20 amp breaker can kill a person: a few hundredths of an amp through the chest is enough to stop a heart. So a person touching a live wire, or a faulty kettle whose metal body has become live, draws a current through their body to the ground that the breaker regards as perfectly normal, and the breaker stays closed. The earth-leakage unit, called a residual current device in many countries, protects the person. It compares the current going out along the live wire with the current coming back along the neutral; in a healthy circuit these are exactly equal, because every charge that leaves returns. If some current is leaking to earth through a person, a wet appliance or a damaged cable, the two no longer match, and the unit trips when the difference exceeds about 30 milliamps, within a few hundredths of a second, before the heart is affected. South African wiring regulations have required an earth-leakage unit on every new installation for decades, which is one reason the home electrocution rate fell. The two devices sit side by side on the distribution board and are tested differently: breakers by their trip under overload, the earth-leakage by its test button, which should be pressed monthly. An illegal connection bypasses both, which is the heart of next week's lesson.

### subtopic_3 — Accepted Component Symbols and Reading a Circuit Diagram
**Assistant:** Why not just draw a picture of the circuit with the real components? It would be clearer for beginners.

**Tutor:** A picture is clearer for the first circuit and worse for every one after it, which is why the symbol language was invented. A picture of a cell, a switch and a bulb is easy to follow, but a picture of the panic-button circuit with its latching relay, two LEDs, a buzzer, three resistors and a battery becomes a tangle of drawn wires in which it is impossible to see which terminal connects to which, and a picture of the inside of a radio is useless. Symbols strip each component down to its function, a lamp is any lamp, a resistor is any resistor, and let the lines show only the one thing that determines how a circuit behaves: what connects to what. That also makes a diagram independent of the physical layout; the same diagram describes a circuit built with crocodile clips on a bench, soldered on a board, or printed in a phone, which is why an electrician in Polokwane can read a diagram drawn in Korea. There are further gains. Standard symbols can be drawn quickly with a ruler, so a diagram can be sketched during a conversation or an exam; they can be labelled with values, 330 ohms, 9 volts, which a picture cannot show; and faults can be found by tracing the lines, because a short or a reversed LED is visible on paper before anything is built, which is how designers check a circuit before spending money on parts. There is a place for pictures: a wiring diagram, which shows the physical positions and colours of wires in a car or a house, and the breadboard layout you might draw when building the panic button, both of which are made from the circuit diagram, not instead of it. Learn the symbols now, with the real components beside you so the two connect in your mind, and the diagrams of Weeks 5 to 7 will read like sentences.
