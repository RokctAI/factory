# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers hydroelectric power stations including pumped storage, and wind-driven turbines. The anchor is the Ingula pumped storage scheme on the Drakensberg escarpment between KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State: two dams 470 metres apart in height, a tunnel between them, and four machines that pump at two in the morning and generate at seven in the evening.

## Subtopic: Hydroelectric Power: Falling Water Turns a Turbine

A hydroelectric station turns the gravitational potential energy of water into electricity. A dam across a river valley holds back a reservoir; water from the reservoir drops through a large pipe, the penstock, to a turbine at the bottom; the water's weight and speed spin the turbine runner, a wheel with curved blades or buckets, and the runner turns a generator exactly as a steam turbine does. The power available depends on two things: the head, which is the height the water falls, and the flow, which is how many cubic metres pass per second; double either and you double the power. The water leaves the turbine and continues down the river, so the fuel is not consumed, only lowered.

Advantages: no fuel cost and no smoke, carbon dioxide, ash or waste in operation; very long station life, a century for the civil works; extremely fast response, since opening a valve brings a hydro turbine from standstill to full power in a minute or two, so hydro is ideal for following demand and covering peaks; high efficiency, around ninety percent of the water's energy becoming electricity, far above any heat engine; and the dam may also store water for towns and farms and control floods. Disadvantages: a dam drowns a valley, displacing people and destroying farmland, forests and river ecosystems, and blocks fish and sediment; the reservoir evaporates large amounts of water in a hot climate; rotting vegetation in new reservoirs releases methane; a dam failure is catastrophic downstream; output depends on rainfall, so a drought can cut it, as has happened in Zambia and Zimbabwe at Kariba; and most importantly for South Africa, we do not have the rivers. Our rainfall is low and erratic and our rivers small, so our own hydro is a few small stations on the Orange River at Gariep and Vanderkloof, a few percent of supply, and the large hydro we use is imported from Cahora Bassa on the Zambezi in Mozambique, over a long transmission line that has been cut in the past by war and storms.

Hydro therefore matters to South Africa less as a source and more as a flexible tool, and that is where pumped storage comes in.

## Subtopic: Pumped Storage: The Grid's Giant Battery

A pumped storage scheme is a hydro station that works in both directions. It has two reservoirs, one high and one low, connected by tunnels and a machine hall holding reversible units that can act either as a turbine driving a generator or as a motor driving a pump. At night, when demand is low and the coal stations are running anyway because they cannot be switched off, the scheme buys their surplus electricity and uses it to pump water from the lower reservoir up to the upper one, storing energy as the height of the water. In the evening peak, when demand soars and electricity is scarce and expensive, the scheme opens the valves and the water falls back through the same machines, now turbines, generating at full power within minutes. The same water goes up and down every day; little is consumed except evaporation.

South Africa has three large schemes: Drakensberg at 1000 megawatts, Palmiet near Grabouw at 400 megawatts, and Ingula at 1332 megawatts, commissioned in 2017, with a combined capacity of nearly 2700 megawatts, roughly a third of the output of a Medupi. Ingula's upper dam, Bedford, sits on the escarpment in the Free State and its lower dam, Braamhoek, lies 470 metres below in KwaZulu-Natal; a tunnel system joins them to an underground machine hall with four 333 megawatt reversible units. During load shedding the pumped storage schemes have been among the grid's most valuable assets, because they can be held in reserve and released in seconds when a coal unit trips.

Advantages: it is the only mature technology that stores electricity on the scale of a power station, and for hours; it turns cheap, otherwise wasted night-time electricity into valuable peak electricity; it responds in seconds to minutes, faster than anything but a battery; it is clean in operation and lasts for generations; and as solar grows, it can soak up midday surplus and return it in the evening, which is exactly the gap solar leaves. Disadvantages: it is a net consumer of energy, because pumping uses about a quarter more than generating returns, so the round trip is about seventy-five to eighty percent efficient and it only makes sense when night power is cheap; it needs a very particular site, two places for reservoirs close together horizontally but hundreds of metres apart vertically, with water to fill them, and such sites are few; the dams and tunnels are enormously expensive and take a decade to build, as Ingula showed; and the reservoirs have the same environmental footprint as any dam, drowning wetland at Ingula that had to be offset with a conservation area.

## Subtopic: Wind Turbines and Weighing Water and Wind

Wind turbines turn the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity. The modern machine has three blades, each fifty to eighty metres long, on a hub at the top of a tower a hundred metres high; the wind pushes on the blades, which are shaped like aircraft wings so that the air flowing over them produces lift and turns the rotor slowly, ten to twenty revolutions a minute; a gearbox or a direct-drive generator in the nacelle behind the hub converts the turning into electricity; sensors turn the nacelle to face the wind and adjust the blade angle to catch more in light wind and less in storms; and the turbine shuts down altogether above a cut-out speed to protect itself. The power in wind rises with the cube of its speed, so doubling the wind gives eight times the power, which is why sites are chosen with great care and why towers are tall, where wind is faster and smoother.

South Africa's wind resource is excellent along the Eastern and Western Cape coasts and parts of the Northern Cape, and some three and a half thousand megawatts of wind farms have been built since 2013 under the renewable energy procurement programme, each farm of thirty to a hundred turbines. Advantages: free fuel and no emissions or water in operation; the cheapest new generation in South Africa alongside solar; quick to build, a farm in two years; the land between turbines remains usable for grazing and crops; and wind often blows in the evening and at night when solar does not, so the two complement each other. Disadvantages: the wind is variable, so a farm produces on average about a third of its rated capacity and sometimes nothing, and the grid needs other sources or storage for calm spells; turbines are large and visible and some people object to them in landscapes; they make a swishing noise close by; they kill some birds and bats, which siting and shutdown rules reduce; the best sites are far from the cities and need transmission lines; and the blades are hard to recycle when they wear out after twenty to twenty-five years.

Weigh water and wind together against thermal. Both have free fuel, no operating emissions and low running costs; both depend on nature, rain for hydro and breeze for wind; hydro and pumped storage are flexible and controllable, wind is not; wind can be built almost anywhere windy, hydro and pumped storage only at rare sites; dams have a heavy footprint on valleys and rivers, wind farms a light one on land. For South Africa the sensible combination is cheap wind and solar for bulk energy, pumped storage and in future batteries to shift it to the evening, and flexible gas and hydro imports to cover the gaps, with coal retiring as the mix matures.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: pumped storage described as a source of energy rather than a store, forgetting that it uses more than it returns. Two: "South Africa should just build hydro like Mozambique", forgetting our rivers. Three: wind's variability treated as a reason it cannot work at all, rather than a reason to pair it with storage and other sources. Four: giving only the clean-fuel advantages and no disadvantages.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a wind turbine works, its advantages and disadvantages, and how water and wind compare with each other and with thermal power.

## Subtopic: Water Downhill

Hydro: a dam holds a reservoir; water drops down a big pipe to a turbine at the bottom; the weight and speed of the water spin the runner, a wheel with curved buckets; the runner spins a generator like any station. Power depends on head, how far the water falls, and flow, how many cubic metres a second; double either, double the power. The water carries on down the river; the fuel is not used up, only lowered.

Good: no fuel, no smoke, no carbon, no waste; a century of life; a valve opens and it is at full power in a minute or two, perfect for peaks; about ninety percent efficient, far above any heat engine; and the dam stores water for towns and farms and holds back floods. Bad: a dam drowns a valley, moves people, kills farmland and river life, blocks fish and silt; a reservoir evaporates a lot in a hot country; rotting plants in new reservoirs release methane; a dam burst is a disaster; a drought cuts output, as at Kariba; and, for us, we do not have the rivers. Low, patchy rain and small rivers mean our own hydro is a few small stations on the Orange at Gariep and Vanderkloof, a few percent; the big hydro we use is imported from Cahora Bassa in Mozambique over a long line that war and storms have cut before.

So for South Africa hydro is less a source than a flexible tool, and that is pumped storage.

## Subtopic: Water Uphill, Then Down Again

Pumped storage is hydro that works both ways. Two reservoirs, one high, one low, joined by tunnels to a hall of machines that can be turbine-and-generator or motor-and-pump. At night, when demand is low and the coal stations are running anyway because they cannot stop, the scheme uses their cheap surplus to pump water up to the top dam, storing energy as height. In the evening peak, when power is short and dear, the valves open, the water falls back through the same machines, and it generates at full power in minutes. Same water, up and down, every day; only evaporation is lost.

We have three: Drakensberg, 1000 megawatts; Palmiet near Grabouw, 400; Ingula, 1332, finished in 2017; nearly 2700 together, about a third of a Medupi. Ingula's top dam is on the escarpment in the Free State and its bottom dam is 470 metres lower in KwaZulu-Natal, with four 333 megawatt reversible machines in an underground hall. In load shedding these schemes have been gold: held in reserve and released in seconds when a coal unit trips.

Good: the only grown-up way to store power-station amounts of electricity for hours; turns cheap wasted night power into valuable evening power; seconds to minutes to respond; clean to run, lasts generations; and as solar grows it can swallow midday surplus and give it back in the evening, exactly solar's gap. Bad: it uses more than it gives back, about a quarter more, so the round trip is seventy-five to eighty percent and it only pays when night power is cheap; it needs a rare site, two reservoir spots close together but hundreds of metres apart in height, with water; dams and tunnels cost a fortune and take a decade, as Ingula did; and the dams drown land, wetland at Ingula, offset with a conservation area.

## Subtopic: Air in Motion, and the Verdict

Wind: three blades fifty to eighty metres long on a hub up a hundred-metre tower; the blades are shaped like wings so the air gives lift and turns the rotor slowly, ten to twenty turns a minute; a gearbox or direct generator in the nacelle behind the hub makes electricity; sensors turn the head into the wind and tilt the blades to catch more in a breeze and less in a gale; above a cut-out speed it shuts down to save itself. Wind power goes with the cube of speed: double the wind, eight times the power, so sites and tall towers matter.

Our wind is excellent along the Eastern and Western Cape coasts and parts of the Northern Cape, and about 3500 megawatts of farms have gone up since 2013. Good: free fuel, no emissions, no water; cheapest new power with solar; a farm in two years; sheep graze between the towers; and wind often blows in the evening and at night when solar sleeps, so they partner well. Bad: the wind varies, so a farm averages about a third of its rating and sometimes nothing, and the grid needs other sources or storage for calm days; turbines are big and some people hate the look; a swish up close; some birds and bats killed, reduced by siting and shutdowns; the best sites are far from cities and need lines; and blades are hard to recycle after twenty-odd years.

Water and wind against thermal: both free fuel, no operating emissions, low running cost; both at nature's mercy, rain and breeze; hydro and pumped storage controllable, wind not; wind buildable anywhere windy, hydro only at rare sites; dams heavy on valleys, wind farms light on land. For us: cheap wind and solar for bulk energy, pumped storage and batteries to shift it to the evening, gas and hydro imports for gaps, coal retiring as the mix matures.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Hydro: head times flow, ninety percent efficient, fast, clean, but dams drown valleys and we lack rivers. Pumped storage: cheap night power up, dear evening power down, three schemes near 2700 megawatts, a store not a source. Wind: lift on wing-blades, cube of speed, cheap and clean but variable. Combine them.
