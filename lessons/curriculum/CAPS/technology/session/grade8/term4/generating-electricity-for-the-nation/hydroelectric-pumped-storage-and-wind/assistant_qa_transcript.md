### subtopic_1 — Hydroelectric Power: Falling Water Turns a Turbine
**Assistant:** If pumped storage uses more electricity than it gives back, how can it possibly be worth it?

**Tutor:** Because electricity is not worth the same amount at every hour, and pumped storage trades cheap electricity for expensive electricity. At two in the morning the country's demand is at its lowest, but the coal stations cannot be switched off and on like a kettle; they must keep running at a minimum output or risk hours of restarting, so there is surplus power that would otherwise be wasted, and its marginal value is close to zero. At seven in the evening demand is at its highest, every unit is running flat out, and the alternative to pumped storage is to start a diesel turbine at many times the cost or to shed load and switch off a suburb. If the scheme takes a hundred units at night and returns seventy-eight at the peak, it has lost twenty-two units of energy but converted something nearly worthless into something very valuable, and the grid as a whole is better off. The same logic applies to a battery in your phone, which also returns less than it stores and is still useful because you need the power at a time and place where the charger is not. As solar grows, the cheap hours move to midday instead of the small hours, and pumped storage becomes even more valuable because it is the only large store that can shift the solar surplus into the evening. The honest description is that pumped storage is an energy cost paid to buy flexibility, and flexibility is what a grid under strain most lacks.

### subtopic_2 — Pumped Storage: The Grid's Giant Battery
**Assistant:** Why can wind turbines not just be made to turn in all winds, so they always produce something?

**Tutor:** They do turn in most winds, and the limits at both ends are set by physics and by the machine's own survival. Below a cut-in speed of about three metres per second, a gentle breeze, there is too little energy in the air to overcome the friction and electrical losses of the drivetrain, so the turbine would consume more than it made; it simply waits. Between cut-in and rated speed, usually around twelve metres per second, the output climbs steeply, because the power in the wind rises with the cube of its speed, so a wind twice as fast carries eight times the energy. Above rated speed the generator is already producing all it is designed for, so the blades are pitched to spill some of the wind and hold output constant. Above a cut-out speed of about twenty-five metres per second, a strong gale, the loads on the blades, tower and foundations approach what they can bear, so the blades feather fully edge-on, a brake holds the rotor and the turbine rides out the storm; making a machine strong enough to generate in a gale would cost far more than the few extra hours of output would earn. The result is the roughly one-third average output that the lesson mentions, which is not a flaw but a design balance between cost and the wind that actually blows. Engineers improve it by building taller towers into steadier wind, by longer blades that catch more energy at low speed, and by choosing sites with consistent rather than merely strong wind.

### subtopic_3 — Wind Turbines and Weighing Water and Wind
**Assistant:** Mozambique and Zambia have huge hydro dams. Why does South Africa not build its own on the Orange or the Vaal?

**Tutor:** Because the resource is not there in the quantity needed, and what is there is already in use. Hydro power is head times flow, and South Africa's rivers have little of either: the Zambezi at Cahora Bassa carries an average of over two thousand cubic metres a second through a gorge hundreds of metres deep, while the Orange, our largest river, averages a few hundred cubic metres a second across a gentle landscape and shrinks to a trickle in dry years. We already have dams at the best sites, Gariep and Vanderkloof, with hydro stations of a few hundred megawatts each, and their water is committed first to irrigation and to towns, so the stations run when water is being released anyway rather than when the grid needs power. The country's rainfall is also among the most variable in the world, so a hydro station sized for a wet year would stand idle in a drought, and evaporation from a large shallow reservoir in the Karoo would waste water that farms and cities need more than the grid does. There is a further option being studied, importing more hydro from the Congo's planned Grand Inga scheme, but that depends on a transmission line across several countries and on a project that has been promised for decades. So South Africa uses its small hydro, imports what Cahora Bassa can send, builds pumped storage where the escarpment offers the height, and looks to sun and wind, which it has in abundance, for the bulk of its clean energy.
