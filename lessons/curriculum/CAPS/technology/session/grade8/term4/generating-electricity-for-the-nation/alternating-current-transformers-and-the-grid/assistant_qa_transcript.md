### subtopic_1 — Alternating Current: Why the Grid Uses AC, Not DC
**Assistant:** If AC swings back and forth, how does any energy actually get to my kettle? The electrons just go back and forth in the same place.

**Tutor:** Because energy is carried by the push, not by the electrons travelling the distance, and a push that reverses still does work on every stroke. Think of a saw: the blade moves back and forth and never arrives anywhere, yet it cuts through the log, because on every stroke force is applied over a distance. In a wire the electrons likewise shuffle a tiny distance one way and then the other, but on every half-cycle the voltage is driving them through the kettle's element against its resistance, and work is done, which appears as heat, on both the forward and the backward strokes; the heating does not care which way the current flows, only how much. This is why we can quote an equivalent steady voltage of 230 volts for a wave that actually peaks at 325: the 230 is the steady voltage that would heat the element exactly as much. Even in a DC circuit the electrons themselves drift at less than a millimetre a second, so your battery's electrons never reach the lamp either; what travels at nearly the speed of light is the electric field that sets all the electrons in the circuit moving at once. In both cases the wire is a shaft transmitting a push, not a pipe delivering a fluid, and AC is simply a shaft that reciprocates instead of rotating.

### subtopic_2 — Step-Up and Step-Down Transformers
**Assistant:** Why do the big transmission lines have three wires plus one thin one, not two like a torch circuit?

**Tutor:** Because the grid uses three-phase AC, three separate alternating supplies spaced evenly in time, and because the three can share a return path that carries almost nothing. A power station generator has three sets of coils arranged around the rotating magnet, so it produces three AC voltages, each reaching its peak a third of a cycle after the previous one. Each phase is carried on its own conductor, the three heavy wires or bundles you see. If the loads on the three phases are balanced, the three currents at any instant add up to zero, so no fourth heavy wire is needed to carry current back; the three phases return through each other. Three-phase is used because it delivers smooth, constant power rather than the pulsing power of a single phase, because it runs large motors in factories and mines efficiently, and because it carries more power for the same amount of metal than three separate single-phase lines would. The thin wire at the very top of the pylon is not a phase at all: it is an earthed shield wire that takes lightning strikes before they hit the live conductors and leads them safely down the steel to the ground, often with an optical fibre inside it carrying the grid's communications. Your house gets one of the three phases and a neutral, hence the familiar two wires plus earth, while a factory or a block of flats gets all three.

### subtopic_3 — The National Grid: From Power Station to Plug
**Assistant:** If the grid can move power from the Cape to cover a trip in Mpumalanga in seconds, why does load shedding happen at all?

**Tutor:** Because the grid can move power only if there is spare power to move, and load shedding happens when there is not. Covering a trip means that other generators across the country, which were running below their maximum, increase their output within seconds, their spinning mass and governors responding to the dip in frequency; the grid's wires carry that extra power to where it is needed. This works as long as there is enough reserve, enough generating capacity standing ready but not fully loaded. South Africa's problem has been that the coal fleet is old and breaks down so often that on many days the units actually available fall short of the demand at peak, even with pumped storage and diesel turbines running flat out. When that happens the operator has two choices: let frequency fall as overloaded generators slow, which within minutes would trip protection across the country and collapse the whole grid into a blackout that could take days or weeks to restart, or disconnect a planned share of demand in rotation so that what remains can be supplied at 50 hertz. Load shedding is that second choice. It is a symptom of a shortage of generation, not a failure of the wires, which is why building new generation and fixing the coal units is the cure and why the national grid, far from being the problem, is the thing that has kept the lights on everywhere else while the shortage is managed.
