### subtopic_1 — How a Thermal Power Station Works: Heat, Steam, Turbine, Generator
**Assistant:** If coal stations only turn forty percent of the energy into electricity, where does the other sixty percent go and why can we not catch it?

**Tutor:** Most of it goes into the air through the cooling towers as warm, moist air, and a smaller part goes up the stack as hot exhaust gas, and the reason we cannot catch it is the nature of heat engines. A turbine extracts work from steam by letting it expand from high pressure and temperature to low pressure and temperature; the bigger the drop, the more work. But the steam at the cold end is still steam, still carrying the heat it took to boil it, and to turn it back into water for the next cycle that heat has to be removed, which is what the condenser does, handing it to the cooling air or water. You cannot skip the step, because a turbine driving against steam that has nowhere to go would simply stop. Engineers have squeezed the fraction up over a century by using hotter steam, higher pressures, better blades and colder condensers, and by combined cycles in gas plants that run the hot exhaust through a second turbine, reaching nearly sixty percent there; but the theoretical ceiling set by the hot and cold temperatures always sits above and the real machine always below it. Some stations do use the leftover heat to warm buildings or greenhouses, which is called combined heat and power, but a station in rural Mpumalanga has few neighbours to warm. The honest accounting is that a thermal station is as much a heater of the sky as a maker of electricity, and that is one of the reasons a solar panel, which has no heat to throw away, is attractive.

### subtopic_2 — Coal, Gas and Nuclear: Three Ways to Make the Heat
**Assistant:** Why does the cooling tower plume look exactly like smoke if it is only water vapour?

**Tutor:** Because it is a cloud, and clouds and smoke both look white and billowing from a distance; the difference is in what they are made of and where they come from. Inside the cooling tower, warm water from the condenser is sprayed over a lattice and air drawn up through the tower evaporates some of it, carrying away heat; the air leaving the top is saturated with water vapour, and as it meets the cooler air above it condenses into tiny droplets, exactly as your breath does on a cold morning or as a kettle's steam does a few centimetres above the spout. That cloud of droplets is the plume, and it is pure water that will evaporate again a few hundred metres downwind, which is why the plume disappears rather than smearing across the sky. Smoke, by contrast, comes from the boiler, where combustion gases, ash particles, sulphur and nitrogen compounds are produced, and it leaves through the tall slender stacks, not the wide cooling towers, often after passing through filters that remove most of the ash; on a clear day the stack's output can be almost invisible, which is part of why people point at the wrong structure. The test is shape and position: the enormous flared concrete towers with a plume that fades are cooling towers; the thin stacks taller than the towers carry the smoke. Knowing the difference matters in an examination and in a debate, because the visible plume is harmless and the invisible stack gas is the problem.

### subtopic_3 — Solar Thermal and Weighing the Advantages and Disadvantages
**Assistant:** Is nuclear power actually safer than coal, or less safe? People say both.

**Tutor:** By the numbers, nuclear has caused far fewer deaths per unit of electricity than coal, and by the feel of it, nuclear frightens people far more, and both facts are real. Coal kills steadily and invisibly: mining accidents, lung disease in miners, and above all air pollution, which studies attribute to hundreds of thousands of premature deaths a year worldwide and thousands in South Africa, through heart and lung disease and childhood pneumonia. Nuclear's harms are concentrated in rare events: Chernobyl in 1986 killed dozens directly and is estimated to have caused some thousands of eventual cancer deaths; Fukushima in 2011 killed almost nobody by radiation but displaced over a hundred thousand people. Even counting those, deaths per unit of nuclear electricity are a small fraction of coal's, comparable with wind and solar. Why then the fear? Because the harm is sudden, invisible, involuntary and long-lasting, which are exactly the features that make humans most afraid, while coal's toll is spread thinly across millions of ordinary days and never makes the news. A balanced answer gives both: nuclear's measured record is good and its carbon record excellent, but its accidents are catastrophic when they happen, its waste must be guarded for thousands of years, and public trust is itself a cost that a democratic country must weigh. That is why the honest comparison is on the table of criteria rather than a single word like safe or unsafe.
