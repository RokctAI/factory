# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the advantages and disadvantages of thermal power stations fuelled by coal, gas, nuclear fuel and solar heat. The anchor is a Grade 8 class on the N11 in Mpumalanga watching the cooling towers and stacks of a coal station on the horizon, with a coal train a kilometre long passing, and the question: what is actually happening inside those buildings, and what does it cost?

## Subtopic: How a Thermal Power Station Works: Heat, Steam, Turbine, Generator

Every thermal power station is the same machine with a different fire. Heat boils water in a boiler into high-pressure steam; the steam rushes through a turbine, a shaft carrying rows of blades like a fan in reverse, and spins it at three thousand revolutions a minute; the turbine turns a generator, a huge magnet rotating inside coils of wire, and the moving magnetic field drives electrons in the coils, producing alternating current; the current leaves through transformers at hundreds of thousands of volts to the grid. The steam, having given up its energy, is cooled back into water in a condenser so that it can be boiled again, and the cooling is done either with water from a river or dam or, in dry South Africa, mostly with air in the huge concrete cooling towers whose white plumes are water vapour, not smoke; the smoke, with its ash and gases, leaves through the tall thin stacks.

Follow the energy. Chemical or nuclear or radiant energy becomes heat in the boiler; heat becomes the kinetic energy of fast steam; the steam's energy becomes the rotational kinetic energy of the turbine; the turbine's rotation becomes electrical energy in the generator. At each step some energy is lost, mostly as heat to the surroundings, and the largest loss is in the condenser, where the steam's remaining heat is thrown away so that the cycle can continue. A good coal station converts about thirty-five to forty percent of the fuel's energy into electricity; the rest warms the air above Mpumalanga. This is not incompetence but physics: a heat engine can never turn all its heat into work, and the fraction it can turn depends on how hot the steam is and how cold the condenser is.

The station runs continuously, because the boiler takes hours to heat and the turbine must be brought up to speed carefully, so thermal stations suit base load, the steady demand present all day and night, and they adjust output slowly. Each generating unit at a large station produces around 600 to 800 megawatts; Medupi and Kusile have six units each, about 4800 megawatts, enough for several million homes, and a station that size burns a trainload of coal every hour or two.

The questions for this section are with you now: the chain from heat to grid, the energy conversions and losses, and why thermal stations suit base load.

## Subtopic: Coal, Gas and Nuclear: Three Ways to Make the Heat

Now the three fires. Coal: South Africa has large, shallow, cheap coal in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, and about eighty percent of our electricity comes from burning it in a dozen large stations; the coal is crushed to powder and blown into the boiler flames. Advantages: a local, abundant fuel with a century of reserves and no need to import; stations and skills that already exist; steady reliable base load; and a mining and transport industry that employs tens of thousands. Disadvantages: coal is the dirtiest fuel, releasing carbon dioxide that drives climate change, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides that cause acid rain and lung disease, fine particles, mercury, and mountains of ash; the Highveld has some of the worst air quality in the world; coal stations use large amounts of water in a dry country; mining scars land and pollutes rivers with acid drainage; the fleet is ageing and unreliable, which is the main cause of load shedding; and new coal is now dearer than new wind or solar.

Gas: natural gas, mostly methane, is burned either in a boiler or, more often, directly in a gas turbine, a jet engine bolted to a generator, with the hot exhaust then boiling water for a steam turbine in a combined cycle. Advantages: about half the carbon dioxide of coal per unit, far less sulphur, ash and particulates; gas turbines start in minutes, so they can follow demand and back up solar and wind; and the plants are quick to build. Disadvantages: South Africa has little gas of its own and must import it by pipeline from Mozambique or as liquefied gas by ship, so the fuel is expensive and priced in dollars; methane leaks are themselves a powerful greenhouse gas; and it is still a fossil fuel. South Africa's existing gas turbines at Ankerlig and Gourikwa actually burn diesel, which is cleaner than coal but very expensive, so they run only at peaks.

Nuclear: at Koeberg, north of Cape Town, the heat comes from splitting uranium atoms in a reactor; the reactor heats water under pressure, which heats a second loop of water into steam, so that the steam never touches the fuel. Advantages: no carbon dioxide or smoke in operation; a tiny amount of fuel gives enormous energy, so a few truckloads of fuel a year run a 1800 megawatt station; very reliable base load; and a long life, with Koeberg now extended past forty years. Disadvantages: very high building cost and long construction times, which is why South Africa's plan for new reactors stalled; radioactive waste that must be stored safely for thousands of years; the small risk of a serious accident, as at Chernobyl and Fukushima, which is why safety systems and regulation are so demanding; large water use for cooling, met at Koeberg by the sea; and public distrust.

The questions for this section are with you now: how each fuel makes its heat, and the advantages and disadvantages of coal, gas and nuclear.

## Subtopic: Solar Thermal and Weighing the Advantages and Disadvantages

The fourth heat source is the sun itself, used not in photovoltaic cells but as heat. A concentrating solar thermal station uses fields of mirrors to focus sunlight: parabolic troughs focus it onto a pipe carrying oil, or thousands of flat heliostats track the sun and focus it onto a receiver at the top of a tower, where it heats molten salt to several hundred degrees. The hot oil or salt boils water into steam and the rest is an ordinary thermal station. South Africa has several such plants in the Northern Cape, including tower plants near Upington and trough plants near Pofadder. Advantages: free, clean fuel in one of the sunniest places on earth; and the molten salt can be stored in insulated tanks, so a tower plant can keep generating for several hours after sunset, into the evening peak, which photovoltaic panels without batteries cannot do. Disadvantages: higher cost per unit than photovoltaics, whose price has fallen faster; nothing in prolonged cloud; large land and some water use; and the technology is less mature and has had teething troubles.

Now weigh all four on the same criteria, as a table. Fuel cost and availability: coal cheap and local; gas imported and dollar-priced; uranium small quantities, partly imported; sun free. Reliability and flexibility: coal and nuclear steady base load but slow to adjust; gas fast and flexible; solar thermal daytime plus a few hours of storage. Air pollution and carbon: coal worst, gas about half, nuclear and solar thermal near zero in operation. Water: coal, nuclear and solar thermal all need cooling water, a serious issue inland; gas turbines less. Waste: coal ash in mountains; nuclear waste small but dangerous for millennia; solar minimal. Safety: coal kills steadily through air pollution and mining; nuclear rarely but spectacularly. Cost to build: coal and gas moderate, nuclear very high, solar thermal high but falling. Jobs: coal many today; the others fewer per unit.

The balanced judgement an examination wants: South Africa depends on coal and cannot switch off its stations overnight without collapsing supply, but the ageing fleet, the pollution and the cost of new coal all point to replacing it over time with a mix of cheap solar and wind, storage, flexible gas, possibly extended nuclear, and solar thermal for the evening, while supporting the coal communities whose jobs will go, which is what the phrase "just transition" means.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: the cooling tower's plume called smoke. Two: nuclear described as producing carbon dioxide, or coal as producing radioactive waste. Three: "nuclear is unsafe, full stop", ignoring coal's steady toll. Four: a one-sided answer when asked for advantages and disadvantages.

The questions for this section are with you now: solar thermal and its storage, the comparison table, and the balanced judgement.

## Subtopic: Boil Water, Spin a Wheel

Every thermal station is one machine with different fuel. Heat boils water into high-pressure steam. Steam blasts through a turbine, a shaft of blades like a fan run backwards, and spins it at three thousand turns a minute. The turbine spins a generator, a huge magnet turning inside coils of wire, and the moving magnet pushes electrons in the coils: alternating current. Transformers send it out at hundreds of thousands of volts. The used steam is cooled back to water to go round again, mostly by air in the big concrete cooling towers, whose white plumes are water vapour, not smoke. Smoke leaves by the tall thin stacks.

Energy trail: fuel to heat, heat to fast steam, steam to spinning turbine, spinning to electricity. Each step leaks heat, and the biggest leak is the condenser, where leftover heat is thrown away so the cycle can repeat. A good coal station turns about thirty-five to forty percent of the fuel's energy into electricity; the rest warms the Highveld sky. That is physics, not carelessness: no heat engine turns all its heat into work.

A boiler takes hours to heat up, so these stations run all the time and change output slowly: base load, the steady demand that is always there. One unit makes 600 to 800 megawatts; Medupi and Kusile have six each, about 4800 megawatts, millions of homes, a trainload of coal every hour or two.

## Subtopic: Three Fires

Coal: shallow, cheap, local, in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, about eighty percent of our power from a dozen big stations, crushed to powder and blown into the flames. Good: ours, plenty, no imports, stations and skills exist, steady base load, tens of thousands of jobs. Bad: the dirtiest fuel; carbon dioxide for climate change, sulphur and nitrogen gases for acid rain and lungs, fine particles, mercury, mountains of ash; the Highveld's air is among the worst in the world; lots of water in a dry country; mines scar land and acid drains into rivers; the fleet is old and breaks, which is why we have load shedding; and new coal now costs more than new wind or solar.

Gas: methane burned in a gas turbine, a jet engine on a generator, with its hot exhaust boiling water for a second turbine. Good: half coal's carbon dioxide, little sulphur or ash, starts in minutes so it follows demand and backs up solar and wind, quick to build. Bad: we have little gas, so we import by pipeline from Mozambique or by ship, in dollars, dear; leaking methane is a strong greenhouse gas; still a fossil fuel. Our turbines at Ankerlig and Gourikwa actually burn diesel, cleaner than coal but very expensive, so only at peaks.

Nuclear: Koeberg, near Cape Town, splits uranium atoms; the reactor heats pressurised water, which heats a second loop into steam, so steam never touches fuel. Good: no carbon dioxide or smoke, a few truckloads of fuel a year for 1800 megawatts, very reliable, long life. Bad: hugely expensive and slow to build, which stalled new reactors; waste that must be kept safe for thousands of years; a tiny chance of a huge accident, Chernobyl, Fukushima, so safety rules are strict; lots of cooling water, sea at Koeberg; and public distrust.

## Subtopic: Mirrors, and the Verdict

Fourth fire: the sun as heat. Mirrors focus sunlight, either troughs onto a pipe of oil or thousands of tracking mirrors onto a tower where molten salt reaches hundreds of degrees; the hot oil or salt boils water and the rest is an ordinary station. We have several in the Northern Cape, towers near Upington, troughs near Pofadder. Good: free clean fuel in one of the sunniest places on earth, and hot salt in insulated tanks keeps generating hours after sunset, into the evening peak, which panels without batteries cannot. Bad: dearer per unit than panels, nothing in long cloud, lots of land, some water, and a young technology with teething troubles.

Table them. Fuel: coal cheap and ours; gas imported; uranium little; sun free. Flexibility: coal and nuclear steady but slow; gas quick; solar thermal day plus a few hours. Air and carbon: coal worst, gas half, nuclear and solar near zero running. Water: coal, nuclear and solar thermal need cooling water. Waste: ash mountains; nuclear small but millennia; solar little. Safety: coal kills slowly through air and mines; nuclear rarely but hugely. Build cost: coal and gas middling, nuclear huge, solar thermal high but falling. Jobs: coal most, now.

Verdict: we depend on coal and cannot switch it off tonight, but old, dirty, expensive coal should be replaced over time with cheap solar and wind, storage, flexible gas, maybe nuclear, solar thermal for evenings, while looking after coal towns: a just transition.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Heat, steam, turbine, generator, transformer; forty percent efficient; base load. Coal: ours and dirty. Gas: cleaner, flexible, imported. Nuclear: clean running, costly, waste and risk. Solar thermal: free fuel with stored heat. Compare on the same criteria and conclude fairly.

Hold the tank of hot salt after sunset in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: mirrors, and the verdict.
