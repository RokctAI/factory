# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers alternating current, step-up and step-down transformers, and the national grid. The anchor is a 400 kilovolt transmission line marching on steel pylons across the Free State, with the question a learner asked on a school trip: if the house uses 230 volts, why is that line at 400 000 volts, and how does it get back down?

## Subtopic: Alternating Current: Why the Grid Uses AC, Not DC

Direct current, DC, is what a cell or a solar panel gives: the electrons drift steadily one way round the circuit, from negative to positive, and the voltage holds steady at 1.5 or 12 volts. Alternating current, AC, is what a generator in a power station gives and what comes out of the wall: the electrons move one way, slow, stop, move the other way, slow, stop, and repeat, so that the current and the voltage swing between positive and negative in a smooth wave. In South Africa the swing repeats fifty times a second, a frequency of 50 hertz, and the voltage at the socket swings between about plus and minus 325 volts, which has the same heating effect as a steady 230 volts, the figure we quote. A lamp on AC is actually flickering a hundred times a second, far too fast to see.

AC comes naturally from a rotating generator: as the magnet turns inside the coils, the field through each coil grows, shrinks, reverses and grows again once per revolution, so the voltage it induces swings with it. A generator turning at 3000 revolutions a minute produces 50 reversals a second. One could rectify this to DC, and early power systems did run on DC, but the grid chose AC for one overwhelming reason: AC voltage can be changed easily and efficiently by a transformer, and DC voltage, until recent power electronics, could not.

Why does changing voltage matter so much? Because of losses in the wires. Any wire has resistance, and current flowing through resistance produces heat, wasting power in proportion to the square of the current: double the current and you lose four times as much. The power delivered is voltage times current, so the same power can be sent as a large current at low voltage or a small current at high voltage, and the high-voltage, low-current version loses far less in the wires. Sending a city's power at 230 volts would need currents of millions of amps and copper cables metres thick, and would still lose most of the energy as heat before it arrived. Sending it at 400 000 volts cuts the current by a factor of nearly two thousand and the losses by that factor squared. But 400 000 volts would kill instantly and arc across a room, so it must come back down before it reaches a house. AC and the transformer make both steps possible.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between DC and AC, where AC comes from, and why high voltage cuts losses in long wires.

## Subtopic: Step-Up and Step-Down Transformers

A transformer is two coils of wire wound on a shared iron core, with no electrical connection between them. The primary coil is connected to the supply; the secondary coil is connected to the load. When alternating current flows in the primary, it creates a magnetic field in the iron that grows, collapses and reverses fifty times a second; that changing field passes through the secondary coil and induces an alternating voltage in it. The size of the induced voltage depends on the number of turns: if the secondary has twice as many turns as the primary, its voltage is twice the primary's; if it has a tenth as many, its voltage is a tenth. The turns ratio sets the voltage ratio.

A step-up transformer has more turns on the secondary than the primary and raises the voltage: at a power station the generator's 20 000 volts or so is stepped up to 275 000 or 400 000 volts or more for transmission. A step-down transformer has fewer turns on the secondary and lowers the voltage: at a substation near a town 400 000 volts is stepped down to 132 000 or 88 000, then to 11 000 for the streets, and finally in the green box on the pavement or the grey can on a pole to 230 volts for homes, with the small transformer in a phone charger taking that down again to five volts.

Energy is conserved, so a transformer does not give power for nothing: if the voltage goes up by a factor of twenty, the current comes down by the same factor, so that volts times amps is nearly the same on both sides, less a few percent lost as heat and hum in the core, which is why large transformers are filled with cooling oil and have fins. A transformer works only on AC: steady DC in the primary makes a steady field that induces nothing in the secondary, which is exactly why the grid is AC. The symbol is two coils facing each other with lines between them for the core.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a transformer induces voltage, how the turns ratio sets step-up or step-down, and why current falls as voltage rises.

## Subtopic: The National Grid: From Power Station to Plug

Now the whole journey. At a power station, Medupi or Koeberg or a wind farm's collector substation, generators produce AC at some thousands of volts; a step-up transformer in the station switchyard raises it to transmission voltage, in South Africa mostly 275 and 400 kilovolts, with a few lines at 765 kilovolts, the highest in Africa. Transmission lines carry this across the country on tall steel pylons, three conductors or bundles for the three phases of the supply plus a thin earth wire on top, with glass or ceramic insulators metres long holding the live conductors clear of the steel. These lines form the national grid, a network operated by the National Transmission Company, formerly part of Eskom, that links every station to every region so that a unit tripping in Mpumalanga is covered within seconds by machines spinning up in the Cape, and so that Cahora Bassa's hydro can reach Johannesburg.

At a transmission substation near each city, step-down transformers bring the voltage to 132 or 88 kilovolts for sub-transmission to the edges of towns; at distribution substations, operated by Eskom or by the municipality, it comes down to 11 or 22 kilovolts, which run along streets on wooden poles or underground; and at the last transformer, pole-mounted in rural areas or in a kiosk in suburbs, it comes down to 230 volts single-phase for houses, or 400 volts three-phase for factories and large buildings. From the kiosk a cable runs to the house's meter and distribution board with its main switch, earth-leakage unit and circuit breakers, and from there to the sockets.

The grid must be balanced at every instant: the power generated must equal the power used, and the signal of imbalance is frequency. If demand exceeds generation, the generators slow and the frequency drops below 50 hertz; if it falls too far, protective relays start disconnecting, and in the worst case the whole system can collapse into a blackout that takes days to restore, which is what load shedding is designed to prevent: switching off some areas deliberately so that the rest keeps 50 hertz. The system operator's control room watches frequency and flows across the country second by second.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the grid uses high voltage because it is "stronger", rather than to cut current and losses. Two: a transformer described as making more power, forgetting that current falls as voltage rises. Three: a transformer drawn working on a battery. Four: load shedding described as the grid running out of electricity like a tank, rather than as deliberate disconnection to hold frequency.

The questions for this section are with you now: the voltages along the journey, the parts of the national grid, and why frequency must be held at 50 hertz.

## Subtopic: Current That Swings

DC, from a cell or a panel: electrons drift one way, steadily, and the volts hold at 1.5 or 12. AC, from a generator and the wall: electrons go one way, stop, come back, stop, over and over, so current and volts swing in a smooth wave. Here it swings fifty times a second, 50 hertz, and the socket swings between about plus and minus 325 volts, which heats as much as a steady 230, the number we quote. A lamp on AC flickers a hundred times a second, too fast to see.

AC is natural from a spinning generator: as the magnet turns in the coils, the field grows, shrinks, flips, grows, once a turn, and the volts swing with it; 3000 turns a minute is 50 a second. You could turn it into DC, and early systems did, but the grid chose AC for one big reason: AC volts can be changed easily by a transformer, DC until recently could not.

Why change volts? Wire losses. Wires have resistance; current through resistance makes heat; the waste goes with the square of the current, double the amps, four times the loss. Power is volts times amps, so the same power can go as big amps at low volts or small amps at high volts, and the high-volt, low-amp way wastes far less. A city's power at 230 volts would need millions of amps and cables metres thick and would mostly turn to heat before arriving. At 400 000 volts the amps fall nearly two thousand times and the losses by that squared. But 400 000 volts kills on sight, so it must come back down before the house. AC plus transformers do both.

## Subtopic: Volts Up, Volts Down

A transformer: two coils of wire on one iron core, not touching electrically. Primary coil to the supply, secondary coil to the load. AC in the primary makes a magnetic field in the iron that grows, collapses and flips fifty times a second; that changing field runs through the secondary and makes an AC voltage in it. The volts depend on turns: twice the turns on the secondary, twice the volts; a tenth the turns, a tenth the volts. Turns ratio is volts ratio.

Step-up: more turns on the secondary, volts go up; at the station, the generator's 20 000 volts becomes 275 000 or 400 000 for the journey. Step-down: fewer turns on the secondary, volts go down; near a town, 400 000 becomes 132 000 or 88 000, then 11 000 for the streets, then the green box on the pavement or the grey can on the pole makes 230 for homes, and the little transformer in your phone charger makes 5.

No free power. Volts up twenty times, amps down twenty times, so volts times amps is about the same both sides, minus a few percent as heat and hum, which is why big transformers sit in oil with fins. Only AC works: steady DC makes a steady field that induces nothing, and that is exactly why the grid is AC. Symbol: two coils face to face with lines between for the core.

## Subtopic: The Wires Across the Country

The journey. Station generators make AC at thousands of volts; a step-up transformer in the switchyard lifts it to 275 or 400 kilovolts, a few lines at 765, Africa's highest. Transmission lines on tall steel pylons carry it across the country, three conductors for the three phases plus a thin earth wire on top, long glass or ceramic insulators keeping live wires off the steel. All these lines together are the national grid, run by the National Transmission Company, which used to be part of Eskom; it joins every station to every region, so a unit tripping in Mpumalanga is covered in seconds from the Cape, and Cahora Bassa's hydro reaches Johannesburg.

Near each city, a transmission substation steps down to 132 or 88 kilovolts; distribution substations, Eskom's or the municipality's, step down to 11 or 22 kilovolts along the streets on poles or underground; and the last transformer, on a pole in the country or in a kiosk in the suburbs, gives 230 volts to houses or 400 volts three-phase to factories. A cable runs to your meter and board, main switch, earth-leakage, breakers, then to the sockets.

The grid must balance every instant: power made equals power used, and frequency tells you. Too much demand, generators slow, frequency drops below 50 hertz; too far and protection starts cutting off, and in the worst case everything collapses into a blackout taking days to fix. Load shedding is deliberate switching-off of some areas so the rest stays at 50 hertz. The control room watches frequency second by second.

Pocket summary of the lesson. DC steady, AC swings at 50 hertz from a spinning generator. High volts, low amps, low losses; transformers do it, AC only, turns ratio sets volts, amps fall as volts rise. Station to 400 kilovolts, pylons, substations down to 132, 11, 230; the national grid links everything; frequency at 50 hertz is the balance, and load shedding protects it.
