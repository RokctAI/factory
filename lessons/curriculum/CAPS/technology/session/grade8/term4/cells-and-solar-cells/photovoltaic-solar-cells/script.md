# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers photovoltaic solar cells and their advantages and disadvantages. The anchor is a small solar panel on the roof of a spaza shop in Khayelitsha, charging a battery that runs the lights, a phone-charging station that earns the owner a few rand a phone, and a small fridge, with the hour-by-hour story of what happens when a cloud comes over and when the sun goes down.

## Subtopic: How a Photovoltaic Cell Turns Sunlight Into Electricity

A photovoltaic cell is a thin wafer of silicon, a semiconductor, made in two layers that have been treated with tiny amounts of other elements so that one layer has spare electrons and the other has spaces for electrons. Where the layers meet, a junction forms with an electric field across it. When light falls on the cell, photons of light with enough energy knock electrons loose in the silicon, and the field at the junction sweeps the freed electrons one way and the spaces the other, so that one face of the cell becomes negative and the other positive. Connect the faces through a circuit and the electrons flow round it as a current, doing work in a lamp or a motor, and return to the cell. No chemical is consumed; as long as light falls, the current flows, and when the light stops, so does the current, instantly and with no damage.

A single silicon cell gives about 0.5 to 0.6 volts regardless of its size, exactly as a chemical cell's voltage is fixed by its chemistry, and its current depends on its area and the brightness of the light: a larger cell in full sun gives more amps. That is why cells are connected in series into panels: a typical panel has sixty or seventy-two cells in series under a sheet of toughened glass, giving thirty to forty volts, and panels are themselves connected in series and parallel to reach the voltage and current a system needs. The output of a panel is rated in watts under standard full sunshine; a common rooftop panel is rated at about 400 watts, and because sunshine is rarely at the standard level all day, a panel delivers over a day perhaps four to six times its rating in watt-hours in South African conditions, which are among the best in the world.

The cell's efficiency, the fraction of the sunlight's energy it turns into electricity, is around twenty percent for good commercial silicon; the rest is reflected or becomes heat. A panel in direct sun therefore produces electricity and gets hot, and a hot panel actually produces slightly less, so panels are mounted with air behind them. Dust, shade and a wrong angle all cut output: a shadow from a chimney over just a few cells of a series string can cut the whole panel's output sharply, because the shaded cells block the current of the rest, the same weakest-cell problem as a torch with a flat cell.

The questions for this section are with you now: how light frees electrons in a silicon cell, why cells are connected in series into panels, and what affects a panel's output.

## Subtopic: Panels, Batteries and Inverters: Solar in Use

A solar cell alone powers a device only while the sun shines, so a system that must work at night or in cloud needs storage and conversion. The parts of a solar home system are the panel, a charge controller, a battery and, where mains-type appliances are to run, an inverter. The panel makes direct current when lit; the charge controller manages the flow into the battery so that it is not overcharged by day or drained too deeply by night, which would shorten its life; the battery, lead-acid or increasingly lithium, stores the energy as chemical energy; direct-current loads such as LED lights and phone chargers run from the battery through the controller; and an inverter turns the battery's twelve or twenty-four volts of direct current into 230 volts of alternating current for a television, a fridge or a laptop. Each part is sized to the others: a panel too small never fills the battery; a battery too small is empty by midnight; an inverter too small trips when the fridge starts.

The spaza shop system shows the day. At sunrise the panel begins to charge the battery; by mid-morning it is also running the fridge and the phone-charging board directly; a cloud passing over drops the panel output to a tenth for a few minutes, and the battery covers the gap without a flicker; by mid-afternoon the battery is full and the controller throttles the panel; at sunset the panel stops and the battery takes the lights and fridge through the night; by dawn the battery is perhaps half-empty, and the cycle begins again. A string of rainy days is the system's weakness: the battery is not refilled, the owner switches off the fridge to save the lights, and by the third day the lights go too. A bigger battery or a grid connection as back-up is the fix, and both cost.

At the other end of the scale, a solar farm in the Northern Cape has hundreds of thousands of panels on steel frames tracking the sun, feeding inverters that push alternating current into the national grid at high voltage; it needs no battery because the grid absorbs its output by day, though as solar grows, large batteries are being added so that afternoon power can be delivered into the evening peak. Rooftop solar on houses and businesses, which has grown enormously in response to load shedding, works the same way, often feeding surplus back into the grid where the municipality allows it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a solar home system and what each does, the day in the life of the spaza system, and how utility-scale and rooftop solar connect to the grid.

## Subtopic: Advantages and Disadvantages of Solar Cells

Now the advantages, stated fairly. The fuel is free and inexhaustible, and South Africa receives more than twice the sunshine of Germany, which nevertheless has more solar installed. There is no smoke, no carbon dioxide, no ash and no water used in generation, where a coal station emits all four. A panel has no moving parts, lasts twenty-five years with little maintenance beyond cleaning, and works anywhere, so a clinic or school a hundred kilometres from the grid can have light and refrigeration for vaccines. Systems scale from a lantern to a gigawatt, so solar reaches households the grid never will and adds to the grid where load shedding bites. Costs have fallen more than ninety percent in fifteen years, so new solar is now cheaper per unit than new coal. And it creates work in installation and maintenance spread across the country rather than at a few mines.

The disadvantages, stated just as fairly. It produces nothing at night, little in cloud and less in winter, when heating demand is highest, so it cannot by itself keep the lights on; storage is needed, and batteries are the most expensive and shortest-lived part of a system, lasting perhaps five to fifteen years against the panel's twenty-five. The up-front cost of a system that does more than light a room is thousands to tens of thousands of rand, out of reach of the households that most need it without subsidy. Making panels and batteries takes energy and mined materials, silicon, silver, lithium, cobalt, with their own environmental and labour costs, mostly in other countries, and old panels and batteries must be recycled, which South Africa is only beginning to do. Large farms occupy land. And a panel's output per square metre is modest, so cooking and heating, the hungriest household uses, remain expensive on solar.

A balanced judgement, the kind an examination rewards, says: solar is the cheapest and cleanest new generation South Africa can build, ideal for daytime supply and remote places, and already essential against load shedding; but it must be paired with storage, with other sources for night and winter, and with subsidy where the poorest households are concerned, and its materials and waste must be managed.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: "solar is free", forgetting panels, batteries and installation. Two: "solar does not work in South Africa because of load shedding", confusing the grid's problem with the panel's. Three: claiming solar has no environmental cost, forgetting mining and disposal. Four: stating only the advantages when the question asks for both.

The questions for this section are with you now: the advantages, the disadvantages, and the balanced judgement.

## Subtopic: Light In, Current Out

A solar cell is a thin slice of silicon in two layers, one with spare electrons, one with spaces for them, and where they meet there is an electric push across the join. Light knocks electrons loose; the push sweeps them to one face and the spaces to the other; so one face is minus and the other plus. Connect a lamp between the faces and current flows while the light shines. Nothing is used up. Light off, current off, no harm.

One cell gives about half a volt whatever its size, like a chemical cell's fixed volts; bigger cell, more amps, not more volts. So panels put sixty or seventy-two cells in series under glass for thirty to forty volts, and panels go in series and parallel for what the system needs. A panel is rated in watts in full sun; a common one is about 400 watts, and over a South African day it gives perhaps four to six times that in watt-hours, because the sun is not full all day.

About a fifth of the sunlight's energy becomes electricity; the rest bounces or becomes heat, and a hot panel gives slightly less, so panels are mounted with air behind. Dust, a wrong angle and shade all cut output, and a shadow on just a few cells can choke the whole series string, the same way one flat cell spoils a torch.

## Subtopic: From Cell to Rooftop

A panel alone works only while the sun is out, so a system for night needs storage. Parts: panel, charge controller, battery, inverter. Panel makes direct current by day. Controller stops the battery being overfilled by day or drained flat by night, which would kill it early. Battery stores the energy as chemicals. LED lights and phone chargers run straight off the battery. Inverter turns twelve or twenty-four volts direct current into 230 volts alternating for a fridge, television or laptop. Size them together: small panel, battery never full; small battery, empty by midnight; small inverter, trips when the fridge starts.

The spaza day: sunrise, panel starts charging; mid-morning, it also runs the fridge and phone board; a cloud cuts output to a tenth for a few minutes and the battery covers it, no flicker; afternoon, battery full, controller eases the panel off; sunset, battery takes over for the night; dawn, battery half-empty, start again. Three rainy days in a row: battery not refilled, fridge off to save the lights, then lights too. Fix: bigger battery or grid back-up, both cost money.

Big end: a Northern Cape solar farm, hundreds of thousands of panels following the sun, inverters pushing alternating current into the grid at high voltage, no battery needed because the grid takes it by day, though big batteries are now being added for the evening peak. Rooftop solar on houses, which exploded because of load shedding, works the same way and can feed surplus back where the municipality allows.

## Subtopic: The Case For and Against

For solar. Free fuel for ever, and we have more than twice Germany's sunshine. No smoke, no carbon dioxide, no ash, no water, where coal gives all four. No moving parts, twenty-five years with a wipe now and then, works anywhere, so a far clinic keeps vaccines cold. Scales from a lantern to a power station. Cost down over ninety percent in fifteen years, so new solar is cheaper than new coal. Jobs spread across the country.

Against solar. Nothing at night, little in cloud, less in winter when heaters are on, so it cannot do the job alone. Batteries are needed and they are the dearest, shortest-lived part, five to fifteen years against the panel's twenty-five. A system that does more than lights costs thousands to tens of thousands of rand, beyond the households that need it most. Panels and batteries need mined silicon, silver, lithium and cobalt, with environmental and labour costs mostly abroad, and old ones must be recycled, which we have barely started. Farms take land. And cooking and heating on solar stays expensive.

Fair verdict: cheapest, cleanest new power we can build, perfect by day and in remote places, already vital against load shedding; but it needs storage, other sources for night and winter, subsidy for the poorest, and care over materials and waste.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Light frees electrons across a silicon junction; half a volt a cell, so cells go in series into panels; shade, dust, heat and angle cut output. Systems add a controller, battery and inverter, and fail on rainy runs. Advantages: free, clean, durable, anywhere, cheap now. Disadvantages: night, winter, storage cost, up-front cost, materials, land. Judge both.
