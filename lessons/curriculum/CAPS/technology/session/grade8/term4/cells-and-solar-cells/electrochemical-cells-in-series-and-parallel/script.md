# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers electrochemical cells and the advantages and disadvantages of series and parallel batteries. The anchor is a tray of 1.5 volt cells, a multimeter, a 3 volt torch bulb and a small motor on a Grade 8 bench in Bloemfontein, with the question chalked up: how do you get 4.5 volts, and how do you make it last all night?

## Subtopic: How an Electrochemical Cell Makes Electricity

An electrochemical cell has three parts: two electrodes made of different materials and an electrolyte between them that conducts ions. In the common zinc-carbon or alkaline dry cell, the negative electrode is zinc, the positive electrode is a carbon rod surrounded by manganese dioxide, and the electrolyte is a paste of ammonium chloride or potassium hydroxide. Chemical reactions at the electrodes push electrons onto the zinc and pull them from the carbon, so a voltage appears between the terminals; when a circuit connects them, electrons flow through the lamp or motor from negative to positive while ions move through the paste inside, and the reactions continue, consuming the zinc and the manganese dioxide. The chemistry fixes the voltage: a zinc-carbon or alkaline cell gives about 1.5 volts, a lead-acid car cell about 2 volts, a lithium cell about 3.7 volts, and no amount of making the cell bigger changes that number; a bigger cell gives the same volts for longer.

Running flat is chemistry running out. As the reactants are consumed the voltage sags and the cell's internal resistance rises until it can no longer push useful current; a dry cell is then finished, because its reactions cannot be reversed, and it is a primary cell. A rechargeable or secondary cell, nickel-metal hydride, lead-acid or lithium-ion, uses reactions that can be driven backwards by pushing current in from a charger, restoring the reactants for hundreds or thousands of cycles. Capacity, how much charge the cell can deliver before it is flat, is measured in ampere-hours or milliampere-hours: a 2000 milliampere-hour cell can supply 200 milliamps for ten hours or 2000 milliamps for one hour, roughly. Voltage is the push; capacity is how long the push lasts.

Two practical facts. Polarity matters: the raised button is positive, the flat end negative, and every holder marks them, because a cell inserted backwards in a stack cancels its neighbour. And a cell is a store of chemicals that are mildly corrosive and, in lithium types, flammable, so flat cells go to a recycling point, not the bin, and a cell is never shorted or heated.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three parts of a cell, why its voltage is fixed by chemistry, and the difference between voltage and capacity.

## Subtopic: Cells in Series: More Voltage, Same Capacity

Now join cells in series: the positive of one touches the negative of the next, end to end, as in a torch tube. The voltages add, because each cell lifts the charge a further 1.5 volts, like steps on a staircase: two cells give 3 volts, three give 4.5, four give 6, and a 9 volt battery is six small cells in series inside one case. The current is the same through all of them, since there is only one path, and the capacity of the stack is the capacity of one cell: three 2000 milliampere-hour cells in series give 4.5 volts at 2000 milliampere-hours, not 6000, because every bit of charge that leaves the stack has passed through all three.

Advantages: higher voltage for devices that need it, a 3 volt bulb, a 6 volt motor, a 9 volt radio, from cheap standard cells; the arrangement is simple, a line of cells in a tube; and the current through each cell is only the device's current, so no cell is overworked. Disadvantages: the stack is only as good as its weakest cell, so one flat or old cell in a chain of fresh ones drags the whole voltage down and, when it is fully flat, can be driven backwards by the others and leak; a cell inserted the wrong way round subtracts its voltage instead of adding it, so four cells with one reversed give 3 volts not 6; and the running time is no longer than a single cell's, so a device that needs long life as well as high voltage must use bigger cells.

The bench demonstration makes it real: a 3 volt bulb on one cell glows dull orange, on two cells in series glows white, on three cells in series glows brilliant white and burns out in seconds, because voltage above the rating drives current above the filament's limit. Series adds push; the device must be rated for the push.

The questions for this section are with you now: how series connection adds voltage, why capacity does not add, and the advantages and disadvantages.

## Subtopic: Cells in Parallel: Same Voltage, More Capacity, and Choosing Between Them

Now join cells in parallel: all the positives together and all the negatives together, side by side. The voltage stays at 1.5 volts, because every cell is pushing from the same level to the same level; but the current the battery can supply divides between the cells, each one giving a share, so the capacity adds: three 2000 milliampere-hour cells in parallel give 1.5 volts at 6000 milliampere-hours, three times the running time of one cell, or three times the current for the same time.

Advantages: longer running time at the same voltage, which is what a solar home system's battery bank or a torch meant to last all night wants; higher available current without stressing any one cell; and if one cell fails open, the others carry on at the same voltage. Disadvantages: the cells must be identical in type and state of charge, because if one is fresher than another, current flows from the fresh cell into the tired one through the parallel connection, wasting energy as heat and sometimes damaging the weaker cell, even with nothing connected; a cell that fails short discharges all the others through itself; and the voltage cannot be raised, so a parallel bank of 1.5 volt cells can never light a 3 volt bulb properly.

Choosing is therefore a matter of asking two questions: what voltage does the device need, and for how long must it run? Series answers the first, parallel the second, and real batteries often combine both, a series-parallel bank, as in an electric car's pack of thousands of lithium cells arranged in series strings to reach several hundred volts and in parallel to reach the capacity for hundreds of kilometres, or a solar home system with two 12 volt lead-acid batteries in parallel to run the lights all night.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding the capacities of cells in series, or the voltages of cells in parallel. Two: mixing old and new cells in either arrangement. Three: putting a cell in backwards and wondering why the torch is dim. Four: three cells in series on a 3 volt bulb and a surprised face when the filament dies.

The questions for this section are with you now: how parallel connection adds capacity, its advantages and disadvantages, and how to choose an arrangement.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tray of cells, a meter, a 3 volt bulb and a question on the board — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Chemistry in a Can

A cell has three parts: two different electrodes, zinc and carbon in the usual dry cell, and a conducting paste between them. Chemical reactions pile electrons onto the zinc and pull them off the carbon, so there is a push, a voltage, between the ends. Connect a lamp and electrons flow through it while the chemicals inside keep reacting and getting used up.

The chemistry sets the volts. A dry cell is about 1.5 volts, a car battery cell 2 volts, a lithium cell 3.7 volts, and a bigger cell does not give more volts, it gives the same volts for longer. Flat means the chemicals are used up. A dry cell cannot be reversed, so it is finished: a primary cell. Rechargeables, nickel-metal hydride, lead-acid, lithium-ion, can be pushed backwards by a charger: secondary cells.

Capacity is how long the push lasts, in milliampere-hours: 2000 mAh gives 200 mA for ten hours, roughly. Volts are the push; capacity is the hours. Button end is plus, flat end is minus; backwards in a stack cancels its neighbour. Flat cells go to recycling, not the bin; never short or heat a cell.

## Subtopic: Stacking for Volts

Series: plus of one against minus of the next, in a line, like a torch tube. The volts add, each cell another step up the stairs: two cells 3 volts, three 4.5, four 6, and a 9 volt battery is six little cells in a row inside. The current is the same through all, one path, so the capacity is one cell's worth: three 2000 mAh cells in series are 4.5 volts at 2000 mAh, not 6000.

Good: high volts from cheap cells for a 3 volt bulb, a 6 volt motor, a 9 volt radio; simple; no cell overworked. Bad: the chain is as good as its weakest cell, one old cell drags the lot down and can get pushed backwards and leak; one cell in backwards subtracts, so four with one reversed is 3 volts not 6; and it lasts no longer than one cell, so for long life and high volts you need bigger cells.

On the bench: 3 volt bulb on one cell, dull orange; on two in series, white; on three in series, brilliant, then dead in seconds. Too many volts for the rating. Series adds push; the device must be built for the push.

Hold the staircase of cells in mind and try the questions for this part: stacking for volts.

## Subtopic: Side by Side for Hours

Parallel: all pluses together, all minuses together, side by side. Volts stay at 1.5, everyone pushing from the same level to the same level. But each cell gives a share of the current, so capacity adds: three 2000 mAh cells in parallel are 1.5 volts at 6000 mAh, three times the hours or three times the current.

Good: longer running at the same volts, what an all-night torch or a solar home battery bank wants; more current without straining one cell; one cell fails open, the rest carry on. Bad: cells must be identical and equally charged, or the fresh one pours current into the tired one and wastes it as heat, even with nothing connected; a cell that fails short drains all the others through itself; and you cannot raise the volts, so no parallel bank of 1.5 volt cells ever lights a 3 volt bulb properly.

Choose by two questions: what volts does the device need, how long must it run? Series for volts, parallel for hours, and often both: an electric car's pack has thousands of lithium cells in series strings for hundreds of volts and in parallel for hundreds of kilometres; a solar home system has two 12 volt batteries in parallel to last the night.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A cell is two electrodes and an electrolyte; chemistry fixes the volts; capacity is the hours; primary cells die, secondary recharge. Series adds volts, same capacity, weakest cell rules. Parallel adds capacity, same volts, cells must match. Ask volts and hours, then arrange.

Hold the three cells side by side in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: side by side for hours.
