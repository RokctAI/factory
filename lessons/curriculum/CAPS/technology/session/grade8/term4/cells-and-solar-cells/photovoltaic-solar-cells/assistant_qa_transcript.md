### subtopic_1 — How a Photovoltaic Cell Turns Sunlight Into Electricity
**Assistant:** If South Africa has the best sunshine, why does load shedding still happen? Why not just build solar everywhere?

**Tutor:** Because load shedding is a shortage of reliable supply at particular hours, and solar supplies most at the hours that are least short. The grid's tightest moment is the evening peak, roughly six to nine at night in winter, when people come home, cook, heat and switch on lights, and that is exactly when solar output is zero. Solar built in huge quantities would flood the grid with cheap power at midday, which helps, especially as daytime industrial demand is large and old coal units can be rested, but it would leave the evening gap untouched unless paired with storage, with hydro and pumped storage that can be released at the peak, with gas or other plants that can start quickly, or with demand shifted to the day. That is why the national plan pairs solar with wind, which often blows in the evening, with batteries, and with flexible generation, and why rooftop solar with a battery has done so much to relieve households: the battery charged by day covers the evening. There are also practical limits: connecting large farms needs new transmission lines from the Northern Cape to the cities, which take years to build, and procurement has been slower than the resource deserves. So the honest answer is that solar is a large part of the solution and is being built fast, but no single source ends load shedding alone; the mix and the wires matter as much as the panels.

### subtopic_2 — Panels, Batteries and Inverters: Solar in Use
**Assistant:** A solar panel has no moving parts and no fuel. How can it have an environmental cost at all?

**Tutor:** The cost sits at the two ends of its life rather than in the middle. Making a panel starts with quartz sand that is melted at high temperature to produce pure silicon, an energy-intensive process usually powered by coal or hydro in the countries that make most panels; the wafers are doped, printed with silver contacts, sealed under glass and framed in aluminium, each step using energy and materials with their own mining footprints. A panel therefore has an energy debt when it leaves the factory, which in sunny South Africa it repays in one to two years of generation, and then runs for another twenty-plus years in credit, so over its life it is enormously cleaner than coal, but not zero. The batteries that make solar useful at night are a bigger concern: lithium, cobalt and nickel mining has damaged landscapes and water in South America, central Africa and elsewhere, and in some places involved child labour, and lead-acid batteries contain lead that poisons if dumped. At the end of life, panels and batteries are electronic waste; panels can be recycled for glass, aluminium and silicon and batteries for metals, but the industry to do so at scale is young and in South Africa barely exists. A balanced report names these costs, compares them fairly against coal's continuous smoke, ash, water use and mining, and concludes that solar is far cleaner but not free, and that recycling and ethical sourcing are the work still to do.

### subtopic_3 — Advantages and Disadvantages of Solar Cells
**Assistant:** Why does a shadow on a few cells cut the whole panel's output so much? Surely the other cells still get sun.

**Tutor:** They do, but they are in series with the shaded ones, and in a series string every bit of current must pass through every cell. A sunlit cell can push a certain current; a shaded cell can push only a fraction of it, and when the sunlit cells try to drive their full current through it, it acts like a resistor, dropping voltage and heating up instead of generating. The string's current falls to roughly what the shaded cell can carry, so a shadow over three cells out of sixty can cost most of the panel's output, and the shaded cells can get hot enough to damage the panel, a fault called hot-spotting. This is the same physics as a torch with one flat cell in a stack of fresh ones: the flat cell limits the whole chain. Panel makers reduce the damage with bypass diodes, small one-way valves wired across groups of cells so that the current can skip a shaded group, which saves the panel from heat but still loses that group's share of the output. System designers avoid it by placing panels where no chimney, tree or neighbouring roof casts a shadow at any hour, by keeping panels clean, and in some systems by using micro-inverters or optimisers on each panel so that one shaded panel does not drag down the others in its string. Shade is the single biggest avoidable loss in rooftop solar, which is why a good installer visits the roof at different times of day before fitting anything.
