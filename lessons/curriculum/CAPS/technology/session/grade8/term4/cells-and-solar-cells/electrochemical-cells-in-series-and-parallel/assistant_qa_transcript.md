### subtopic_1 — How an Electrochemical Cell Makes Electricity
**Assistant:** If a bigger cell gives the same voltage, why do D cells exist? Why not just use AAA cells everywhere?

**Tutor:** Because capacity and current matter as much as voltage, and both scale with the size of the cell. A D cell and an AAA cell of the same chemistry both give 1.5 volts, but the D cell contains perhaps fifteen times as much zinc and manganese dioxide, so it stores perhaps fifteen times the charge, around 12000 milliampere-hours against 800 or so, and it has larger electrodes with more surface area and lower internal resistance, so it can deliver a heavier current without its voltage sagging. A big torch with a bright bulb, or a radio run for a month, or a toy with a motor drawing an amp, would flatten AAA cells in an hour or stall them with the voltage drooping under load; D cells run the same device for days. The small cells exist because many devices, remotes, clocks, small lights, draw tiny currents and need to be small and light more than they need to last. The engineering choice is always the same pair of questions this lesson asks: what voltage, which sets the number of cells in series, and what current for how long, which sets the size of each cell or the number in parallel.

### subtopic_2 — Cells in Series: More Voltage, Same Capacity
**Assistant:** Why does a fresh cell in parallel with a tired one push current into it, if nothing is connected?

**Tutor:** Because the two cells are connected to each other, and that is a circuit. A fresh 1.5 volt cell and a tired one that has sagged to 1.2 volts, joined plus to plus and minus to minus, form a loop in which the fresh cell's voltage exceeds the tired cell's by 0.3 volts. Any voltage difference in a closed loop drives current, so the fresh cell pushes current backwards through the tired one, trying to charge it. In a rechargeable cell some of that energy is actually stored, which is why parallel banks of matched rechargeables equalise themselves harmlessly over time. In a primary cell the reactions cannot run backwards usefully, so the energy is simply dissipated as heat in both cells, the fresh one wastes its charge, the tired one may heat, swell or leak, and both are worse off than before. The same effect, with a larger voltage difference, is why connecting a fresh lithium battery in parallel with a deeply discharged one can be dangerous. The rule that parallel cells must be of the same type, age and state of charge is not fussiness; it is the voltage difference in a loop that this whole term is about.

### subtopic_3 — Cells in Parallel: Same Voltage, More Capacity, and Choosing Between Them
**Assistant:** The torch has four cells in series and it is dim. The cells are all new. What is wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly one cell is in backwards, and the voltages tell you so. Four new 1.5 volt cells correctly in series give 6 volts. If one is reversed, it does not merely drop out; it subtracts, because its push is now against the other three: three forwards give 4.5, one backwards takes away 1.5, and the stack gives 3 volts, half what the bulb expects, so the bulb glows dimly instead of brightly. The reversed cell is also being driven backwards by the others, which for a primary cell means it heats and can leak, so the fault should be found quickly. Check the holder markings against the button ends, or measure the stack with a multimeter and look for 3 volts instead of 6. If all four are the right way round and the stack still reads low, the next suspects are a holder spring not touching a cell, so the chain is broken or resistive at one joint, or a bulb of the wrong rating. But new cells, right rating, dim light, is the classic signature of one cell reversed, and once you have seen a 3 volt reading on a four-cell stack you will never forget it.
