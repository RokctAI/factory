# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers revision and consolidation of the term's gear systems, mechanical advantage calculations and the headgear design project. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Rustenburg with the finished headgear models on a side table, a gear kit on each desk, and the board divided into three columns headed gears, MA and project.

## Subtopic: Gear Systems Revisited: Direction, Ratio, Idlers and Bevels

Gears come first because most of the paper's calculations depend on them. Two meshed spur gears counter-rotate, always; the driver is the gear the effort turns, the driven gear is the one it turns. Velocity ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth, and because meshing gears share a tooth size it is also driven diameter over driver diameter. The driven gear turns at the driver's speed divided by the velocity ratio: a 12-tooth driver at 1 200 revolutions per minute meshed with a 36-tooth gear gives a ratio of 3 and a driven speed of 400. More teeth on the driven gear, slower and stronger; fewer, faster and weaker.

An idler between two gears reverses direction a second time so the first and last gears turn the same way, and its size does not enter the ratio, which remains last gear's teeth over first gear's teeth; idlers are often made of a harder material because each tooth meets two meshing points. In a compound train, gears fixed on one shaft turn together, and the ratios of the meshing pairs multiply: 60 over 12 and 60 over 12 give 25, not 10. Each mesh reverses direction, so a two-stage train turns the output the same way as the input and a three-stage train reverses it. Bevel gears have teeth on cones and mesh with their apexes meeting to turn the axis through 90 degrees, with the same ratio rules; the hand drill's crank wheel driving a small pinion is a speed increaser, the car axle's pinion driving the crown wheel is a reducer.

Typical questions: a drawing of three gears with the driver's arrow, add the other arrows; a 20-tooth driver and a 50-tooth driven gear, find the velocity ratio and the driven speed from a given driver speed; a two-stage train with tooth counts, find the overall ratio and the output direction; name the gear that turns the axis through 90 degrees and give an example. The method is always the same: identify the driver, count teeth, divide driven by driver per mesh, multiply the meshes, flip direction at each mesh and ignore idlers for ratio.

The questions for this section are with you now: direction and ratio for a meshed pair, the idler's two properties, compound trains, and bevel gears.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Advantage Calculations for Levers and Gears

Mechanical advantage is one idea with two formulas, and the paper tests both. For any machine, mechanical advantage is load divided by effort, both in newtons, no unit. For a lever, it also equals effort arm divided by load arm, both measured from the fulcrum in the same unit, with no moments required. A crowbar with effort arm 1.5 metres and load arm 0.25 metres has mechanical advantage 6; to lift 900 newtons it needs an ideal effort of 150 newtons, and the measured effort is a little higher because of friction and the bar's weight. First-class levers, fulcrum between effort and load, can have mechanical advantage above or below one; second-class, load in the middle, always above one, wheelbarrow and bottle opener; third-class, effort in the middle, always below one, broom and forearm, trading force for speed and distance.

For gears, mechanical advantage equals the velocity ratio, driven teeth over driver teeth, or equivalently driven diameter over driver diameter; above one when a small gear drives a large one, below one when a large drives a small. In a compound train the mechanical advantages multiply like the velocity ratios. Nothing is free: a mechanical advantage of 4 means the driver turns four times for one turn of the driven gear and the effort moves four times as far as the load, and the actual advantage is below the ideal because of friction.

Typical questions: given load and effort, find mechanical advantage; given arm lengths, find mechanical advantage and then the effort for a given load; given a gear pair's teeth, state mechanical advantage and whether it multiplies force or speed; given a wheelbarrow with dimensions, find the effort to lift a stated load; explain why a bicycle in top gear has mechanical advantage below one. The traps are inverting the ratio, mixing units on the two arms, measuring arms from somewhere other than the fulcrum, and forgetting that the measured value is lower than the calculated one.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two formulas for mechanical advantage, the lever classes and their ranges, gear mechanical advantage from teeth, and the common traps.

## Subtopic: Structures With Mechanisms: Drawings, Systems and the Design Process

The project thread supplies the drawing and writing questions. Drawing gear systems: a gear is its pitch circle with its tooth count written beside it, a centre mark, and a curved arrow for direction; meshing circles touch at one point with centres apart by the sum of their pitch radii; the driver and driven gear are labelled and the velocity ratio written. A systems diagram has input, process and output boxes joined by arrows carrying the numbers: for a 4 to 1 reducer, 400 revolutions per minute weak and clockwise in, 12T driving 48T with ratio 4 and direction reversed in the middle, 100 revolutions per minute with four times the torque and anticlockwise out; for a speed increaser, the same gears with the 48T as input and 400 out. A working drawing uses first-angle projection, front view, plan below, side view to the right, thick lines for visible edges, dashed for hidden, chain for centres, dimensions in full-size millimetres each given once, and a parts list; the back-leg is dimensioned by its ends, not its slope.

The design process questions ask for definitions and applications: a design brief states the problem, users, setting and purpose without naming the solution; specifications are measurable requirements with reasons, constraints are limits; investigation uses sources that are recorded; two or more ideas are sketched and compared against the specifications; a working drawing precedes making; a budget lists materials, labour, plant, fees, contingency and tax with sources; evaluation tests each specification with numbers and records failures with causes; the presentation gives evidence. A question may describe a headgear and ask for the forces in its members, back-leg in compression against the rope's pull, legs in compression, bracing in tension and compression, sheave beam in bending, and why it is triangulated. Another may ask what acid mine drainage is, how Iron Age smelting worked or why women were excluded from mining and what has changed, in a short paragraph with one fact and one judgement.

Exam habits for this paper. Show the ratio working even when the answer is a single number, because method marks are awarded. Write units where they exist and none for ratios. Draw with instruments where the question says draw; circles with a compass or template. Flip direction at every mesh and say so. Read whether the question gives the small or the large gear as the driver before dividing. For written questions, one fact, one reason, one example earns most marks fastest. Leave nothing blank.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: ratio inverted, driver over driven. Two: stage ratios added, not multiplied. Three: arrows not alternating, or an idler counted in the ratio. Four: a brief that names the solution, or specifications without numbers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the gear drawing and systems diagram conventions, the working drawing rules, the design process definitions, and the exam habits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a gear kit on every desk and the board split into three — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Which Way, How Fast, How Hard

Gears first, because most of the sums rest on them. Two meshed gears turn opposite ways, always. The driver is the one you turn; the driven is the one it turns. Velocity ratio: driven teeth over driver teeth, or driven diameter over driver diameter, same thing. Driven speed is driver speed divided by the ratio: 12 teeth at 1 200 rpm driving 36 teeth gives ratio 3 and 400 rpm. More teeth on the driven gear, slower and stronger; fewer, faster and weaker.

An idler in the middle flips direction back so the ends match, and its size does not touch the ratio, which stays last teeth over first teeth. Idlers are made harder because each tooth meets two meshing points. Compound train: gears on one shaft turn together; multiply the pairs. 60 over 12 twice is 25, not 10. Every mesh flips direction: two stages, same way as the input; three, opposite. Bevel gears: teeth on cones, tips meeting, turning the axis 90 degrees, same ratio rules; hand drill speeds up, car axle slows down.

Exam shapes: here is the driver's arrow, add the rest; 20 teeth driving 50, find the ratio and speed; two stages, find the overall ratio and direction; which gear turns a corner, give an example. Always: find the driver, count teeth, divide driven by driver per mesh, multiply the meshes, flip at each mesh, ignore idlers for ratio.

Hold the 400 rpm in mind and try the questions for this part: which way, how fast, how hard.

## Subtopic: Two Divisions, Same Answer

Mechanical advantage: one idea, two formulas. Any machine: load over effort, newtons over newtons, no unit. A lever: effort arm over load arm, both from the fulcrum, same unit, no moments. Crowbar, 1.5 metres and 0.25 metres: MA 6; to lift 900 newtons you need 150 ideal, a bit more in real life for friction and the bar's weight. First class, fulcrum in the middle: MA above or below one. Second class, load in the middle: always above one, barrow, bottle opener. Third class, effort in the middle: always below one, broom, forearm, swapping force for speed.

Gears: MA equals the velocity ratio, driven over driver teeth or diameters. Small drives big, above one; big drives small, below one. Compound trains multiply. Nothing free: MA 4 means the driver turns four times for one, effort moves four times as far, and the real MA is below the ideal because of friction.

Exam shapes: load and effort given, find MA; arms given, find MA then effort for a load; a gear pair's teeth, state MA and whether it multiplies force or speed; a barrow with sizes, find the effort; why a bike in top gear has MA below one. Traps: ratio upside down, mixed units on the arms, arms not from the fulcrum, forgetting measured is less than calculated.

Hold the crowbar at 6 in mind and try this part's questions: two divisions, same answer.

## Subtopic: The Headgear in Four Documents

The project gives the drawing and writing questions. Gear drawing: pitch circle, tooth count beside it, centre cross, curved arrow; meshing circles kiss with centres apart by the sum of the radii; label driver and driven; write the ratio. Systems diagram: input, process, output boxes with numbers on the arrows; 4 to 1 reducer: 400 rpm weak clockwise in, 12T to 48T ratio 4 direction flipped, 100 rpm four times the torque anticlockwise out; speed increaser: same gears, 48T in, 400 out. Working drawing: first angle, front view, plan below, side right; thick visible, dashed hidden, chain centres; real millimetres, each once; parts list; back-leg by its ends.

A headgear question: back-leg squashed against the rope's pull, legs squashed, bracing pulled and squashed, sheave beam bends, triangles stop racking. A short paragraph on acid water, Iron Age smelting or women in mining: one fact, one judgement.

Habits. Show the ratio working even for a one-number answer; method marks. Units where they exist, none for ratios. Draw means instruments; compass for circles. Flip at every mesh and say so. Check which gear is the driver before dividing. Written answers: one fact, one reason, one example. No blanks.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Meshed gears counter-rotate; ratio is driven over driver, idlers change direction only, stages multiply, bevels turn corners. MA is load over effort or effort arm over load arm; for gears it equals the ratio; above one is force, below one is speed. Draw gears as circles with counts, systems as three boxes with numbers, working drawings in first angle; define the design process steps and name the forces in a headgear.

Hold the three columns on the board, and take the final questions of the lesson: the headgear in four documents.
