### subtopic_1 — Gear Systems Revisited: Direction, Ratio, Idlers and Bevels
**Assistant:** In the exam, how do I tell quickly whether a gear question is about a reducer or an increaser, and does it change the method?

**Tutor:** Look at which gear the input arrow or the word "driver" points to, and compare its teeth with the driven gear's; the method is identical and only the number comes out above or below one. If the driver has fewer teeth than the driven gear, the ratio driven over driver is above one, the output is slower and stronger, and it is a reducer, the winder case. If the driver has more teeth, the ratio is below one, the output is faster and weaker, and it is an increaser, the hand drill case. Learners lose marks here in two ways. They assume the small gear is always the driver because that is the case they practised most, so when a question drives the 48-tooth gear they still write 48 over 12 and get 4 instead of a quarter. Or they divide the right way but then apply it to speed the wrong way, multiplying the input speed by 4 for a reducer when the output speed should be the input divided by the ratio. Protect yourself with a sentence before the arithmetic: "the 48T is the driver, the 12T is driven, so the driven gear turns faster." Then the ratio, 12 over 48, a quarter, confirms the sentence, and the speed, input times 4, follows. Do the same check on direction: one mesh, opposite; an idler or a second stage, back to the same. Writing that sentence costs ten seconds and earns the method mark even if a later multiplication slips. A final check: in a reducer the mechanical advantage is above one and the output turns through a smaller angle; if your answer has a fast output with a large mechanical advantage, something is inverted, because gears never give both.

### subtopic_2 — Mechanical Advantage Calculations for Levers and Gears
**Assistant:** A question gives a lever with the effort arm and load arm in different units, say 120 centimetres and 0.4 metres. What do I do?

**Tutor:** Convert one so both are in the same unit before you divide, and write the conversion down so the marker sees it. Mechanical advantage from arms is a ratio of two lengths, and a ratio only means something when the lengths are measured the same way; 120 over 0.4 would give 300, an absurd answer for a lever and a sure sign of a units error. Convert the metres to centimetres, 0.4 metres is 40 centimetres, or the centimetres to metres, 120 centimetres is 1.2 metres; either way the ratio is 120 over 40 or 1.2 over 0.4, which is 3. Write "effort arm 1.2 m, load arm 0.4 m, MA = 1.2 / 0.4 = 3". The same discipline applies to forces: if the load is given in kilograms, as the mass of a sack, and the effort in newtons, convert the mass to a force first, about 10 newtons per kilogram at Grade 8 level, before dividing. Examiners set mixed units deliberately to test whether you check, and the mark scheme usually gives a mark for the conversion itself. Two related traps: arms must be measured from the fulcrum, so if a question gives the total length of the lever and the position of the load from one end, you must work out the two arms from the fulcrum before dividing; and the answer, being a ratio, carries no unit, so writing "MA = 3 m" loses a mark. A useful habit is to glance at your answer for sense: lever mechanical advantages in everyday examples run from about a third to about ten, and a gear pair's from a quarter to five or so; an answer of 300 or 0.003 is almost always a units slip, and spotting it saves the question.

### subtopic_3 — Structures With Mechanisms: Drawings, Systems and the Design Process
**Assistant:** For the written questions about the project, how much should I write, and how do I use the headgear as an example without just describing my model?

**Tutor:** Write to the marks, and use the headgear as a worked example of the general idea rather than as a story about your team. A two-mark question wants two points; a four-mark "describe" wants about four; a six-mark "explain" or "discuss" wants reasons and an example, in five or six sentences. The general idea comes first, in one sentence that would be true of any project, and the headgear follows as the example that shows you understand it. For "what is a design brief?", write: "A design brief states the problem to be solved, who it is for, where the solution must work and what it must achieve, without saying what the solution should be. For example, a brief for a mine headgear says that a shaft 800 metres deep needs a structure to carry a winding system that lifts workers and ore safely, with the winder 40 metres away, and does not say whether it should be an A-frame or a tower." For "why are structures triangulated?", write the principle, a triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of a side so braced panels do not rack, then the example, the headgear's faces are triangulated so the tower does not sway when the rope pulls it towards the winder. For "name the forces in a headgear", give each member and its force with a reason: back-leg in compression because it props the tower against the rope's horizontal pull; legs in compression from the sheave and rope loads; bracing in tension or compression depending on direction; sheave beam in bending because the sheave load acts across its span. What loses marks is a paragraph about how your team's model stalled at 300 grams with no general principle stated, or a principle stated with no example at all. The examiner is checking that you can move between the general and the particular, which is exactly what an engineer does when applying the design process to a new problem, and your term's project has given you one rich example to draw on for every question.
