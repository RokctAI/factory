### subtopic_1 — What a Tender Board Looks For
**Assistant:** Why would a board choose a bid with a plainer model over one with a more impressive model?

**Tutor:** Because the board is buying a 60 metre steel headgear, not a model, and the model is only evidence about the company that would build it. A board member looks at an impressive model and asks: does its drawing match it, so that I know the company can build what it draws; does its gear train do what the systems diagram says, so that I know the calculations are real; did it pass the specification tests with numbers, so that I know the company tests its work; and is the budget behind it complete, so that I know the company will not come back for more money. If the impressive model has no working drawing, or its ratio is unstated, or its evaluation table says "worked well", or its budget has no labour, then what the board has learned is that this company builds attractive things without the discipline that a mine shaft needs, and that is a risk it will not take with workers' lives and a very large sum of money. The plainer model, with a working drawing that matches it to the millimetre, a stated 64 to 1 that the board member can check by counting teeth, a table that says 300 grams in 16 seconds and 6 millimetres of sway, a failed topple test honestly recorded with the fix, a budget with sources and a crane, and answers that cite evidence, tells the board that this company understands the problem, designs to specifications, tests honestly and costs completely. Real tender boards make this choice constantly, and the public projects that go wrong in South Africa are often the ones where an impressive proposal beat a sound one. The rubric encodes this: the presentation and the model's appearance carry a modest share of the marks, while understanding, design, performance against specifications, method, budget and responsibility carry most. Build the best model you can, by all means, but make certain that every document behind it would stand on its own if the model were not in the room.

### subtopic_2 — Structuring the Bid Presentation: Research to Budget
**Assistant:** What if a board member asks something we think is wrong, for example that an idler changes the gear ratio?

**Tutor:** You answer with the evidence, politely, and without saying the board member is wrong, because you may be the one who has misunderstood the question and because the manner of your answer is itself being scored. Start by making sure you heard the question as intended: "I think you are asking whether our idler affects the 64 to 1 ratio; is that right?" Then give the fact and the reason: "it does not; the idler is driven and drives with the same teeth, so its tooth count cancels, and the overall ratio is the final gear's teeth over the first gear's teeth; we included the idler to turn the drum in the direction that winds the rope on from the correct side, and the gear drawing shows the arrows." Point to the drawing while you say it, so the board member can see the proof rather than take your word. If the board member presses, offer the demonstration: "if it would help, we can count the turns on the model." Two things are happening. The board member may genuinely have forgotten the idler rule, in which case a clear, evidenced answer is exactly what they want and you have just scored well on technical understanding and on question handling. Or the board member may be testing you, which real boards and examiners do, asking a question with a false premise to see whether you know your own design well enough to correct it calmly; a team that agrees with the false premise to be polite fails that test, and a team that argues fails it another way. The tone to aim for is the one you would use with a client who is paying you for your expertise: respectful, direct, and anchored to the drawing and the numbers. Afterwards, note the question in the portfolio with your answer; if on reflection the board member had a point you missed, say so in the evaluation, because that too is evidence of a company that learns.

### subtopic_3 — The Demonstration, the Questions and the Board's Decision
**Assistant:** How should we handle the budget questions, since none of us is sure the prices are right?

**Tutor:** You handle them by being clear about what you know, what you estimated and how, and what you would do to firm it up, which is precisely what a professional estimator says when questioned by a board. Your budget already distinguishes sourced rates from estimates if you built it as the budget session taught, and that distinction is your answer's backbone. For a sourced line, "steel at this rate per tonne, from a merchant's quote on this date, and a published index in the same range", you give the source and the date and stop; the board can check it, and the fact that you can name the source is what they are testing. For an estimated line, "the sheave assemblies we could not price from a supplier; we estimated them at this proportion of the structural cost based on a published project, and we marked the line as an estimate", you give the method and the mark; an honest estimate with a method is a legitimate budget line and the board knows it. For an omission the board finds, "you have no crane", you do not defend it; you say "that is correct, we omitted it; a mobile crane of the size needed hires at roughly this per day, and over six weeks of erection that adds about this to the plant section, which our contingency would not cover, so our total should be higher by that amount", and you have just turned a gap into evidence that you understand plant costs and contingency. For the question "are your prices right?", the answer is that they are the best available to a company at tender stage, that a real bid would be firmed up with supplier quotations before contract, and that your contingency exists for exactly this uncertainty. The member who compiled the budget answers, with the summary page in hand, because they know where every number lives. What you never do is invent a source, defend an obvious omission or let someone who did not build the budget guess. A board forgives uncertainty that is labelled; it does not forgive confidence that is false.
