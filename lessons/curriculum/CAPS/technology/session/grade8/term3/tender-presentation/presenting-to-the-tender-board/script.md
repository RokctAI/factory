# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers presenting research, plans, flow chart, model and budget to the tender board. The anchor is a Grade 8 classroom in Rustenburg set as a boardroom: a long table with three board members, the teacher, a parent who is a mine fitter and a Grade 11 learner from the engineering stream, a presentation table with a company's model, drawings and budget, a 1 metre shaft drop clamped to the table edge, and a stopwatch.

## Subtopic: What a Tender Board Looks For

A tender board is not a class audience, and the difference shapes the presentation. The board represents the client; its members are judged by whether the headgear they choose is built on time, within budget and safely, so they look for risk as much as for excellence. They have read the bid documents beforehand, or expect to see them in the room, and the presentation is their chance to test what the documents claim: to see the model lift, to probe the budget, to ask the engineers why they chose what they chose. They are often specialists, a mine fitter who knows what a real winder sounds like, a Grade 11 learner who has done the mechanical advantage calculations, a teacher with the rubric, and each asks from their own knowledge. And they are comparing: four companies present in one afternoon, against one set of criteria, and the board scores each as it goes.

The criteria are published in advance as a scoring rubric, and the company reads them before planning a word. Typical headings for this tender: understanding of the problem, shown by the brief and the investigation; technical design, shown by the gear calculations, the systems diagram and the working drawing; the model's performance against the specifications, shown by the demonstration and the evaluation table; the construction method, shown by the flow chart and the making record; the budget, for completeness and realism; social and environmental responsibility, shown by the Week 5 report and its link to the design; and the presentation itself, for clarity, teamwork and the handling of questions. Each heading carries marks, and the company allocates its time in proportion.

What earns a board's confidence is the same as in Term 2, sharpened: evidence over claims, numbers over adjectives, candour over polish. A board member who hears "our winder has a ratio of 64 to 1, lifts 300 grams in 16 seconds, and stalled at 400, which we traced to the drum and have costed a fix for" trusts the company more than one who hears "our winder is very strong". The board also watches how the team works together under pressure, because that is how it will work on site.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a board differs from a class audience, the rubric headings and how time follows them, and what earns confidence.

## Subtopic: Structuring the Bid Presentation: Research to Budget

The presentation follows the bid, which follows the design process, and the six documents are its spine: the investigation record, the brief with specifications, the drawings including the gear drawing, systems diagram and working drawing, the flow chart with the making record, the model with its evaluation table, and the budget with the impact report alongside. Seven minutes might divide so.

One minute: the company introduces itself and the problem, from the brief, with one or two facts from the investigation that show it understands real headgear, "we studied three South African headgear and borrowed the back-leg from the A-frame form because the rope's pull from a winder 40 metres away must be resisted"; the model is held up or pointed to so the board knows what is coming. One minute: the design, from the drawings: the working drawing on the board, the chosen form and why, the two ideas rejected in one sentence each, and the key specifications with their numbers. One minute: the mechanism, from the gear drawing and systems diagram: motor speed in, ratio and stages, drum speed and lift speed out, direction, mechanical advantage against the load, with the systems diagram pointed to and the gear train visible on the model. Two minutes: the demonstration, described below, with the evaluation table on the board and each test's result stated as the model performs it. One minute: the construction method and the budget: the flow chart in a sentence, what changed in the build and why, then the budget summary with the grand total, the three largest items, the contingency and the sources, and a sentence on what the impact report found and what the design does about it. Thirty seconds: conclusion and recommendations, then questions.

Each document is in the room, large enough to point to, and each team member presents the part they did and carries that document. Handovers are planned. The budget is presented by the member who compiled it and who knows where every rate came from, because the board will ask. Notes on cards; the documents and the model do the remembering. Rehearsed twice with a timer, once in front of another company who ask the hardest questions they can think of, and cut to seven minutes so that the demonstration is never squeezed.

## Subtopic: The Demonstration, the Questions and the Board's Decision

The demonstration is the moment the bid is tested, and it is planned so that it works. Before the session, the model is tested on the presentation table itself, with the shaft drop clamped where it will be, fresh batteries fitted, the string wound correctly, the cage loaded to the specification mass and weighed in front of the team, and the brake and bumper checked. A checklist taped to the base is ticked before the company stands up. During the two minutes, one member operates the switch and brake, one narrates against the evaluation table, one holds the stopwatch and a rule for sway, and the fourth watches the board and is ready to answer. The sequence: show the empty cage at the bottom; state the load and lift it; time it aloud, "300 grams, 1 metre, 15 seconds, within specification"; stop at mid-shaft and apply the brake, "holding"; continue to the top and show the bumper; measure the sway at the top with the 1 kilogram on the platform, "6 millimetres"; swing the cage, "no topple"; lower it under power. If a test fails on the day, the narrator says what was measured and what the evaluation table recorded earlier, and moves on; the board respects a team that reports a failure in the moment far more than one that fiddles with the model in silence.

Questions follow the rules of Term 2, with the board's edge in mind. A specialist's question is answered with the evidence and the method, not with a guess: "what torque does your motor deliver?" is answered "we did not measure torque directly; we measured that the bare motor lifts 20 grams on a 20 millimetre drum, which is how we sized the ratio". The budget draws the sharpest questions: "why is your steel rate lower than the other bids?" is answered with the source and the date; "where is your crane?" is answered by pointing to the plant line or by admitting the omission and saying what it would add. The impact report draws questions about what the company would actually do; the answer connects to the design. The member who did the work answers; a teammate may add one sentence; "we would need to check that" is a complete answer, and the question is written down for the portfolio.

The board decides by scoring each company against the rubric during and after the presentations, conferring briefly, and announcing the award with reasons: which bid met the specifications most completely, whose budget was most credible, who handled questions best, and what each company did well and could improve. The decision is itself a lesson, because the winning bid is rarely the one with the most impressive model. Companies record the board's feedback in their portfolios as the final evaluation of the term's project.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a demonstration run for the first time in front of the board. Two: a budget presented by someone who does not know where the rates came from. Three: the model shown without the drawings, so the board cannot check that what was built is what was drawn. Four: a team that argues with a board member's question instead of answering it with evidence.

Before the session: test the model on the actual presentation table, shaft clamped where it will be, fresh batteries, string wound right, cage loaded and weighed in front of the team, brake and bumper checked.
