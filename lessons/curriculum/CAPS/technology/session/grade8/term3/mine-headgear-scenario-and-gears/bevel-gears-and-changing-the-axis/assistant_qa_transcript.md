### subtopic_1 — Bevel Gears: Teeth on a Cone
**Assistant:** Why can't two ordinary spur gears be set at right angles to turn a corner?

**Tutor:** Because spur teeth are straight lines parallel to the axle, and two sets of parallel lines at right angles to each other can only touch at a point, not along a line. Try it with the kit: hold one spur gear flat and another upright so their rims meet; the corner of one tooth catches the corner of another, it may even turn jerkily for a moment, and then the teeth slide past, jam or snap, because all the force of the drive is being carried on a tiny corner of material instead of along the whole width of a tooth. Gear teeth are designed to roll against each other with their faces in contact along a line, which spreads the force and lets the teeth transfer motion smoothly; that is only possible when the two surfaces the teeth are cut on roll together without slipping. Two cylinders roll together only if their axes are parallel; two cones roll together if their apexes coincide, whatever the angle between their axes. That is the whole reason bevel gears are cones: a cone is the surface that rolls on another cone with a shared apex, so bevel teeth cut on those cones meet along a line and carry the force properly. The same idea produces other gear types for other geometries: helical gears, with teeth cut at an angle on a cylinder, can be crossed at 90 degrees for light loads; a worm and wheel uses a screw thread to drive a gear at right angles with a very large ratio; a rack and pinion turns rotation into straight-line motion. Each is the shape that makes the teeth meet along a line for the geometry in question, and the designer picks by angle, load and ratio.

### subtopic_2 — Turning the Axis Through 90 Degrees
**Assistant:** The hand drill gears up to make the bit spin fast, but the winder gears down. Can a bevel pair do either?

**Tutor:** Yes; the bevel pair only fixes the angle between the shafts, and the ratio is set, exactly as with spur gears, by which gear has more teeth and which one drives. In the hand drill the crank turns the large bevel wheel, perhaps 60 teeth, which drives a small pinion of perhaps 15 teeth on the chuck: velocity ratio 15 over 60, a quarter, so the chuck turns four times per crank turn with a quarter of the torque. That is a speed increaser, mechanical advantage below one, chosen because a drill bit cuts best spinning fast and the loads on a small bit are light. Swap the roles and drive the pinion, as a car does with the bevel pinion on the propeller shaft driving the large crown wheel on the axle: velocity ratio about 4, a speed reducer, mechanical advantage above one, because the wheels must turn slowly and push hard. In your model winder you would do what the car does: put the small bevel on the motor and the large one on the drum shaft, so that turning the corner also delivers a stage of reduction. If the kit's bevels are an equal pair, mitre gears, the corner is turned at 1 to 1 and all the reduction has to come from spur stages before or after. One caution: the bevel pair's teeth carry the full torque at the drum end, which is the highest in the train, so if the kit's bevels are small and plastic it is better to place them nearer the motor, where the torque is low and the speed high, and let the big reduction happen in robust spur gears at the drum. Turn the corner where the forces are small; multiply the force where the gears are strong.

### subtopic_3 — Bevel Gears in Machines and in the Headgear
**Assistant:** What is the 'end thrust' the lesson warned about, and how do we deal with it on a model?

**Tutor:** When two bevel gears push on each other's teeth, the force between the teeth acts at right angles to the sloping tooth face, and because that face lies on a cone, part of the force points along each shaft, trying to push the gear away from the other along its own axis. That axial component is the end thrust. On spur gears the tooth faces are parallel to the shaft, so the push between them is sideways only and the gears stay put; on bevels the push always has a component that tries to separate them, and the heavier the load, the stronger it is. If nothing resists it, the gears slide along their shafts, the cones no longer meet at the apex, the teeth mesh only at their tips, and under the weight of the cage the pair skips or jumps out of mesh entirely, which usually happens at the most embarrassing moment of a demonstration. Real gearboxes take the thrust with bearings designed for axial load, tapered roller bearings, and set the gears' position with shims. On a model the fixes are simple. Fix each bevel gear firmly to its shaft so it cannot slide, with a grub screw, a pin, or glue if the kit allows. Then stop the shafts themselves from moving along their length: a collar, a bead, a wrap of tape or a drop of glue on the shaft on each side of its bearing hole, so the shaft can turn but not slide. Mount the bearings, the holes the shafts run in, in a rigid part of the frame so they cannot flex apart under load, with the two holes placed so the shafts cross at exactly 90 degrees and the apexes meet; test the mesh by hand before fitting the motor. Finally, load-test the pair with the full model load and a little more before the presentation, and look for the gears creeping apart. If they do, the collars are the first thing to check.
