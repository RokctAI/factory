### subtopic_1 — The Scenario: A Tender for a Mine Shaft Headgear
**Assistant:** Why does the shaft need a tall tower at all? Could the winder not just sit at the top of the shaft and lower the rope straight down?

**Tutor:** The tower exists because the winder cannot sit directly over the shaft, and the sheave wheels are what let the rope change direction from the winder to the shaft. A winding drum with its motor and gearbox is a large, heavy machine that needs a foundation, maintenance access and a building; placing it directly over an open shaft would block the shaft collar where cages are loaded and unloaded, put the heaviest equipment over the most dangerous hole, and make the drum impossible to reach. So the winder is placed on the ground some distance away, and the rope runs horizontally from the drum to the headgear, over the sheave wheel at the top, and vertically down the shaft. The height of the tower is set by the conveyance: when a skip reaches the surface it must rise above the loading level so that it can tip its ore into a bin or the cage can be stopped at the right landing, and above that there must be room for the rope to over-wind a little without pulling the conveyance into the sheave if the winder fails to stop; the law requires a safe over-wind distance. Add the sheave wheel itself, several metres across, and a modern headgear is commonly 40 to 90 metres tall. The tall back-leg sloping towards the winder house takes the horizontal pull of the rope, which is why most steel headgear are A-shaped in side view, with the sheave at the apex and the back-leg pointing at the drum. Some deep modern shafts do put the winder at the top, inside a concrete tower, using a friction winder where the rope passes over a driven wheel rather than winding onto a drum; the tower is then even taller and solid, and the problem of access is solved with lifts and heavy cranes.

### subtopic_2 — What a Headgear Is and What It Must Do
**Assistant:** What does the tender board actually judge? Is it just the best model?

**Tutor:** The model is one piece of evidence, and in real tenders it is often the least important. A tender board compares bids against the criteria it published, and those usually fall into four groups. Technical: does the design meet the specifications, will it carry the loads safely, is the winding system correctly specified, and is the design shown clearly in drawings; this is where the gear calculations, the working drawing and the model demonstrate competence. Financial: is the budget realistic, complete and honest; a bid that is far cheaper than the others is suspected of having left something out, and a bid with no labour costs is rejected. Capability and delivery: can this company actually build it, shown by its plan of construction steps, its list of materials and tools, and its understanding of the real headgear it investigated. And responsibility: has the company understood the impact of the project on people and the environment, which is why the Week 5 report on mining's impact, indigenous knowledge or bias is part of the bid, and why real mining tenders include environmental and social commitments. Boards also judge the presentation itself: whether the company answers questions with evidence, admits what it does not know, and keeps to time. In class the teacher will give the criteria as a rubric before Week 9, and the rubric follows the design process steps you already know. A team with a modest model, correct calculations, a complete budget and honest answers will usually beat a team with a spectacular model and nothing behind it, which is exactly what happens in real tenders and is the point of the scenario.

### subtopic_3 — The PAT Ahead: Gears, Structure, Tender
**Assistant:** Why platinum, and why does the scenario care where the reef is?

**Tutor:** Because the scenario is built on real South African geography and industry, and the details shape the design. The Bushveld Complex north of Pretoria and around Rustenburg holds about three quarters of the world's known platinum-group metals, in two thin layers of rock, the Merensky Reef and the UG2 chromitite, that dip gently into the earth. Near the outcrop they are mined from shallow shafts and declines; further from it, the reef lies hundreds of metres down and vertical shafts with headgear are needed, which is why the notice sets the reef at 800 metres. Platinum ore is dense and the reef is narrow, so the mines hoist large tonnages of rock relative to the metal they recover, and the skips and winders are sized for that; the number of workers underground is also high, because narrow-reef mining is still largely done by people with drills, so the cages and their safety systems matter as much as the skips. Knowing the setting also feeds the Week 5 investigation: platinum mining has created employment and towns around Rustenburg and Mogalakwena, and also dust, water use, disputes over land and the strikes of 2012 and 2014 that marked the industry; the tender board will expect a company that knows where it is working. If your school is in a gold, coal or iron area, the teacher may adapt the scenario to the local mine, and the headgear design barely changes, but the depth, the tonnage and the impact questions do. A bid that says "we are designing for an 800 metre shaft on the UG2 reef near Rustenburg, hoisting a skip of 12 tonnes and a cage of 80 workers" is already ahead of one that says "a mine".
