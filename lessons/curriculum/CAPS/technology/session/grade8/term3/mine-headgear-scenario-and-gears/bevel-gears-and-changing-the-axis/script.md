# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers bevel gears that transfer the axis of rotation through 90 degrees. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Rustenburg passing round an old hand drill, the kind with a side crank, with its gear cover removed to show the large crank wheel meshing at right angles with a small bevel pinion on the chuck shaft.

## Subtopic: Bevel Gears: Teeth on a Cone

Spur gears have teeth cut parallel to the axle on a cylinder, and they mesh only when their shafts are parallel. A bevel gear has its teeth cut on the surface of a cone, so that the teeth converge towards the cone's apex. When two bevel gears are mounted so that the apexes of their cones meet at a single point, their teeth mesh along a line and rotation passes from one shaft to the other through the angle between the shafts. Most bevel gears are cut for 90 degrees, so that a horizontal shaft drives a vertical one, but any angle can be made; a pair cut for 90 degrees with equal numbers of teeth is called a mitre gear pair, and it changes direction without changing speed.

The teeth may be straight, running directly towards the apex, or spiral, curving across the cone face, which makes the gears quieter and smoother because more than one pair of teeth is in contact at any moment; a car's rear axle uses a spiral bevel of this kind, and so do large winder gearboxes. Straight bevel gears are simpler to make and are what a model kit provides.

The ratio rules are the same as for spur gears, because the one-for-one tooth rule holds at the meshing line. Velocity ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth; a 12-tooth bevel pinion driving a 36-tooth bevel wheel turns the wheel at a third of the pinion's speed with three times the torque, so the pair both turns the corner and gears down. Mechanical advantage equals the velocity ratio, above one when the small gear drives the large. Teeth remain proportional to diameter at the large end of the cone, so diameters give the same ratio. The direction of rotation is not simply clockwise or anticlockwise, because the shafts are not parallel; the usual way to describe it is to say which way the driven shaft turns when viewed from its free end, and a drawing shows it with a curved arrow on each shaft.

The questions for this section are with you now: how bevel teeth differ from spur teeth, how two bevels are mounted to mesh, and the ratio of a 12-tooth pinion driving a 36-tooth wheel.

## Subtopic: Turning the Axis Through 90 Degrees

Changing the axis of rotation is one of the most common problems in mechanisms, because motors and engines are shaped one way and the things they drive are shaped another. A motor's shaft comes straight out of its end; a drum that lifts a cage sits across the frame; a drill's chuck points forward while the hand that cranks it moves most naturally in a vertical circle beside it. Bevel gears solve all three in one component.

The hand drill is the clearest teaching example and sits in many homes and workshops. The crank handle turns a large bevel wheel on a horizontal axis across the body of the drill; its teeth mesh with a small bevel pinion on the chuck shaft, which points forward at right angles. One turn of the crank turns the chuck several times, a velocity ratio below one, so the drill bit spins fast, which a drill wants, with reduced torque, which a small drill can afford. An egg beater uses the same arrangement with two pinions on either side of the crank wheel so that the two beaters counter-rotate. A car's differential uses a bevel pinion on the propeller shaft running along the car to drive a large crown wheel on the axle running across it, turning the drive through 90 degrees and gearing down at the same time; inside the crown wheel, a set of small bevel gears lets the two wheels turn at different speeds on a corner.

Other everyday cases: the right-angle head of an angle grinder, the gearbox at the top of a windmill or wind pump that turns the horizontal shaft from the vanes into the vertical pump rod, the mechanism of a hand-cranked coffee grinder, and the gear that turns a rotating sprinkler. In each, the designer has a source of rotation on one axis and a need on another, and a bevel pair bridges them in a compact, non-slipping way. Alternatives exist, a worm and wheel, a flexible shaft, a belt twisted through 90 degrees, but the bevel pair is the standard answer where force and precision matter.

The questions for this section are with you now: why changing the axis is a common problem, how the hand drill uses bevels, and how the car's differential turns the drive through 90 degrees.

## Subtopic: Bevel Gears in Machines and in the Headgear

In the mine winder the drum turns on a horizontal axis across the winder house, and the motor that drives it is usually mounted with its shaft parallel to the drum's, so the main reduction is by spur or helical gears on parallel shafts, not bevels. Bevel gears appear elsewhere in the headgear and its machinery: in the right-angle drives of the conveyor and skip-tipping gear at the top of the headgear, in the drives for the shaft signalling and depth-indicator mechanisms that must show the winder driver where the cage is, and in older winders whose steam engines or motors sat at right angles to the drum. In the sheave wheels there are no gears at all; the sheave is a free-running pulley.

In the model headgear a bevel pair can solve a real frame problem. The drum must sit across the top or at the base of the model with its axis horizontal; the motor or hand crank may not fit beside it in the same orientation, especially on a narrow frame, but may fit end-on or vertically. A bevel pair at the drum shaft lets the motor stand where there is room, and if the bevel wheel on the drum is larger than the pinion on the motor, the pair also contributes a stage of reduction towards the mechanical advantage the lift needs. The pair must be mounted carefully: the two cone apexes must meet, which in practice means the shafts must cross at exactly 90 degrees at the right distance, and bevel gears push apart along their axes when loaded, so each shaft needs a stop or collar so that the gears do not walk out of mesh under the weight of the cage. A model whose bevels disengage when the load comes on is the classic failure to design out.

Drawing a bevel pair for the working drawing uses the conventions of the graphics session: each gear is shown as a trapezium in side view with its pitch cone as a line, and the pair is drawn with the apexes meeting; the tooth counts are written beside them, and the ratio and direction arrows are given. On a systems diagram the bevel pair is a process block labelled "bevel pair 1:3, axis turned 90 degrees".

The error museum, four exhibits. One: trying to mesh two spur gears at right angles, which touches only at a corner and strips teeth. Two: mounting bevels with the apexes not meeting, so they bind or skip. Three: forgetting the end thrust, so the gears walk apart under load. Four: assuming bevel gears cannot change the ratio; a small bevel driving a large one gears down just as spur gears do.

The questions for this section are with you now: where bevels appear in winding machinery, how a bevel pair solves a model frame problem, and the two mounting requirements that make bevels work.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an old hand drill with its cover off — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Gears Shaped Like Cones

Spur gears have straight teeth on a cylinder and only work with parallel shafts. Bevel gears have their teeth cut on a cone, pointing towards the tip. Put two cones tip to tip and the teeth mesh along a line, so rotation goes from one shaft to another at an angle. Nearly always the angle is 90 degrees: a flat shaft drives an upright one. Equal bevels at 90 degrees are mitre gears and only change direction.

Teeth can be straight or curved across the cone; curved is quieter and smoother because more teeth touch at once, which is what a car axle and a big winder gearbox use. Model kits give you straight ones.

All the ratio rules still hold. Teeth pass one for one. Velocity ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth: a 12-tooth bevel driving a 36-tooth bevel gives a third of the speed and three times the force. So a bevel pair can turn the corner and gear down in one go. Direction is described by looking along each shaft from its free end, with a curved arrow on a drawing.

## Subtopic: Round the Corner

Motors point one way; the things they drive sit another way. Turning the axis is one of the commonest jobs in a machine, and bevel gears do it.

The hand drill: the crank turns a big bevel wheel across the body; it meshes with a small bevel pinion on the chuck shaft pointing forward. One turn of the crank, several turns of the bit; fast and weak, which is what a small drill wants. An egg beater is the same with two pinions, so the beaters turn opposite ways. A car: the shaft along the car carries a bevel pinion that drives a big crown wheel on the axle across the car, 90 degrees and a gear-down together; inside it, little bevels let the two wheels turn at different speeds round a bend.

Also: the head of an angle grinder, the gearbox at the top of a wind pump turning the vanes' flat shaft into the up-and-down pump rod, a hand coffee grinder, a rotating sprinkler. Same job each time: rotation here, needed there, at a right angle, without slipping.

## Subtopic: Hand Drill, Egg Beater, Winder

In the real winder the motor usually sits parallel to the drum, so the big gear-down is by spur or helical gears. Bevels turn up in the right-angle drives at the top of the headgear, in the skip-tipping gear, and in the depth indicator that tells the driver where the cage is. The sheave wheels have no gears at all; they are just pulleys.

In your model, bevels can save the day. The drum must lie across the frame; the motor might not fit beside it on a narrow tower, but it will fit end-on or standing up. A bevel pair at the drum shaft puts the motor where there is room, and if the drum's bevel is bigger than the motor's, you get a stage of gear-down as a bonus. Two rules: the cone tips must meet, so the shafts cross at exactly 90 degrees at the right distance; and bevels push apart along their shafts under load, so each shaft needs a collar or stop, or the gears walk out of mesh the moment the cage gets heavy.

On a drawing, each bevel is a trapezium in side view with the cone line, tips meeting, tooth counts beside them, arrows for direction. On a systems diagram it is a block: "bevel pair 1:3, axis turned 90 degrees".

Pocket summary of the lesson. Bevel gears have teeth on cones and mesh tip to tip to turn rotation through 90 degrees. Ratios follow the same tooth rule. They are in hand drills, egg beaters, car axles and wind pumps. In the model they let the motor stand where there is room, if the tips meet and the shafts have stops.
