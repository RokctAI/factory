### subtopic_1 — Gear System Concepts: Counter-Rotation, Idler, Ratios
**Assistant:** Why do the two meshing gears have to have the same size teeth, and what happens to the ratio if they do not?

**Tutor:** They must have the same tooth size because the teeth have to fit into each other's gaps as they roll past; a large tooth will not enter a small gap and a small tooth will rattle in a large one and break. Engineers call the tooth size the module, or the pitch, and two gears can mesh only if their modules match. Once that is fixed, the number of teeth on a gear is set entirely by its diameter: a gear twice the diameter has twice the circumference and so holds twice as many teeth of the same size. That is the fact that lets the velocity ratio be taken either from teeth or from diameters, since the two are in the same proportion. It also means that on your model you cannot mix gears from two different kits unless you check the module, and that a gear drawn with the compass and template in the graphics session is sized by its diameter while its tooth count is written beside it. If, hypothetically, two gears of different tooth sizes were forced together, they would not mesh properly: the teeth would jam or skip, the ratio would be undefined because teeth would no longer pass one for one, and the gears would wear out or shear teeth off very quickly. In a real winder gearbox the gears are made of hardened steel to a precise module and are checked for wear at every inspection, because a stripped tooth on a drum gear means a runaway cage, which is why winders also have brakes that act directly on the drum and do not depend on the gears at all.

### subtopic_2 — Spur Gears of Unequal Size: Velocity Ratio
**Assistant:** If the small gear driving the big gear gives four times the force, where does the extra force come from?

**Tutor:** From the distance the effort moves, which is the same trade a lever makes. Think of the teeth at the meshing point: the driver's tooth pushes the driven gear's tooth with a certain force, and both teeth move the same small distance as they pass. On the small gear that tooth is close to the axle, so pushing it round with the motor's torque takes a modest force but the gear must turn many times. On the large gear the same tooth force acts four times further from the axle, because the radius is four times larger, so it produces four times the torque at the driven gear's shaft. The driven gear turns once while the driver turns four times, so the effort has been applied through four times the angle. Work is force times distance, and the work put in, modest force through a long path, equals the work got out, large force through a short path, apart from the small loss to friction between the teeth and in the bearings. There is no extra energy, only a change in how it is delivered. This is also why you cannot simply gear a tiny motor up to lift anything you like: the motor still has to supply the total work, and a 10 to 1 gear train that lifts a skip ten times heavier takes ten times as long to lift it the same height, so the motor runs for ten times as long. In the winder, the engineer chooses the motor power from the work rate needed, tonnes lifted per hour up the shaft, and then chooses the gear ratio to match the motor's natural speed to the drum's required speed. Gears do not create power; they shape it.

### subtopic_3 — Force Ratio and Mechanical Advantage Above and Below One
**Assistant:** Our gear kit has gears with 8, 16, 24 and 40 teeth. How do we get a ratio bigger than 5 to 1?

**Tutor:** You use more than one stage, which is what every real winder gearbox does. The largest single ratio in your kit is 40 over 8, which is 5, and a bigger single ratio would need a very large or a very small gear, both impractical: huge gears are heavy and slow to make, and gears with fewer than about 8 teeth have weak, pointed teeth that wear and jam. So you compound the ratios. Put the 8-tooth gear on the motor shaft driving a 40-tooth gear, ratio 5; fix another 8-tooth gear on the same shaft as that 40-tooth gear, so they turn together as one piece; let that 8-tooth gear drive a second 40-tooth gear, another ratio of 5. The total ratio is the product of the stages, 5 times 5, which is 25 to 1: the motor turns 25 times for one turn of the final gear, and the final gear has 25 times the torque, less friction. A third stage gives 125 to 1. The rule is that gears on the same shaft turn together and do not change the ratio; only meshing pairs do, and the overall velocity ratio is the product of the driven teeth over driver teeth for each meshing pair. Two things to watch in your model. Each stage reverses direction, so two stages turn the output the same way as the motor and three stages reverse it; plan the drum's direction accordingly or add an idler. And each stage adds friction, so the real force gain is a little less than the arithmetic says; a model with 25 to 1 might deliver 20 to 1 in practice. For a small motor lifting a model skip up a one metre shaft, between 20 and 60 to 1 is usually right, and the mechanical advantage session will show you how to choose the number from the load.
