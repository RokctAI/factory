# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers spur gears linked by an idler, synchronised rotation and the choice of a harder material for the idler. The anchor is a Grade 8 gear board in Rustenburg with three gears in a row, a 30-tooth driver, a 12-tooth idler and a 30-tooth driven gear, each with a marker dot, and a learner turning the driver while the class watches both outer dots move together.

## Subtopic: The Idler: Direction Without Ratio

Two meshed gears counter-rotate; that is fixed by the geometry of teeth pushing teeth. When a design needs the output to turn the same way as the input, a third gear is placed between them. The driver turns the middle gear backwards, and the middle gear turns the final gear forwards again, so the first and last gears rotate in the same direction. The middle gear is the idler. It is called that because it transmits motion without being connected to any load or effort of its own: it idles between the two gears that matter.

The idler's second property follows from the one-for-one tooth rule. Suppose a 30-tooth driver meshes with a 12-tooth idler, which meshes with a 30-tooth driven gear. One turn of the driver pushes 30 teeth past the first meshing point, which turns the idler 30 of its 12 teeth, two and a half turns. The idler's two and a half turns push 30 teeth past the second meshing point, which turns the driven gear 30 of its 30 teeth, exactly one turn. The driver turned once and the driven gear turned once: velocity ratio 1 to 1, in the same direction. Now replace the idler with a 20-tooth gear: one turn of the driver pushes 30 teeth, the idler turns one and a half times, pushing 30 teeth, the driven gear turns once. The ratio is still 1 to 1. The idler's teeth cancel out because it is driven and driving at once, with the same number of teeth passing both meshing points.

The general result: in any gear train, the overall velocity ratio is the number of teeth on the final driven gear divided by the number on the first driver, and the idlers between them affect only direction. Each mesh reverses direction, so an odd number of gears in a simple train gives the same direction at both ends and an even number gives the opposite. Idlers also let a designer bridge a distance between two shafts that are too far apart for the gears to mesh directly, without having to make either gear enormous.

The questions for this section are with you now: why an idler restores direction, the proof with teeth that it does not change the ratio, and the rule for direction by counting gears.

## Subtopic: Synchronised Rotation and Where It Is Needed

Synchronised rotation means two or more shafts turning at exactly the same speed and in a fixed relationship, so that they stay in step however long they run. A belt or a chain can connect two shafts, but belts slip and chains stretch; gears cannot slip, so when two shafts must turn in perfect step, gears link them, and when they must turn in the same direction as well, an idler sits between them.

The mine winder gives the clearest example. A double-drum winder has two drums on one shaft, or on two shafts geared together, with the two ropes wound in opposite senses so that as one conveyance rises the other descends. If the two drums were driven separately they would drift out of step and one cage would arrive at the surface while the other was still metres from the bottom; geared together they are synchronised, and the relationship between the two cages is fixed by the gear teeth. In the model headgear a team may run two drums from one motor so that a cage and a counterweight move in balance, and an idler gives the second drum the right direction.

Other examples surround you. A conveyor belt's rollers may be geared so that they all turn at the same speed in the same direction, which an idler between each pair of roller gears provides. A printing press turns its plate cylinder and its impression cylinder in step so that the image lands in the same place every time; the two cylinders counter-rotate where they meet, but the feed rollers upstream must turn with them, through idlers. A car's engine turns its camshaft at exactly half the speed of the crankshaft through a chain or belt, because the valves must open at the same point of every cycle. A mechanical clock's hands are synchronised through a train of gears that includes idlers to keep the hour hand turning the same way as the minute hand while turning twelve times slower. In every case the point is the same: gears hold a fixed relationship between shafts, and idlers set the direction.

The questions for this section are with you now: what synchronised rotation means and why gears rather than belts achieve it, the double-drum winder, and two other examples.

## Subtopic: Materials for Idlers and Wear in Gear Trains

An idler works harder than it looks. It meshes with two gears, so every one of its teeth is pushed by the driver and pushes the driven gear on every pass; a tooth on the driver or the driven gear meets a tooth once per revolution at one meshing point, while an idler tooth meets a tooth twice per revolution, once at each. And a small idler turns faster than its larger neighbours, as the 12-tooth idler turning two and a half times for each turn of the 30-tooth gears shows, so each of its teeth passes a meshing point far more often. Both effects mean an idler's teeth suffer more contact, more sliding and more wear than the teeth of the gears it connects.

Designers answer this with material. The idler is made of a harder or tougher material than the gears beside it: hardened steel in a train of mild steel gears, bronze or steel in a train of brass, nylon or acetal in a train of softer plastic, or, in a plastic model kit, the one metal gear in a set of plastic ones. A harder idler wears slowly, and because the other gears bear less contact per tooth, the whole train wears evenly rather than one gear failing early. The cost is that the harder idler can wear the softer gears it meshes with, so the designer balances hardness against toughness and often chooses an idler that is somewhat harder but not brittle; a hard, brittle idler can chip a tooth and jam the train. Lubrication reduces wear in all gear trains, and in a winder gearbox the gears run in an oil bath and are inspected for pitting and tooth wear on a schedule, with the idlers and the smallest pinions checked most closely because they wear first.

In the model headgear, the practical rule is this: if the kit has one gear of a different, harder material, it is the idler; if all gears are the same plastic, the smallest and busiest gear is given the least load by keeping the drive train short and the idler no smaller than it needs to be, and the train is checked after test runs for a gear that is running warm or shedding plastic dust, which is the sign of a gear doing more work than it should.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: claiming that a large idler slows the train down; idlers of any size leave the ratio unchanged. Two: counting the idler's teeth in the overall ratio. Three: using an even number of gears when the output must turn the same way as the input. Four: making the idler from the softest material in the kit and wondering why it strips first.

The questions for this section are with you now: why an idler's teeth see more contact, why idlers are made harder, and what to watch in the model.

## Subtopic: The Gear in the Middle

Two gears always turn opposite ways. Need the output to turn the same way as the input? Put a third gear in the middle. The driver turns the middle gear backwards, the middle gear turns the last gear forwards. First and last now agree. The middle gear is the idler; it idles, carrying motion without any load or effort of its own.

Now the clever bit. 30-tooth driver, 12-tooth idler, 30-tooth driven gear. One turn of the driver pushes 30 teeth, so the idler turns two and a half times. Those two and a half turns push 30 teeth into the last gear, which turns exactly once. One in, one out, same direction. Swap in a 20-tooth idler: it turns one and a half times, still pushes 30 teeth, the last gear still turns once. The idler's size cancels out, because it is being pushed and pushing with the same teeth.

Rule: the ratio of a train is the last gear's teeth over the first gear's teeth, and the idlers only set the direction. Odd number of gears, same direction at both ends; even, opposite. Idlers also bridge a gap between shafts too far apart to mesh.

## Subtopic: Two Wheels Turning Together

Synchronised rotation: two shafts turning at the same speed in a fixed relationship, never drifting apart. Belts slip and chains stretch; gears cannot slip, so gears do it, and an idler makes the direction match.

The winder is the big example. Two drums, two ropes wound opposite ways, one cage up as the other comes down. Drive them separately and they drift; gear them together and the two cages stay locked in step. Your model might run a cage and a counterweight from one motor; an idler gives the second drum the right direction.

Other places: conveyor rollers all turning together through idlers. A printing press whose cylinders land the image in the same spot every time. A car engine's camshaft turning at exactly half the crankshaft's speed so the valves open at the same point every cycle. A clock's hour hand turning the same way as the minute hand, twelve times slower, through a train with idlers. Same idea each time: gears hold shafts in a fixed relationship; idlers set direction.

## Subtopic: Why the Idler Wears First

The idler works harder than it looks. Each of its teeth gets pushed by the driver and pushes the next gear, twice per turn instead of once. And a small idler spins faster than its big neighbours, so each tooth passes a meshing point far more often. More contact, more rubbing, more wear.

So make it harder. Hardened steel among mild steel gears; bronze or steel among brass; the one metal gear in a plastic kit. The hard idler wears slowly and the train wears evenly. Too hard and brittle, though, and it chips a tooth and jams, so designers pick harder but still tough. Oil reduces wear in every gearbox, and in a winder the idlers and the smallest gears are inspected most, because they go first.

In your model: if the kit has one gear of a different, harder material, that is your idler. If they are all the same plastic, keep the train short, do not make the idler smaller than it needs to be, and after test runs feel for a warm gear or look for plastic dust, the signs of a gear working too hard.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An idler between two gears makes them turn the same way and leaves the ratio unchanged, whatever its size; the train's ratio is last teeth over first teeth. Gears, not belts, give synchronised rotation, as in a double-drum winder. Idler teeth see twice the contact, so idlers are made harder and are inspected first.
