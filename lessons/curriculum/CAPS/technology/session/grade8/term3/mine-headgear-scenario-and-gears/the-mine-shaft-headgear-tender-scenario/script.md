# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the PAT scenario: a tender for a mine shaft headgear to transport miners and raise ore on a platinum reef. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Rustenburg looking out of the window at a real headgear on the skyline, a steel A-frame tower with two great wheels at its top, while the teacher reads the tender notice aloud.

## Subtopic: The Scenario: A Tender for a Mine Shaft Headgear

The scenario is written as a tender notice. A platinum mining company operating on the western limb of the Bushveld Complex, the band of rock north of Rustenburg that holds most of the world's platinum, is sinking a new vertical shaft to reach a reef about 800 metres below the surface. It invites engineering companies to tender for the design and construction of the shaft headgear: the structure above the shaft that supports the winding system which lowers and raises the cages carrying miners and the skips carrying ore. The notice sets out what the client wants: a headgear strong enough to carry the loads safely, a winding mechanism using gears to raise and lower the conveyance, a working scale model to demonstrate the design, a budget for the real structure, and a presentation to the company's tender board, which will choose one bid.

A tender is a competitive bid. The client describes what it needs; each company submits a design, a price and evidence that it can deliver; the client's board compares the bids against its criteria and awards the contract to one. Tenders are how most large engineering work is bought in South Africa, from roads to power stations to mine shafts, and the process rewards the same things this course teaches: understanding the problem, designing to specifications, communicating clearly in drawings and words, costing honestly and defending the design under questioning.

For the class, the scenario turns the term into a single project with a real-world shape. Each team is a company and names itself. The teacher and invited guests form the tender board. The portfolio becomes the bid document. Every lesson of the term contributes a part of the bid, and the final presentation in Week 9 is the tender board meeting at which the bids are heard.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the tender notice asks for, what a tender is and how it is decided, and how the class is organised around the scenario.

## Subtopic: What a Headgear Is and What It Must Do

A headgear, also called a headframe, is the structure over a mine shaft that carries the sheave wheels, the large pulleys at the top over which the winding rope runs. The rope goes from the winding drum in the winder house on the surface, up to the sheave wheel at the top of the headgear, and down the shaft to the conveyance, a cage for people or a skip for ore. As the drum turns one way the conveyance rises; turning the other way it descends. Most shafts run two conveyances in balance, one rising as the other falls, which halves the work the winder must do.

The headgear is therefore both a structure and a mechanism, which is why it sits at the centre of a Technology term. As a structure it must carry the weight of the sheave wheels, the tension in the ropes, the weight of the loaded conveyance, the pull of the rope towards the winder house, and the wind; it must not topple, buckle or shake, and it is usually a braced steel frame, often an A-frame or a four-legged tower with a back-leg sloping towards the winder, triangulated with the diagonal bracing of Term 1. Some modern shafts use a concrete tower with the winder on top instead. As a mechanism it contains the winding system: a motor turning a drum through a gearbox that reduces speed and multiplies force, the rope, the sheave, and the brakes and safety devices that stop a conveyance if the rope fails or the winder overspeeds.

South African gold and platinum mines are among the deepest in the world, some over three kilometres, and their headgear and winders are correspondingly large: sheave wheels several metres across, ropes 50 millimetres thick, winder drums turned by motors of thousands of kilowatts, and cages carrying a hundred people. The law requires the winding system to be inspected daily and the ropes to be tested and replaced on a schedule, because the lives of everyone underground depend on it. Headgear are also landmarks: the skyline of Johannesburg, Welkom, Rustenburg and Carletonville is defined by them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the path of the rope from drum to conveyance, why a headgear is both a structure and a mechanism, and the loads it must carry.

## Subtopic: The PAT Ahead: Gears, Structure, Tender

The term is a sequence in which each week prepares the next, and seeing the map at the start makes the pieces make sense. Weeks 1 to 3 build the mechanisms knowledge the winder needs: the concepts of gear systems, counter-rotation, idlers, velocity ratio and force multiplication; spur gears of unequal size and what their ratios do; idler gears for synchronised rotation; bevel gears for turning the axis through 90 degrees; mechanical advantage calculated by ratios for levers and gears; and the graphic communication of gear trains and systems diagrams. By the end of Week 3 every team can specify and draw the gear train that turns a small motor's fast, weak rotation into a drum's slow, strong one.

Weeks 4 to 6 design the headgear. The team investigates real wire-rope headgear in South African mines, writes the design brief with specifications and constraints from the tender notice, sketches initial ideas, and produces a two-dimensional working drawing with dimensions. In Week 5 the term pauses to investigate the impact of mining and the people in it: acid mine drainage, dust from mine dumps, indigenous iron mining before the modern era, or gender bias in mining careers, and the report joins the bid. In Week 6 the teams, acting as engineering companies, evaluate each other's sketches and select the strongest idea to carry into the bid.

Weeks 7 and 8 cost and build: a realistic budget of materials and labour for the real headgear, a list of tools, materials and construction steps, and a working scale model with a geared winder that lifts a load up a model shaft, built with safe working practices. Week 9 is the tender board: each company presents its research, plans, flow chart, model and budget, and answers the board's questions. Week 10 revises the term.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating the headgear as only a tower and forgetting the winding mechanism inside the bid. Two: treating it as only a mechanism and building a model that sways and topples. Three: leaving the budget and the impact report to the last week, when they draw on investigation that must start now. Four: forgetting that it is a competition judged on evidence, so that the presentation claims instead of shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Weeks 1 to 3 build, what Weeks 4 to 6 design, and what the tender board will see in Week 9.

Here is the story. A platinum mine north of Rustenburg is digging a new shaft 800 metres down to the reef. It needs a headgear, the tower over the shaft that lifts the cages of miners and the skips of rock. It has put out a tender: companies send in a design, a model, a price and a presentation, and the mine's board picks one.

A tender is a competition for a job. The client says what it needs; each company bids; the board compares bids against its rules and picks a winner. That is how roads, power stations and mine shafts get built in South Africa, and it rewards exactly what this course teaches: understand the problem, design to the rules, draw and explain clearly, cost honestly, and defend your design when questioned.

In class, your team is a company with a name. The teacher and guests are the tender board. Your portfolio is your bid. Every lesson this term adds a piece, and Week 9 is the board meeting.

## Subtopic: Lifting People and Rock Safely

A headgear carries the big wheels, the sheaves, at its top. The rope runs from the winding drum in a building on the ground, up over the sheave, and down the shaft to the cage or skip. Drum turns one way, the cage rises; the other way, it falls. Most shafts run two in balance, one up while one comes down, so the winder does half the work.

So it is two things at once. A structure: it holds the wheels, the rope's pull, the loaded cage, the sideways tug towards the winder house and the wind, and it must not topple, buckle or shake, which is why it is a braced steel A-frame or tower with a back leg leaning towards the winder, triangulated like the Term 1 pylon. A mechanism: a motor turns a drum through a gearbox that slows the speed and multiplies the force, plus rope, sheave, brakes and safety catches that stop a cage if the rope breaks.

South African mines are the deepest on earth, some past three kilometres, so the kit is huge: wheels metres across, ropes as thick as your wrist, motors of thousands of kilowatts, cages for a hundred people. The law says inspect daily and replace ropes on schedule, because everyone underground depends on it.

Hold the rope's path in mind and try this part's questions: lifting people and rock safely.

## Subtopic: The Term's Journey in One Picture

The term's map. Weeks 1 to 3: gears. How gears turn opposite ways, what an idler does, speed and force ratios, big and small gears, bevel gears that turn a corner, mechanical advantage by ratios for levers and gears, and how to draw gear trains and systems diagrams. By Week 3 you can draw the gear train that turns a fast weak motor into a slow strong drum.

Weeks 4 to 6: design. Research real headgear, write the brief with rules from the tender notice, sketch ideas, make a dimensioned working drawing. Week 5 steps aside to investigate mining's impact: acid water from old mines, dust from dumps, iron mining in South Africa long before the modern era, or why so few women work in mining. Week 6, the companies judge each other's sketches and pick the best idea for the bid.

Weeks 7 and 8: budget the real thing, list tools, materials and steps, and build a working model with a geared winder that lifts a load. Week 9: the tender board. Week 10: revision.

The headgear is a braced tower carrying the sheave wheels and a geared winding system that lifts cages and skips up a shaft. Weeks 1 to 3 gears, 4 to 6 research and design, 7 and 8 budget and build, 9 the tender board.

Hold the map of ten weeks, and take the final questions of the lesson: the term's journey in one picture.
