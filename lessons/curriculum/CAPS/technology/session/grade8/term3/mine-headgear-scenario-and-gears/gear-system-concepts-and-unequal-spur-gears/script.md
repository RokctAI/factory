# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers gear system concepts and spur gears of unequal size. The anchor is a Grade 8 desk in Rustenburg with a plastic gear kit: a 40-tooth gear and a 10-tooth gear meshed on a pegboard, a marker dot on each so the class can count turns as the small one is spun by hand.

## Subtopic: Gear System Concepts: Counter-Rotation, Idler, Ratios

A gear is a wheel with teeth around its edge, and two gears whose teeth interlock, which is called meshing, turn together so that one drives the other. The gear that is turned by the effort, the motor or the hand, is the driver; the gear it turns is the driven gear. Four ideas from Grade 7 describe what happens between them.

Counter-rotation: two meshed gears turn in opposite directions, because the tooth of the driver pushes the tooth of the driven gear forward at the point where they meet, and a point moving forward on the far side of the driven gear means the driven gear turns the other way. Clockwise drives anticlockwise and vice versa, always, for any pair of external spur gears.

The idler: if a third gear is placed between the driver and the final driven gear, it reverses the direction a second time, so the first and last gears turn the same way. The middle gear is called an idler because it changes direction without changing the speed ratio between the first and last gears, whatever its size; it will be the subject of its own session.

Velocity ratio: meshed gears turn at speeds set by their numbers of teeth. Because every tooth of the driver that passes the meshing point pushes exactly one tooth of the driven gear past it, a gear with more teeth turns more slowly than one with fewer. The velocity ratio compares the two speeds.

Force multiplication: a gear that turns more slowly than its driver turns with more force, and one that turns faster turns with less. Gears, like levers, trade speed for force, and the trade is exact apart from friction. That is why a winder has a gearbox: the motor is fast and relatively weak, the drum must be slow and very strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: driver and driven, why meshed gears counter-rotate, what an idler does, and the trade between speed and force.

## Subtopic: Spur Gears of Unequal Size: Velocity Ratio

Spur gears are the simplest gears, flat wheels with straight teeth parallel to the axle, meshing on parallel shafts. When two spur gears of unequal size mesh, the velocity ratio follows from a single fact: at the meshing point, teeth pass one for one. If the driver has 10 teeth and the driven gear has 40, then one full turn of the driver pushes 10 teeth past the meshing point, which moves the driven gear 10 of its 40 teeth, a quarter of a turn. The driver must turn four times for the driven gear to turn once. Velocity ratio is written as driven teeth divided by driver teeth, here 40 over 10, which is 4, meaning the driver turns four times for every one turn of the driven gear, or as 4 to 1. The driven gear turns at one quarter of the driver's speed.

Reverse the roles and drive the 40-tooth gear: now one turn of the driver pushes 40 teeth past, which is four full turns of the 10-tooth driven gear. The velocity ratio is 10 over 40, a quarter, and the driven gear turns four times faster. The same pair of gears is a speed reducer one way round and a speed increaser the other, which is a point worth remembering when a gear train is drawn: the direction of drive matters, not just the gears.

The rule generalises. Driven speed equals driver speed times driver teeth divided by driven teeth. A motor at 1 200 revolutions per minute driving a 12-tooth gear meshed with a 60-tooth gear turns the 60-tooth gear at 1 200 times 12 over 60, which is 240 revolutions per minute. Because tooth size must be the same on two meshing gears, teeth are proportional to diameter, so the ratio can equally be taken from diameters: a 20 millimetre gear driving an 80 millimetre gear gives a velocity ratio of 4 as well. Counting teeth is more accurate than measuring diameters on small gears, which is why the course works mostly with teeth.

The questions for this section are with you now: the one-for-one tooth rule, the velocity ratio of a 10-tooth driver and a 40-tooth driven gear each way round, and calculating driven speed from driver speed.

## Subtopic: Force Ratio and Mechanical Advantage Above and Below One

The force ratio is the mirror of the velocity ratio. When the 10-tooth driver turns the 40-tooth driven gear, the driven gear turns a quarter as fast and, apart from friction, turns with four times the turning force, or torque. This is the mechanical advantage of the gear pair: the load the driven gear can turn divided by the effort applied at the driver, which for gears equals the velocity ratio, driven teeth over driver teeth. A small gear driving a large gear gives a mechanical advantage greater than one: slower, stronger, a force multiplier. A large gear driving a small gear gives a mechanical advantage less than one: faster, weaker, a speed multiplier. Equal gears give a mechanical advantage of exactly one and change only direction.

Nothing is gained for free. The work put in at the driver equals the work got out at the driven gear, less friction, so if the driven gear turns with four times the force it turns through a quarter of the distance in the same time. A winder motor that turns 1 200 times a minute with a modest torque is geared down through two or three stages until the drum turns 20 times a minute with enough torque to lift a loaded skip; the drum turns slowly, which is also what the safe speed of a cage in a shaft requires. A bicycle's gears run the other way: a large chainwheel driving a small sprocket gives a mechanical advantage less than one, so the wheel turns faster than the pedals, trading the rider's force for road speed on the flat, and the rider shifts to a larger sprocket on a hill to get force back.

The headgear design uses this directly. The team's model winder will be driven by a small electric motor or a hand crank, both fast and weak, and must lift a load slowly and reliably. The gear train must therefore have a mechanical advantage well above one, built from small gears driving large ones in one or more stages, and the drum must turn in the right direction, which an idler or an odd or even number of meshes controls. Choosing the numbers is the work of the mechanical advantage session; this session fixes the direction of the trade.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying meshed gears turn the same way; a pair always counter-rotates. Two: inverting the velocity ratio, dividing driver teeth by driven teeth and calling the result the speed reduction. Three: thinking a gear pair multiplies both speed and force; it trades one for the other. Four: forgetting that swapping which gear is the driver reverses the whole effect.

The questions for this section are with you now: mechanical advantage as driven over driver teeth, what MA greater than and less than one mean, and why the winder needs MA well above one.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a plastic gear kit and two marker dots — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Teeth That Push Teeth

A gear is a wheel with teeth. Two gears whose teeth lock together are meshed; turn one and the other turns. The one you turn is the driver; the one it turns is the driven gear. Four ideas.

They turn opposite ways. A tooth on the driver pushes a tooth on the other gear forward where they meet, so the other gear goes round the other way. Clockwise drives anticlockwise, every time.

An idler is a third gear in the middle. It flips the direction again, so the first and last gears turn the same way, and it does not change the speed ratio between them, whatever its size.

Speed ratio: teeth pass one for one at the meeting point, so a gear with more teeth turns slower. Force: whatever a gear pair does to speed, it does the opposite to force. Slower means stronger; faster means weaker. That is why a winder has a gearbox: the motor is fast and weak, the drum must be slow and strong.

Hold the two marker dots in mind and try the questions for this part: teeth that push teeth.

## Subtopic: Big Gear Slow, Small Gear Fast

Spur gears are the flat ones with straight teeth, on parallel axles. Mesh a 10-tooth driver with a 40-tooth driven gear. One turn of the driver pushes 10 teeth past, which is 10 of the big gear's 40 teeth, a quarter turn. The driver turns four times for one turn of the big gear. Velocity ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth: 40 over 10, which is 4, or 4 to 1. The big gear turns at a quarter of the speed.

Swap them. Drive the 40-tooth gear and one turn pushes 40 teeth past, which is four full turns of the 10-tooth gear. Velocity ratio 10 over 40, a quarter; the small gear turns four times faster. Same gears, opposite effect; it depends on which one you drive.

Rule: driven speed equals driver speed times driver teeth over driven teeth. Motor at 1 200 turns a minute, 12-tooth gear driving 60 teeth: 1 200 times 12 over 60 is 240 turns a minute. Teeth are proportional to diameter, so diameters give the same ratio, but counting teeth is more accurate.

Hold the quarter turn in mind and try this part's questions: big gear slow, small gear fast.

## Subtopic: Trade Speed for Force

Force is the mirror. The 10-tooth driver turning the 40-tooth gear: a quarter the speed, four times the turning force. That is the mechanical advantage, MA, and for gears it equals the velocity ratio: driven teeth over driver teeth. Small driving big: MA bigger than one, slower and stronger. Big driving small: MA less than one, faster and weaker. Equal gears: MA of one, only the direction changes.

Nothing is free. Four times the force means a quarter of the distance in the same time. A winder motor spins 1 200 times a minute with little force; two or three gear stages bring the drum down to 20 turns a minute with enough force to lift a loaded skip, and slow is also what a cage full of people needs. A bicycle does the opposite: big chainwheel driving a small sprocket, MA under one, the wheel spins faster than your feet; on a hill you shift to a bigger sprocket to get force back.

Your model winder will have a small motor or a hand crank, fast and weak. So its gear train needs MA well above one, small gears driving big ones, maybe in stages, and it must turn the drum the right way.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Meshed gears counter-rotate; an idler restores direction without changing the ratio. Velocity ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth; more teeth, slower. MA for gears equals that ratio: above one is slower and stronger, below one is faster and weaker, and swapping the driver flips it.

Hold the slow strong drum, and take the final questions of the lesson: trade speed for force.
