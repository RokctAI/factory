# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers mechanical advantage of levers calculated using ratios, load over effort and load arm over effort arm, without moments. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Rustenburg prising up a loose paving slab outside the Technology room with a steel crowbar, a brick as the fulcrum and a spring balance hooked to the handle to read the effort.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Advantage as a Ratio of Forces

A simple machine lets a small effort move a large load, or a small movement produce a large one, and mechanical advantage is the number that says how much. Its first definition is in forces: mechanical advantage equals load divided by effort, where the load is the force the machine exerts on the object being moved and the effort is the force applied to the machine. Both are measured in newtons, so the ratio has no unit. If a crowbar lifts a slab weighing 600 newtons when the hand pushes down with 100 newtons, the mechanical advantage is 600 over 100, which is 6; the machine makes the lift six times easier in force.

The number tells its own story. Mechanical advantage above one means the load is larger than the effort: the machine multiplies force. Mechanical advantage of exactly one means the machine changes only the direction or the position of the force, like a single fixed pulley or a first-class lever with equal arms. Mechanical advantage below one means the load is smaller than the effort: the machine does not make the force easier but instead multiplies distance or speed, which is what a fishing rod, a broom, a cricket bat and a pair of tweezers do, and what the forearm does when the biceps lifts the hand.

Nothing is free. When mechanical advantage is 6, the effort moves six times as far as the load, because the work put in, effort times its distance, equals the work got out, load times its distance, apart from friction. The crowbar handle swings through a long arc while the slab rises a little. This is the same trade the gears made in the previous sessions, and the mechanical advantage of a gear pair, driven teeth over driver teeth, is the same kind of ratio, which is why the two topics sit together in this week.

Measuring mechanical advantage from forces requires a spring balance or a known mass on each side, and the measured value is always slightly below the ideal because friction at the fulcrum and the weight of the lever itself take their share. The ideal value, with no friction, comes from the geometry of the machine, which for a lever is the next section.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of mechanical advantage as load over effort, what values above, equal to and below one mean, and why a high mechanical advantage means a long effort movement.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Advantage from the Arms of a Lever

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point, the fulcrum or pivot. The effort acts at one point on the bar and the load at another, and the distance from the fulcrum to the effort is the effort arm while the distance from the fulcrum to the load is the load arm. Because the bar is rigid, when it turns through a small angle every point on it moves through an arc proportional to its distance from the fulcrum, so the effort moves a distance proportional to the effort arm and the load moves a distance proportional to the load arm. Since work in equals work out, effort times effort arm equals load times load arm, and rearranging gives the second definition: mechanical advantage equals effort arm divided by load arm. This is a ratio of two lengths, both in the same unit, so again it has no unit.

The two definitions agree. For the crowbar, if the hand is 1.2 metres from the brick and the slab's edge is 0.2 metres from it on the other side, the mechanical advantage from arms is 1.2 over 0.2, which is 6, the same as the 600 over 100 from forces. In an examination you may be given forces, or arm lengths, or one force and both arms and asked for the other force; the two ratios let you move between them: load equals effort times effort arm over load arm; effort equals load times load arm over effort arm.

The three classes of lever follow from where the fulcrum sits. A first-class lever has the fulcrum between effort and load, as in the crowbar, a seesaw, scissors and pliers; its mechanical advantage can be above or below one depending on the arms. A second-class lever has the load between the fulcrum and the effort, as in a wheelbarrow, a bottle opener and a nutcracker; the effort arm is always longer than the load arm, so its mechanical advantage is always above one. A third-class lever has the effort between the fulcrum and the load, as in a fishing rod, a broom, tweezers and the forearm; the effort arm is always shorter than the load arm, so its mechanical advantage is always below one and the lever is used to gain speed or distance. The Term 1 crank was a second-class lever in this sense; this week's gear train uses the same force-for-distance trade in rotation.

The questions for this section are with you now: effort arm and load arm, mechanical advantage as effort arm over load arm, and why the second-class lever always has mechanical advantage above one.

## Subtopic: Worked Examples: Crowbar, Wheelbarrow, Brake Lever

Worked examples fix the method. One: the crowbar. Effort arm 1.2 metres, load arm 0.2 metres. Mechanical advantage 1.2 over 0.2 equals 6. If the slab weighs 600 newtons the ideal effort is 600 over 6, which is 100 newtons; the spring balance reads about 110 newtons because of friction and the bar's own weight, so the actual mechanical advantage is 600 over 110, about 5.5. Both the ideal and actual values are reported, with the reason for the difference.

Two: the wheelbarrow. The fulcrum is the wheel axle; the load, 400 newtons of sand, acts at the centre of the tray, 0.5 metres from the axle; the hands lift at the handles, 1.5 metres from the axle. Mechanical advantage 1.5 over 0.5 equals 3. The effort to lift the load end is 400 over 3, about 133 newtons; moving the load forward in the tray towards the wheel shortens the load arm and raises the mechanical advantage, which is why a barrow is loaded over the wheel.

Three: the bicycle brake lever. The pivot is at the lever's base; the finger pulls 80 millimetres from the pivot; the cable attaches 20 millimetres from the pivot on the same side. Mechanical advantage 80 over 20 equals 4; a finger pull of 30 newtons puts 120 newtons on the cable, and the cable moves a quarter as far as the finger, which is why a brake lever has a long travel for a small pad movement.

Four: a pair of pliers, two first-class levers joined at the pivot. Handles 150 millimetres from the pivot, jaws gripping 30 millimetres from it: mechanical advantage 5. Squeezing with 50 newtons grips with 250 newtons. Five, a third-class example: a broom held with the top hand as fulcrum, the lower hand 0.4 metres down pushing, the bristles 1.2 metres down. Mechanical advantage 0.4 over 1.2 equals one third; the hand pushes with three times the force the bristles exert, but the bristles sweep three times as far and fast, which is the point of a broom.

In the headgear project the lever appears in the brake and the safety catch of the model winder, where a long handle must clamp the drum, and in the examination where a lever question is nearly always set beside a gear question so that the same ratio thinking is tested twice.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: inverting the ratio, dividing load arm by effort arm and reporting a mechanical advantage below one for a crowbar. Two: measuring the arms from the wrong point instead of from the fulcrum. Three: mixing units, metres on one arm and centimetres on the other. Four: treating the ideal value as the measured one and never mentioning friction.

The questions for this section are with you now: the crowbar, wheelbarrow and brake-lever calculations, the broom as a third-class lever, and why loading a barrow over the wheel helps.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a crowbar, a brick and a spring balance — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: How Many Times Easier

A machine lets a small push move a big load. Mechanical advantage, MA, says how many times. First way to get it: load divided by effort, both in newtons. The crowbar lifts a 600 newton slab with a 100 newton push: MA is 600 over 100, which is 6. Six times easier. No unit, because it is newtons over newtons.

Read the number. Above one: force multiplied, the machine makes it easier. Exactly one: only the direction or position changes, like a single pulley or a seesaw with equal arms. Below one: the machine multiplies distance or speed instead, like a fishing rod, a broom, a cricket bat, your forearm.

The catch: MA of 6 means your hand moves six times as far as the slab. Work in equals work out, minus friction. Same trade as the gears: force for distance.

Measured MA is always a bit less than the ideal, because friction at the pivot and the bar's own weight eat some effort. Ideal MA comes from the shape of the machine, and for a lever that is the next bit.

Hold the slab rising a little as your hand swings a lot in mind and try the questions for this part: how many times easier.

## Subtopic: Long Arm, Small Effort

A lever is a stiff bar turning on a fixed point, the fulcrum. Effort arm: fulcrum to where you push. Load arm: fulcrum to the load. When the bar tips, each point moves in proportion to its distance from the fulcrum, so effort times effort arm equals load times load arm, and that gives the second way: MA equals effort arm over load arm. Lengths over lengths, no unit.

Crowbar: hand 1.2 metres from the brick, slab 0.2 metres the other side. 1.2 over 0.2 is 6. Same answer as the forces gave. Given forces, use forces; given arms, use arms; given one force and both arms, cross over: load equals effort times effort arm over load arm.

Three classes. First: fulcrum in the middle, crowbar, seesaw, scissors, pliers; MA can be above or below one. Second: load in the middle, wheelbarrow, bottle opener, nutcracker; the effort arm is always the longer, so MA is always above one. Third: effort in the middle, fishing rod, broom, tweezers, forearm; the effort arm is always the shorter, so MA is always below one and you gain speed. Term 1's crank was second class; this week's gears make the same trade going round.

Hold the three classes in mind and try this part's questions: long arm, small effort.

## Subtopic: Numbers From Real Levers

Examples. Crowbar: 1.2 over 0.2, MA 6; 600 newton slab needs 100 newtons ideal; the balance reads about 110 because of friction, so actual MA about 5.5. Report both and say why.

Wheelbarrow: fulcrum at the axle, sand 0.5 metres back, hands 1.5 metres back. 1.5 over 0.5, MA 3; 400 newtons of sand needs about 133 newtons. Push the load towards the wheel and the load arm shrinks and MA climbs; that is why you load a barrow over the wheel.

Brake lever: finger 80 millimetres from the pivot, cable 20 millimetres. MA 4; 30 newtons of finger gives 120 on the cable, and the cable moves a quarter as far, which is why the lever travels a long way for a small pad movement. Pliers: handles 150, jaws 30, MA 5; a 50 newton squeeze grips with 250. Broom: top hand is the fulcrum, lower hand 0.4 metres down, bristles 1.2 metres: 0.4 over 1.2 is a third. You push three times harder than the bristles do, and they sweep three times further and faster.

In the project, the lever is the brake handle on your model drum, and in the exam a lever question sits next to a gear question to test the same ratio thinking twice.

Pocket summary of the lesson. MA is load over effort or effort arm over load arm; above one multiplies force, below one multiplies distance. Measured MA is below ideal because of friction. Second-class levers always have MA above one, third-class always below. Arms are measured from the fulcrum in the same unit.

Hold the barrow loaded over the wheel, and take the final questions of the lesson: numbers from real levers.
