### subtopic_1 — Mechanical Advantage of Gears From Teeth and Diameters
**Assistant:** If stages multiply, why not use one huge gear on the drum instead of three stages? It would be simpler.

**Tutor:** It would be simpler to describe and far harder to build, which is why no real gearbox does it. To get 64 to 1 in a single mesh with a 12-tooth pinion you would need a 768-tooth gear, and at the kit's tooth size that gear would be about 60 centimetres across, bigger than the whole model, heavier than everything else in it, slow to make and impossible to mount on a light frame. In a full-size winder the same logic would call for a gear tens of metres in diameter. Compounding solves it: three ordinary gears of 60 teeth each, stacked on shafts a few centimetres apart, give the same ratio in a box the size of a fist. There are other advantages. Each stage carries torque appropriate to its position, low near the motor and high near the drum, so the gears can be sized accordingly, small and fast at the input, larger and stronger at the output. The load on each tooth stays within what the material can take, whereas a single enormous ratio puts all the output torque on one small pinion's teeth, which would shear. And compound trains can be laid out to fit the space available, folding back on themselves so that the input and output shafts are where the frame wants them. The costs of compounding are extra bearings, extra friction at each mesh, perhaps 5 to 10 percent per stage in a plastic kit, and more parts to align, which is why a designer uses as few stages as will give the ratio and no more. Real winders typically use two or three helical stages; your model will use two or three spur stages; the principle and the arithmetic are the same.

### subtopic_2 — Velocity Ratio, Speed and Compound Trains
**Assistant:** Our motor lifts 20 grams on a 40 millimetre drum. Does the drum size change what the gears must do?

**Tutor:** Yes, and it is one of the most useful adjustments you have. The rope pulls at the drum's edge, so the torque the drum needs is the load times the drum's radius; halve the radius and the same load needs half the torque, which means half the gear ratio for the same lift, or the same gear ratio lifts twice the load. It also halves the rope speed for a given drum speed, which helps with the problem of a cage that rises too fast. So a smaller drum is a free stage of mechanical advantage. The limits are practical. A very thin drum winds the rope in many layers, which changes the effective radius as the rope builds up and makes the lift speed up as the cage rises; a drum narrower than the rope needs can jam or let the rope cross over itself; and a drum must be stiff enough not to bend or crush under the rope's tension. Real winders use a large drum, two to six metres across, because their rope is thick and must not be bent too sharply or it fatigues, and they get the ratio from the gearbox instead. For your model, where the rope is thin string, a drum of 15 to 25 millimetres is often the right compromise: it roughly doubles your effective advantage compared with 40 millimetres, slows the lift to a stately pace, and is still wide enough to wind a metre of string in a single layer if the drum is long enough. When you do your six-step design, treat the drum diameter as a variable alongside the gear ratio, record the pair you choose, and remember that when the motor is tested on its own it must be tested with the same drum you will use, or the effort figure is wrong.

### subtopic_3 — Sizing the Winder: From Load to Gear Train
**Assistant:** We calculated 25 to 1 and the model still stalls with the loaded cage. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** Several things can, and finding which is the evaluation skill the tender board wants to see. Start with the effort figure. If the motor's lifting capacity was measured with fresh batteries and the demonstration runs on tired ones, the available torque may have halved; measure the motor again with the batteries you will actually use. If it was measured with a different drum, the effort was wrong from the start, as the previous question explains. Next, friction. The tooth ratio gives the ideal advantage; three plastic meshes, several shafts running in rough holes, a bevel pair pushing apart, and a rope rubbing over a sheave that does not turn freely can together eat a third or more of the torque, so an ideal 25 can deliver an actual 15. Spin the train by hand with the motor removed; it should turn freely with no tight spots, and every shaft should have a drop of oil or a smear of soap at its bearing. Check the meshes: gears pressed too tightly together bind, gears too far apart skip, and bent shafts do both. Then the load: a cage weighed empty but demonstrated with a cargo, or a rope that lifts the counterweight as well as the cage, doubles the demand. Finally the margin: if the design margin was small, any one of these takes it. The fixes follow the cause: fresh batteries, a smaller drum, another stage of 4 or 5, freeing the bearings, resetting the mesh, or lightening the cage. Record what you found, the number before and the number after each fix, and put it in the evaluation; a bid that says "the first train stalled at 250 grams, we found the sheave was seized and the drum too large, we fixed both and it now lifts 450 grams" is far stronger than one that never stalled.
