# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers mechanical advantage of gears calculated from tooth ratios, gear wheel diameters and velocity ratios. The anchor is a Grade 8 team in Rustenburg with a spring balance, a small battery motor, a plastic gear kit and a cardboard drum, measuring how much the bare motor can lift and working out how many times stronger it must become to raise the model cage.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Advantage of Gears From Teeth and Diameters

The mechanical advantage of a pair of meshed gears is the ratio of the turning force delivered at the driven gear to the turning force applied at the driver, and because teeth pass one for one at the mesh, it equals the number of teeth on the driven gear divided by the number on the driver. A 15-tooth driver meshed with a 60-tooth driven gear has mechanical advantage 60 over 15, which is 4: the driven shaft turns with four times the torque, less friction, and a quarter as fast. The same number is the velocity ratio, driver turns per driven turn, so for gears the two quantities are one number looked at twice.

Because two meshing gears must have the same tooth size, the number of teeth on each is proportional to its circumference and so to its diameter, and the ratio can equally be taken from diameters: a 30 millimetre driver meshed with a 120 millimetre driven gear has mechanical advantage 120 over 30, again 4. The diameter used is the pitch diameter, measured roughly to the middle of the teeth, not to their tips; on a drawing the pitch circle is the circle the gears are drawn with, and the teeth are added outside and inside it. Teeth are counted when a kit is at hand because counting is exact; diameters are used when a drawing or a photograph of a real gearbox is all that is available.

Mechanical advantage above one, small gear driving large, is a speed reducer and force multiplier; below one, large driving small, is a speed increaser; the idler between two gears contributes a factor of exactly one and affects only direction. Everything said about levers last session applies: work in equals work out apart from friction, so a mechanical advantage of 4 means the driver turns through four times the angle of the driven gear, and the measured advantage falls a little short of the tooth ratio because the teeth rub and the bearings drag. The ideal value from teeth is what a designer uses to choose the gears; the actual value, measured with a spring balance at the drum, is what the evaluation reports.

The questions for this section are with you now: mechanical advantage as driven over driver teeth, the same from diameters, and the relation to velocity ratio and friction.

## Subtopic: Velocity Ratio, Speed and Compound Trains

The velocity ratio turns into speeds with one line: driven speed equals driver speed divided by the velocity ratio, or equivalently driver speed times driver teeth over driven teeth. A toy motor running at 3 000 revolutions per minute driving a 60-tooth gear through a 15-tooth pinion turns the 60-tooth gear at 3 000 over 4, which is 750 revolutions per minute, still far too fast for a winder drum and with only four times the motor's small torque. One pair of gears from a kit rarely gives a ratio above 5 or 6, and a model winder usually needs 20 to 60, so the stages are compounded.

In a compound train two gears are fixed on one shaft so that they turn together: a large gear is driven by the previous stage, and a small gear on the same shaft drives the next. Each meshing pair contributes its own ratio, and because the gears on a shared shaft turn as one, the ratios multiply. A 15-tooth pinion driving a 60-tooth gear, with a 15-tooth gear on that gear's shaft driving a second 60-tooth gear, gives 4 times 4, which is 16: the motor turns 16 times for one turn of the output, 3 000 revolutions per minute becomes 187.5 at the output, and the torque is multiplied by 16 less friction. A third identical stage gives 64. The rule for any train is that the overall velocity ratio and ideal mechanical advantage are the product of driven over driver teeth for each mesh, idlers contributing one, shared shafts contributing nothing because they are not meshes.

Direction follows the mesh count: each mesh reverses, so a two-stage compound train turns its output the same way as the motor and a three-stage train the opposite way; an idler added anywhere flips it back. Speed follows the ratio: at 16 to 1 a drum of 40 millimetre diameter, circumference about 126 millimetres, turning at 187.5 revolutions per minute would wind rope at about 23 metres per minute, far too fast for a model cage on a one metre shaft; at 64 to 1 the drum turns at about 47 revolutions per minute and lifts at about 6 metres per minute, about 10 seconds for a one metre lift, which is a demonstrable speed. The designer therefore has two reasons to pick the ratio, force and speed, and checks both.

The questions for this section are with you now: converting velocity ratio to speed, how compound stages multiply, and how direction and lifting speed follow from the train.

## Subtopic: Sizing the Winder: From Load to Gear Train

The design calculation runs backwards from the load. Step one, find the load: the model cage with its cargo weighs, say, 300 grams, which is about 3 newtons, and the rope pulls on the drum's edge with that force. Step two, find the effort: measure what the motor can supply at the drum's radius by running the bare motor with a drum on its shaft and finding the largest mass it can lift before stalling, say 20 grams, about 0.2 newtons; or for a hand crank, decide the comfortable force a finger can keep applying at the crank's radius and convert through the crank's lever ratio. Step three, required mechanical advantage is load over effort, 3 over 0.2, which is 15, and a margin for friction and for a cage that may be loaded heavier on the day lifts it to about 25. Step four, choose stages from the kit whose product is at least 25: two stages of 60 over 12 give 5 times 5, 25; or 60 over 15 and 60 over 10 give 4 times 6, 24, just short, so a third small stage or a bigger gear is added. Step five, check speed: motor 3 000 revolutions per minute over 25 gives 120 at the drum, which at a 40 millimetre drum is about 15 metres per minute, too fast to look controlled, so a ratio nearer 60 is chosen for a 6 metre per minute lift, or a smaller drum is used; a smaller drum also raises the effective mechanical advantage at the rope. Step six, check direction and fix it with an idler, a winding sense or a motor reversal, and record the train as a list: motor, 12-tooth pinion to 60-tooth gear, shaft, 12 to 60, shaft, 12 to 48, drum, overall 100 to 1, output same direction as motor, lift speed about 4 metres per minute.

Then the real headgear, for the budget and the bid. A full-size winder motor at 1 000 revolutions per minute driving a drum that must turn at about 25 revolutions per minute needs a ratio of 40, delivered in two helical stages in an oil-filled gearbox; the same arithmetic, scaled up, and the tender board will ask for it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding stage ratios instead of multiplying them. Two: choosing the ratio for force alone and presenting a cage that shoots up the shaft. Three: counting a shared shaft or an idler as a stage. Four: forgetting the margin, so the model lifts the empty cage and stalls when the cargo goes in.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six-step design calculation, why a margin is added, and how the drum's diameter affects speed and effective advantage.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a toy motor, a spring balance and a cardboard drum — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Count the Teeth, Get the Number

For a gear pair, mechanical advantage is driven teeth over driver teeth. A 15-tooth driver on a 60-tooth gear: 60 over 15, MA 4. The driven shaft turns four times harder and four times slower. That same 4 is the velocity ratio; for gears the two numbers are the same thing.

Diameters work too, because meshing gears have the same tooth size, so teeth go with size. A 30 millimetre gear driving a 120 millimetre gear: MA 4. Measure to the middle of the teeth, the pitch circle, which is the circle you draw gears with. Count teeth when you have the kit; use diameters when you only have a drawing or a photo.

Small driving big: above one, slow and strong. Big driving small: below one, fast and weak. Idler: exactly one, direction only. And as with levers, the driver turns four times the angle for four times the force, and friction means the measured MA is a little under the tooth ratio. Teeth for designing; spring balance for reporting.

Hold the 60 over 15 in mind and try the questions for this part: count the teeth, get the number.

## Subtopic: Stages Multiply

Speed: driven speed equals driver speed divided by the ratio. A 3 000 rpm toy motor through 15 to 60 gives 750 rpm. Still far too fast for a drum, and only four times the torque. One pair rarely beats 5 or 6, and a winder needs 20 to 60. So stack stages.

Compound train: fix two gears on one shaft so they turn together. A big gear is driven by the stage before; a small gear on the same shaft drives the next. Ratios multiply. 15 to 60, then 15 to 60 again: 4 times 4 is 16. Motor at 3 000 becomes 187.5 at the output, torque times 16. A third stage: 64. Rule: multiply driven over driver for every mesh; idlers count as one; a shared shaft is not a mesh.

Direction: each mesh flips. Two stages, same way as the motor; three, opposite; an idler flips it back. Speed check: at 16 to 1 a 40 millimetre drum winds about 23 metres a minute, far too fast for a one metre shaft. At 64 to 1, about 6 metres a minute, ten seconds for the lift, which looks right. Pick the ratio for force and for speed.

Hold the two gears on one shaft in mind and try this part's questions: stages multiply.

## Subtopic: Design the Lift

Design backwards from the load. One: weigh the loaded cage, say 300 grams, about 3 newtons on the rope. Two: find what the bare motor can lift at the drum, say 20 grams, 0.2 newtons; for a crank, decide a comfortable finger force. Three: MA needed is 3 over 0.2, which is 15; add a margin for friction and a heavier cage, call it 25. Four: pick stages that multiply to at least 25: 60 over 12 twice is 25; 60 over 15 and 60 over 10 is 24, just short, add a stage. Five: check speed: 3 000 over 25 is 120 rpm, about 15 metres a minute on a 40 millimetre drum, too fast, so go nearer 60 to 1 for 6 metres a minute, or shrink the drum, which also helps the force. Six: check direction, fix with an idler, the winding sense or the motor wires, and write the train down: motor, 12 to 60, shaft, 12 to 60, shaft, 12 to 48, drum, 100 to 1, same direction as the motor, about 4 metres a minute.

The real winder is the same sum scaled up: motor at 1 000 rpm, drum at 25 rpm, ratio 40, two helical stages in an oil bath. The tender board will ask.

Pocket summary of the lesson. MA of a gear pair is driven over driver teeth, or diameters, and equals the velocity ratio. Stages multiply; idlers and shared shafts do not. Design from load over effort plus a margin, then check speed and direction, and write the train as a list.

Hold the list of stages, and take the final questions of the lesson: design the lift.
