### subtopic_1 — From Sketch to Working Drawing: Views and Scale
**Assistant:** Why first-angle projection? Some books put the top view above the front view.

**Tutor:** Both arrangements are valid conventions, and the difference is only where the views are placed on the sheet; the views themselves are identical. First-angle projection, used in South Africa, most of Europe and much of the world under the ISO standards, places each view on the far side of the object from the viewer: you look at the front, and the top view, what you see looking down, is drawn below the front view, as though the object had been rolled forward onto the paper; the view from the left is drawn on the right. Third-angle projection, used in the United States and Canada and in some books from there, places each view on the near side: the top view goes above the front view and the view from the left goes on the left, as though the object sat in a glass box and each view were drawn on the pane nearest the viewer. A drawing must say which it uses, which is why the title block carries a projection symbol, a small truncated cone drawn in two views whose arrangement shows the convention; in first angle the circle view is on the right of the cone's side view, in third angle on the left. Reading a drawing in the wrong convention puts the back-leg on the wrong side of the tower and the winder on the wrong face, which is exactly the kind of error that a mixed set of textbooks produces. Pick first angle, draw the symbol, and keep every drawing in the bid consistent. If you see a third-angle drawing in a source, note it and read it accordingly; the information is the same, only the layout differs.

### subtopic_2 — Dimensioning and Line Conventions for the Headgear
**Assistant:** The back-leg is the hardest part to draw. How do we get its slope right in the front view and its foot right in the plan?

**Tutor:** Work from the two ends, exactly as the lesson says for any sloping member, and let the views build each other. Decide first where the back-leg's top meets the tower: in the front view that is a point at a known height on the tower's rear leg, say 520 millimetres up on a 600 millimetre tower, and you mark it with a dimension from the base. Decide where its foot sits: in the plan that is a point on the base at a known distance behind the tower's rear legs, say 180 millimetres, which must still fit inside the 300 millimetre base constraint, and you dimension it there. Project the foot's position up from the plan to the base line of the front view, draw the line from the foot to the top meeting point, and the slope is set; measure its angle with a protractor and write it only if the builder will need to cut an angled end. The sloping length the builder cuts is read from the drawing with a scale rule, and the parts list carries it, but the drawing dimensions the two ends because those are what the builder actually sets out on the base and the tower. In the side view the back-leg is seen end-on or nearly so, as a short thick line or a narrow rectangle behind the tower, which tells the builder whether there is one central leg or two legs at the tower's width. If there are two back-legs forming a second A, the plan shows both feet with the distance between them and the front view shows them overlapping as one line. Check the geometry against the specifications: the foot position sets how much of the 300 by 300 base the tower occupies, and the angle sets how well the leg resists the rope's pull, since a leg closer to the rope's line works mostly in compression while a very steep leg does little. Most teams find that 30 to 45 degrees from the ground fits the base and does the job, which is close to what real headgear use.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing the Mechanism and the Assembly
**Assistant:** Our model will change as we build it. Does the drawing have to be redrawn every time?

**Tutor:** Not redrawn, but revised, and the revision letter in the title block exists for exactly this. A working drawing is a living document during making: when a dry fit shows that a diagonal is 5 millimetres too long, or the gear plate must move to clear a leg, the drawing is corrected, the revision letter advances from A to B, the date of the change is written in a small revision table on the sheet with a one-line description, and the old dimension is struck through or replaced. Everyone then builds from revision B, and the tender board sees a drawing that matches the model. What you must not do is change the model and leave the drawing as it was; a bid whose drawing says 620 and whose model measures 580 looks careless, and worse, the budget built from the drawing is then wrong. Practical habits help. Draw in pencil so that small changes are clean. Keep the parts list on the same sheet so a change to a length updates the list at the same time. Photograph the drawing before each revision so the history is kept, which also provides the "what changed and why" evidence for the evaluation. Where a change is large, a new leg layout for instance, redraw the affected view only, aligned with the others, rather than the whole sheet. In industry drawings go through many revisions and the letter can reach the second alphabet; a drawing at revision C or D is a sign of a design that was tested, not of a team that could not draw. The rule to carry is simple: the drawing and the model must agree at the moment of presentation, and the revision letter is how you prove it.
