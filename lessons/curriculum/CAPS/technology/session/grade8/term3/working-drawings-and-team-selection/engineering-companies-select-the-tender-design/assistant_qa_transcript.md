### subtopic_1 — The Simulation: Companies Evaluating Sketches
**Assistant:** Why should another team's opinion count? They are competing against us for the same tender.

**Tutor:** Because in this simulation, as in real engineering, the review is separate from the competition, and a company that cannot take a review is not one a board trusts. Two things make the other teams' opinions worth having. First, they see what you cannot. After a week staring at three sketches your team has stopped seeing them; the reviewer who has never seen them before notices in thirty seconds that the back-leg foot sits off the base or that the rope path crosses a diagonal, because they are reading the drawing rather than remembering the idea. That fresh reading is exactly what a design review buys, and it is cheaper to get it now than to discover it with the model half built in Week 8. Second, the review is structured so that rivalry has little room to act. Reviewers score against your specifications, not their taste, on a 0 to 3 scale with the scoring rule fixed; they score alone before agreeing as a team; they sign the sheet; and the written lines must name a feature, a risk and a suggestion in terms of the specifications. A reviewer who marks every row 0 out of spite produces a sheet that is obviously out of line with the others and that the teacher will read; a reviewer who does the job well produces something you can use. It is also true that the competition cuts the other way: the team that reviews you today is reviewed by you, and both have the same incentive to be fair. In practice, teams discover that the best ideas in their final bid often came from a rival's suggestion line, and that is how real engineering works too: review panels, standards committees and tender boards are full of competitors improving one another's work because the alternative is a headgear that fails.

### subtopic_2 — Criteria and Scoring Against the Specifications
**Assistant:** What if our three ideas all score badly on the same specification? Does that mean we start again?

**Tutor:** It usually means the specification has found a real difficulty in your situation, and the response is design, not despair. Suppose all three ideas score 0 or 1 on "does not topple when the cage swings 100 millimetres". That tells you that the problem is not the tower form but the base, because it affects every form the same way; your base is too small, too light, or not anchored. The fixes are in the base: widen the footprint to the full 300 by 300, add mass low down, a card box filled with sand under the winder, clamp or weight the base to the table during the demonstration if the constraints allow, or lower the centre of mass by keeping the motor and gears at the bottom. Any of these can be added to whichever idea you choose, so the low scores do not change the choice between ideas but they do change the design of the winner. Take a different case: all three score low on "winder mechanical advantage at least 25 with a stated direction" because none of the sketches actually shows a gear train with tooth counts. That is not a design flaw; it is a drawing flaw, the sketches did not communicate what you already calculated, and the fix is to put the gear train from your gear sheet onto the working drawing. A third case is the hard one: all three score low because the specification is impossible with the materials and time, say a sway limit of 2 millimetres in a card tower. Then revisit the specification and ask whether it was traceable to a real need; if 10 millimetres would satisfy the brief, change the specification with a note saying why, which is legitimate design work, and if it is genuinely needed, change the material. Starting again is only right if all three ideas have a fatal flaw no fix can reach, and even then the brief and most of the specifications stand. In every case, record what the review found and what you did about it; a decision note that says "all three ideas were marked down on toppling, so we redesigned the base as follows" is strong evidence for the board.

### subtopic_3 — Giving and Receiving Criticism, Then Choosing
**Assistant:** Two of our members want the A-frame and two want the four-legged tower, and the scores are close. How do we decide without a vote?

**Tutor:** You decide by going back to the specifications and the risks, and by asking what a hybrid would look like, and the decision note records the reasoning so that the board, and the two members who did not get their way, can see it was made on evidence. Start with the spread. If the A-frame scored high with every reviewer and the four-legged tower scored high with two and low with one, read the low sheet: a single reviewer naming a real risk, say the rope fouling the inner bracing, is a warning the totals hide. Then weight the rows as the client would. The specifications that carry the client's purpose, lifting safely and standing still, count more than the depth pointer; re-add the scores with lift and stability doubled, which your company should have set before the review, and see whether the close race opens up. Then look at the risk lines for each idea and ask which risks you can test and fix in the two building periods you have. A back-leg that overhangs the base is fixed by a steeper leg in an hour; a rope that fouls the bracing may need the whole inside of the tower rethought. Then consider the hybrid honestly: an A-frame tower with the four-legged idea's winder mounted inside the base, so that the back-leg can be shorter because the rope pull is lower, may satisfy both halves of the team and more of the specifications than either pure idea. If after all that it is still a draw, choose the idea whose biggest risk you understand best, because you can test for it. Write the note: the totals, the weighted totals, the spread, the risks, the hybrid considered, the choice and the three reasons, and the names of the features borrowed from the other idea so its supporters see their work in the bid. A team that decides this way builds the loser's good ideas into the winner; a team that votes builds resentment into the next six weeks.
