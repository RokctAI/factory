# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the simulation in which teams act as engineering companies evaluating sketches and selecting the best idea for the tender bid. The anchor is a Grade 8 classroom in Rustenburg rearranged into four stations, each company's three sketches pinned at a station with its specification table beside them, and the other companies rotating with clipboards and scoring sheets.

## Subtopic: The Simulation: Companies Evaluating Sketches

In engineering practice a design is reviewed before it is built. A design review gathers people who did not do the design, gives them the brief and the specifications, shows them the drawings, and asks them to find the weaknesses the designers can no longer see because they are too close to the work. The reviewers are not rivals in the room; their job is to make the design better, and a company that reviews well is trusted with harder work. In today's simulation each team plays both parts in turn: reviewer of the other companies' ideas and recipient of their review.

The simulation has rules so that it works. Each company's three idea sketches are displayed with the company's own brief and specification table, so reviewers judge the ideas against the designers' stated targets and not against their own preferences. Reviewing teams rotate between stations with a fixed time at each, perhaps eight minutes for three sketches, and a scoring sheet per idea. Reviewers do not talk to the designers during the review; questions are written on the sheet, so that an idea must stand on how clearly it is drawn and annotated, which is why last week insisted the sketches be readable by strangers. Each reviewer scores independently first and the reviewing team then agrees a team score, which reduces the effect of one loud voice. Sheets are signed by the reviewing company and handed to the designing company at the end, so that criticism is owned.

The reviewers look at what a tender board would look at. Does the idea meet each specification, from the evidence in the sketch: is there triangulation in every face, a brace in the direction of the rope's pull, a base that fits 300 by 300 without the leg overhanging, a sheave on an axle, a brake, guides, a gear train with a stated ratio in a place where it can be mounted? Is the rope path clear of the structure? Can it be built from the stated materials in two double periods? What is the biggest risk in this idea, the one thing most likely to make it fail its test? The last question is often the most valuable line a designer receives.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a design review is for, the rules of the simulation, and the questions reviewers ask of each sketch.

## Subtopic: Criteria and Scoring Against the Specifications

Scoring turns opinion into something that can be compared. The scoring sheet lists the designing company's specifications and constraints down the side, taken from their table, and for each gives a score from 0 to 3: 0, the sketch shows the specification is not met or is not addressed; 1, partly met or unclear; 2, met as drawn; 3, met with a feature that makes it robust, such as a brace that both triangulates and resists the rope's pull. Weighting may be added by the designing company before the review: the lift and the stability specifications might count double, because they are the client's purpose, while the depth indicator counts single. The scores are summed, and the sheet also carries three written lines per idea: the strongest feature, the biggest risk, and one suggestion.

Scoring against the designers' own specifications is what keeps the review fair. A reviewer who prefers four-legged towers must still score an A-frame on whether it meets the A-frame company's targets; a reviewer who thinks a brake is unnecessary must still score the brake row, because the specification demands one. The written lines are where preference and experience are allowed in, labelled as suggestions, and the designing company is free to take or leave them.

Numbers do not decide alone. When the sheets come back, the designing company sees three totals, one per idea, from each reviewing company, and the spread matters as much as the sum: an idea that scores high with every reviewer is a safe choice; one that scores high with some and low with others has a feature that divides opinion and needs examining; one that scores low everywhere on the same row has a real flaw. The company reads the written lines for the pattern. If three reviewers name the same risk, the back-leg overhanging the base, the rope fouling a diagonal, a motor at the top making the tower top-heavy, that risk is real whatever the total says. The scoring sheet is evidence; the decision is the company's, and it must be made with reasons the board can follow.

The questions for this section are with you now: the 0 to 3 scoring scale and weighting, why scoring is against the designers' own specifications, and how spread and repeated risks are read.

## Subtopic: Giving and Receiving Criticism, Then Choosing

Giving criticism well is a skill the simulation teaches. The reviewer describes what the sketch shows and what it does not, in terms of the specification: "the side face has no diagonals, so the specification on sway is not met as drawn" rather than "this will fall over". The reviewer names the risk and, where possible, a fix: "the back-leg foot appears to sit 50 millimetres outside the base; a steeper leg braced to the tower would fit". The reviewer credits what works: "the gear plate inside the base keeps the drum low and the rope path clear; strongest feature". Criticism is of the drawing and the design, never of the drawer; the sheets are read by learners and the tone is the tone of a professional review, direct and without contempt.

Receiving criticism well is harder. The designing company reads every sheet before responding to any of it, sorts the comments into agree, disagree with reasons, and need to check, and resists defending each line. A comment that is wrong because the reviewer misread the sketch is still useful: it means the sketch was unclear, and the working drawing must fix that. A comment that stings because it is right is the most useful of all. The company then does what Term 2 taught: fills a comparison table of its three ideas against its specifications, now with the reviewers' scores and risks added as columns, and chooses.

The choice is recorded as a decision note in the portfolio: the idea selected, the totals and spread from the review, the three main reasons for the choice in terms of the specifications, the features borrowed from the rejected ideas or from reviewers' suggestions, and the risks the company has accepted and how it will test them in Week 8. A company may select a hybrid, an A-frame with the four-legged tower's internal winder, if the note explains why; what it may not do is select by vote, by whose sketch it was, or by what looks most impressive. The selected idea goes to the working drawing, which may already be under way for the front-runner and is now revised for the final choice.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reviewing against your own preferences instead of the designers' specifications. Two: comments that attack the person or praise everything, neither of which helps. Three: a designing team that argues with every sheet and changes nothing. Four: a choice made by vote or by seniority with no decision note, which the tender board cannot follow.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to phrase criticism against the specification, how a company sorts and uses the sheets, and what the decision note contains.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with four stations, clipboards and scoring sheets — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Judging Someone Else's Tower

Real engineers get their designs reviewed before building. People who did not do the work read the brief and the rules, look at the drawings, and hunt for the weaknesses the designers can no longer see. Reviewers are not enemies; their job is to make the design better. Today each company is a reviewer of the others and then gets reviewed.

Rules. Each company pins its three sketches with its own brief and rules beside them, so you judge against their targets, not your taste. Teams rotate, fixed time per station, one sheet per idea. No talking to the designers; write questions down, so the sketch must speak for itself. Score alone first, then agree a team score, so one loud voice does not run it. Sign the sheet and hand it over at the end; own what you wrote.

Ask what a tender board would ask. Does the sketch meet each rule: triangles in every face, a brace towards the rope's pull, a base inside 300 by 300 with no overhanging leg, a real sheave, a brake, guides, a gear train with a ratio in a place it can be mounted? Is the rope clear of the frame? Can it be built from these materials in two double periods? And the best question of all: what is the one thing most likely to make it fail?

## Subtopic: Score It, Do Not Vote It

Score so ideas can be compared. The sheet lists the designing company's own rules down the side. For each, 0 to 3: 0 not met or not shown; 1 partly or unclear; 2 met as drawn; 3 met with something that makes it strong, like a brace that does two jobs. The designers may weight rows beforehand: lift and stability double, depth pointer single. Add up. Then three written lines per idea: strongest feature, biggest risk, one suggestion.

Scoring against their rules keeps it fair. If you love four-legged towers you still score the A-frame on its own targets. If you think brakes are pointless you still score the brake row, because the rule demands one. Your opinions go in the suggestion line, labelled as suggestions, to take or leave.

Numbers do not decide alone. Back at your station you see three totals per idea from each reviewer. Look at the spread. High everywhere: safe. High with some, low with others: a feature that splits people; look at it. Low everywhere on one row: a real flaw. Read the written lines for the pattern. If three reviewers name the same risk, the back-leg off the base, the rope catching a diagonal, a top-heavy motor, that risk is real whatever the total says. Sheets are evidence; the decision is yours, with reasons a board can follow.

Hold the three reviewers naming the same risk in mind and try this part's questions: score it, do not vote it.

## Subtopic: Take the Criticism, Make the Call

Give criticism in the language of the rules. Not "this will fall over" but "the side face has no diagonals, so the sway rule is not met as drawn." Name the risk and a fix: "the back-leg foot sits 50 millimetres off the base; a steeper leg braced to the tower would fit." Credit what works: "gear plate inside the base keeps the drum low and the rope clear; strongest feature." Criticise the drawing, never the drawer. Direct, no contempt.

Receiving is harder. Read every sheet before you answer any. Sort into agree, disagree with reasons, need to check. Do not defend every line. A comment that is wrong because they misread your sketch still tells you the sketch was unclear; fix it in the working drawing. A comment that stings because it is right is the most useful thing you will get today. Then do Term 2's table: three ideas against your rules, with the reviewers' scores and risks as extra columns. Choose.

Write a decision note for the portfolio: the idea chosen, the totals and spread, three reasons in terms of the rules, features borrowed from the losers or from suggestions, the risks you accept and how Week 8 will test them. A hybrid is fine if the note explains it. A vote is not. "It was my sketch" is not. "It looks coolest" is not. The chosen idea goes to the working drawing.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A design review by people who did not design it, scored 0 to 3 against the designers' own rules, with the strongest feature, biggest risk and a suggestion written. Read the spread and the repeated risks. Criticise the design, not the person; receive it by sorting, not defending. Decide with a written note the board can follow.

Hold the decision note, and take the final questions of the lesson: take the criticism, make the call.
