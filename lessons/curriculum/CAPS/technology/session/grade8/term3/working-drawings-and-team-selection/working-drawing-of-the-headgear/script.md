# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the two-dimensional working drawing of the headgear showing one or more views with dimensions and line conventions. The anchor is a Grade 8 drawing board in Rustenburg with an A3 sheet divided into three views, a 600 millimetre A-frame headgear drawn at 1 to 5, a 30-60-90 set square, a scale rule and the gear drawing clipped alongside for the winder's centre distances.

## Subtopic: From Sketch to Working Drawing: Views and Scale

A working drawing is the instruction set for making. Unlike the free-hand sketch, which explores an idea, and unlike the isometric, which explains a shape, the working drawing uses flat orthographic views, each looking straight at one face of the object, drawn to scale with instruments, so that every length can be measured and every part made. The Term 1 single-view drawing introduced the conventions; the headgear needs more than one view, because a tower with a sloping back-leg and a sheave at the top cannot be fully described by one face.

The usual set is three views arranged in first-angle projection, the convention used in South Africa: the front view in the centre, the top view directly below it, and the side view to the right, looking at the object from the left. For a headgear the front view looks at the tower face that contains the back-leg, so the A shape, the slope of the leg and the height of the sheave are all visible; the side view shows the tower's width and the bracing in that face; the top view, the plan, shows the base, the footprint of the tower and the back-leg's foot, which is where toppling and the 300 millimetre constraint are checked. Views are aligned: the height of the sheave in the front view is projected across to the side view at the same level, and the width of the base in the front view projects down to the plan.

Scale is chosen so the three views fit the sheet with room for dimensions and a title block. A 600 millimetre tall model on an A3 sheet works at 1 to 4 or 1 to 5, giving a front view 120 to 150 millimetres tall; at 1 to 5 the front and side views each take about a third of the sheet height and the plan sits below. The scale is written in the title block, and all dimensions are written as full-size model dimensions, never scaled values, so the builder reads 600 and cuts 600. If the drawing also serves as the drawing of the real headgear for the budget, a second scale note gives the model-to-real factor, 1 to 100, and the real dimensions are tabulated beside the drawing rather than written on it, to avoid confusion.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the headgear needs three views, how first-angle projection arranges them, and how scale and dimension values are handled.

## Subtopic: Dimensioning and Line Conventions for the Headgear

Line conventions let a reader tell what each line means at a glance, and the headgear uses all the main ones. Thick continuous lines for visible outlines and edges: the legs, the bracing, the sheave beam, the base. Thin continuous lines for dimension lines, extension lines, hatching and leaders. Thin dashed lines for hidden detail: the back-leg's foot behind the base plate in the plan, a shaft passing behind a frame member, the drum inside a housing. Thin chain lines, long dash and short dash, for centre lines: the shaft centre line down the middle of the tower, the axis of the sheave, the axis of each gear shaft in the winder. Thin continuous lines with zigzags or a break symbol where a long member is shortened to fit the sheet. Construction lines are drawn so lightly that they vanish under the final lines or are erased.

Dimensioning follows the Term 1 rules and adds the needs of a frame. Each dimension appears once, on the view where the feature is clearest. Overall dimensions, the height to the sheave centre, the base length and width, go outermost; the positions of the bracing nodes, the points where diagonals meet the legs, go inside them, usually as a chain of dimensions up one leg; the back-leg is dimensioned by the position of its foot on the base and its top on the tower, never by its sloping length, which is non-isometric in the sense of Term 2 and is found by the builder from the two ends; angles are given only where the builder needs to set one, as the angle of the back-leg to the ground, in degrees. Member sizes, "6 mm dowel", "craft stick 10 by 2", are given as notes with leaders or in a parts list, not as dimensions on the view. Dimension lines sit outside the outline where possible, with small arrowheads, figures above the line in millimetres without the unit, and extension lines leaving a small gap from the outline.

The sheave is a circle in the side view with its centre marked and its diameter dimensioned, and a rectangle in the front view; the beam it sits on is dimensioned for length and position. Guides for the cage are two chain lines down the shaft centre in the front view, dimensioned apart. Every joint type that matters, glued lap, pinned, slotted, is called out with a leader and a short note, because the budget and the construction steps will count them.

The questions for this section are with you now: which line type is used for outlines, hidden detail, centre lines and dimensions, how the bracing nodes and the back-leg are dimensioned, and how member sizes are given.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Mechanism and the Assembly

The mechanism is drawn in its place in the structure, so that the drawing shows a headgear with a winder, not a tower and a separate gearbox. In the front view the drum appears as a rectangle at the foot of the back-leg or inside the base, with its shaft as a chain line and its diameter dimensioned; the motor is a rectangle beside it with its shaft on another chain line; the gear train between them is shown as pitch circles in the side view, where the shafts are seen end-on, with tooth counts beside them and the centre distances dimensioned, taken straight from the gear drawing. The rope is a thin line from the drum's edge up to the sheave and down the shaft centre line to a small rectangle for the cage, with the shaft depth dimensioned below the base. The brake, a lever or pawl on the drum shaft, is drawn where it acts, with its pivot dimensioned. The bumper under the sheave beam and the depth pointer on the drum shaft are shown and noted.

Where the winder's detail is too small for the main views, a detail view is added: a part of the drawing enclosed in a circle and redrawn at a larger scale, "DETAIL A, SCALE 1:2", showing the gear train plate with its shaft holes and centre distances, or the brake pawl and its ratchet. A section through the tower at a stated level, marked on the front view by a cutting plane line with arrows, can show how the four legs and the bracing are arranged in plan at that height and how the gear plate is fixed to the frame.

The drawing is completed by a parts list, a table listing every member and component by number, with its description, material, size, length and quantity: "1, leg, 6 mm dowel, 620 long, 4; 2, diagonal, craft stick, 95 long, 24; 3, sheave, 40 mm wheel, plastic, 1; 4, gear 60T, kit, 2". The part numbers are written in circles, balloons, on the views with leaders to the parts. The parts list is the bridge to the budget and the construction steps of the coming weeks: the quantities and lengths give the materials to buy, and the balloons give the order of assembly. The title block holds the drawing title, the company name, the date, the scale, the projection symbol and a revision letter that changes whenever the drawing changes, so that the model is built from the current drawing and not an old one.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: views not aligned, so a height in the front view does not match the side view. Two: the back-leg dimensioned by its sloping length, which the builder cannot set out directly. Three: the mechanism drawn on a separate sheet with no position in the structure, so the rope path and shaft positions are undecided. Four: no parts list, so the budget and the construction steps start from scratch.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the drum, gears, rope and brake are shown in the views, what a detail view and a section add, and what the parts list contains and feeds.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Front, Side and Top

A working drawing is the instruction for building. The sketch explored; the isometric explained; the working drawing is flat views, each looking straight at one face, to scale, with instruments, so every length can be measured and every part made. Term 1 did one view. The headgear needs three, because a tower with a sloping back-leg and a sheave on top cannot be described by one face.

First-angle projection, the South African way: front view in the middle, top view directly below it, side view to the right, looking from the left. Front view: the face with the back-leg, so you see the A shape, the slope and the sheave height. Side view: the tower's width and the bracing on that face. Top view, the plan: the base and the back-leg's foot, where toppling and the 300 millimetre limit are checked. The views line up: sheave height in the front view projects straight across to the side view; base width projects straight down to the plan.

Scale: a 600 millimetre model on A3 fits at 1 to 4 or 1 to 5, front view 120 to 150 millimetres tall. Write the scale in the title block, and write every dimension full size, 600 not 120, so the builder cuts 600. If the same drawing stands for the real headgear, say 1 to 100 as a second note and put the real sizes in a table beside it, not on the drawing.

Hold the three lined-up views in mind and try the questions for this part: front, side and top.

## Subtopic: Numbers the Builder Can Trust

Lines mean things. Thick continuous: what you can see, legs, bracing, beam, base. Thin continuous: dimension and extension lines, leaders. Thin dashed: hidden, the back-leg's foot under the base plate in plan, a shaft behind a member, the drum inside a housing. Thin chain, long-short-long: centre lines, the shaft down the middle, the sheave axis, each gear shaft. Break lines where a long member is shortened to fit. Construction lines so faint they disappear.

Dimensions: each once, on the view where it is clearest. Overall sizes outermost: height to sheave centre, base length and width. Inside them, the bracing nodes, where diagonals meet the legs, as a chain of numbers up one leg. The back-leg by where its foot sits on the base and where its top meets the tower, never by its sloping length; the builder finds that from the two ends. Angles only where the builder must set one, like the back-leg to the ground. Member sizes, "6 mm dowel", as notes with leaders or in the parts list, not on the view. Dimension lines outside the outline, small arrowheads, numbers above the line in millimetres with no unit, extension lines with a small gap from the outline.

Sheave: a circle in the side view, centre marked, diameter given; a rectangle in the front view; its beam dimensioned for length and position. Guide strings: two chain lines down the shaft, dimensioned apart. Joint types, glued lap, pinned, slotted, noted with leaders, because the budget and the steps will count them.

Hold the chain of numbers up the leg in mind and try this part's questions: numbers the builder can trust.

## Subtopic: Where the Winder Sits

Draw the winder where it lives. Front view: drum as a rectangle at the foot of the back-leg or inside the base, shaft as a chain line, diameter given; motor as a rectangle beside it on its own chain line. Side view, where the shafts are end-on: the gears as pitch circles with tooth counts and centre distances straight off the gear drawing. The rope: a thin line from the drum edge up to the sheave and down the centre line to a small cage rectangle, with the shaft depth dimensioned below the base. Brake lever or pawl drawn where it acts, pivot dimensioned. Bumper under the sheave beam and depth pointer on the drum shaft shown and noted.

Too small to read? Add a detail: circle the area, redraw it bigger, "DETAIL A, SCALE 1:2", the gear plate with its holes and distances, or the pawl and ratchet. A section: a cutting line with arrows on the front view and a separate view showing the legs and bracing in plan at that height and how the gear plate fixes to the frame.

Finish with the parts list: every member and part numbered, with description, material, size, length, quantity. "1, leg, 6 mm dowel, 620 long, 4. 2, diagonal, craft stick, 95 long, 24. 3, sheave, 40 mm plastic wheel, 1. 4, gear 60T, kit, 2." Numbers in little circles on the views with leaders. That list is next week's shopping list and build order. Title block: title, company, date, scale, projection symbol, and a revision letter that changes every time the drawing changes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Three aligned views in first-angle projection to a stated scale, dimensions full size and each given once, line types for visible, hidden, centre and dimension lines. The back-leg by its ends, bracing nodes up the leg, member sizes as notes. The winder, rope, brake and sheave drawn in place, details and sections where needed, and a numbered parts list that feeds the budget and the build.

Hold the balloon numbers on the parts, and take the final questions of the lesson: where the winder sits.
