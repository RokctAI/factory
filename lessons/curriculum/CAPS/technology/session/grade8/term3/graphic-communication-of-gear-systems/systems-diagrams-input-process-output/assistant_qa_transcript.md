### subtopic_1 — Systems Thinking: Input, Process, Output
**Assistant:** Why does the systems diagram leave out the gears themselves? Surely the gears are the whole point.

**Tutor:** The gears are the whole point of the gear drawing, and that is why the systems diagram does not repeat them. The two documents answer different questions for different readers. The gear drawing answers "how is it built?" with pitch circles, centre distances and tooth counts, and it is read by whoever makes the thing. The systems diagram answers "what does it do?" with inputs and outputs and the change between them, and it is read by whoever has to decide whether the thing does the job: the client, the tender board, the safety inspector, and the team itself when it checks the design against the brief. A board member who sees "input: motor 3 000 rpm; process: reduction 64:1; output: drum 47 rpm, lifts 3 N at 6 m per minute" can check in seconds whether the lift speed and load meet the specification, without knowing what a pitch circle is. The process box does name the mechanism and its ratio, so the gears are present as a fact about the system; what is left out is their geometry, which does not change the answer to "what does it do?" There is a deeper reason too. Systems thinking lets you swap the inside of a box without redrawing the system: if the team replaces a three-stage spur train with a two-stage train and a bevel pair that gives the same 64 to 1, the systems diagram is unchanged except for the words in the process box, and everything downstream still holds. Engineers design at the systems level first, deciding what each box must do, and only then draw the parts inside each box; the two documents together are the design, and the numbers must agree between them.

### subtopic_2 — Systems Diagram for a Gear System With MA 4:1
**Assistant:** Does the diagram have to have exactly three boxes? Our winder has a battery, a motor, three gear stages, a drum and a brake.

**Tutor:** Three is the minimum, not the maximum, and your winder's diagram should have as many boxes as it has distinct stages that change the flow, arranged so that the three roles are still clear. The energy source is a legitimate box before the input: "battery, 3 V, electrical energy" with an arrow into "motor, 3 000 rpm, low torque, clockwise", which is the mechanical input to the gear system. Each gear stage is its own process box because each changes the speed and torque by its own ratio, and writing the intermediate speeds on the arrows between them, 3 000 to 750 to 187 to 47 revolutions per minute, is exactly what a reader checking the arithmetic wants to see; a bevel stage gets a box that also says "axis turned 90 degrees". The drum is the output of the gear system and the beginning of the lifting system: "drum, 20 mm diameter, 47 rpm, winds 3 m of rope per minute" leads to "cage rises at 3 m per minute carrying 3 N". The brake is different: it does not sit in the chain of motion but acts on it, so it is drawn as a box beside the drum with an arrow pointing at the drum box and labelled "brake: locks drum when motor stops", and in a system with a safety catch on the cage a second such box points at the cage. Keep the boxes in one line where you can, equal in size, and keep the title above the whole chain; if it runs long, break it after the gear system and continue on a second line with an arrow linking them. The test of a good diagram is that every arrow carries a number and every number can be traced to your calculation sheet; a diagram with eight honest boxes is far better than one with three vague ones.

### subtopic_3 — Systems Diagram for a Train Whose Output Is Faster
**Assistant:** In the exam, a systems diagram had the output speed blank and the input was 600 rpm through 15T driving 45T. How do I fill it in, and what else should go in the box?

**Tutor:** Work the ratio from the teeth first, then apply it to speed and torque in opposite directions, then settle the direction. Velocity ratio is driven over driver teeth, 45 over 15, which is 3, so this is a reducer with mechanical advantage 3. Output speed is input speed divided by the velocity ratio: 600 over 3, 200 revolutions per minute. Output torque is input torque times the mechanical advantage, three times the input, less friction, and if the input torque is given as a number, multiply it; if it is given only as "low", write "three times the input torque". Direction: one mesh reverses rotation, so if the input is clockwise the output is anticlockwise, and if the diagram shows an idler the output is clockwise again with the ratio unchanged. The output box should therefore read something like "driven gear 45T: 200 rpm, anticlockwise, torque 3 times input, less friction", and it is worth adding the one-line meaning, "slower and stronger", because the marker is checking that you understand what the numbers mean, not only that you can divide. Two traps. If the question had the large gear as the driver, 45T driving 15T, the ratio is 15 over 45, a third, and the output is faster, 1 800 revolutions per minute with a third of the torque; read which gear the input arrow enters before you divide. And if units are mixed, revolutions per second on one side and per minute on the other, convert before you fill the blank. Finally, check the whole diagram for consistency: the process box should state the same ratio you used, and if it does not, say which you believe and why, because an examiner's diagram can carry a deliberate inconsistency to see whether you notice.
