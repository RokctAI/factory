# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers systems diagrams of input, process and output for a gear system with mechanical advantage 4:1 and for a gear train whose driven gear turns faster than the driver. The anchor is a Grade 8 whiteboard in Rustenburg with three boxes chalked in a row, a toy motor taped beside the first, a plastic gear pair beside the second and a cardboard drum with a thread and a paper-clip cage beside the third.

## Subtopic: Systems Thinking: Input, Process, Output

A system is a set of parts that work together to do a job, and systems thinking describes the job without describing every part. The simplest systems diagram has three boxes joined by arrows: input, what goes into the system, which for a mechanical system is usually a force or a motion with a speed and a direction; process, what the system does to the input, the mechanism and the change it makes; and output, what comes out, again a force or motion, now changed. The arrows show the direction in which energy, motion or information flows. A fourth element, feedback, is added when part of the output is used to adjust the input, as a thermostat does, but the gear systems of this term are open systems without feedback and need only the three boxes.

The convention is simple and strict. Boxes are rectangles of equal size drawn with a ruler, arranged left to right, each labelled with one or two words in its top line and a short description beneath: not "gear" but "process: gear pair 12T driving 48T". Arrows run from box to box with their heads on the receiving box, and quantities are written along them, speed in revolutions per minute, force in newtons, direction. Where a system has more than one process in sequence, the process boxes follow one another, each with its own arrow, which is how a multistage gear train is shown. The whole diagram has a title stating the purpose: "winder: lift cage up shaft".

Systems diagrams matter because they separate what a mechanism is for from how it is built. The gear drawing of last session shows how; the systems diagram shows why, and a reader who sees "input: motor 3 000 rpm, low torque; process: gear train 64:1; output: drum 47 rpm, high torque, lifts 3 N cage at 6 m per minute" understands the winder before seeing a single circle. In a bid, the systems diagram is read by the board members who are not engineers, and it is the frame on which the flow chart of the whole headgear, from motor to cage, is built.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three boxes and what each contains, the drawing convention, and why a systems diagram is drawn separately from the gear drawing.

## Subtopic: Systems Diagram for a Gear System With MA 4:1

The first required diagram is a gear system with a mechanical advantage of 4 to 1. Three boxes. Input: rotation from a driver, for instance a hand crank or motor turning the 12T driver clockwise at 400 revolutions per minute with low torque. Process: a gear pair, 12T driver meshing with a 48T driven gear; velocity ratio 48 over 12, 4 to 1; mechanical advantage 4; direction reversed by one mesh. Output: rotation of the 48T gear's shaft anticlockwise at 100 revolutions per minute with four times the input torque, less friction. The arrows are labelled with the speeds and the direction, and the title reads "gear system, MA 4:1: speed reduced four times, torque multiplied four times".

Every number in the diagram connects to the calculations of the mechanical advantage session. The output speed is the input speed divided by the velocity ratio. The output torque is the input torque times the mechanical advantage, and "less friction" is written to keep the diagram honest. The direction changes because a single mesh reverses rotation, and if an idler were added for same-direction output the process box would say so and the ratio would be unchanged. If the mechanical advantage of 4 is delivered by two stages of 2, the process box becomes two boxes in sequence, each with its own ratio, and the arrow between them carries the intermediate speed, 200 revolutions per minute.

This is the shape of the winder's diagram. Input: motor, fast, weak. Process: reduction gear train, ratio stated, direction stated. Output: drum, slow, strong, lifting a stated load at a stated speed. A systems diagram may also have a box before the input for the energy source, the battery supplying electrical energy to the motor, and a box after the output for the final effect, the rope winding onto the drum and the cage rising. The team decides how far to extend the chain, but the three central boxes are always present and always labelled with numbers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three boxes for the MA 4:1 system with their numbers, how a two-stage process is shown, and how the winder's diagram follows the same shape.

## Subtopic: Systems Diagram for a Train Whose Output Is Faster

The second required diagram is a gear train whose driven gear turns faster than the driver. Input: rotation of a 48T driver turning clockwise at 100 revolutions per minute with high torque, perhaps from a hand crank. Process: gear pair, 48T driver meshing with 12T driven gear; velocity ratio 12 over 48, 1 to 4; mechanical advantage 0.25; direction reversed. Output: rotation of the 12T gear anticlockwise at 400 revolutions per minute with one quarter the input torque, less friction. The title reads "gear train, driven faster than driver: speed increased four times, torque reduced to a quarter". The hand drill, the egg beater and the bicycle are its real cases, and the diagram shows at a glance that this system trades force for speed.

The two diagrams are mirror images, and drawing them side by side is the best way to see that a gear pair is neutral and the direction of drive decides what it does. The same 12T and 48T gears appear in both; in the first the small gear is the input and the system is a force multiplier, in the second the large gear is the input and the system is a speed multiplier. In an examination the question may give a systems diagram with gaps and ask for the missing numbers, give a gear drawing and ask for the systems diagram, or give a purpose and ask which of two diagrams fits it; the method is always to find the ratio from the teeth, apply it to speed one way and torque the other, and flip direction at each mesh.

For the bid, the team now draws the complete systems diagram of the headgear winder, which the tender board calls the flow chart. It begins with the energy source, battery or hand, then the input, motor or crank with its speed; the process boxes, each stage of the train with its ratio, the bevel pair if used with its angle, the drum with its diameter; the output, drum speed and torque; and the final effect, the cage rising at a stated speed with a stated load, plus a box for the brake or safety catch acting on the drum. Every number comes from the design calculation and matches the gear drawing, which is how the three documents are checked against one another.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting the gears in the input box; the gears are the process, the motion into them is the input. Two: writing the output speed as input times the ratio for a reducer instead of divided by it. Three: leaving the direction off, so the diagram cannot tell a reader which way the drum turns. Four: a flow chart whose numbers disagree with the gear drawing and the calculation, which a board member will notice.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three boxes for the faster-output train with their numbers, why the two diagrams mirror each other, and what the full winder flow chart contains.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with three chalked boxes and a motor, a gear pair and a drum beside them — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Boxes and Arrows

A system is parts working together to do a job, and a systems diagram tells the job without the parts. Three boxes, left to right, joined by arrows. Input: what goes in, for a mechanism a motion or force, with its speed and direction. Process: what the system does to it, the mechanism and the change it makes. Output: what comes out, changed. Arrows show which way the motion flows. Thermostats add feedback; gears this term do not, so three boxes is enough.

Rules: equal rectangles drawn with a ruler, one or two words on top, a short line underneath, like "process: gear pair 12T driving 48T". Arrow heads on the box receiving. Numbers along the arrows: rpm, newtons, clockwise or anticlockwise. More than one process? More boxes in a row. A title saying the purpose: "winder: lift cage up shaft".

Why bother, when you have the gear drawing? The gear drawing says how; the systems diagram says why. "Motor 3 000 rpm weak in; gear train 64 to 1; drum 47 rpm strong out, lifts 3 newtons at 6 metres a minute" tells a board member who is not an engineer exactly what your winder does. It is also the skeleton of the flow chart for the whole headgear.

Hold the three chalk boxes in mind and try the questions for this part: boxes and arrows.

## Subtopic: Slow Strong Box

Diagram one: MA 4 to 1. Input: crank or motor turns the 12T driver clockwise at 400 rpm, low torque. Process: 12T meshes with 48T; VR 48 over 12, 4 to 1; MA 4; one mesh, so direction flips. Output: the 48T shaft turns anticlockwise at 100 rpm with four times the torque, less friction. Title: gear system MA 4 to 1, speed down four times, torque up four times.

The numbers are last week's sums. Output speed is input speed divided by the ratio. Output torque is input torque times the MA, and "less friction" keeps it honest. One mesh flips direction; an idler would flip it back and the process box would say so. Two stages of 2 instead of one of 4? Two process boxes in a row, 200 rpm written on the arrow between them.

This is your winder's shape. Fast weak motor in; reduction train, ratio and direction stated; slow strong drum out, lifting a stated load at a stated speed. You can add a box before for the battery and a box after for the cage rising. The middle three always stay, always with numbers.

Hold the 400 rpm in and 100 rpm out in mind and try this part's questions: slow strong box.

## Subtopic: Fast Weak Box

Diagram two: driven gear faster. Input: 48T driver turns clockwise at 100 rpm, high torque, a hand crank perhaps. Process: 48T meshes with 12T; VR 12 over 48, 1 to 4; MA 0.25; direction flips. Output: the 12T turns anticlockwise at 400 rpm with a quarter of the torque, less friction. Title: gear train, driven faster than driver, speed up four times, torque down to a quarter. Hand drill, egg beater, bicycle.

Put the two diagrams side by side. Same two gears. Small one in: force multiplier. Big one in: speed multiplier. The gears are neutral; who drives decides. Exams give a diagram with blanks, or a gear drawing and ask for the diagram, or a job and ask which diagram fits. Always: ratio from teeth, speed one way, torque the other, flip at each mesh.

Now the bid. Draw the full winder as a chain: battery or hand; motor or crank with speed; each gear stage with its ratio; bevel pair and angle if you have one; drum and its diameter; drum speed and torque; cage rising at its speed with its load; and a brake box acting on the drum. Every number from the calculation, every number matching the gear drawing. That is the flow chart the board sees.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Input, process, output, in ruled boxes with numbers on the arrows and a title stating the job. MA 4 to 1: 400 rpm weak in, 100 rpm strong out, direction flipped. Faster output: 100 rpm strong in, 400 rpm weak out. The winder flow chart chains every stage from battery to rising cage.

Hold the two mirror diagrams, and take the final questions of the lesson: fast weak box.
