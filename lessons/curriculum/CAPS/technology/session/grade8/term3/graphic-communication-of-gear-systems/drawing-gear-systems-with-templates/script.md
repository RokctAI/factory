# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing gear systems with templates or compasses: counter-rotation, same direction with an idler, and faster and slower driven gears. The anchor is a Grade 8 drawing board in Rustenburg with a compass, a circle template, a 30-60-90 set square and a sheet on which a learner is drawing the two-stage train for the team's winder, each gear a crisp circle with its tooth count beside it.

## Subtopic: Conventions for Drawing Gears

Technical drawings use conventions so that a gear train can be drawn quickly and read without ambiguity. A gear is represented by its pitch circle, the circle through the middle of the teeth on which two meshing gears roll against each other, drawn as a thin continuous line, or in formal drawings as a thin chain line, with its centre marked by a small cross. Teeth are not drawn individually except in a detail or a pictorial; instead the number of teeth is written beside the gear, as "40T", and if needed the outside diameter is shown as a second circle slightly larger than the pitch circle. Because two meshing gears have the same tooth size, their pitch diameters are proportional to their tooth counts, so once a scale is chosen, 1 millimetre of pitch diameter per tooth for instance, every gear's circle size follows from its teeth, and two meshing gears are drawn with their pitch circles exactly touching at one point.

Direction of rotation is shown by a curved arrow around part of each gear's circle, clockwise or anticlockwise, and the driver is labelled "driver" or "input" with the driven gear labelled "driven" or "output". Shafts are shown as small circles at the centres, or as lines if the drawing is a side view. If two gears are fixed on one shaft in a compound train, they are drawn concentric in the front view, as two circles sharing a centre, and side by side in a side view with a line for the common shaft. Idlers are labelled. Bevel gears, which are not in this session's set, appear in side view as trapeziums with apexes meeting.

The drawing also carries the numbers. Beside or below the train go the velocity ratio, the mechanical advantage, and a note of what the train does: "VR 4:1, driven gear turns at one quarter the speed of the driver". A title block gives the drawing's name, the team, the date and the scale. Circles are drawn with a compass for larger gears and with a circle template for small ones, lightly first for construction and then firmed; free-hand circles are not acceptable in a working drawing, because the point of the drawing is that distances and sizes can be read from it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the pitch circle convention, how tooth count and direction are shown, and how concentric gears on one shaft are drawn.

## Subtopic: Drawing Counter-Rotation and an Idler Train

The first drawing is the basic pair. Choose a scale, say 1 millimetre per tooth of pitch diameter, so a 20-tooth gear has a 20 millimetre pitch circle and a 40-tooth gear a 40 millimetre one. Mark the centre of the driver. The distance between the centres of two meshing gears is the sum of their pitch radii, here 10 plus 20, so 30 millimetres; measure that along a horizontal construction line and mark the second centre. Draw both circles with the compass; they should touch at exactly one point on the line between the centres. Label the driver 20T and the driven gear 40T, draw a clockwise arrow on the driver and an anticlockwise arrow on the driven gear, and write "counter-rotation; VR 40/20 = 2:1; driven turns at half speed, twice the torque". Equal gears drawn the same way show counter-rotation with VR 1:1.

The second drawing adds an idler. Three centres on a line, with each pair of neighbours spaced by the sum of their radii: a 20T driver, a 12T idler and a 20T driven gear give centre distances of 16 millimetres and 16 millimetres. Draw the three circles, check each pair touches, and add arrows: driver clockwise, idler anticlockwise, driven gear clockwise. Label the middle gear "idler" and write "same direction at ends; VR 20/20 = 1:1; idler does not affect ratio". If the task asks for the idler to bridge a gap between two fixed shafts, the idler's size is found from the gap: its diameter is the gap between the two outer pitch circles, which is a useful exercise in reading a drawing backwards.

Both drawings should be done with construction lines first, light and thin, then the circles and arrows firmed, and then the labels in neat capitals. Check that the circles touch and do not overlap or leave a gap, which on the drawing means the gears would jam or not mesh, and check that the arrows alternate at every mesh. A common assessment task is to give a drawing with the driver's arrow and ask the learner to add the others; the rule, flip at every mesh, never fails.

The questions for this section are with you now: how centre distances are found from pitch radii, drawing the counter-rotating pair, and drawing the three-gear idler train with its arrows.

## Subtopic: Drawing Faster and Slower Driven Gears

The third and fourth drawings show ratio. A slower driven gear: a small driver, 12T, meshing with a large driven gear, 48T. At 1 millimetre per tooth the pitch circles are 12 and 48 millimetres and the centre distance is 6 plus 24, which is 30 millimetres. Draw, label, arrow, and write "VR 48/12 = 4:1; driven gear turns at one quarter the speed of the driver, with four times the torque, MA 4". This is the winder's arrangement, and it may be drawn with a second stage: a 12T gear concentric with the 48T, drawn as a small circle inside it sharing the centre, driving a second 48T gear whose centre is another 30 millimetres along; the drawing then reads "two-stage compound train, VR 4 x 4 = 16:1, output same direction as input".

A faster driven gear: a large driver, 48T, meshing with a small driven gear, 12T. The same two circles with the roles swapped; the driver label moves to the large gear, the arrows still alternate, and the note reads "VR 12/48 = 1:4; driven gear turns four times faster, with one quarter the torque, MA 0.25". The hand drill and the bicycle are its examples. Drawing the pair both ways on one sheet makes the point that the ratio belongs to the direction of drive, not to the gears alone.

Scale and layout need a decision before drawing starts. A train with a 48T gear at 1 millimetre per tooth fits an A4 sheet easily; a model with 60T gears and three stages may need half a millimetre per tooth, or a scale of 1:2, written in the title block. Centres are usually laid along a horizontal line for clarity, but a compound train may be folded, with the second stage's centre above or below the line, if that is how the model's frame places the shafts; the drawing then shows the real layout, which is what a working drawing is for. The finished sheet, with its four arrangements or with the team's own winder train, becomes part of the bid's drawings and is the source for the systems diagram next session.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing every tooth, which wastes time and hides the pitch circle that matters. Two: circles that overlap or leave a gap, so the gears as drawn would jam or miss. Three: arrows that do not alternate at a mesh. Four: a ratio written without saying which gear is the driver, so the drawing cannot be read.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing the slower and faster arrangements, how a compound stage is shown, and how scale and layout are chosen.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a compass, a circle template and a sheet of crisp circles — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Circles That Stand for Gears

A gear on a drawing is a circle, not a picture of teeth. The circle is the pitch circle, the line through the middle of the teeth where two gears roll on each other. Thin line, a small cross at the centre, and the number of teeth written beside it: "40T". Meshing gears have the same tooth size, so the circle's size follows the teeth; pick a scale, say 1 millimetre per tooth, and a 40-tooth gear is a 40 millimetre circle. Two meshing gears are drawn with their circles just touching at one point.

Direction: a curved arrow round part of each circle, clockwise or anticlockwise. Label the driver and the driven gear. Shafts are little circles at the centres. Two gears on one shaft in a compound train are drawn as two circles with the same centre. Label idlers.

Write the numbers beside the train: velocity ratio, MA, and what it does in a sentence. Title block: name, team, date, scale. Use a compass for big circles and a template for small ones, light first, then firm. No free-hand circles; the whole point is that sizes can be measured off the drawing.

Hold the circle with 40T beside it in mind and try the questions for this part: circles that stand for gears.

## Subtopic: Two Gears, Then Three

Drawing one: two gears. Scale 1 millimetre per tooth: 20T is a 20 millimetre circle, 40T is 40. Mark the driver's centre. Centre distance is the two radii added, 10 plus 20, 30 millimetres along a faint horizontal line. Draw both circles; they must just kiss. Label 20T and 40T, arrow the driver clockwise and the driven gear anticlockwise, and write: counter-rotation, VR 40 over 20 is 2 to 1, driven turns at half speed, twice the torque.

Drawing two: add an idler. 20T, 12T, 20T on a line, centres 16 and 16 apart. Three circles, each pair kissing. Arrows: clockwise, anticlockwise, clockwise. Label the middle one idler and write: same direction at the ends, VR 1 to 1, idler does not change the ratio. If the two outer shafts are fixed and you must find the idler, its diameter is the gap between the two outer circles.

Always: faint construction lines, then firm circles and arrows, then neat capital labels. Check the circles touch, no overlap, no gap. Check arrows flip at every mesh. A favourite test: here is the driver's arrow, add the rest. Flip at every mesh and you cannot go wrong.

Hold the three kissing circles in mind and try this part's questions: two gears, then three.

## Subtopic: Small to Big, Big to Small

Drawing three: slower output. Small driver 12T, big driven gear 48T, circles 12 and 48, centres 30 apart. Label, arrow, and write: VR 48 over 12 is 4 to 1, driven gear at a quarter speed, four times the torque, MA 4. That is your winder. Add a second stage: a 12T circle inside the 48T sharing its centre, driving another 48T 30 millimetres further on. Write: two-stage compound, 4 times 4 is 16 to 1, output same direction as input.

Drawing four: faster output. Same two circles, roles swapped: the 48T is now the driver. Arrows still alternate. Write: VR 12 over 48 is 1 to 4, driven gear four times faster, a quarter the torque, MA 0.25. Hand drill, bicycle. Draw both on one sheet and you see that the ratio belongs to which gear drives.

Before you start, choose scale and layout. 48T at 1 millimetre per tooth fits A4; 60T gears in three stages may need half a millimetre per tooth, written as the scale. Centres along a horizontal line is clearest, but if your frame puts the second stage above the first, draw it there; a working drawing shows the real layout. This sheet goes into the bid and feeds next week's systems diagram.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gears are pitch circles with tooth counts, centres marked, arrows for direction, driver and driven labelled. Centre distance is the sum of the radii; circles kiss. Arrows flip at every mesh; idlers keep the ratio. Write VR and MA beside the train and say which gear drives.

Hold the two drawings with the roles swapped, and take the final questions of the lesson: small to big, big to small.
