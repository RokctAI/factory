### subtopic_1 — Conventions for Drawing Gears
**Assistant:** Why draw the pitch circle rather than the outside of the gear? The outside is what you can see.

**Tutor:** Because the pitch circle is the one that carries the information an engineer needs, and the outside diameter does not. Two meshing gears roll against each other on their pitch circles; those circles touch at exactly one point, their diameters are in the same ratio as the tooth counts, and the distance between the gears' centres is the sum of their pitch radii. Everything you calculate about a train, the ratio, the speeds, the shaft spacing, comes off the pitch circles. The outside diameters, by contrast, overlap when gears mesh, because each gear's teeth reach into the gaps of the other; two outside circles drawn touching would represent gears that are too far apart to mesh, and two drawn at the correct centre distance would cross each other on the page, which looks like an error and tells you nothing about the ratio. Drawing teeth individually has the same problem magnified: it takes a long time, it is almost impossible to draw them so that they actually interlock on paper, and the one fact the drawing exists to convey, how many teeth there are, is easier to read as "40T" than by counting forty tiny shapes. Engineering drawing conventions exist to strip a drawing down to the lines that carry meaning, and for gears that is the pitch circle, the centre, the tooth count and the direction. When the outside size matters, for instance to check that a gear will clear the frame, it is added as a second, slightly larger circle, often as a thin dashed line, and labelled; the pitch circle stays as the working line.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing Counter-Rotation and an Idler Train
**Assistant:** How do I find the idler size if the question gives me two fixed shafts that are too far apart to mesh directly?

**Tutor:** Treat it as a geometry problem on the drawing and read the answer off the page, which is a skill the examination tests. Draw the two outer gears first, each as its pitch circle around its given centre at the chosen scale, say 1 millimetre per tooth. Measure the gap between the two circles along the line joining their centres; that gap is the pitch diameter the idler must have, because the idler must touch both outer circles. Suppose the shafts are 46 millimetres apart and the gears are 20T and 24T; their radii are 10 and 12 millimetres, so the gap is 46 minus 22, which is 24 millimetres, and the idler is a 24T gear with its centre 22 millimetres from the first shaft, 10 plus 12, and 24 millimetres from the second, 12 plus 12. Draw it, check all three circles kiss, add the alternating arrows and label the idler. Then check the tooth count is one the kit actually has; if the gap works out to 23 millimetres and the kit has only 20T and 24T gears, the idler cannot sit on that line, and a real designer would move one shaft or place the idler off the line, where two centre distances fix its position by intersecting arcs with the compass, which is the same construction you used for triangles in Mathematics. Remember to write that the idler changes direction only and leaves the ratio at 24 over 20, 1.2 to 1. The exercise also shows why idlers are useful: they let a designer connect shafts wherever the structure needs them, which in your headgear is the motor on the frame and the drum at the top.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing Faster and Slower Driven Gears
**Assistant:** Our winder train has three stages and does not fit on the sheet in a straight line. What should the drawing look like?

**Tutor:** It should look like the train actually looks, folded to fit the frame, which is both allowed and expected in a working drawing. A straight line of centres is a teaching convenience that makes the ratios easy to see; a real gearbox folds its stages back and forth so that the input and output shafts end up where the machine needs them and the box stays compact. On your drawing, place the motor pinion's centre where the motor sits on the model's frame, place the drum's centre where the drum sits, and lay the intermediate shafts between them at whatever positions keep every meshing pair at its correct centre distance. The construction is always the same: for each new gear, its centre lies on a circle of radius equal to the sum of the two pitch radii around the gear it meshes with, so you swing an arc with the compass and choose the point on that arc that fits the frame. Concentric gears on one shaft are drawn as two circles sharing a centre, and it helps to draw the small gear with a slightly heavier line or to label the pair "12T and 48T on shaft B" so a reader knows they are one piece. If the stages are stacked so that one gear hides behind another in the front view, add a side view: shafts as horizontal lines, gears as rectangles standing on them, with the meshing pairs aligned, which shows the order of the gears along the shafts and is exactly how gearbox drawings are made. Choose a scale so the whole train fits with room for labels, half a millimetre per tooth if needed, and write it in the title block. Finally, write the overall ratio, the direction of the output relative to the input, and the lift speed beside the train, so the drawing and the design calculation agree.
