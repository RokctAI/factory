### subtopic_1 — Listing Tools and Materials From the Parts List
**Assistant:** Why build the tower faces flat on the bench and then stand them up, rather than building the tower upright from the base?

**Tutor:** Because flat on the bench you can make the face accurate, and accuracy at this stage decides whether the tower stands straight and resists sway. A tower face is a tall, thin rectangle of two legs and a set of diagonals, and its job is to be a stiff plane. Lying flat on the bench over the drawing, or over a full-size layout drawn on paper, each leg can be placed exactly on its line, each diagonal cut to fit between the legs at the node positions from the drawing, and the whole face checked for squareness with a set square and for straightness with a rule before the glue sets, while clamps or weights hold everything still. Gravity is your friend: the parts lie where you put them. Building upright from the base, every leg must be held vertical by hand while glue sets, diagonals are fitted into a frame that is moving, and errors accumulate upward so that the top of the tower ends up out of line with the base and the sheave sits off the shaft centre. Real steel headgear are built the same way at a larger scale: sections of the frame are fabricated flat in a workshop, trial assembled, transported and then lifted into place by crane and bolted, which is why the budget had a large crane and why the construction steps listed fabrication before erection. Two faces built flat and then joined by cross members gives you a box tower that is square by construction; the remaining diagonals in the other two faces are fitted once it stands, where they are short and easy to cut to fit. The one thing to plan is the glue time: the faces need to set fully before they are stood up, which is why the timetable puts them before a break.

### subtopic_2 — Logical Construction Steps: Sequence and Dependencies
**Assistant:** What if a step on the critical path goes wrong, say a tower face is glued out of square and sets before we notice?

**Tutor:** First, the plan should make this unlikely, which is what the decision diamond "tower plumb and square?" placed before the glue sets is for; a check at the right moment is cheaper than any repair. If it still happens, the response depends on how far out it is and how much time is left, and the company decides quickly rather than arguing. A face a few millimetres out of square can usually be corrected when the two faces are joined: the cross members and the diagonals of the other two faces pull the tower true as they are fitted, if they are cut to the drawing lengths and the tower is checked plumb against a square before those joints set; that is one of the virtues of triangulation, a stiff box tends towards its correct shape. A face badly out, so that the legs are visibly not parallel or the diagonals are in the wrong places, is better rebuilt than rescued, and this is where the spare material on the list and the slack in the timetable earn their place: cut new diagonals, re-glue flat over the drawing, and let the other members continue on parallel steps, the gear plate, the cage, the sheave beam, so that no one is idle while it sets. What you do not do is build the rest of the tower onto a face you know is wrong, because every later step inherits the error and the sheave ends up off centre, the cage rubs the guides and the sway test fails. Record what happened and how it was fixed in the making record with a photograph, because the evaluation in Week 8 asks what went wrong and what the team did, and the tender board respects a team that caught and corrected a fault more than one that claims there were none. Then adjust the timetable: move the test lift later if necessary and shorten the finishing, but protect the test, because a model that has not lifted a load cannot be presented.

### subtopic_3 — The Flow Chart and the Build Timetable
**Assistant:** Why does the flow chart need decision diamonds? Our steps are just a list of things to do.

**Tutor:** Because a list of things to do assumes everything works, and a real build does not. The decision diamond is where the plan says "check, and if the check fails, here is what to do", which is the difference between a plan and a hope. Consider the three diamonds in the headgear chart. "Gears turn freely?" sits after the gear plate is drilled and the shafts fitted dry. If the answer is yes, the team moves on and glues nothing it will regret. If no, the chart says "adjust mesh": a hole slightly off, a shaft bent, gears pressed too tight, found and fixed in two minutes while the plate is still loose and before the motor, the drum and the frame are built around it; discovering the same fault in the final test lift means dismantling half the model. "Tower plumb and square?" sits before the glue sets, for the reason the previous question explained. "Lifts 300 grams in 10 to 20 seconds?" is the specification test, and its no branch lists the likely causes in the order to check them, batteries, drum size, friction, ratio, so that the team diagnoses systematically instead of guessing. The diamonds also communicate. The tender notice asked for a flow chart because a board wants to see that a company builds with checks, which is how mine construction is regulated: holding points at which an inspector must sign before work continues. A chart with diamonds shows that; a plain list does not. And they help the team on the day, when the room is noisy and the knives are out: a member who reaches a diamond knows to stop and check rather than carry on, which is exactly the habit that keeps a real site safe. Three or four diamonds are enough; one at every box is clutter. Put them where a fault would be expensive to find later, and write the no branch as a specific action.
