### subtopic_1 — What a Realistic Budget Is and Why the Board Wants One
**Assistant:** Why would a tender board be suspicious of the cheapest bid? Surely the client wants to pay less.

**Tutor:** The client wants to pay the least for a headgear that is actually finished and safe, which is not the same as accepting the lowest number on the day. Boards have learned, often painfully, that a bid far below the others usually means one of three things. The company has left something out, labour, foundations, the crane, the tests, and will discover it after the contract is signed, at which point the client faces either paying for it as a variation, so the real price was never the low one, or a half-built headgear and a contractor walking away. The company has priced with unrealistic rates, cheap steel that does not meet the specification or wages below what skilled erectors accept, and the work will be late, poor or unsafe. Or the company knows exactly what it is doing and intends to claim its way back to a normal price through disputes, which is a strategy some contractors have used on South African public projects and which costs the client far more than an honest bid would have. So boards use a technique called tender evaluation: they compare each bid line by line with the others and with their own estimate, and a bid more than 10 or 15 percent below the pack is asked to explain itself, item by item. A company that can explain, because it has a cheaper fabrication method or owns its own crane, may still win; one that cannot is rejected as unrealistic, and in many public tenders that rejection is written into the rules. For your bid, the lesson is to price completely and honestly, with sources, and to compete on the quality of the design, the completeness of the budget and the credibility of the team. If your budget comes out lower than another company's, be ready to say exactly which lines are lower and why.

### subtopic_2 — Costing Materials for the Real Headgear
**Assistant:** How do we find real prices for steel, cranes and wages? We are Grade 8 learners, not contractors.

**Tutor:** You find them the way a junior estimator does on their first week: from published sources, from suppliers who will talk to you, and from people you know, and you record where each came from. Steel: the large South African steel merchants publish price lists or quote by phone for standard sections per tonne or per length; a teacher or parent can make the call with you, and the merchant's name, the date and the figure go in your budget. Construction industry bodies and the quantity surveying profession publish building cost indices and typical rates per square metre or per tonne, often summarised in the business press; a search for "structural steel rate per tonne South Africa" with the current year will give a range, and you quote the range and the source. Wages: the Bargaining Council for the Civil Engineering Industry and the metal and engineering industries publish minimum rates by grade, which are public documents; a parent or neighbour who works in construction or on a mine can tell you what a welder, rigger or crane driver actually earns, recorded with permission as "interview, 2 September, rigger, 14 years' experience". Plant: crane hire companies advertise daily and weekly rates for mobile cranes by tonnage; a 100 tonne mobile crane for a day costs tens of thousands of rand and the advertisement is your source. Components: sheave wheels and winders are specialist items with no public price list, so you estimate from any published project cost you can find, or from the proportion they typically form of a headgear budget, and you label the line "estimate" with your reasoning. Two rules protect you. Never present a number without its source and date, because prices change and the board will ask. And prefer a sourced range to a precise invention: "R25 000 to R35 000 per tonne, merchant quote and press index, September" is a professional line; "R30 000" with nothing behind it is a guess.

### subtopic_3 — Costing Labour, Plant and Contingency
**Assistant:** What is contingency really for? It feels like padding the price.

**Tutor:** It is the opposite of padding: it is an honest admission that the drawings and the site do not yet tell you everything, with a number attached and a reason stated, which is what separates a realistic budget from a hopeful one. Every construction project meets things nobody could have priced on the day of the tender. The ground under one leg turns out to be softer than the test boreholes showed and the foundation must be deeper. Steel prices rise 8 percent between tender and delivery. A month of rain stops concrete work. The mine changes the winder specification and the sheave beam must be redesigned. A crane is late. If the budget has no contingency, every one of these becomes a crisis, a variation claim, a dispute or a cut in quality to stay within the price, and the client ends up paying more than an honest contingency would have cost. If it has a contingency of 10 to 15 percent, these are absorbed and the project finishes at or near the quoted figure, which is what a client actually wants. Padding is different: it is inflating individual lines, a crane at twice its real rate or steel at twice its tonnage, so that the budget looks complete while hiding profit or carelessness, and it is dishonest precisely because it is hidden. Contingency is visible: a separate line, a stated percentage, an amount, and a sentence saying what it is for, so the board can see it and judge whether it is reasonable for this project; a straightforward steel frame might carry 10 percent, a deep shaft in uncertain ground 20. Unused contingency is normally returned to the client at the end of a well-run contract, which is a further reason it is not profit in disguise. In your model budget a contingency of a few rand for a broken dowel or a second tube of glue teaches the same habit at a scale you can feel.
