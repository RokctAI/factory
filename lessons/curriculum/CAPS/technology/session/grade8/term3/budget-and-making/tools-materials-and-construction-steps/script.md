# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers listing tools, materials and logical construction steps for the headgear model. The anchor is a Grade 8 company in Rustenburg with the parts list and working drawing on the table, a tray from the class store with dowel, craft sticks, card and string, the gear kit and a 3 volt motor, and a sheet of A3 on which a flow chart of numbered boxes is taking shape.

## Subtopic: Listing Tools and Materials From the Parts List

The materials list is taken directly from the parts list on the working drawing, which is why last week's drawing had one. Each part has a material, a size, a length and a quantity; the materials list groups parts by material and adds up what must be collected: so many metres of 6 millimetre dowel, cut from so many 1 metre lengths with the cuts planned to waste as little as possible; so many craft sticks; so much card of what thickness for the base box and gear plate; string of a stated type and length for the rope and guides; the specific gears from the kit by tooth count and quantity; the motor, battery holder, switch and wire; the sheave wheel; glue of the types chosen for each joint; pins, split pins or small screws for mechanical joints. Each line carries a small spare, because a dowel will split and a stick will snap, and the list is checked against the class store before the build, with anything missing sourced or substituted now, not on the day.

The tools list follows from the operations each part needs. Cutting dowel: a junior hacksaw or fine saw and a bench hook or clamp, with a mitre box if angled ends are needed for the back-leg. Cutting craft sticks and card: craft knives, steel rules and cutting mats, scissors for string. Shaping: a file or sandpaper for dowel ends, a hand drill or awl with the correct bit for shaft holes in the gear plate. Marking out: pencils, a steel rule, a set square, a try square for right angles on the base. Joining: glue gun and glue sticks, PVA and clamps or clothes pegs, pliers for bending wire. Testing: a spring balance, a stopwatch, a ruler for sway, a 1 kilogram mass and a 300 gram load. Safety: eye protection for everyone cutting or drilling. Tools are signed out by name on the day, so the list is also the sign-out sheet.

The two lists sit beside the model budget and the parts list in the bid, and the board will check that they agree: a part on the drawing that has no material on the list, or a joint that needs a tool nobody has, is a sign of a company that has not planned.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the materials list is derived from the parts list, what the tools list contains and why, and how both are checked.

## Subtopic: Logical Construction Steps: Sequence and Dependencies

Construction steps are the order in which the parts are made and put together, and the order matters because most steps depend on others. A step cannot start until the parts it needs exist and the structure it attaches to is built; the sheave beam cannot be fitted until the tower stands, the tower cannot stand until the base is finished, and the gear plate cannot be fixed to the base until the shaft holes are drilled at the right centre distances. Writing the steps as a numbered list and checking each one against the question "what must already exist for this to happen?" produces a sequence with no dead ends.

A sequence for an A-frame headgear model might run: 1, mark out and cut all dowel legs, back-leg and beams to length from the parts list, label each; 2, cut all diagonals and base members; 3, make the base: assemble the base frame square, add the base plate, fix the gear plate position; 4, drill the gear plate at the centre distances from the gear drawing, fit the shafts dry and check the gears mesh and turn freely, test the motor; 5, assemble the two tower faces flat on the bench, legs plus diagonals, glued and clamped, checked square against the drawing; 6, stand the faces up and join them with the cross members and the remaining diagonals to make the tower, check it is plumb; 7, fit the tower to the base; 8, fit the back-leg from its foot on the base to its top on the tower; 9, fit the sheave beam and the sheave on its axle; 10, fit the drum, gears and motor to the gear plate, string the rope over the sheave and down to the cage, fit the guides; 11, fit the brake, the bumper and the depth pointer; 12, test lifts with increasing load, record, adjust; 13, finish, label, photograph.

Within this sequence some steps can run in parallel, because different members of the team can do them at once: cutting dowel and cutting card are independent, the gear plate can be drilled while the tower faces are glued, the cage can be made at any time. Other steps are strict dependencies: 6 after 5, 7 after 3 and 6, 9 after 7. Marking which steps are parallel and which are dependent is what turns a list into a plan, and glue drying time, often the longest wait in a model build, is placed so that other work fills it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the question that fixes the order of steps, a sequence for the A-frame model, and which steps can run in parallel.

## Subtopic: The Flow Chart and the Build Timetable

A flow chart draws the steps as boxes joined by arrows in the order they happen, using the conventions of systems and process diagrams: a rounded box for start and end, rectangles for steps, diamonds for decisions with a yes and a no arrow, and parallel branches where steps run at the same time and rejoin. The headgear flow chart begins with "start: materials collected, tools signed out, drawing revision checked", runs through the numbered steps, branches where the gear plate and the tower faces are built in parallel, and places a decision diamond at each test: "gears turn freely?" with no looping back to "adjust mesh"; "tower plumb and square?" with no looping to "adjust before glue sets"; "lifts 300 grams in 10 to 20 seconds?" with no looping to "check batteries, drum, friction, ratio". It ends with "end: model tested, labelled, photographed; evaluation sheet complete". The flow chart is part of the bid because the tender notice asks for it and because it shows the board that the company has a method with built-in checks, which is what the law requires of real mine construction.

The timetable fits the steps into the time available, two double periods of about 90 minutes each, with the team's members allocated to parallel steps. A table with time down the side in 15 minute blocks and team members across the top shows who does what when: in the first period, two members cut and label dowel while two cut card and make the gear plate; the base and the tower faces are glued before the break so they set during it; in the second period the tower is stood, the back-leg and sheave fitted, the winder installed and the first test lift made with 30 minutes left for adjustment and recording. Steps that must wait for glue are placed before a break; steps that need the hot glue station are staggered so the team does not queue; the safety rules of Term 2 making apply throughout.

The plan also names the materials and tools for each step on the flow chart itself, in a small note under the box, so that when a member reaches a step they can see what to fetch. The list, the sequence, the flow chart and the timetable are four views of one plan, and they must agree: a step on the flow chart with no materials on the list, or a timetable that puts a step before the one it depends on, is corrected now.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a materials list written from memory instead of from the parts list, so a part is missing on the day. Two: steps in the wrong order, the sheave beam fitted before the tower is square. Three: a flow chart with no decision diamonds, so tests are forgotten until the end. Four: a timetable with no slack, so the first snapped dowel breaks the whole plan.

The questions for this section are with you now: the flow chart's symbols and decision points, how the timetable allocates people and time, and why the four documents must agree.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tray of dowel and sticks and a flow chart of numbered boxes — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What You Need Before You Start

The materials list comes straight off the parts list on your drawing. Group the parts by material and add up: so many metres of 6 millimetre dowel, planned so the cuts waste little; so many craft sticks; card of a stated thickness for the base box and gear plate; string for rope and guides; the gears by tooth count; motor, battery holder, switch, wire; the sheave wheel; the right glue for each joint; pins or small screws. A little spare on every line, because dowel splits and sticks snap. Check it against the class store now; fix gaps today, not on build day.

The tools list comes from what each part needs done to it. Cutting dowel: a small saw and a bench hook or clamp, a mitre box if the back-leg ends are angled. Sticks and card: craft knives, steel rules, mats; scissors for string. Shaping: file or sandpaper, a hand drill or awl for shaft holes. Marking: pencils, rule, set square, try square. Joining: glue gun, PVA, clamps or pegs, pliers. Testing: spring balance, stopwatch, ruler, a 1 kilogram mass, a 300 gram load. Eye protection for everyone cutting or drilling. Tools get signed out by name, so this list is the sign-out sheet too.

Both lists go in the bid beside the budget and the parts list, and the board checks they match. A part with no material or a joint with no tool says you did not plan.

Hold the tray from the store in mind and try the questions for this part: what you need before you start.

## Subtopic: Base Before Tower, Tower Before Sheave

Steps have an order because most depend on others. Ask of every step: what must already exist for this to happen? The sheave beam needs a standing tower; the tower needs a finished base; the gear plate needs holes drilled at the right distances before it is fixed.

A sequence for the A-frame model: 1, cut and label all dowel: legs, back-leg, beams. 2, cut diagonals and base members. 3, make the base square, add the plate, mark the gear plate spot. 4, drill the gear plate at the gear drawing's centre distances, fit shafts dry, check the gears turn freely, test the motor. 5, build the two tower faces flat on the bench, legs plus diagonals, glued, clamped, checked square. 6, stand them up, join with cross members and remaining diagonals, check plumb. 7, fit tower to base. 8, fit the back-leg, foot on base, top on tower. 9, fit the sheave beam and sheave on its axle. 10, fit drum, gears and motor, string the rope over the sheave to the cage, fit guides. 11, brake, bumper, depth pointer. 12, test lifts with rising load, record, adjust. 13, finish, label, photograph.

Some steps can happen at the same time by different people: dowel and card cutting; drilling the gear plate while the faces glue; the cage any time. Others are strict: 6 after 5; 7 after 3 and 6; 9 after 7. Mark which is which. Put glue-drying, the longest wait, where other work fills it.

Hold "what must already exist?" in mind and try this part's questions: base before tower, tower before sheave.

## Subtopic: Boxes, Arrows and a Clock

Draw the steps as a flow chart. Rounded box for start and end, rectangles for steps, diamonds for decisions with yes and no arrows, side-by-side branches for parallel work that join again. Start: materials collected, tools signed out, drawing revision checked. Steps in order, branching where gear plate and tower faces are built together. A diamond at every test: gears turn freely? No: adjust mesh. Tower plumb and square? No: fix before the glue sets. Lifts 300 grams in 10 to 20 seconds? No: check batteries, drum, friction, ratio. End: tested, labelled, photographed, evaluation sheet done. It goes in the bid because the notice asked for it and because it shows the board you have a method with checks built in, as the law demands on a real site.

The timetable fits it into two double periods of about 90 minutes. Fifteen-minute blocks down the side, team members across the top. Period one: two people cut and label dowel while two cut card and make the gear plate; base and faces glued before the break so they set. Period two: stand the tower, fit the back-leg and sheave, install the winder, first test lift with 30 minutes spare for fixing and recording. Glue waits before breaks; hot glue station turns staggered; Term 2 safety rules throughout.

Under each flow chart box, a small note of the materials and tools for that step, so whoever gets there knows what to fetch. List, sequence, flow chart, timetable: four views of one plan, and they must agree.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Materials from the parts list with spare, tools from the operations, both checked against the store. Steps ordered by what must already exist, with parallel work marked and glue time used. A flow chart with decision diamonds at every test, and a timetable with people, blocks and slack.

Hold the diamond that says "lifts 300 grams?", and take the final questions of the lesson: boxes, arrows and a clock.
