# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers preparing a realistic budget of materials and labour costs for a real headgear. The anchor is a Grade 8 company in Rustenburg with the working drawing and parts list on the table, a printed page of steel merchant prices per tonne, a note of a crane's daily hire and a boilermaker's hourly rate, and a spreadsheet drawn by hand on A3 with columns for item, quantity, unit, rate and amount.

## Subtopic: What a Realistic Budget Is and Why the Board Wants One

A budget is a reasoned estimate of what a project will cost, itemised so that every number can be checked, and a realistic budget is one whose quantities come from the drawings and whose rates come from the market. The tender board reads it for three things: completeness, whether every part of the work is in it; realism, whether the quantities and rates match the drawing and current prices; and honesty, whether the company has shown its working. A budget that is far lower than the others is not a winning bid but a warning sign, because the board assumes something has been left out and that the company will come back for more money, which in South African public tenders is a familiar and expensive story.

The structure of a construction budget is standard. Materials: everything that becomes part of the finished headgear, listed by type with quantity, unit, rate and amount. Labour: the people who build it, by trade, with hours or days and rates. Plant and equipment: the cranes, welding sets, scaffolding and transport hired for the job, by days and rates. Design and professional fees: the engineers' time to design and supervise, often a percentage of the construction cost. Contingency: an allowance, typically 10 to 15 percent, for what the drawings and the site have not revealed. Value-added tax at 15 percent on the total. And a summary that adds these to a single figure, with the date and the basis of the rates, because prices move.

For the headgear project the company budgets the real structure at full scale, using the working drawing's dimensions multiplied by the model-to-real factor, 1 to 100, so a 600 millimetre model represents a 60 metre headgear, and the parts list's quantities scaled accordingly. It also budgets the model itself, in rand, as a check that the method works and because the constraint set a limit on model cost. The two budgets look alike in structure and differ by a factor of some millions, which is itself a lesson in scale.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three things the board reads a budget for, the standard sections of a construction budget, and how the model's dimensions are scaled to the real headgear.

## Subtopic: Costing Materials for the Real Headgear

Materials are costed from quantities taken off the drawing. For a steel headgear the main material is structural steel: the legs, bracing, sheave beams and platforms, measured as lengths of each section from the parts list, converted to mass using the section's mass per metre from a steel table, and summed to tonnes. A 60 metre A-frame headgear of moderate size might use 150 to 300 tonnes of steel; a learner budget shows the working, "legs: 4 at 62 m of 305 by 305 column section at about 100 kg per metre, 24.8 tonnes", and adds the bracing, beams and back-leg the same way, with a percentage, say 10 percent, for connections, bolts and plates. Structural steel supplied, fabricated and erected is priced per tonne, and the rate in South Africa at the time of the lesson is of the order of tens of thousands of rand per tonne; the company quotes the rate it found with its source and date.

Foundations are the second material item: reinforced concrete bases under each leg and the back-leg, sized to spread the loads into the ground, measured in cubic metres of concrete and tonnes of reinforcing steel, each with a rate per unit. The sheave wheels, bearings and rope are specialist components priced as units: a sheave wheel assembly of several metres diameter, two of them, each costing in the hundreds of thousands of rand; a winding rope of the required length and diameter, priced per metre; the winder itself with motor, gearbox, drum and brakes, which is a very large single item and in a real tender would be a separate contract, so the company notes whether it is included or excluded. Smaller items follow: the headgear's platforms, stairs and guards, lighting, lightning protection, paint, the shaft collar steelwork and the cage guides.

Each line of the materials section reads item, quantity, unit, rate, amount, with the amount as quantity times rate, and a note of where the quantity came from, drawing or parts list, and where the rate came from, merchant, published price index or supplier quote. A total for materials closes the section. Where a company cannot find a rate, it estimates and marks the line "estimate" rather than leaving it out; an honest estimate can be questioned and corrected, an omission cannot.

The questions for this section are with you now: how structural steel is quantified and priced, what the foundations and specialist components are, and what each budget line must show.

## Subtopic: Costing Labour, Plant and Contingency

Labour is the cost of the people who build the headgear, and leaving it out, or guessing it as a single round number, is the commonest failure in a learner budget and a serious one in a real tender, where labour can be a third or more of the cost. Labour is costed by trade and time: how many people of each trade, for how many days or hours, at what rate. The trades on a headgear site include the structural steel erectors and riggers who lift and bolt the frame, boilermakers and welders who fabricate it, the crane operators, the concrete workers for the foundations, electricians for lighting and the winder, the mechanical fitters who install the sheaves and winder, the surveyors who set out and check alignment, the safety officer the law requires, the site supervisor and the project manager. Rates are hourly or daily, and a learner budget uses published sector rates or quotes from a parent or local contractor, with the source noted. Time comes from the construction steps planned next session: if the frame takes six weeks with a crew of twelve working eight hours a day, that is 12 times 30 days times 8 hours, 2 880 labour hours for erection, at the mix of rates on that crew.

Plant and equipment are hired by the day: a mobile crane of the capacity needed to lift the sheave beams to 60 metres, which is a large and expensive machine, scaffolding or access platforms, welding sets and compressors, generators, transport for the steel from the fabricator to the mine. Professional fees cover the engineers' design and site supervision and are often expressed as a percentage of the construction cost, 8 to 12 percent. Then the lines that a careless budget forgets: insurance; site establishment, the offices, fencing, water and sanitation a site needs; the environmental and safety measures the Week 5 report pointed to, dust suppression, water management, waste disposal; and testing and commissioning, the load tests and inspections the regulations demand before a single person rides the cage.

Contingency comes last before tax: 10 to 15 percent of the subtotal, stated as a percentage and an amount, with a sentence saying what it covers, ground that is softer than expected, steel price rises, weather, a design change. Value-added tax at 15 percent is added to the whole. The summary page lists each section's total, the contingency, the tax and the grand total, and under it the basis: the date of the rates, the sources, the items excluded such as the winder if it is a separate contract, and the assumptions. The model budget sits on a second sheet in the same format, in rand, with the class-store prices, and its total is compared with the cost constraint.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a budget with materials only and no labour, plant or fees. Two: rates without sources or dates, which the board cannot check. Three: quantities that do not match the drawing, because they were guessed instead of taken off. Four: no contingency, so the first surprise breaks the price.

The questions for this section are with you now: how labour is costed by trade and time, what plant, fees and the forgotten lines include, and how contingency and tax are added.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a hand-drawn spreadsheet and a page of steel prices — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Counting the Rand

A budget is a reasoned guess at the cost, broken into lines so every number can be checked. Realistic means the quantities come from your drawing and the prices from the real market. The board reads it for three things: is everything in it; do the amounts match the drawing and today's prices; did you show your working. A bid that is far cheaper than the rest does not win; the board assumes you forgot something and will come back asking for more, which happens in South Africa all the time.

The sections are always the same. Materials: what becomes part of the headgear, with quantity, unit, rate and amount. Labour: the people, by trade, hours or days, rates. Plant: cranes, welders, scaffolding, trucks, by the day. Professional fees: the engineers, usually a percentage. Contingency: 10 to 15 percent for what nobody foresaw. VAT at 15 percent. A summary adding it all, with the date and where the prices came from, because prices move.

You budget the real headgear by scaling your drawing: 1 to 100, so a 600 millimetre model is a 60 metre tower, and the parts list scales too. You also budget the model itself, in rand, to check the method and because the rules capped the model's cost. Same shape, a few million times apart.

## Subtopic: Steel by the Tonne

Materials come off the drawing. Steel first: legs, bracing, beams, platforms as lengths from the parts list, turned into mass with a steel table's kilograms per metre, added up in tonnes. A 60 metre A-frame might take 150 to 300 tonnes. Show the working: "legs: 4 at 62 metres of 305 by 305 column at about 100 kilograms a metre, 24.8 tonnes," then bracing, beams, back-leg, plus about 10 percent for bolts and plates. Steel supplied, fabricated and erected is priced per tonne, tens of thousands of rand a tonne at the time of the lesson; quote the rate you found with its source and date.

Foundations next: concrete bases under each leg and the back-leg, in cubic metres of concrete and tonnes of reinforcing, each with a rate. Then the special parts as units: two sheave wheel assemblies, each hundreds of thousands of rand; winding rope by the metre; the winder itself, motor, gearbox, drum, brakes, a huge single item that in a real tender is often a separate contract, so say whether it is in or out. Then platforms, stairs, guards, lights, lightning protection, paint, shaft collar steel, cage guides.

Every line: item, quantity, unit, rate, amount, where the quantity came from, where the rate came from. Total the section. Cannot find a rate? Estimate and write "estimate". An honest estimate can be fixed; a missing line cannot.

Hold the 24.8 tonnes of legs in mind and try this part's questions: steel by the tonne.

## Subtopic: People, Cranes and the Unexpected

Labour: the people. Leaving it out, or writing one round number, is the commonest mistake, and on a real site labour is a third or more of the cost. Cost it by trade and time: how many people, how many days or hours, at what rate. Erectors and riggers, boilermakers and welders, crane drivers, concrete workers, electricians, fitters for the sheaves and winder, surveyors, the safety officer the law demands, the supervisor, the project manager. Rates from published sector figures or a parent or local contractor, source noted. Time from next week's construction steps: twelve people for six weeks at eight hours a day is 2 880 hours of erection.

Plant by the day: the big mobile crane that lifts beams to 60 metres, scaffolding, welding sets, generators, trucks from the fabricator. Professional fees, 8 to 12 percent of the construction cost, for the engineers who design and supervise. The forgotten lines: insurance; site set-up, offices, fencing, water, toilets; the dust, water and waste measures your Week 5 report pointed to; and load tests and inspections before anyone rides the cage.

Contingency: 10 to 15 percent of the subtotal, as a percentage and an amount, with a line saying what it covers, soft ground, steel prices, weather, a change. VAT 15 percent on everything. Summary page: each section, contingency, tax, grand total, then the basis: date of prices, sources, what is excluded, assumptions. Model budget on a second sheet, same format, class-store prices, checked against the cost cap.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A realistic budget takes quantities from the drawing and rates from the market, in sections for materials, labour, plant, fees, contingency and VAT, with a summary stating date, sources and exclusions. Steel by the tonne with working shown; labour by trade and time; plant by the day; 10 to 15 percent contingency. The model gets the same treatment in rand.
