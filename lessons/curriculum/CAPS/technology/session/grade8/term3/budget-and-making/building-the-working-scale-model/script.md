# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers building the working scale model of the headgear using safe working practices. The anchor is a Grade 8 Technology room in Rustenburg in the second double period: a 600 millimetre dowel tower standing on its base with its back-leg fitted, a learner on a stool fitting the sheave beam while a teammate feeds the string over the sheave, and the gear plate with its three stages turning freely as a third member tests the motor.

## Subtopic: Safe Working Practices for the Headgear Build

The safety habits of Term 2 apply, and the headgear build adds tools and situations of its own. Saws: dowel is cut with a junior hacksaw or fine saw against a bench hook or in a clamp, never held in the hand, with the fingers of the holding hand behind the blade and the cut started slowly with light strokes until the blade has a groove; a mitre box guides angled cuts for the back-leg ends. Drills: shaft holes in the gear plate are drilled with a hand drill or an awl on a scrap board beneath, with the plate clamped or held flat on the bench and never in the hand, and with eye protection, because card and plywood throw fragments; the bit size matches the shaft so the shaft turns freely without slop. Knives follow the Term 2 rules exactly: along a steel rule, drawn towards the body with fingers on top of the rule, light passes, capped when set down. Hot glue is used at its station for joints that must grab quickly, dowel to dowel and gear plate to base, with PVA for the long, strong, unhurried joints of the tower faces; hot glue on skin goes under cold water, never wiped. Eye protection is worn by anyone sawing, drilling or working near a stretched string, which can snap and whip.

The build adds height. A 600 millimetre tower on a bench puts the sheave above eye level, so the sheave beam and the rope are fitted standing on the floor, not on a chair, with the model moved to a low table if necessary; nobody reaches over a working motor or a turning drum, and fingers stay away from the gear train while it runs. The motor is tested with the gears disengaged first, then engaged with no load, then with load, and the switch is in the hand of one named person during every test so that it can be stopped at once. Loads are built up gradually and nobody stands under the cage. Loose string is a trip and tangle hazard and is kept wound on its drum.

The room habits stand: bags under desks, walkways clear, one person at the hot station, hair tied and sleeves rolled at the saw and the drill, every cut or burn reported, tools counted back at the end. The making record runs alongside from the first cut, with a photograph at each step of the flow chart and a note of anything that changed from the drawing.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rules for saws and drills, the rules for working at height and around the running winder, and who holds the switch during tests.

## Subtopic: Building the Structure True: Base, Faces, Tower, Back-Leg

The structure is built in the order the flow chart set, and three words govern it: square, plumb and braced. The base is made first on the bench: a frame of dowel or a card box, checked square with a try square at every corner before the glue sets and again after, with the base plate fixed on top to make it a rigid plate; the gear plate's position is marked from the drawing. A base that is not square cannot carry a square tower, and the base is also where mass is added if the plan calls for it, sand in the box or a weight under the winder, to lower the centre of mass.

The tower faces are built flat over a full-size layout drawn on paper from the working drawing: the two legs laid on their lines, the node positions marked, each diagonal cut to fit between the legs at its nodes and glued with PVA, the face weighted or clamped flat and checked with a set square and a rule for squareness and straightness before the glue sets. Diagonals in each panel alternate direction or form a K or an X as the drawing shows, so that every rectangle is triangulated; a panel left as a rectangle will rack and the tower will sway. The faces set fully before they are stood up, which the timetable placed over the break.

Standing the faces: the two faces are held upright and parallel at the drawing's spacing by two members while a third fits the cross members at the top and bottom with hot glue, then the remaining cross members and the diagonals of the two open faces, cut to fit. The tower is checked plumb against a square held on the bench and sighted from two sides; a tower that leans is corrected before the open-face diagonals set, because those diagonals lock the shape. The tower is then fixed to the base at its marked position, each leg glued and, if the plan allows, pinned. The back-leg is fitted from its foot on the base to its top on the tower at the positions dimensioned on the drawing, cut to fit after measuring the actual distance on the model, because a small error in the tower's height changes the leg's length; its ends are shaped to bear fully, and a short gusset of card at each end spreads the joint. The sheave beam is fitted across the top on the shaft centre line with the sheave on its axle, turning freely, and the axle secured against sliding out with collars or glue blobs.

After each of these stages the model is tested against the drawing: dimensions measured, squareness and plumb checked, the top pushed gently sideways to feel for racking. Any change from the drawing, a diagonal moved, a leg shortened, is written in the record and the drawing revision advanced.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the base and faces are made square, how the tower is stood and plumbed, and how the back-leg and sheave beam are fitted.

## Subtopic: Installing the Winder and Testing the Lift

The winder is installed last, onto a structure that already stands. The gear plate, drilled and dry-tested in the first period, is fixed to the base at its marked position, square to the shaft centre line so that the drum's axis is at right angles to the rope's path up to the sheave; a drum skewed to the rope winds badly and pulls the rope off the sheave. The motor is mounted so that its pinion meshes with the first gear at the correct centre distance, usually on a small card bracket whose position is adjusted until the gears turn freely by hand with no tight spot and no skipping, then fixed. Each shaft gets its collars so that no gear can walk along it, especially if a bevel pair is used. The drum goes on the output shaft with its collar; the string is tied through a hole in the drum, wound on in the correct sense so that the drum's rotation raises the cage, led up over the sheave in its groove and down the shaft centre line to the cage; the guides, two taut strings or thin dowels, are fitted either side of the shaft centre with the cage running between them. The brake, a pawl engaging a ratchet or a lever pressing on the drum, is fitted so that it holds the drum with the loaded cage stationary; the bumper is fixed under the sheave beam where the cage would hit before reaching the sheave; the depth pointer is fitted to the drum shaft or as a mark on the string.

Then the tests, run in the order of the flow chart's diamonds and recorded with numbers. Gear train free by hand: yes or no, and what was adjusted. Motor runs the train with no load: direction of drum rotation correct, yes or no; if no, swap the motor leads or re-wind the string. Lift with the empty cage: time for 1 metre. Lift with 100, 200, 300 grams and, if the margin allows, 400: time for each, and whether the motor laboured, the gears skipped or the rope slipped. Brake holds 300 grams at mid-shaft: yes or no. Sway at the top with 1 kilogram on the platform and the loaded cage hanging: millimetres, measured against a fixed rule. Topple test with the loaded cage swung 100 millimetres: yes or no. Each result goes into the evaluation table against its specification, with the measured value and a verdict, exactly as in Term 2. A failed test is diagnosed with the flow chart's no branch and the fix is made and recorded with the before and after values: "stalled at 300 g; drum reduced from 40 to 20 mm; lifts 300 g in 16 s".

The model is finished with labels: the company's name, the scale, the gear ratio, and small tags naming the sheave, the back-leg, the brake and the bumper for the board. It is photographed from the front, side and top to match the working drawing's views, and from close up at the winder.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a tower glued up before it is checked square, then braced into the wrong shape. Two: a drum skewed to the rope path, so the rope climbs off the sheave. Three: shafts without collars, so gears walk apart under load. Four: tests done without numbers, so the evaluation has nothing to report.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the gear plate, motor and drum are installed and aligned, the order and content of the tests, and how a failed test is recorded.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a dowel tower on the bench and a gear train spinning under a learner's hand — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Knives, Saws and Hot Glue, Safely

Term 2's safety rules still stand, and this build adds a few. Saws: dowel in a bench hook or clamp, never in your hand, fingers behind the blade, slow light strokes to start the groove, a mitre box for the angled back-leg ends. Drills: the gear plate clamped flat on a scrap board, never held in the hand, eye protection on, bit matched to the shaft so it turns freely without wobble. Knives exactly as before: along the steel rule, towards you, fingers on top, light passes, capped when down. Hot glue at its station for quick joints, dowel to dowel, plate to base; PVA for the long strong tower joints that can wait. Hot glue on skin: cold water, never wipe. Eyes covered when sawing, drilling or near a stretched string, which can snap and whip.

Height: a 600 millimetre tower puts the sheave above your eyes. Fit the beam and rope standing on the floor, not a chair; move the model to a low table if you must. Nobody reaches over a running motor or a turning drum; fingers out of the gears. Test the motor with gears disengaged, then engaged with no load, then with load, and one named person holds the switch every time so it can stop at once. Add load slowly; nobody under the cage. Loose string trips and tangles; keep it on the drum.

Room: bags under desks, paths clear, one at the hot station, hair tied, sleeves up, every cut reported, tools counted back. Record from the first cut: a photo at each flow chart step and a note of every change.

Hold the one hand on the switch in mind and try the questions for this part: knives, saws and hot glue, safely.

## Subtopic: Square, Plumb and Braced

Three words: square, plumb, braced. Base first on the bench: a dowel frame or card box, try square at every corner before and after the glue sets, base plate on top to make it a stiff slab, gear plate position marked. A crooked base cannot carry a straight tower. Add mass here if planned, sand in the box or a weight under the winder.

Faces flat over a full-size layout drawn from the working drawing: legs on their lines, nodes marked, each diagonal cut to fit between the legs and glued with PVA, the face weighted flat and checked square and straight before the glue sets. Every rectangle gets a diagonal, alternating, K or X as drawn; a panel left as a rectangle will fold and the tower will sway. Let the faces set fully, over the break.

Stand them: two people hold the faces upright and parallel at the drawn spacing, a third hot-glues the top and bottom cross members, then the rest and the diagonals of the open faces, cut to fit. Check plumb with a square from two sides; fix any lean before those diagonals set, because they lock the shape. Glue, and pin if allowed, the tower to the base at its marks. Back-leg: measure the real distance from its foot mark to its top mark on the model, cut to that, shape the ends to sit flat, card gussets at each end. Sheave beam across the top on the shaft centre line, sheave spinning freely on its axle, axle stopped from sliding with collars or glue blobs.

After each stage, measure against the drawing, check square and plumb, push the top gently to feel for racking. Write every change and bump the drawing's revision letter.

Hold the two people holding the faces in mind and try this part's questions: square, plumb and braced.

## Subtopic: Does It Lift? Measure It

Winder last, onto a tower that already stands. Fix the gear plate at its mark, square to the shaft line, so the drum's axle is at right angles to the rope going up to the sheave; a skewed drum winds badly and pulls the rope off. Mount the motor on a small card bracket, slide it until the pinion meshes and the whole train turns freely by hand with no tight spot or skip, then fix it. Collars on every shaft so no gear can wander, especially bevels. Drum on the output shaft with its collar; tie the string through a hole, wind it on the right way so the drum turning raises the cage, up over the sheave in its groove, down the centre line to the cage between two taut guide strings. Brake, a pawl on a ratchet or a lever on the drum, set to hold the loaded cage still. Bumper under the sheave beam. Depth pointer on the drum shaft or a mark on the string.

Tests, in the flow chart's order, with numbers. Train turns freely by hand: yes or no, what you adjusted. Motor with no load: drum turns the right way, yes or no; if no, swap the motor wires or rewind the string. Empty cage: seconds for 1 metre. 100, 200, 300 grams, then 400 if it will: seconds each, and did the motor labour, gears skip, rope slip. Brake holds 300 grams mid-shaft: yes or no. Sway with 1 kilogram on top and the loaded cage hanging: millimetres against a fixed ruler. Swing the loaded cage 100 millimetres: does it topple. Every result into the evaluation table next to its rule, as in Term 2. A fail gets the flow chart's no branch, the fix, and before and after numbers: "stalled at 300 g; drum cut from 40 to 20 mm; now lifts 300 g in 16 s".

Finish: labels with company, scale, ratio, and tags on the sheave, back-leg, brake and bumper for the board. Photos front, side and top to match the drawing, and a close-up of the winder.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Saw in a hook, drill clamped, knife along the rule, hot glue at its station, eyes covered, one hand on the switch. Base square, faces flat and triangulated, tower plumb before the locking diagonals set, back-leg cut to the real distance, sheave free on its axle. Winder square to the rope, train free by hand, collars on shafts, then tests with numbers and fixes recorded.

Hold the stopwatch at 16 seconds, and take the final questions of the lesson: does it lift? Measure it.
