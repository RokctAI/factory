### subtopic_1 — Safe Working Practices for the Headgear Build
**Assistant:** Why cut the back-leg to the measured distance on the model rather than to the length on the parts list?

**Tutor:** Because the back-leg is the one member whose length depends on everything built before it, and small errors accumulate exactly there. Its foot sits at a dimensioned point on the base and its top at a dimensioned height on the tower, and the parts list gives the length between those two points as drawn. But the tower you have built is a few millimetres taller or shorter than drawn, its legs are not perfectly vertical, the base plate is a card thickness higher than assumed, and the glue joints have added or lost a millimetre each; each is within tolerance, but the back-leg spans all of them at once. A leg cut to the list length will then be either too short, so it does not reach and the joint is a blob of glue in tension that fails under the rope's pull, or too long, so it pushes the tower out of plumb when forced in. Measuring the real distance on the standing model with a rule or a scrap of dowel held in place and marked, then cutting to that mark, gives a leg that bears fully at both ends. This is standard practice in construction: members that close a frame are often cut to fit on site, which real steel erectors call "site measure", and the parts list length is the estimate for ordering material, which is why the list carries a spare. The same logic applies to the diagonals of the two open tower faces, which are fitted after the faces are stood: cut each to fit its actual panel. Members that are made before the frame exists, legs and the diagonals of the flat-built faces, are cut to the drawing because there is nothing yet to measure and because the drawing is what makes those faces accurate in the first place.

### subtopic_2 — Building the Structure True: Base, Faces, Tower, Back-Leg
**Assistant:** The model lifts the cage but the top of the tower leans towards the winder while it lifts. Is that a failure?

**Tutor:** It is a measurement, and whether it is a failure depends on how far it leans against the specification, but a visible lean towards the winder during the lift tells you exactly which member is not doing its job. The rope runs from the sheave down to the drum at the base of the tower on the winder side, so while the cage is lifted the rope's tension pulls the top of the tower towards the drum, which is the horizontal force the back-leg exists to resist. If the tower leans, either the back-leg is not bearing, because it was cut short and is held only by glue, because its foot is not fixed to the base and slides, or because its top joint is flexing, or the tower itself is racking in the plane of the pull because a panel in the faces parallel to the rope has no diagonal or has one that has come unstuck. Diagnose by watching: lift the load and look at the back-leg's foot and top joints for movement, then at each panel of the two faces parallel to the rope for a changing shape. Fix the one that moves: re-seat the back-leg so it bears, gusset its ends, pin its foot; or add or re-glue the missing diagonal. Then measure. The specification says less than 10 millimetres of sway at the top with 1 kilogram on the platform and the loaded cage hanging, measured against a fixed rule; take the reading before and after the fix and record both, because the pair of numbers is strong evaluation evidence and because it demonstrates to the tender board the very principle the investigation taught, that the back-leg carries the rope's pull. A lean of 4 millimetres is a pass with a note; a lean of 25 is a fail that you fixed, which, written up honestly, scores better than a pass you cannot explain.

### subtopic_3 — Installing the Winder and Testing the Lift
**Assistant:** What is the right order to try fixes if the loaded cage stalls, and when should we stop fixing and present what we have?

**Tutor:** Follow the flow chart's no branch in the order it lists, cheapest and most likely first, measuring after each change so you know which one worked, and stop when the specification is met or when the time left is what you need to record and label. The order: batteries first, because a tired cell halves your torque and costs nothing to swap, and test the lift again with fresh ones. Then friction: disconnect the motor and turn the train by hand through a full revolution of the drum; any tight spot, skipping or rubbing of the rope on the sheave or guides is removed, shafts are oiled or soaped, collars loosened a fraction if they bind. Then the drum: a smaller drum needs less torque and is the quickest real gain; swap to a 20 millimetre drum if you were on 40 and re-test. Then the load path: check the rope runs over the sheave in its groove and not against the beam, that the cage runs free between the guides, and that the cage itself is not heavier than you think, weighed, not assumed. Only then the ratio: adding a stage is the biggest change and the slowest to make, so it comes last, and if you add one remember it reverses the output direction. Record each attempt as a line: what you changed, the load and time after. Knowing when to stop is part of the skill. If the model lifts 250 grams reliably and 300 stalls, and twenty minutes remain, use ten on the next fix and the rest on recording, labelling and photographing; a model that lifts 250 grams with a documented path to 300 is a strong bid, and a model with a half-finished fourth gear stage and no evaluation sheet is not. Write the unmet specification honestly in the evaluation with the measured value and the recommendation, and in the presentation say it before the board asks. Protect the tests and the record; they are what the board judges.
