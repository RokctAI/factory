### subtopic_1 — Acid Mine Drainage and Dust From Mine Dumps
**Assistant:** Why is this report part of a bid to build a headgear? It seems like a separate subject.

**Tutor:** Because a real tender for mining work is judged on far more than the steel, and a company that cannot show it understands the impact of its project does not win. Every mining right in South Africa is granted under laws that require an environmental impact assessment, a social and labour plan, and compliance with the Mining Charter's targets for women and historically disadvantaged people. A board awarding a headgear contract will ask the bidding engineers how their design handles water, dust, the surrounding community and the workforce, and expects a knowledgeable answer. That is the formal reason. The deeper one is the one this course has pressed since Term 2: technology always has impacts beyond its purpose, and designers who do not look for them leave the costs to others. The acid water running out of the West Rand is the result of engineers and owners who designed excellent winders and pumps and never designed for what would happen when the pumps stopped. The dust over Soweto is the result of treating the dump as outside the design. The exclusion of women was written into the law and then into the equipment, the hostels and the rosters, and the engineers who built those did not count it as their concern. Your report, one page, is a small act of counting those things as your concern; and the connection to your own design is direct. If you studied acid water, your bid can say the shaft collar design keeps surface water out and the budget allows for pumping and treatment. If you studied dust, it can say where the waste rock from sinking will go. If you studied gender bias, it can say the cage and signalling are designed for a range of bodies. If you studied indigenous smelting, it can say what the company learned about making with local, renewable materials and about who holds knowledge. The board will notice which bids thought this far.

### subtopic_2 — Indigenous Iron Mining in South Africa Before the Modern Era
**Assistant:** How do we find three reliable sources on something like dust from mine dumps when we only have the school library and a phone?

**Tutor:** Three good sources are closer than they seem, and knowing what counts as reliable is the skill. Start with sources that have a reason to be accurate. Government and regulatory publications: the Department of Mineral Resources and Energy and the Department of Water and Sanitation publish reports on acid mine drainage; the Mine Health and Safety Council publishes on dust and silicosis; Statistics South Africa and the Minerals Council publish workforce numbers by gender. Academic and museum sources: the Wits Origins Centre, the Ditsong museums and the Iziko museums have material on Iron Age metallurgy, and university researchers at Wits and Pretoria have published on both acid drainage and dust, often summarised in accessible articles. Established news media with named journalists and dates: the Mail and Guardian, Daily Maverick, GroundUp, News24 and the SABC have reported for years on the West Rand decant, on Snake Park and Riverlea dust, and on women in mining. Civil society: the Bench Marks Foundation and the Federation for a Sustainable Environment publish community-based reports on dumps and water, which give the resident's view that official reports may not. And people: a parent, neighbour or relative who has worked on a mine or lived beside a dump is a primary source, recorded with name, date and permission. For each source, write down who produced it, when, and why they might emphasise what they do; a mining company report and a community organisation's report on the same dump will differ, and your job is to present both and say what they agree on. Avoid anonymous web pages, pages with no date, and anything you cannot trace to an author or institution. Three sources from different kinds, say a regulator, a news report and an interview, make a far stronger report than three from the same kind.

### subtopic_3 — Gender Bias in Mining Careers and Writing a Fair Report
**Assistant:** Is it fair to call Iron Age smelting 'technology' when it was done with clay and charcoal and no machines?

**Tutor:** It is not only fair, it is the only accurate description, and the discomfort with the word comes from a bias worth naming. Technology, in this course's own definition, is the use of knowledge, skill and resources to meet human needs by designing and making products, systems and processes. An Iron Age smelter in the Soutpansberg investigated which rocks held iron, selected and prepared ore, designed a furnace whose height, wall thickness and air supply controlled a chemical reaction at over 1 100 degrees Celsius, made and maintained bellows and tuyeres, judged the state of the smelt by the colour of the flame and the sound of the bellows, and evaluated the bloom by working it; the smith then forged tools whose shapes evolved over centuries to suit the soil and the crops. Every step of the design process is there, carried out with materials to hand and refined by generations of specialists, which is precisely how all technology develops. The absence of machines is not the absence of technology; the furnace is the machine, and a sophisticated one. Modern blast furnaces use the same chemistry, carbon stripping oxygen from iron oxide, at larger scale with forced air from fans instead of bellows. The reflex to call indigenous technology primitive and European technology advanced is a bias in the sense this lesson defines: it assumes one kind of knowledge holder and discounts the others, and it has done real harm, erasing African achievement from school books for most of the twentieth century. Your report can correct it by describing the method with the same precision you would give a modern process, by naming the sites and dates, and by noting what modern engineers have learned from studying these furnaces about airflow and local materials. The lesson for your headgear design is practical, too: the smelters built what the site needed from what the site provided, which is a good test for your choice of model materials and your budget.
