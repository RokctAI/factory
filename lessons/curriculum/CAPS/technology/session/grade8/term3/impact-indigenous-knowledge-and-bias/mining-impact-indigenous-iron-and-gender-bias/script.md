# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the investigation and report on one of: acid mine drainage, dust pollution from mine dumps, indigenous iron mining in South Africa before the modern era, and gender bias in mining careers. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Rustenburg looking at four photographs on the board: an orange stream below an old Witwatersrand mine, a yellow dust cloud over a Soweto dump, a reconstructed clay furnace at an Iron Age site, and a woman in overalls and a cap lamp at a shaft collar.

## Subtopic: Acid Mine Drainage and Dust From Mine Dumps

Acid mine drainage is water that has flowed through mined rock and come out acidic and loaded with metals. The Witwatersrand gold reefs contain pyrite, iron sulphide. Underground, the rock was dry and the pyrite stable; mining opened it to air and water, and when the mines closed and pumping stopped, the workings flooded. Water and oxygen react with the pyrite to form sulphuric acid, which dissolves iron, aluminium, manganese and traces of uranium and other heavy metals from the rock. The acid water rises through the old shafts and tunnels and decants at the surface, as it did in 2002 on the West Rand near Krugersdorp, running orange with iron into streams that feed the Crocodile and Vaal systems and into the Cradle of Humankind's dolomite caves. It kills aquatic life, corrodes concrete and steel, makes water unfit for drinking or irrigation, and the metals accumulate in soil and sediment. The remedy is to pump and treat: neutralising the acid with lime and settling out the metals, at plants built from 2012 on the West, Central and East Rand at a cost of billions of rand and an annual running bill that will continue for decades. The cause is historical: the mines that made the profit closed before the law required them to secure the water, and the public pays.

Dust pollution from mine dumps is the other visible legacy. Gold ore was crushed to powder to extract the metal and the waste, tailings, was piled in dumps and slimes dams across the Witwatersrand, Welkom and the platinum belt; coal and other mines have their own. The dumps are fine, dry and often unvegetated, and the wind lifts their dust into the townships built beside them, Soweto, Riverlea, Diepkloof, Snake Park, and across Johannesburg on windy August and September days. The dust carries silica, which causes silicosis, and in gold tailings low levels of uranium and other metals. Residents report asthma, chest infections, eye irritation and dust in food and homes. Remedies include vegetating the dumps, spraying them, reprocessing them for the gold they still contain, which removes the dump altogether, and relocating or not building settlements beside them. Both acid water and dust are negative impacts in the sense of Term 2: costs the industry's designers did not weigh, falling largely on people who did not get the benefit.

The questions for this section are with you now: how acid mine drainage forms and what it does, how dust from dumps affects nearby communities, and the remedies for each.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Iron Mining in South Africa Before the Modern Era

Mining in South Africa did not begin with the diamond rush of 1867 or the gold of 1886. People were extracting and working metals here for well over a thousand years before, and the knowledge they held is part of the technology this term studies. From about the third century of the common era, Iron Age farming communities in what are now Limpopo, Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and Gauteng mined iron ore, usually surface deposits of haematite and magnetite or iron-rich laterite, and smelted it in clay furnaces. The furnace was a shaft of clay a metre or more high, loaded with alternating layers of charcoal and crushed ore, with air blown in through clay pipes called tuyeres by hand-operated bellows made of skin bags. Charcoal burning with the forced air reached temperatures high enough for carbon to strip the oxygen from the ore, leaving a spongy mass of iron, the bloom, at the base, which the smith hammered to drive out slag and then forged into hoes, spear heads, axes, knives and ornaments. Sites such as those at Mapungubwe, at Phalaborwa, in the Soutpansberg and along the Tugela show furnaces, slag heaps and mine pits; at Phalaborwa, copper and iron were worked for centuries on the same hill that is today an open-pit mine.

This was technology in the full sense: investigation of which rocks yield iron, design of furnaces whose shape, height and air supply controlled the temperature, skilled making, and evaluation passed down through specialist smelters whose craft carried ritual and social rules, often excluding women and outsiders from the smelting site. Copper was worked at Phalaborwa and Musina; gold was mined and traded from Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe to the East African coast by the thirteenth century; tin was smelted at Rooiberg. The metals supported farming, hunting, trade and political power, and the knowledge spread and changed across centuries.

Indigenous knowledge is studied in Technology for two reasons. It corrects the idea that technology arrived with colonists; it was here, developed by African communities to meet their needs with local materials, which is exactly what the design process describes. And it offers lessons: the smelters used local materials, renewable charcoal, human and animal power and small-scale workings whose impact was limited and whose waste, slag, is still being studied; their furnace designs show understanding of airflow and heat that modern engineers recognise. A report on this topic describes the method, names the sites, places it in time, and reflects on what it shows about technology and knowledge.

The questions for this section are with you now: how an Iron Age furnace worked, where and when iron was mined and smelted in South Africa, and why indigenous knowledge is studied in Technology.

## Subtopic: Gender Bias in Mining Careers and Writing a Fair Report

For most of the twentieth century South African law barred women from working underground. The Mines and Works Act of 1911 and its successors reserved underground and most skilled surface work for men, and for white men in particular through the job colour bar, while black men were recruited as migrant labour to live in single-sex hostels. Women's role in mining was confined to a few surface and clerical jobs, and the communities that sent men to the mines were left for months at a time without them. The ban on women underground was lifted only in 1996, when the Mine Health and Safety Act came into force, and the Mining Charter from 2004 set targets for women's participation. Today women are around 15 to 20 percent of the mining workforce, including engineers, geologists, rock drill operators, winding engine drivers and mine managers, but they remain a minority, especially in core underground and senior technical roles, and they report barriers that are not in the law: equipment and protective clothing designed for men, inadequate changing and toilet facilities underground, harassment, assumptions about strength and about who belongs on a shift, and the difficulty of combining mine rosters with family responsibilities that society still assigns mainly to women.

Bias in technology means that the design of tools, jobs and systems assumes one kind of user and so excludes or disadvantages others. The headgear your company is designing lifts a cage of workers; a bid that assumes every worker is a tall man has built bias into the design. A report on gender bias investigates the history, the law, the numbers, the experiences of women who have worked in mining, available in interviews, company reports and the Minerals Council's publications, and the changes being made, from redesigned protective equipment to mentoring programmes and targets. It also considers the design implications for the company's own project: cages, signalling and safety gear sized and placed for a range of bodies.

Whichever topic the company chooses, the report follows the same shape, and it is the balanced report that Term 4 will ask for again. It states the question. It gives background with dates and places. It presents evidence from at least three sources, cited, with numbers where they exist. It gives more than one perspective: the mine's, the community's, the worker's, the regulator's. It reaches a conclusion the evidence supports, and it recommends what should be done and, where relevant, what the company's design will do differently. It is written in plain language of about a page, with a sketch, map or table, and the sources listed at the end.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a report that is all outrage and no evidence. Two: a report that gives only the company's view, or only the community's. Three: treating indigenous iron smelting as primitive rather than as technology. Four: a gender report that stops at the law and never asks what the design of equipment and facilities does.

The questions for this section are with you now: the history and current state of women in South African mining, what bias in technology means, and the shape of a balanced report.

## Subtopic: Iron Before the Mines

Mining here is far older than diamonds in 1867 or gold in 1886. And because they used local, renewable materials, human power and small workings whose impact was small, and furnace designs that engineers still respect.

## Subtopic: Who Gets to Go Underground

For most of the 1900s women were not allowed underground. The 1911 Mines and Works Act and the laws after it kept underground and skilled work for men, white men especially through the colour bar, with black men as migrant labour in single-sex hostels. Women got a few surface and office jobs, and the villages that sent men went months without them. The ban ended only in 1996 with the Mine Health and Safety Act; the Mining Charter from 2004 set targets. Today women are about 15 to 20 percent of mining workers, engineers, geologists, drill operators, winding engine drivers, managers, but still a minority, especially underground and at the top, and they describe barriers the law does not fix: kit and overalls made for men, poor changing rooms and toilets underground, harassment, assumptions about strength and belonging, rosters that fight family duties society still puts mainly on women.

Bias in technology means a tool, job or system was designed assuming one kind of user, shutting others out. Your headgear lifts a cage of workers; if your bid assumes every worker is a tall man, the bias is built in. A gender report covers the history, the law, the numbers, women's own accounts from interviews and reports, the changes under way, and what your own design will do: cages, signals and safety gear for a range of bodies.
