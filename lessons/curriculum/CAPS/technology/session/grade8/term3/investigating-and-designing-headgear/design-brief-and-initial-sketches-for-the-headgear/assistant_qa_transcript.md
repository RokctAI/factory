### subtopic_1 — Writing the Headgear Design Brief
**Assistant:** Our investigation showed the A-frame is the proven form. Why must we still sketch three ideas when we already know the answer?

**Tutor:** Because the A-frame is the proven form for a full-size steel headgear with a drum winder 40 metres away, and your model is none of those things exactly, so the proven answer may not be the best answer at your scale and with your materials. The investigation tells you why the A-frame works: the back-leg carries the rope's horizontal pull as compression. Whether that is the best way to carry it in a 600 millimetre card-and-dowel model on a 300 millimetre base is a separate question, and the sketches are how you answer it. A back-leg that reaches the base at a realistic angle may need a base longer than the 300 millimetres the constraint allows, which the four-legged tower with the winder inside avoids; a closed box tower may turn out to be the stiffest thing you can make from card. You will not know until the three are drawn and compared against the table. There is also the matter of the comparison itself. The tender board, like the assessor in Term 2, wants evidence that the company considered alternatives and chose on reasons; a bid that says "we built an A-frame because that is what mines use" is weaker than one that says "we compared three forms against our specifications, found the A-frame's back-leg needed more base than we had, modified it to a steeper leg braced to the tower, and here is the table". Finally, sketching the alternatives often improves the winner: the four-legged tower's idea of mounting the winder inside the frame can be borrowed into the A-frame so the back-leg is shorter, which is exactly the kind of hybrid the Week 6 evaluation tends to produce. Draw all three well; the A-frame may still win, and it will win better.

### subtopic_2 — Specifications and Constraints From the Tender
**Assistant:** How do we decide the numbers in the specifications, like 300 grams and 10 to 20 seconds? They feel made up.

**Tutor:** They should not be made up, and each one has a source you can point to. The load comes from scale. If a real cage carries 80 workers at about 80 kilograms each plus its own mass, the loaded cage is roughly 10 tonnes; at your model's scale of 1 to 100 a true scale load would be far too small to demonstrate anything, so the convention in model making is to choose a load that visibly tests the structure and the mechanism, and 300 grams, a cup of sand or a few batteries, is heavy enough to make a weak tower bend and a weak gear train stall but light enough for a 3 volt motor through a 25 to 1 train to lift. Write that reasoning down; it is the trace. The time comes from two directions. A real cage travels at up to 15 metres per second in a deep shaft, which at scale would be a blur and would look unsafe to a board; demonstration speeds of 5 to 10 centimetres per second, giving 10 to 20 seconds for a metre, let the audience watch the lift and let you stop at mid-shaft to show the brake. The same figure falls out of your gear calculation: a 3 000 revolutions per minute motor through 60 to 1 on a 20 millimetre drum gives about 3 metres per minute, 20 seconds for the metre, so the specification and the calculation agree, which is what the board will check. The sway limit of 10 millimetres is roughly 1 in 60 of the height, a usual stiffness limit for towers; the 1 kilogram on the platform represents the sheave, its bearings and a dynamic allowance; the 100 millimetre swing is what a cage on a string does when the lift starts. Every number is defensible from scale, from safety, from the calculation or from a standard practice, and the trace is written in the reason column so that you can answer "why 300 grams?" in one sentence.

### subtopic_3 — Initial Idea Sketches of the Headgear
**Assistant:** What should the sketch show about the gear train and the winder, given that we drew the gears properly last week?

**Tutor:** Enough to show where the mechanism lives in the structure and how the rope gets from the drum to the cage, without redrawing the gear sheet. The free-hand sketch is about the relationship between the structure and the mechanism, which the gear drawing does not show at all. So mark on the sketch the position of the motor or crank, the drum and the gear train between them, drawn as rough circles with their tooth counts and the overall ratio written beside them, and show the drum's axis and the direction it turns so that the rope winds on from the correct side. Then draw the rope path: from the drum, to the sheave at the top, over the sheave's groove and down the shaft to the cage, noting where it might rub against bracing, which is the four-legged tower's known weakness and should be visible in that sketch. Show how the gear train is held: on a card or plywood plate fixed to the base, with the shafts in holes at the correct centre distances, and note "centre distances from gear sheet". Mark the brake, a pawl or lever on the drum shaft, and say what it bears against. If the winder is at the top, as in the closed tower idea, show how its weight is carried and how the motor's wires or the crank reach it. In the annotations, refer to the gear drawing by name, "train as per gear sheet 2, 64:1, output same direction as motor", so a reader knows the detail exists and where to find it. This division of labour is normal in engineering: a general arrangement sketch shows where things go and how they connect, and detail drawings show each part. Your bid will have the free-hand sketch, the gear drawing, the systems diagram and the working drawing all describing one winder, and the board will check that they agree on the ratio, the direction and the position.
