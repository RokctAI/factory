### subtopic_1 — Investigating Real Headgear: Sources and Method
**Assistant:** Why does the back-leg of an A-frame headgear slope towards the winder house and not away from it?

**Tutor:** Because the rope pulls the top of the tower towards the winder, and a strut resists a pull most efficiently by pushing straight back along the line of the pull. Picture the sheave at the top of the tower with the rope leaving it in two directions: straight down the shaft to the conveyance, and at a slope down to the winder drum on the ground some distance away. The vertical part of the rope pulls the sheave straight down, which the tower carries as compression in its legs. The sloping part pulls the sheave towards the winder, which tries to drag the top of the tower over in that direction and would bend or topple a plain vertical tower. The back-leg runs from near the sheave platform down to the ground on the winder side, roughly in line with the sloping rope, so that the horizontal pull is carried down the back-leg as compression into its foundation, like a prop placed against a wall that is being pushed. If the back-leg sloped away from the winder it would need to work in tension to hold the tower back, which a slender steel leg can do but its foundation would have to resist being pulled out of the ground, a much harder thing to anchor than a foundation that is pushed into it. In some headgear two back-legs form a second A behind the first, and in four-legged towers the winder is placed so that its pull is small or is balanced by a second winder on the opposite side. Your model has the same geometry in miniature: the motor and drum on the base pull the string from the sheave towards themselves, and without a brace on that side the tower leans towards the motor the moment the cage is lifted. Build the back-leg, or a diagonal brace in that direction, and the tower stands.

### subtopic_2 — How Wire-Rope Winding Systems Work
**Assistant:** How can we estimate the height of a headgear from a photograph?

**Tutor:** By finding something in the picture whose real size you know, measuring it and the headgear on the print with a ruler, and scaling. Suppose a worker stands at the foot of the tower and measures 6 millimetres tall on your print, while the tower from ground to sheave centre measures 210 millimetres. An adult is about 1.7 metres tall, so the scale is 1.7 metres per 6 millimetres, about 0.28 metres per millimetre, and the tower is roughly 210 times 0.28, about 60 metres. Other reference objects work as well or better: a standard shipping container is 2.4 metres high and 6 or 12 metres long, a bakkie is about 1.8 metres high and 5 metres long, a railway wagon about 3.5 metres high, a doorway about 2 metres, a storey of a building about 3 metres. Two cautions keep the estimate honest. The reference object must be at about the same distance from the camera as the headgear, or perspective distorts the scale; a person in the foreground of a photograph taken from a kilometre away is useless, while a person standing at the tower's base is good. And the photograph should be taken roughly level, not looking steeply up, or the tower's height is foreshortened. Record the estimate as an estimate, with the reference used and the measurements: "height about 60 m, from a worker at the base, 6 mm against 210 mm on the print". If a written source gives the actual height, use it and cite it, and compare it with your estimate as a check on your method. The same technique gives the sheave diameter, often four to six metres, and the span of the base. Doing this on two or three photographs puts real numbers in your comparison table and gives you a scale for your model: a 60 metre headgear at 1 to 100 is a 600 millimetre model, which is about right for the classroom.

### subtopic_3 — Structural Forms and Safety Features to Borrow
**Assistant:** We cannot visit a mine. Is a photograph-only investigation good enough for the tender board?

**Tutor:** Yes, if it is done carefully and honestly, and most professional investigations of distant structures are done exactly that way. What the board judges is whether the team understood how real headgear work and used that understanding, not whether it stood under one. A good photograph-based investigation has several things in it. It uses more than one photograph of each headgear, from different sides where possible, so the back-leg, the bracing and the winder house can all be seen; a single front view hides the most important structural feature. It uses photographs of three or more headgear of different types, a steel A-frame, a concrete tower, a heritage example, so that comparison is possible. It records the source of every image, the mine, the place and the date if known, because an unsourced picture could be anywhere in the world. It extracts measurements with the scaling method and labels them as estimates. It is supplemented by at least one non-photographic source: a diagram of a drum or friction winder from a textbook or safety publication that explains what the photograph cannot show, the mechanism inside the winder house, and if at all possible one conversation with someone who has worked at a mine, which in most South African towns is a parent, grandparent or neighbour, recorded with permission. And it draws conclusions in the team's own words: what the three headgear have in common, how they differ, and which features the team will borrow. A record like that, three or four pages with sketches and a table, is exactly what a consulting engineer would produce before tendering for a site they had not yet visited, and a board member who asks "did you visit?" will accept "no, but here are five sources and here is what we learned from each" from a team that can then point to the borrowed back-leg on its model.
