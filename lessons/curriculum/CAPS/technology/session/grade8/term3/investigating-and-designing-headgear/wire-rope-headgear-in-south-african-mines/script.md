# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating wire rope-driven mine headgear lifting mechanisms used in South African mines. The anchor is a Grade 8 team in Rustenburg at a table of printed photographs, a steel A-frame headgear at a Carletonville gold mine, the concrete tower of a Free State shaft, and a preserved 1890s headgear at a Johannesburg heritage site, with a magnifying glass, a ruler and a worksheet headed Investigation.

## Subtopic: Investigating Real Headgear: Sources and Method

Investigation is the first step of the design process and the one the tender board will ask about first, because a company that has not studied existing headgear cannot be trusted to design one. The investigation has a purpose, questions, sources and a record. The purpose is to understand how real headgear lift loads safely so that the team's design is informed by what works. The questions are specific: how is the rope driven, what does the structure look like and why, how are people and ore carried, what stops a falling cage, what materials and sections are used, and what has changed over time.

Sources are of several kinds and each is recorded with its origin. Photographs of South African headgear are plentiful, in mining company reports, heritage archives, newspapers and online, and a photograph can be measured: a known object in it, a person, a vehicle, a standard container, gives a scale from which the tower's height and the sheave's diameter are estimated. Drawings and diagrams appear in textbooks, in the Mine Health and Safety Council's publications and in museum displays such as the Gold Reef City mine and the Kimberley Mine Museum. A visit, if a mine or a heritage headgear is within reach, allows the structure to be seen in three dimensions and the sheave wheels watched turning. Interviews with people who work or have worked on mines, a parent, a neighbour, a retired winding engine driver, give detail no photograph does, and are recorded with the person's name, role and date, with permission. Written sources are summarised in the team's own words with the source noted.

The record is a set of annotated sketches and notes, one page per headgear studied, with a table comparing them: location, mine and mineral, approximate height, structural form, winder type, number of ropes, conveyance, year. Three to five examples give enough to see patterns. The record ends with a list of features the team intends to borrow and the reasons, which flows directly into the brief and the sketches.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose and questions of the investigation, the kinds of source and how each is recorded, and what the comparison table contains.

## Subtopic: How Wire-Rope Winding Systems Work

A wire-rope winding system has four main parts. The winder is the machine that drives the rope, sited on the surface. The rope is steel wire rope, many strands of steel wires twisted around a core, typically 40 to 60 millimetres in diameter for a deep shaft, chosen because it combines enormous tensile strength with the flexibility to bend over a sheave and wind onto a drum. The sheave wheel at the top of the headgear turns the rope from horizontal to vertical; it is a large grooved pulley, often four to six metres across, on bearings, and it carries the full tension in the rope plus the rope's weight. The conveyance is what hangs on the rope: a cage with decks for people, a skip that is loaded with ore at the bottom and tips at the top, or a combination.

Two kinds of winder dominate. In a drum winder the rope winds onto a cylindrical drum turned by a motor through a gearbox; a double-drum winder has two drums on one shaft with ropes wound in opposite senses so that one conveyance rises as the other descends, in balance. The drum stores the whole length of rope, so for very deep shafts drums become large, up to six metres across with rope in several layers, and the sheaves sit high on an A-frame headgear with a back-leg sloping towards the winder house. In a friction winder, also called a Koepe winder after its inventor, the rope does not wind on; it passes over a large driven wheel and is gripped by friction in its grooves, with a conveyance on each end and a tail rope below to balance the weight; several ropes run side by side. Friction winders are often placed at the top of a concrete tower directly over the shaft, which is why some South African shafts have a tall solid tower instead of a steel frame, and they suit very deep shafts where a drum would be impossibly large.

Safety features are required by law under the Mine Health and Safety Act and its regulations. Ropes are inspected daily, tested and replaced on a fixed life. The winder has brakes that act on the drum or wheel and apply automatically if power fails or the winder overspeeds. Overwind and underwind devices stop the conveyance before it can be pulled into the sheave or run out of rope. Safety catches on cages grip the guides if the rope breaks, and arrester gear at the top and bottom of the shaft absorbs an overwind. Depth indicators and signalling systems tell the driver exactly where each conveyance is. Every one of these is a design feature the team can represent in a model: a brake, a stop, a catch, an indicator.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four parts of a winding system, the difference between drum and friction winders, and the safety features the law requires.

## Subtopic: Structural Forms and Safety Features to Borrow

Headgear take a few structural forms, each an answer to the same loads. The steel A-frame, or sloping-leg headgear, is the classic South African shape: a vertical tower of braced steel over the shaft carrying the sheave platform, with one or two back-legs running at an angle to the ground towards the winder house. The rope's pull from the sheave towards the winder tries to drag the top of the tower sideways, and the back-leg resists that pull as a strut in compression, exactly as a prop holds up a leaning wall. The tower itself is a braced frame of angles, channels and I-sections, triangulated with diagonal bracing in every face so that no rectangle can rack, and the sheaves sit on heavy beams at the top. The four-legged tower headgear is a square braced tower without a separate back-leg, used where the winder is close or where two winders pull in opposite directions and balance each other. The concrete tower headgear is a reinforced concrete shaft structure, sometimes 80 metres tall, with the friction winder at its top; its strength comes from the material and the closed section rather than from triangulation, and it resists the rope loads internally because the winder is directly above the shaft.

Features worth borrowing for a model are clear from the comparison. Triangulation in every face, so the tower does not sway. A back-leg or an equivalent brace in the direction the rope pulls, since a model winder on the base of the frame pulls the top towards itself exactly as the real one does. A wide, rigid base, and a base plate or anchor so the model cannot topple when the cage swings. A sheave on a proper axle at the top with a groove for the string, on a beam that spans the tower. Guides for the cage, two taut strings or rods down the shaft, so it rises straight and the safety catch has something to grip. A brake: a simple lever or pawl on the drum shaft. An overwind stop: a bumper under the sheave beam. A depth indicator: a pointer on the drum shaft or a marked string. Each borrowed feature is written in the investigation record with the headgear it came from and the reason, because the tender board is interested not only in what the team designed but in why.

Finally, the investigation notices what has changed. The oldest headgear, from the 1880s Witwatersrand, were timber; steel followed by 1900 and the A-frame form settled by the 1930s; concrete towers with friction winders arrived in the 1950s and 1960s for the deep Free State and West Wits shafts. Changes came from depth, from the size of the loads and from safety law written after accidents, which is a story the Week 5 investigation of mining's impact will continue.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: an investigation with no sources recorded, which the board treats as invention. Two: studying one headgear only, so nothing can be compared. Three: borrowing the look of a tower without the bracing that makes it stand. Four: ignoring the back-leg, so the model's top is dragged towards the winder when the load comes on.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three structural forms and why each stands, the features to borrow and why, and how headgear changed over time.

## Subtopic: Rope, Drum, Sheave, Cage

Four parts. The winder drives the rope from the surface. The rope is steel wire rope, many wires twisted round a core, as thick as a wrist, hugely strong yet able to bend over a wheel. The sheave at the top turns the rope from flat to down; a grooved pulley metres across on bearings, carrying the whole rope tension. The conveyance hangs on the end: a cage with decks for people, a skip for rock that fills at the bottom and tips at the top.

Two kinds of winder. Drum winder: rope winds onto a drum turned by a motor through a gearbox. Two drums on one shaft, ropes wound opposite ways, one cage up as one comes down. The drum holds all the rope, so deep shafts need huge drums and a tall steel A-frame with a back-leg leaning towards the winder house. Friction winder: the rope does not wind on; it runs over a big driven wheel that grips it by friction, a cage on each end and a tail rope underneath to balance, several ropes side by side. It usually sits on top of a concrete tower right over the shaft, which is why some shafts have a tall solid tower, and it suits the deepest shafts.

Safety is law. Ropes inspected daily and retired on schedule. Brakes on the drum that grab automatically if power fails or speed runs away. Overwind and underwind stops. Safety catches on the cage that grip the guides if the rope breaks. Arresters at top and bottom. Depth indicators and bells so the driver knows exactly where each cage is. Every one can appear in your model.

Hold the two drums turning opposite ways in mind and try this part's questions: rope, drum, sheave, cage.

## Subtopic: Steal Like an Engineer

Three shapes. Steel A-frame: a braced tower over the shaft with one or two back-legs angled down towards the winder house. The rope pulls the top towards the winder; the back-leg pushes back, a strut in compression, like a prop against a leaning wall. Every face triangulated so no rectangle can fold. Four-legged tower: a square braced tower, no back-leg, where the winder is close or two winders pull opposite ways. Concrete tower: a reinforced concrete tube with the friction winder on top, strong from material and closed shape, loads kept inside because the winder is right over the shaft.

Steal these for your model. Triangles in every face. A back-leg or brace towards your winder, because your motor on the base pulls the top towards it just like the real one. A wide rigid base, anchored so a swinging cage cannot topple it. A sheave on a real axle at the top, grooved for the string, on a beam across the tower. Two taut guide strings down the shaft so the cage rises straight and the catch has something to grip. A brake lever or pawl on the drum shaft. A bumper under the sheave beam as an overwind stop. A pointer on the drum shaft as a depth indicator. Write each one down with where it came from and why.

Notice the history too: 1880s timber, steel by 1900, the A-frame settled by the 1930s, concrete towers from the 1950s for the deep Free State and West Wits shafts. Depth, load and safety laws written after accidents drove the changes; Week 5 picks that story up.
