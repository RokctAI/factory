### subtopic_1 — Planning the Build
**Assistant:** Why cut everything first? It seems faster to cut a part when we need it.

**Tutor:** It feels faster but it is slower and more dangerous, for three reasons that experienced makers learn the hard way. The first is safety. If parts are cut as needed, the knife is out and in use for the whole lesson, often in the middle of a desk where other people are gluing and reaching across, which is exactly how fingers get cut. If all cutting is done in the first twenty minutes by the two people assigned to it, on the mat, with everyone else marking out or making the pulley, the knife is then retracted and put in the tray, and the rest of the lesson has no blade in play. The second is accuracy. Cutting all four uprights one after another, against the same ruler setting, from the same sheet, produces four identical parts; cutting one now and another later, from a different scrap, produces four slightly different ones, and a frame built from unequal uprights leans. The third is materials. When you cut from the full cutting list at the start, you lay the parts out on the sheet first and discover immediately whether there is enough card, and you can nest the parts to waste the least. Cutting as you go uses the best card for the first parts and leaves off-cuts too small for the last ones, which is when teams run out and start begging material from the team next door. Real factories work the same way: a cutting department produces all the parts to a list, and an assembly line puts them together, because mixing the two wastes time, material and blood. Follow the sequence: cut, check, assemble, test.

### subtopic_2 — Safe Working Practices
**Assistant:** Why does the frame have to be square? A slightly skew frame would still hold the mechanism.

**Tutor:** It would hold it, but it would hold it badly, and the mechanism would tell you so the moment you turned the crank. The axle runs through holes in two opposite uprights. If the base is square and the uprights are vertical and parallel, those two holes are in line and the dowel passes through both freely, turning smoothly. If the base is out of square, even by a few millimetres, the uprights lean towards or away from each other, the two holes are no longer on the same line, and the dowel must bend slightly to pass through both. A bent axle binds against the sides of the holes as it turns, so the crank is stiff, the card around the holes wears quickly and tears, and a gear on the axle moves in and out of mesh with its partner once per turn, skipping teeth. A skew frame also has a second problem: its diagonals are unequal, so when you add the X-braces one diagonal is too long and the other too short for the panel, and the braces either do not fit or pull the frame further out of shape as the glue dries. Checking squareness is cheap. Measure the two diagonals of the base rectangle with a ruler; if they are equal, the rectangle is square, because only a true rectangle has equal diagonals. If they differ, push the long diagonal's corners gently together until they match, then let the glue set on a flat surface. Thirty seconds of checking saves an afternoon of fighting a stiff crank, and it is the same check a builder makes when setting out the foundations of a house.

### subtopic_3 — Building Frame and Mechanism Together
**Assistant:** What if a part breaks or a joint fails during testing? Does that count against us?

**Tutor:** It counts for you, provided you record it and respond to it, because a failure found and fixed during making is the design process working as intended. Testing is in the sequence precisely to find weaknesses while there is still time and material to correct them. If an upright bows when the load goes on, that is information: the member is not stiff enough, which you now know from the structures lessons means it needs depth or bracing, so you glue a second strip to it or add a cross-member, test again and write down what happened and what you did. If a glued corner opens, you have learned that the joint area was too small, so you add a gusset or a tab, and again you record it. The portfolio gains a short note and a photograph: "Under 300 g the left upright bowed inward; we added a horizontal brace at mid-height and re-tested to 500 g without bending." That sentence shows the examiner exactly the understanding the make and evaluate steps are supposed to develop, and it is worth more than a model that happened to work first time with no explanation. What does count against you is the opposite behaviour: hiding the failure, forcing the load on anyway so the model collapses at the presentation, or writing an evaluation that claims everything worked when the teacher watched it break. Engineers prototype and test for exactly this reason; the first version of almost everything fails somewhere, and the skill is in finding where, understanding why and fixing it. Load gradually, watch closely, keep the camera ready and treat every failure as a lesson you can write down.
