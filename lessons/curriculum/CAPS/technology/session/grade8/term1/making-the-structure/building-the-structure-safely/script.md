# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the Make step of the PAT: building the structure that houses the mechanism using safe working practices. The anchor is a Technology room in Mitchells Plain where four teams have two lessons to cut, assemble and test their frames, with one craft knife, one cutting mat, one pair of pliers and one glue gun between every two learners. The governing idea is that making is planned from the working drawing as a cutting list and a sequence of steps, that every tool has rules that keep hands safe, and that the frame and mechanism are assembled in an order that lets each part be checked before the next is added. Producing a cutting list and sequence, stating the safety rules for each tool and describing the assembly order are examinable.

## Subtopic: Planning the Build

Making begins at the drawing, not at the cardboard. The first task is the cutting list: a table of every part, how many are needed, its real size from the dimensions, and the material it is cut from. For the Welkom frame: four uprights, 400 by 20 mm, from double corrugated card; two base rails, 300 by 30 mm; two cross-rails, 200 by 30 mm; four diagonal braces, length measured from the drawing; one axle, 6 mm dowel, 260 mm long; one crank arm from card and wire; one pulley from two card discs; string. Counting the parts first prevents the commonest making disaster, running out of material halfway through because an upright was cut wrong and there is no card left for another.

The second task is the sequence of manufacture: the order of steps, written as a numbered list or a flow chart. A good sequence cuts all the parts first, then assembles the base, then the uprights, then the braces, then fits the axle and mechanism, then tests, in that order. It groups similar jobs so that the knife is used once for all the cutting and put away, and the glue gun is used once for all the joints. It also marks the points where a check must be made before going on: after cutting, are all parts the right size; after the frame, is it square and stiff; after the axle, does it turn freely. A sequence written before the lesson means the team starts working in the first minute instead of arguing about what to do.

The third task is to share the work. With four members, two can cut while two mark out; one can make the pulley and crank while others assemble the frame. Everyone should have a job at every moment, and the sequence should say who does what. A team member with nothing to do is the one who gets hurt playing with the knife.

Materials and tools are gathered and checked before cutting starts: enough card, straight dowels, sharp blades, a cutting mat for every knife, a steel ruler for cutting against, a hot glue gun or PVA glue, pliers for wire, a pencil and a set square for marking out. Marking out is done in pencil on the card from the cutting list, with every part labelled, before any cutting.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a cutting list and a sequence of manufacture contain, and how work is shared.

## Subtopic: Safe Working Practices

Craft knives cause most Technology room injuries, and almost all of them are avoidable. Always cut on a cutting mat, never on the desk or on your lap. Always cut against a steel ruler, not a plastic one, which the blade can ride over. Always cut away from your body and keep the hand holding the ruler behind the blade's path, fingers well back from the edge. Make several light passes rather than one heavy cut; a blade forced through thick card slips. Keep the blade retracted or capped when not cutting, and carry a knife with the blade retracted, handle first. Never catch a falling knife; step back and let it fall. Snap-off blades are replaced when blunt, because a blunt blade needs more force and slips more. Scissors are used for thin card and string, held closed when carried.

Wire needs its own rules. Cut it with pliers or wire cutters, never with scissors, and hold the off-cut so it does not fly: flying wire ends are an eye hazard, so eye protection is worn whenever wire is cut. Bend wire with pliers, not fingers, and fold or tape every exposed end so that it cannot scratch or stab. A crank handle made of wire must have its end bent back on itself or covered with a bead or tape.

Glue has rules too. Hot glue guns reach temperatures that blister skin: hold the gun by the handle, rest it on its stand, never touch the nozzle or the fresh glue, and if glue touches skin, run cold water over it at once rather than pulling it off. PVA glue is safer but slower; parts must be held or clamped while it sets. Solvent glues and spray adhesives are used only with the teacher's permission, in a ventilated room. Keep all glue off the cutting mat, where it sticks to blades.

General rules protect everyone. Work at a cleared desk with bags on the floor out of the walkway. Keep tools in a tray in the middle of the desk, not at the edge where they fall. Tie back long hair and remove dangling jewellery when using any tool. Report every injury, however small, to the teacher immediately. At the end, count the tools back into the box, because a knife left in a pile of card off-cuts is a knife waiting to cut whoever clears the desk.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rules for knives, wire and glue, and the general room rules.

## Subtopic: Building Frame and Mechanism Together

Assembly follows the sequence. The base goes together first: the two base rails and the two cross-rails are glued into a rectangle, checked for squareness with a set square or by measuring both diagonals, which are equal when the rectangle is square, and left to set on a flat surface. Then the uprights are glued to the corners of the base, held vertical with a set square while the glue takes, and a cross-rail is fitted across the top of each pair. Only when the basic box frame stands by itself are the diagonal braces added, one to each side panel, glued at both ends and at any point where they cross a member. The frame is now tested: push the top sideways; it should not rack. If it does, a brace is missing or a joint has not taken.

The mechanism goes in next. The axle holes are made before the uprights are glued if possible, by marking from the drawing and piercing with a sharp pencil or a hole punch, then opened to size with a round file or a rolled piece of sandpaper so the dowel turns freely without wobble. A card washer glued around each hole spreads the load and reinforces the bearing. The axle is pushed through, the pulley or gear fixed to it with glue or a tight-fitting card collar, and the crank arm attached at one end. Turn the crank: the axle should run smoothly without binding, which means the two holes are in line. If it binds, one hole is enlarged slightly in the direction needed.

Then the string, platform or follower is added, and the whole system is tested under load. Load is added gradually, not all at once, while the frame is watched for bending and the joints for opening. Problems found now are fixed now: a bowing upright is braced, a slipping string is given a knot through the axle, a loose joint is re-glued. Photographs are taken at each stage for the portfolio.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: cutting parts as they are needed instead of all at once, so that the knife is in use throughout the lesson and the sequence is lost. Two: gluing the uprights before the base has set, so the whole frame leans. Three: making the axle holes too small and forcing the dowel, which tears the card and seizes the axle. Four: testing with the full load first; load is added gradually so a weakness is seen before it becomes a collapse.

The questions for this section are with you now: the order of assembly, how to fit the axle and bearings, and how to test under load.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two lessons, four learners, one knife and one glue gun — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Before You Cut

Before you touch the cardboard, make two lists.

The cutting list: every part, how many, how big, what it is made of. Four uprights, 400 by 20. Two base rails, 300 by 30. One dowel, 260 long. And so on, straight from the drawing. Count them so you do not run out.

The order of work: cut everything, then build the base, then the uprights, then the braces, then the axle and mechanism, then test. Write down who does what so nobody is standing around.

Get the tools together and check them: sharp blade, cutting mat, steel ruler, glue, pliers, pencil. Mark every part out in pencil, with its name, before anybody cuts.

Keep the two lists in mind and try the questions for this part: planning before cutting.

## Subtopic: Knives, Wire and Glue

Knives first. Always on a cutting mat. Always against a steel ruler. Cut away from your body, fingers well behind the blade. Light passes, not one heavy push. Blade in when you are not cutting. If it falls, let it fall.

Wire. Cut it with pliers, hold the off-cut, wear eye protection. Bend it with pliers. Tape or fold every sharp end.

Glue. The hot glue gun burns: handle only, rest it on its stand, never touch the nozzle. If hot glue lands on skin, cold water, do not pull it. PVA is safer but you must hold the part while it sets.

The room. Clear desk, bags on the floor, tools in a tray in the middle, hair tied back. Tell the teacher about every cut. Count the tools back at the end.

Hold the cutting mat and the steel ruler in mind and try this part's questions: knives, wire and glue.

## Subtopic: Putting It Together

Build in order. Base first: a rectangle of rails, check it is square by measuring both diagonals, let it set. Uprights next, held upright with a set square while the glue grabs. Top rails across. Then the diagonal braces. Push the top sideways: if it wobbles, a brace or a joint is missing.

Now the mechanism. Make the axle holes before the uprights go on if you can. Open them up with a file or sandpaper until the dowel turns freely. Glue a card washer round each hole. Push the dowel through, fix the pulley or gear on it, add the crank. Turn it: smooth means the holes line up.

Add the string or platform. Then load it bit by bit, watching for bending and loose joints. Fix anything you see. Take photos for the portfolio.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plan with a cutting list and an order of work. Cut on a mat against a steel ruler, away from yourself; pliers and eye protection for wire; handle only on the glue gun. Build base, uprights, braces, then axle, mechanism and test, checking at each stage and loading gradually.

Hold the set square and the card washer, and take the final questions of the lesson: putting it together.
