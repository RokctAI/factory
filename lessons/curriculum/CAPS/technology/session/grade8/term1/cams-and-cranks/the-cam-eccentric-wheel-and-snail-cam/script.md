# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the cam as a mechanism that converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion, and compares the eccentric wheel with the snail cam. The anchor is a hand-cranked wooden toy sold at a craft market in Hout Bay: a carved giraffe whose head bobs up and down as the handle turns, driven by a cam hidden in the base. The governing idea is that a cam is a specially shaped wheel on a rotating shaft, and a follower resting on its edge rises and falls once per turn, with the shape of the cam deciding exactly how the follower moves. Naming the parts, describing the two kinds of motion, and contrasting the smooth motion of an eccentric wheel with the slow-lift-sudden-drop of a snail cam are examinable.

## Subtopic: Rotary Motion to Reciprocating Motion

Mechanisms are described by the kind of motion they produce. Rotary motion is turning in a circle, like a wheel or a handle. Linear motion is moving in a straight line, like a lift going up. Reciprocating motion is moving backwards and forwards, or up and down, along a straight line, like a piston or a sewing needle. Oscillating motion is swinging to and fro along an arc, like a pendulum. Many machines need to change one kind into another, and the cam is the standard way of changing rotary motion into reciprocating motion.

A cam is a wheel with a shaped edge, fixed to a rotating shaft. Resting on the edge of the cam is a rod called the follower, which is held upright in a guide so that it can only move up and down. As the cam turns, the high part of its edge pushes the follower up, and as the cam turns further the follower slides down the low part again, pulled by gravity or a spring. One turn of the cam gives one complete up-and-down cycle of the follower. The shaft spins; the follower bobs.

The bobbing giraffe works exactly like this. The handle turns a shaft under the body. On the shaft is a cam. The giraffe's neck is the follower, resting on the cam, and as the cam turns the neck rises and falls. The same principle drives the needle of a sewing machine, the valves of a car engine, which open and close hundreds of times a second, and the hammers of a musical box.

Two words matter for every cam. The rise is the part of the turn during which the follower moves up. The fall is the part during which it moves down. Some cams also have a dwell, a part of the turn during which the follower stays still at the top or bottom because the cam edge is at a constant distance from the shaft there. The shape of the cam is a drawing of how the follower will move.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four kinds of motion, the names cam and follower, and what one turn of the cam does to the follower.

## Subtopic: The Eccentric Wheel

The simplest cam is the eccentric wheel: an ordinary round disc, but with the shaft fixed off centre instead of through the middle. Because the shaft is off centre, the distance from the shaft to the edge of the disc is not the same all the way round. On one side the edge is far from the shaft, on the other side it is close. As the disc turns, the follower resting on the edge rises when the far side comes under it and falls when the near side comes under it.

The motion of the follower on an eccentric wheel is smooth and even. It speeds up gently, slows down gently at the top, comes down gently and slows at the bottom, in a continuous wave. There is no sudden jolt, because the edge of a circle has no corners. The follower is up for half the turn and down for half the turn, and the total distance it moves, called the stroke or the lift, is twice the distance by which the shaft is off centre. Move the shaft further from the middle and the stroke grows.

Eccentric wheels are chosen wherever the designer wants gentle, regular motion. The bobbing giraffe uses one so that the head nods smoothly rather than jerking. Water pumps driven by windmills use an eccentric on the rotor shaft to lift the pump rod up and down. Some washing machine agitators and the tumbling drums of concrete mixers use eccentric motion. In the Grade 8 PAT a cardboard eccentric wheel is the easiest cam to make, because it is simply a circle with the axle hole drilled off centre.

To draw an eccentric wheel, draw a circle, mark its centre lightly, then mark the shaft position some distance from the centre and draw the shaft there. Draw the follower as a rod with a flat or rounded foot resting on the top of the circle. Arrows show the shaft turning and the follower moving up and down.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes an eccentric wheel a cam, the kind of motion it produces, and what sets its stroke.

## Subtopic: The Snail Cam

The snail cam, also called a drop cam, is shaped like a snail shell: its edge spirals outwards, getting steadily further from the shaft, until it reaches a step where it drops suddenly back to the smallest radius. As the cam turns, the follower is lifted slowly and steadily up the spiral for most of the turn, then falls suddenly off the step. Slow rise, sudden fall, once per revolution.

This is a very different motion from the eccentric wheel. The eccentric gives equal time up and down and a smooth wave. The snail gives a long, gentle lift followed by an instant drop. Designers choose it when they want a hammer action or a sudden release. A toy woodpecker that taps a tree uses a snail cam: the beak is drawn slowly back and then snaps forward. Stamping machines use snail cams to lift a weight slowly and let it fall hard. Some old clocks use a snail cam to control how many times the bell is struck.

A snail cam can only turn one way. If it is turned backwards, the follower rides up the step instead of dropping off it, and the mechanism jams or breaks. The eccentric wheel has no such limit, since it looks the same turned either way. This is a design consideration: a snail cam needs a crank or motor that always turns in the correct direction, and the drawing must show the direction of rotation with an arrow.

To draw a snail cam, start with the shaft and sketch a spiral that grows outward for almost a full turn, then join the outer end to the inner start with a straight or slightly curved step. Place the follower resting on the spiral. Add the rotation arrow so that the follower climbs the spiral and then drops off the step.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a cam must be oval or pear shaped; an ordinary circle becomes a cam as soon as its shaft is off centre. Two: believing the follower turns with the cam; the follower only moves up and down in its guide. Three: assuming a snail cam can run either way; backwards it jams on the step. Four: drawing the follower hanging in the air; it must always rest on the cam edge, pushed down by gravity or a spring.

The questions for this section are with you now: the motion of a snail cam, why it can only turn one way, and how the two cams differ.

## Subtopic: Round and Round Becomes Up and Down

Motion comes in kinds. Round and round is rotary. Straight along is linear. Up and down, or back and forth, is reciprocating. Swinging is oscillating.

A cam turns round and round into up and down. It is a wheel with a special shape on a turning shaft. A rod called the follower rests on its edge. As the shaft spins, the high part of the cam lifts the rod and the low part lets it drop. One turn, one bob.

The wooden giraffe at the market works like this. Turn the handle, the cam turns, the neck rests on it and nods.

Keep the giraffe in mind and try the questions for this part: cam spins, follower bobs.

## Subtopic: The Off-Centre Wheel

The easiest cam is a round disc with the shaft off centre. That is the eccentric wheel.

Because the shaft is not in the middle, one side of the disc sticks out further than the other. As it turns, the far side pushes the follower up and the near side lets it down.

The motion is smooth, like a wave. Up for half a turn, down for half a turn, no jolts. Move the shaft further from the middle and the follower moves further.

For your PAT, this is the cam to make: a cardboard circle with the hole drilled off centre.

Hold the off-centre circle in mind and try this part's questions: smooth up and down from a plain disc.

## Subtopic: The Shell-Shaped Cam

The snail cam looks like a snail shell. Its edge spirals outwards and then drops in a step.

As it turns, the follower climbs slowly up the spiral, then falls suddenly off the step. Slow lift, sudden drop. That is what a tapping woodpecker toy or a hammer machine needs.

A snail cam only works one way round. Turn it backwards and the follower hits the step and jams. Always draw the arrow.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A cam changes rotary motion into reciprocating motion. The follower rests on the cam and moves up and down once per turn. An eccentric wheel is a disc on an off-centre shaft and gives smooth, even motion. A snail cam spirals out and drops at a step, giving a slow rise and a sudden fall, and must turn in one direction only.

Hold the giraffe and the woodpecker, and take the final questions of the lesson: which cam gives which motion.
