# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the crank as an adaptation of a second-class lever that converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion and back again. The anchor is a borehole hand pump in a village near Giyani, whose long handle a child pushes down to lift water, and the pedals of a bicycle whose crank arms turn the chainring. The governing idea is that a crank is an arm fixed at right angles to an axle, so that a force on the arm turns the axle with a mechanical advantage equal to the arm length divided by the axle radius, exactly like a second-class lever with the pivot at the axle centre, and that a crank joined to a connecting rod changes rotary motion into reciprocating motion or the reverse. Identifying the crank's parts, explaining why it behaves as a second-class lever, and describing the crank-and-slider mechanism are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Crank Is

A crank is an arm attached at right angles to the end of an axle or shaft, with a handle or pin at the far end of the arm. Turning the handle in a circle turns the axle. Bicycle pedals are cranks: each pedal arm is fixed to the chainring axle, and your foot on the pedal turns the arm, which turns the axle and the chainring. The handle of a fishing reel, a pencil sharpener, a hand drill, a car window winder and a meat mincer are all cranks. So is the starting handle on a vintage car and the winding handle on a well.

The crank is one of the most useful ways of applying rotary motion to a machine by hand, because a hand moving in a circle can keep pushing continuously, round and round, without stopping to reset as it must on a lever. A crank also lets you choose where the effort is applied: the further from the axle the handle sits, the longer the crank arm, and the easier the turning becomes.

The parts to name on a drawing are: the axle or shaft at the centre, the crank arm, the handle or crank pin at the end of the arm, and the circle that the pin traces as it turns. On a working mechanism, the crank arm is often a web or a disc rather than a thin bar, which is stronger, but it still functions as an arm from the axle centre to the pin.

In the Grade 8 PAT, a structure that houses mechanisms, the crank is usually the input: a learner turns a handle made from wire or a dowel and a cardboard arm, and that turning drives the gears, cam or pulley inside the structure. Designing a comfortable crank, with a long enough arm and a handle that turns freely, makes the model work smoothly.

## Subtopic: The Crank as a Second-Class Lever

Grade 7 defined a second-class lever as one in which the load lies between the pivot and the effort, so that the effort arm is always longer than the load arm and the lever always gives a mechanical advantage. A wheelbarrow, a nutcracker and a bottle opener are the classic examples. The crank is a second-class lever that has been bent to travel in a circle.

Here is why. Freeze the crank at one instant. The pivot is the centre of the axle, the point about which everything turns. The effort is your hand on the handle at the end of the crank arm. The load is the resistance at the surface of the axle, where it turns the gear, the rope or the mincer blades: a short distance from the centre, equal to the radius of the axle. The load, at the axle surface, lies between the pivot at the axle centre and the effort at the handle. Load in the middle: second class. The effort arm is the length of the crank arm; the load arm is the radius of the axle. Because the crank arm is always longer than the axle radius, the crank always gives a mechanical advantage, equal to the crank arm length divided by the axle radius.

This explains why a longer crank arm makes turning easier. The borehole pump handle is over a metre long so that a child can lift a column of water from thirty metres down. Bicycle cranks are around 170 mm long, a compromise between an easy push and a comfortable circle for the legs. A well windlass has a long handle because a bucket of water is heavy. If you have ever struggled to turn a tiny key in a stiff lock, you have felt a crank arm too short for its load.

The crank is also a wheel and axle in disguise. The circle the handle traces is the rim of an imaginary wheel, and the axle is the axle. Wheel and axle, crank and second-class lever are three ways of describing the same geometry: a long effort arm turning a short load arm about a common centre.

## Subtopic: Crank, Connecting Rod and Slider

A crank on its own turns hand motion into rotary motion. Join a connecting rod to the crank pin and the mechanism gains a second power: it converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion, or reciprocating into rotary. This is the crank and slider, and it is the heart of every petrol and diesel engine, every steam engine, every reciprocating pump and the needle drive of a sewing machine.

The parts are the crank, a connecting rod joined to the crank pin at one end, and a slider at the other end of the rod, held in a straight guide so that it can only move backwards and forwards. As the crank turns through a full circle, the crank pin moves round, pulling and pushing the connecting rod, and the slider moves from one end of its guide to the other and back. One turn of the crank gives one complete back-and-forth stroke of the slider, and the length of that stroke is twice the length of the crank arm.

Run it the other way and it still works. In an engine, exploding fuel pushes the piston, which is the slider, down its cylinder; the connecting rod pushes the crank pin round; the crankshaft turns and drives the wheels. Reciprocating in, rotary out. In the borehole pump, the handle is a crank or lever, the pump rod is the slider, and up-and-down motion of the rod works the piston in the pump far below. In a hand-operated sewing machine, the hand wheel is the crank, and linkages carry the rotary motion to the reciprocating needle.

Compare this with the cam. A cam also turns rotary motion into reciprocating motion, but only one way, and the follower's motion is set by the cam's shape. A crank and slider works in both directions and always gives a smooth, even stroke of twice the crank arm length. Designers choose a cam when they want a special motion pattern, and a crank and slider when they want to convert between rotary and reciprocating motion efficiently in either direction.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a crank a first-class lever because it turns; the load at the axle surface lies between the pivot and the effort, so it is second class. Two: thinking a longer crank arm gives more speed; it gives more force and needs a bigger circle, so your hand travels further. Three: believing the crank only works hand-to-rotary; with a connecting rod it converts in both directions. Four: confusing the stroke of a crank and slider, which is twice the crank arm, with the length of the connecting rod, which only has to be long enough to reach.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a crank and slider, its stroke, and how it compares with a cam.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a bicycle pedal and a village water pump — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Handle on an Axle

A crank is a handle on an arm sticking out from an axle. Turn the handle round and the axle turns with it. Bicycle pedals, a fishing reel, a pencil sharpener, a window winder: all cranks.

A crank is handy because your hand can keep going round and round without stopping, unlike a lever that you have to lift back up each time.

Parts to name: the axle in the middle, the crank arm, and the handle or pin at the end.

In your PAT, the crank is probably the handle you turn to make everything else move.

Keep the pedal in mind and try the questions for this part: a handle on an arm that turns an axle.

## Subtopic: Why a Long Handle Helps

Remember second-class levers from Grade 7: load in the middle, like a wheelbarrow, always makes you stronger.

A crank is a second-class lever bent into a circle. Pivot: the centre of the axle. Effort: your hand on the handle. Load: the axle surface, where the rope or gear is turned, close to the centre. Load in the middle. Second class.

So a crank always makes you stronger, and the longer the arm, the stronger you are. That is why a village water pump has a long handle and why a tiny key in a stiff lock is so hard to turn.

A crank is also a wheel and axle: the handle traces the rim of an imaginary wheel.

Hold the pump handle in mind and try this part's questions: where the pivot, load and effort are, and why long arms help.

## Subtopic: Making Round Into Back-and-Forth

Add a rod to the crank pin and a slider at the other end of the rod, kept in a straight groove. Now turn the crank. The pin goes round, the rod pushes and pulls, and the slider goes back and forth. Round and round became back and forth.

It works backwards too. In a car engine the exploding fuel pushes the piston, the rod pushes the crank round, and the wheels turn. Back and forth became round and round.

The slider moves twice the length of the crank arm each stroke.

A cam does the rotary-to-reciprocating trick too, but only one way and with whatever pattern its shape gives. The crank and slider works both ways and gives a smooth even stroke.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A crank is an arm on an axle with a handle at the end. It is a second-class lever bent into a circle, with the pivot at the axle centre, the effort at the handle and the load at the axle surface, so it always gives a mechanical advantage and a longer arm gives more. With a connecting rod and slider it converts between rotary and reciprocating motion in either direction.
