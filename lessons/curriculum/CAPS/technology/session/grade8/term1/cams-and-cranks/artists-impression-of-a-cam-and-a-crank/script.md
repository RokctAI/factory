# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the graphic skill of producing an artist's impression of a cam and a crank using colour, shading and texture. The anchor is the rendered picture on the box of a construction toy sold at a shop in Menlyn, which shows the finished model in colour and shadow so that a buyer can imagine owning it, compared with the black-and-white instruction sheet inside the box that shows how to build it. The governing idea is that an artist's impression communicates the look and feel of a design to a non-technical audience, using colour to separate parts, shading to show form and light, and texture to show material, while a working drawing communicates exact sizes to a maker. Explaining the purpose of an artist's impression, applying the three rendering techniques, and producing a rendered sketch of a cam and a crank are examinable.

## Subtopic: What an Artist's Impression Is For

Technology uses two very different kinds of drawing. A working drawing is for the person who makes the object. It is drawn to scale, with dark outlines, feint construction lines, dashed hidden detail and dimensions in millimetres, and it has no colour or shading because those would get in the way of measuring. An artist's impression is for everyone else: the client, the user, the judge of a competition, the shopper looking at a box. It shows what the finished object will look like, in three dimensions, with colour, light and shadow, and it does not need to be exactly to scale.

Designers produce artist's impressions at two moments. Early in the design stage, a quick rendered sketch helps the designer and the team picture an idea and decide whether it is worth developing. Late in the process, a finished impression is used in the presentation to communicate the design to people who cannot read a working drawing. Architects show clients a coloured picture of the building before the plans; car companies release a rendered image of a new model long before the engineering drawings are seen.

The purpose shapes the drawing. Because the audience is not going to build from it, an artist's impression leaves out dimensions and hidden detail and concentrates on appearance: the shape, the materials, the way light falls on the surfaces and the way the parts relate to each other. Because the audience must understand it instantly, the drawing uses a 3D view, usually oblique or isometric, and makes the important parts stand out with colour.

In the Grade 8 PAT presentation, teams show both: a working drawing that proves the model was built to a plan, and an artist's impression that shows the examiner what the finished structure with its mechanisms looks like and how it is meant to be used. The two drawings do different jobs, and marks are given for each.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between an artist's impression and a working drawing, who each is for, and when a designer produces each.

## Subtopic: Colour, Shading and Texture

Three techniques turn a flat line drawing into a picture that looks solid. The first is colour. Colour separates the parts: a red cam on a grey shaft and a blue follower can be told apart at a glance, where three outlines in pencil blend together. Colour also suggests material, with warm browns for wood, cool greys and blues for steel, bright flat colours for plastic. Use a few colours, chosen on purpose, rather than every pencil in the box. Colour is applied evenly in the direction of the surface, keeping inside the outlines.

The second is shading. Shading shows form, the roundness or flatness of a surface, and the direction of the light. Decide where the light comes from, usually the top left, and keep it there for the whole drawing. Surfaces facing the light are left pale; surfaces turned away are darkened; a curved surface such as the edge of a disc or the round shaft grades smoothly from pale to dark. Shading is built up in layers of light pencil strokes, not scrubbed on hard. A cast shadow on the ground beneath the object anchors it and stops it floating.

The third is texture. Texture shows what the surface is made of and how it feels. Wood grain is drawn as flowing parallel lines with occasional knots. Polished metal is shown with a sharp band of highlight next to a band of dark reflection. Rough cast metal is a stipple of small dots. Rubber or plastic is smooth, flat colour with a soft highlight. Texture is applied lightly on top of colour and shading, following the direction of the surface, and only where it will be seen.

Order matters: outline lightly in pencil first, then colour, then shading, then texture, then a final clean outline to sharpen the edges. Beginners who shade before colouring smear the drawing; beginners who outline darkly first find the heavy lines fight with the shading.

The questions for this section are with you now: what colour, shading and texture each show, how to choose a light direction, and the order of working.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Cam and the Crank

To draw the cam, start with an oblique or isometric view of a disc on a shaft. Draw the shaft as a cylinder passing through the disc off centre for an eccentric wheel, or draw the snail shape in place of the disc. Add the follower as a vertical rod with a flat foot resting on the cam edge, held in a simple guide block. Colour the cam one colour, the shaft a metal grey and the follower a third colour. Shade with the light from the top left: the top edge of the disc pale, the lower curve dark, the shaft graded from a bright highlight down one side to a dark edge on the other. Add a soft shadow under the disc and the guide. Give the shaft a sharp metallic highlight line, and if the cam is wooden, flowing grain lines across its face. Finish with a small arrow showing the rotation and a double arrow on the follower for its up-and-down motion.

To draw the crank, draw the axle as a horizontal cylinder, the crank arm as a flat bar rising from its end at right angles, and the handle as a short cylinder sticking out from the top of the arm towards the viewer. Colour the axle and handle as metal, the arm perhaps as painted steel in a flat bright colour. Shade the cylinders with a highlight along their length and darkness on the side away from the light. Show the arm's thickness with a darker side face. Add the circle traced by the handle as a feint dashed line, and an arrow for the direction of turning. If you include a connecting rod and slider, show the rod as a bar with rounded ends and the slider in its guide, with a double arrow for its stroke.

Judge the drawing by asking whether someone who had never seen a cam or a crank would understand from the picture alone what each is, what it is made of and how it moves. If the answer is yes, the impression has done its job.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: adding dimensions to an artist's impression; sizes belong on the working drawing. Two: changing the light direction halfway through so that one part is lit from the left and another from the right. Three: colouring every part a different bright colour so that nothing stands out; use a few colours with purpose. Four: pressing hard with the pencil for the first outline, which cannot be shaded over or erased cleanly.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps for rendering the cam and the crank, and how to judge whether an impression communicates.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a toy box picture and a set of colour pencils — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Picture That Sells the Idea

Think of a toy box. On the outside is a colourful picture of the finished toy, with shadows and shine. Inside is a black-and-white sheet with measurements showing how to build it. The outside picture is an artist's impression. The inside sheet is a working drawing.

The artist's impression is for people who want to see what the thing will look like. The working drawing is for the person making it. The impression has colour and shadow and no measurements. The working drawing has measurements and no colour.

In your PAT presentation you show both. One proves you built it to plan. The other shows what it looks like.

Keep the toy box in mind and try the questions for this part: which drawing is for whom.

## Subtopic: Making Flat Shapes Look Solid

Three tricks make a flat drawing look solid.

Colour: use it to tell the parts apart and hint at the material. Brown for wood, grey for metal, bright for plastic. A few colours, chosen on purpose.

Shading: pick where the light comes from, usually top left, and keep it there. Surfaces facing the light stay pale, surfaces facing away go dark, and round things fade from light to dark. Put a shadow on the ground underneath.

Texture: show what it is made of. Wavy lines for wood grain, a sharp bright stripe for shiny metal, dots for rough metal.

Work in this order: light outline, colour, shading, texture, then a final neat outline.

Hold the light in the top left in mind and try this part's questions: colour, shading, texture, in order.

## Subtopic: Your Own Cam and Crank Drawing

Now draw the cam. A disc on a shaft, hole off centre, a rod resting on top. Colour the disc, grey the shaft, colour the rod. Light from the top left: pale on top, dark underneath, a bright stripe along the shaft. Shadow under it. Arrow for turning, double arrow for the rod bobbing.

Now the crank. A horizontal rod for the axle, a flat arm sticking up from its end, a short handle pointing at you. Grey metal, shaded with a highlight along each cylinder. A faint dashed circle for the path of the handle and an arrow for the turn.

Then ask: would someone who had never seen one understand what it is, what it is made of and how it moves? If yes, well done.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An artist's impression shows what a design will look like, for people who do not build it; a working drawing gives exact sizes for the maker. Colour separates parts and suggests material, shading shows form and light, texture shows the surface. Keep one light direction, work from light outline to colour to shading to texture, and leave the dimensions for the working drawing.

Hold the shaded shaft and the dashed circle, and take the final questions of the lesson: rendering the cam and the crank.
