### subtopic_1 — What an Artist's Impression Is For
**Assistant:** If the artist's impression is not to scale and has no measurements, how can it be useful to a designer? It sounds like decoration.

**Tutor:** It is useful precisely because it does the job that measurements cannot do, which is to let people see the design as an object before it exists. Most of the people a designer must persuade cannot read a working drawing: the client paying for the work, the community that will use a building, the judges at a science expo, the shopper at the toy shelf. If you hand them a sheet of dimensioned views they see lines; if you hand them a rendered picture they see the thing, and they can tell you at once whether it looks right, whether it seems safe, whether they would want it. That feedback at the design stage saves enormous amounts of money, because changing a drawing is cheap and changing a built structure is not. The impression is also a tool for the designer's own thinking. Sketching an idea in three dimensions with light and shadow forces you to decide how thick the parts are, how they join and how the mechanism will be seen, and problems of proportion that are invisible in a flat view jump out immediately. Many designers keep a sketchbook of quick rendered ideas for exactly this reason. Finally, the impression is a record of intent: when the model is finished, you can hold it next to the picture and judge how close you came, which is the evaluation step of IDMEC in action. So the impression is not decoration added at the end; it is a thinking and communicating tool used from the first idea to the final presentation, and it is marked as such in the PAT.

### subtopic_2 — Colour, Shading and Texture
**Assistant:** Why does everyone say light from the top left? What if I want the light somewhere else?

**Tutor:** You may put the light anywhere you like, as long as you choose once and keep to it for the whole drawing; top left is simply the convention most people use, and it has two practical advantages. The first is habit: because most rendered drawings, maps and even computer icons are lit from the top left, viewers read a top-left-lit object as solid without thinking, whereas a drawing lit from below can look unnatural, like a face lit by a torch held under the chin. The second is that a right-handed person shading with the pencil tilted to the lower right naturally leaves the upper left lighter, so the convention matches the way most hands move. What matters far more than the position is consistency. Every surface facing your chosen light must be pale, every surface facing away must be dark, and every cast shadow must fall on the opposite side from the light. If the cam is lit from the top left and the follower from the top right, the eye cannot make the two parts belong in the same space and the drawing falls apart, however neatly each part is shaded. A good habit is to draw a small sun or arrow in the corner of the page before you start and glance at it every time you shade a new surface. If you deliberately want a dramatic effect, for instance a mechanism lit from below to look menacing on a poster, that is a legitimate design choice, but make it on purpose and make it everywhere.

### subtopic_3 — Drawing the Cam and the Crank
**Assistant:** How do I show that the shaft is shiny metal rather than just grey plastic?

**Tutor:** The difference is in the contrast and sharpness of the highlights, because shiny surfaces reflect their surroundings and dull surfaces do not. A plastic or painted shaft is shaded softly: pale on the lit side, darkening gradually round the curve, with at most a gentle, blurred highlight where the light strikes most directly. Polished metal is shaded hard: along the length of the cylinder, run a thin, sharp stripe left completely white where the light would reflect straight to the eye, and immediately next to it a narrow band that is almost black, representing the dark surroundings reflected in the curved surface. Then grade the rest of the cylinder in grey, and add a second, fainter pale stripe near the dark edge where light bouncing up from the ground is reflected. That alternation of sharp white and sharp dark is what the eye reads as chrome or polished steel; the softer the transitions, the duller the material looks. For the ends of the shaft, show the circular face slightly darker than the lit side of the cylinder with a crisp edge, and a touch of reflected colour from a nearby coloured part, perhaps a hint of the cam's red, sells the shininess further. Avoid outlining a shiny shaft heavily, because real chrome has no dark edge; let the dark reflection band do that job. Use the same thinking for other materials: wood has soft shading plus grain lines, rubber has soft shading and a dull broad highlight, rough cast iron has stipple and almost no highlight at all. Material is told by how light behaves on the surface, and texture lines only confirm what the shading has already said.
