### subtopic_1 — What a Crank Is
**Assistant:** If a crank is a second-class lever and a wheel and axle at the same time, which should I call it in a test?

**Tutor:** Call it what the question asks for, and know that both are right because they describe the same geometry from two points of view. The syllabus wording is that the crank is an adaptation of a second-class lever, so if a question asks which class of lever a crank represents, answer second class and justify it: pivot at the axle centre, effort at the handle, load at the axle surface in between, effort arm longer than load arm, mechanical advantage always greater than 1. If instead a question asks you to identify the simple machine that a crank handle and its shaft form, answer wheel and axle, because the handle traces the rim of a wheel whose radius is the crank arm, and the shaft is the axle. The mechanical advantage comes out identical either way, crank arm length divided by axle radius, which is a good check that the two descriptions agree. The reason the subject bothers with both is that each view is useful at a different moment. The lever view helps you reason about a single push: how hard, how far from the centre. The wheel-and-axle view helps you reason about continuous turning: how many turns, how it links to gears and pulleys. Engineers switch between them without thinking, and so should you. One warning: never call a crank a first-class lever because the handle goes round; the position of the load, not the motion, decides the class.

### subtopic_2 — The Crank as a Second-Class Lever
**Assistant:** Why are bicycle cranks all about the same length? If a longer arm makes pedalling easier, why not make them half a metre long?

**Tutor:** Because a crank arm sets two things at once, the force you need and the circle your foot must travel, and the second one is limited by your legs. A 170 mm crank means your foot moves in a circle 340 mm across, which suits the range of a knee bending comfortably at normal pedalling speed. Double the crank to 340 mm and the circle becomes 680 mm across; your knee would have to come up almost to your chest at the top of each stroke and your leg would be nearly straight at the bottom, and you could not spin the pedals quickly at all. The pedals would also strike the ground when cornering. There is a second reason, which is the force and distance trade that runs through all of mechanical systems. A longer crank halves the force on your foot but doubles the distance your foot travels each turn, so the work per turn is the same and you have simply chosen to push more gently for longer; the bicycle then has gears to make exactly the same trade in a more adjustable way, by changing the ratio between chainring and sprocket rather than the length of your leg's circle. Manufacturers do vary crank lengths slightly, with shorter cranks for children and smaller riders and longer ones for tall riders, but always within a few centimetres of the leg's comfortable range. The borehole pump, by contrast, can have a very long handle because a person's arms moving up and down through a large arc is a natural movement, and because the pump is used for a short time with a heavy load rather than for an hour of fast spinning.

### subtopic_3 — Crank, Connecting Rod and Slider
**Assistant:** How does the connecting rod know whether to turn rotary motion into reciprocating or the other way round?

**Tutor:** It does not know anything; it simply connects two points, and the direction of energy flow is decided by which end is pushed. A connecting rod is a stiff bar with a pivot at each end, one on the crank pin and one on the slider. Geometry fixes the relationship between them: for every position of the crank pin on its circle there is exactly one position of the slider in its guide, and for every position of the slider in the middle of its stroke there are two possible crank positions, one on each half of the circle. If you drive the crank, by hand or by motor, the pin moves round the circle and the rod drags the slider back and forth: rotary in, reciprocating out, as in a hand pump or a sewing machine. If instead you drive the slider, as the expanding gas does to a piston in an engine, the rod pushes the pin round its circle: reciprocating in, rotary out. The mechanism is the same; only the source of the push changes. There is one subtlety that makes engines need a flywheel. When the slider is at the very end of its stroke, the rod and crank arm are in a straight line and a push on the slider cannot turn the crank at all; it would just press straight through the axle. Engines get past these two dead points because the heavy flywheel on the crankshaft keeps turning from its own momentum, and multi-cylinder engines arrange their cranks so that one piston is always pushing while another is at a dead point. A hand pump avoids the problem because your hand simply keeps moving the handle through the dead point.
