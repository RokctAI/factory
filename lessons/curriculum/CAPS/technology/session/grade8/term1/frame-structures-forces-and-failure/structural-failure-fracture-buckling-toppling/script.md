# Part 1 — Expert

This session classifies structural failure into fracture from lack of strength, bending or buckling from lack of stiffness, and toppling from lack of stability. The anchor is a school science expo in Kimberley where three teams' model bridges are loaded to destruction: one snaps at a glued joint, one sags and its thin uprights bow until the deck sinks, and one, tall and narrow, tips over sideways before the load is even halfway on. The governing idea is that strength, stiffness and stability are three different properties, that each failure mode points to the one that was lacking, and that the cures, stronger material or joints, deeper or braced members, and a wider base with a lower centre of gravity, are different too. Naming the three failure modes, matching each to its missing property and proposing the correct cure are examinable.

## Subtopic: Fracture: a Lack of Strength

Strength is the ability of a material or member to carry a load without breaking. When a structure fails because something in it has broken, snapped, torn or crushed, that is fracture, and it means a lack of strength. A rope that parts under a heavy load, a cardboard strut that tears across, a glued joint that comes apart, a bolt that shears through, a concrete lintel that cracks and drops: all are fractures. The material or the joint was asked to carry more force than it could.

Fracture can be sudden or slow. Brittle materials such as glass, cast iron, chalk and dry pasta fail suddenly, with little warning: they look fine until they snap. Ductile materials such as mild steel, copper and most plastics stretch and deform noticeably before they break, which gives warning and is one reason structural steel is preferred to cast iron. Fatigue is fracture that comes after many cycles of loading and unloading, each too small to break the part on its own; a paper clip bent back and forth snaps after a few bends, and bridges, aircraft and crank axles are inspected for fatigue cracks for the same reason.

The cure for a lack of strength is more material in the right place, a stronger material, or a better joint. Thicken the strut, double the cardboard, use a dowel instead of a straw, choose steel cable instead of string, lengthen the glued overlap, add a gusset at the corner. Joints deserve special attention because most model fractures happen there: the members are often strong enough but the small glued area between them is not. Spreading the load over a larger joint, with a glued tab or a card gusset, cures most of these.

Finding the weakest point is the design skill. Load follows a path through the structure, and the first thing to break is the member or joint carrying the greatest force relative to its strength. Tracing that path, often from the load down to the ground, and reinforcing the critical points is more effective than making everything thicker.

The questions for this section are with you now: what fracture is, brittle versus ductile failure, and the cures for a lack of strength.

## Subtopic: Bending and Buckling: a Lack of Stiffness

Stiffness is the ability of a structure to hold its shape under load without bending, sagging or twisting too much. A structure can be strong, with no member anywhere near breaking, and still fail because it deforms so much that it no longer does its job: a shelf that sags until the books slide off, a bridge deck that bounces, a frame that racks sideways so that the mechanism inside jams. That is failure from a lack of stiffness, and its two common forms are bending and buckling.

Bending is the gradual curving of a member under a load across it. The remedy is depth: a beam resists bending in proportion to the cube of its depth, so a strip of card stood on edge, folded into a channel or made into a box becomes many times stiffer than the same card flat. Triangulation also cures bending in frames, because it replaces long bending members with short members in pure tension and compression, which hardly deform at all. A frame of rectangles bends at its joints and racks; a frame of triangles does not.

Buckling is the sudden sideways bowing of a long, thin member under compression. Press down on a plastic ruler held upright and at a certain load it suddenly bows out to one side; that is buckling, and it happens at a load far below what the material could carry if it were short. A long thin column, a thin cardboard strut, the slender legs of a pylon: all buckle if not restrained. Buckling is dangerous because it is sudden and because once a member has bowed it carries almost nothing, so the load dumps onto its neighbours and the collapse spreads.

The cures for buckling are to make the compression member shorter, by bracing it at intervals so that each unbraced length is short, exactly as pylon bracing holds the legs; to make it fatter, especially as a tube, which keeps material far from the axis and resists bowing in every direction; or to change the design so that the member is in tension instead, since tension members never buckle. In a model, a straw strut that bows can be replaced with two straws glued together, a rolled paper tube, or a dowel, or it can be braced at its midpoint with a cross-member.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between strength and stiffness, what bending and buckling are, and the cures for each.

## Subtopic: Toppling: a Lack of Stability

Stability is the ability of a structure to stay upright and in position. A structure can be strong and stiff and still fail by toppling, tipping over as a whole, because its centre of gravity has moved outside its base. The tall narrow model bridge at the expo was not broken and not bent; it simply fell over. That is a lack of stability.

Three things decide stability. The width of the base: a wide base means the centre of gravity must move a long way sideways before it passes the edge, while a narrow base tips at the slightest lean. The height of the centre of gravity: a low centre of gravity, with the weight concentrated near the ground, needs a large tilt before it reaches the edge, while a high centre of gravity tips at a small angle. The weight: a heavier base resists sideways forces more and lowers the centre of gravity. Pylons, cranes, cooling towers and traffic cones all follow the same recipe: wide at the bottom, heavy at the bottom, light at the top.

Sideways loads are the usual trigger. Wind on a tower, a hand pushing a crank at the top of a frame, a load lifted off to one side, a bump from a passing learner. If the sideways force is large enough to swing the centre of gravity past the edge of the base, the structure topples. Anchoring the base to the ground, with bolts, pegs or clamps, adds stability without adding weight, which is why pylons have concrete foundations and why a model frame can be taped to the desk during testing if the brief allows.

The three properties are independent and the three cures are different. Fracture needs strength: more or better material, bigger joints. Bending and buckling need stiffness: deeper members, tubes, triangulation, bracing. Toppling needs stability: wider base, lower centre of gravity, more weight at the bottom, anchoring. Diagnosing the failure first saves effort, because adding glue to a frame that toppled, or widening the base of a frame whose strut snapped, fixes nothing.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling every collapse a lack of strength; a bent or toppled structure may have had no broken member at all. Two: thinking a stiff structure is automatically stable; a rigid tower on a narrow base still tips. Three: assuming a member that bowed was weak; buckling is a stiffness failure, and a fatter or braced member of the same material cures it. Four: adding weight to the top of a structure to "hold it down"; weight at the top raises the centre of gravity and makes toppling more likely.

The questions for this section are with you now: what decides stability, how toppling happens, and matching each failure to its cure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with three model bridges loaded until they fail — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: When It Snaps

Three model bridges at a science expo, each loaded until it fails. Three different failures.

The first one snaps. A glued joint comes apart and the deck drops. That is fracture: something broke because it was not strong enough.

Glass and chalk snap with no warning. Steel and plastic stretch first, which gives you a warning. A paper clip bent back and forth snaps after a few bends; that is fatigue.

The cure for breaking: more material where it breaks, a stronger material, or a bigger joint. Most models break at the glue, so make the glued area bigger with a tab or a corner piece.

Keep the snapped joint in mind and try the questions for this part: fracture means not strong enough.

## Subtopic: When It Bends or Bows

The second bridge does not break. It sags, and its thin uprights bow outward until the deck sinks. Nothing snapped. It was not stiff enough.

Bending is slow curving. The cure is depth: stand the card on its edge or fold it into a box, and it bends far less. Triangles also stop frames bending at the joints.

Buckling is a long thin strut under a push suddenly bowing sideways, like a ruler pressed from the top. It happens long before the material would break. The cure: make the strut fatter, roll it into a tube, or brace it halfway so each piece is short.

Hold the bowing ruler in mind and try this part's questions: bending and buckling mean not stiff enough.

## Subtopic: When It Falls Over

The third bridge is tall and narrow. It does not break and it does not bend. It falls over. It was not stable enough.

Stability comes from three things: a wide base, a low centre of gravity and weight near the ground. Wide at the bottom, heavy at the bottom, light at the top. Traffic cones, cranes and pylons all follow this rule.

A sideways push, wind or a hand on the crank, tips the structure if the base is too narrow. Anchoring the base helps without adding weight.

Three failures, three cures. Snapped: strength, more material and bigger joints. Bent or bowed: stiffness, deeper members, tubes, triangles, bracing. Fell over: stability, wider base, lower weight, anchoring. Diagnose first, then fix.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Fracture is a lack of strength. Bending and buckling are a lack of stiffness. Toppling is a lack of stability. Each has its own cure, and the wrong cure fixes nothing.

Hold the three bridges, and take the final questions of the lesson: name the failure, name the cure.
