# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the four forces that act on structural members: tension, compression, shear and torsion. The anchor is a bakkie parked under a steel carport in Rustenburg: its drive shaft twists as the engine turns the wheels, the bolts holding the carport posts to the concrete are being sliced sideways by a gust of wind, the posts are compressed and the cable bracing is in tension. The governing idea is that every force on a member can be classified as one of these four by the direction in which it acts relative to the member, and that identifying the force correctly tells a designer how the member might fail and how to shape it. Defining all four forces, giving an example of each in a real structure and marking them on a drawing with the correct arrows are examinable.

## Subtopic: Four Forces on a Member

Tension and compression act along the length of a member, pulling its ends apart or pushing them together. The other two forces act differently. Shear acts across a member, when two forces push in opposite directions on either side of a plane through it, so that one part tends to slide past the other. Scissors cut paper by shear: the two blades push in opposite directions on either side of the cut line and the paper's fibres are sliced. A bolt joining two plates is in shear when the plates are pulled in opposite directions and try to slide past each other with the bolt between them. A rivet in a bridge, a nail in a timber joint and the pin of a hinge are all shear members.

Torsion is twisting. It acts when a member is turned at one end while the other end is held or turned the other way, so that the member's cross-sections rotate relative to each other along its length. Wringing out a wet cloth puts it in torsion. Turning a screwdriver puts its shaft in torsion. The drive shaft of a vehicle, the axle of a crank, a key in a lock and the shaft of a windmill are all in torsion when they work. A member in torsion tends to fail by its outer fibres being stretched and sheared along a spiral, which is why a chalk stick twisted in your hands breaks on a slanted line.

The four forces are distinguished by direction. Tension: along the member, outward. Compression: along the member, inward. Shear: across the member, in opposite directions on either side of a line. Torsion: around the member's axis, in opposite directions at the two ends. A good test is to ask what the member would do if it failed: stretch and snap for tension, crush or buckle for compression, slice through for shear, twist off for torsion.

Members often carry more than one force at once. A beam bending under a load has compression on top, tension underneath and shear near its supports where the load is transferred to the wall. A crank axle has torsion from the handle and bending from the load on the gear. Designers identify each force and shape the member to resist all of them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four forces, the direction of each, and how each would make a member fail.

## Subtopic: Shear and Torsion in Real Structures

Shear is the hidden force of connections. Wherever two parts of a structure are joined, the fastener between them is usually in shear. In the carport, each post is fixed to the concrete slab by bolts; when the wind pushes the roof sideways, the posts try to slide along the concrete and the bolts resist by shear across their shanks. In a steel pylon, every joint is bolted and the bolts carry shear from the members meeting there. In a timber truss, the nail plates that join the rafters to the tie beam are loaded in shear. Earthquakes and floods destroy buildings mainly by shear, as upper floors try to slide sideways over lower ones, and shear walls, solid panels running from foundation to roof, are built to resist them.

Shear also appears inside beams. Near the support of a beam, the load on the beam must be transferred down into the wall, and the material just above the support is being sliced vertically between the part of the beam over the wall and the part hanging out beyond it. This is why a beam that is deep enough to resist bending can still fail by a diagonal crack near its end, and why the web of an I-beam, the thin vertical part, must be thick enough to carry shear.

Torsion is the force of everything that turns. Any shaft that transmits rotary motion from one place to another is in torsion: the drive shaft between a bakkie's gearbox and its back axle, the propeller shaft of a boat, the axle between the crank and the gear in your PAT model, the spindle of a drill. Torsion also appears where a load is applied off the centre line of a member. A balcony fixed along one edge of a building twists the beam it hangs from. A signpost with its sign on one side of the post twists in the wind. A bridge with heavy traffic in one lane only twists along its length.

Resisting torsion needs a shape that keeps material far from the axis. A hollow tube resists torsion far better than a solid rod of the same weight, which is why drive shafts, bicycle frames and scaffold poles are tubes. A flat strip twists easily; a square or round section twists less. Resisting shear needs enough material across the plane of sliding, which is why bolts carrying shear are thick and often used in pairs.

The questions for this section are with you now: where shear appears in connections and beams, where torsion appears in shafts and off-centre loads, and which shapes resist each.

## Subtopic: Identifying Forces on a Drawing

Technology drawings show forces with arrows, and the arrangement of the arrows names the force. Tension: two arrows along the member, pointing away from each other, out through the ends. Compression: two arrows along the member, pointing towards each other, in through the ends. Shear: two arrows across the member, parallel and close together but pointing in opposite directions, one on each side of the plane of sliding. Torsion: two curved arrows around the member, one at each end, turning in opposite directions. These four symbols are standard and examinable.

To identify the force on a member of a drawn structure, first find the loads: where does the weight press, where does the wind push, where does the crank turn. Then follow the load into the member and ask what it is trying to do to it. A member that the loads are trying to pull apart is in tension; look for members whose ends would separate if cut, such as ties, cables and hanging posts. A member the loads are trying to shorten is in compression; look for columns, struts, rafters and arches. A fastener between two parts that are trying to slide past each other is in shear. A shaft or beam that is being turned at one point and held at another is in torsion.

Practice with the carport. The posts carry the roof weight downward: compression. The diagonal cable from the top of one post to the bottom of the next stops the frame racking in wind and can only pull: tension. The bolts at the base of each post, when the wind pushes the roof sideways: shear. The horizontal beam along the front edge, if the roof sheeting is fixed only along one side of it and the wind lifts the sheeting: torsion. Four members, four forces.

In the PAT investigation you will be asked to label the forces on members of your own frame and on the structures you study. Draw the member, draw the arrows, write the name. Then say how you have designed the member to resist it: a dowel for compression, string for tension, a doubled joint for shear, a tube or thicker dowel for torsion.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing shear arrows along the member instead of across it, which turns the diagram into tension or compression. Two: thinking torsion only happens in machines; any off-centre load twists a beam. Three: forgetting that a bending beam has tension and compression at the same time, on opposite faces. Four: labelling a member by its name rather than its force; a strut is in compression and a tie in tension, but a "beam" can carry bending, shear and torsion at once.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four arrow symbols, the steps for identifying a force on a drawing, and the four forces in the carport.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with scissors, a wet cloth and a bakkie under a carport — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pull, Push, Slice, Twist

Two forces you know: tension pulls along a bar, compression pushes along a bar. Two more today.

Shear slices. Scissors cut paper by pushing one way on one side of the line and the other way on the other side. A bolt between two plates that are pulled in opposite directions is being sliced. That is shear.

Torsion twists. Wring out a wet cloth, turn a screwdriver, twist a stick of chalk until it snaps on a slant. That is torsion.

Four forces, four directions: pull along, push along, slice across, twist around.

If it fails: tension snaps, compression crushes or bows, shear slices through, torsion twists off.

Keep the scissors and the cloth in mind and try the questions for this part: pull, push, slice, twist.

## Subtopic: Where Slicing and Twisting Happen

Shear hides in joints. Every bolt, nail, rivet and pin that holds two parts together is being sliced when the parts try to slide past each other. The bolts at the foot of a carport post are sheared when the wind shoves the roof sideways. Shear also hides inside a beam right next to the wall, where the load has to get down into the wall.

Torsion lives in anything that turns. The drive shaft of a bakkie, the axle from your crank to your gear, a drill spindle. It also appears when a load hangs off one side of a beam, like a sign on one side of a pole in the wind.

Tubes fight twisting better than solid rods of the same weight, so shafts and bicycle frames are tubes. Thick bolts, often in pairs, fight slicing.

Hold the bolt and the drive shaft in mind and try this part's questions: where slicing and twisting happen.

## Subtopic: Reading the Arrows

On a drawing, the arrows tell you the force. Two arrows along the bar pointing away from each other: tension. Pointing towards each other: compression. Two arrows across the bar, side by side, opposite ways: shear. Two curved arrows at the ends turning opposite ways: torsion.

To work out the force on a member: find the loads, follow them into the member, ask what they are trying to do to it. Pulling apart, tension. Squashing, compression. Sliding one part past another, shear. Turning one end against the other, torsion.

The carport: posts squashed, cable brace pulled, base bolts sliced, front beam twisted by lifting sheeting.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Tension pulls along, compression pushes along, shear slices across, torsion twists around. Shear lives in bolts, nails, pins and beam ends; torsion lives in shafts and off-centre loads. Tubes resist twisting; thick paired bolts resist slicing. Draw the arrows, name the force, say how your member resists it.
