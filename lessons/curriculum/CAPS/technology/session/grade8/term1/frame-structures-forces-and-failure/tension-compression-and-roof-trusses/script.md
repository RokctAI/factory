# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies structural members under tension and compression, using the roof truss as the example. The anchor is a new RDP house in Soshanguve whose roof trusses, prefabricated from pine and nailed with metal plates, are being lifted onto the walls by three workers before the corrugated sheets go on. The governing idea is that every member of a frame structure is either in tension, being pulled and stretched, or in compression, being pushed and shortened, and that a truss is a triangulated frame in which rafters and struts carry compression while tie beams and ties carry tension, with king post and queen post trusses as the two standard patterns. Defining tension and compression, naming the members of a truss and stating the force in each are examinable.

## Subtopic: Members Under Tension and Compression

A frame structure is made of members, the individual bars, beams or struts that are joined together. When a load is placed on the frame, each member carries part of the load, and it does so in one of two ways. A member in tension is being pulled from both ends; it tends to stretch and get longer. A member in compression is being pushed from both ends; it tends to shorten and, if it is long and thin, to buckle sideways. Every member in a simple triangulated frame is in one or the other.

A rope can carry tension but not compression: pull a rope and it holds, push a rope and it folds. A stack of bricks can carry compression but not tension: push down on the stack and it stands, pull it and it comes apart. Steel, timber and many other materials can carry both, which is why they are used for frames, but even these are usually better at one than the other. Steel is excellent in tension, and a thin steel rod or cable can carry a huge pull. Timber and concrete are better in compression. A good designer puts each material where it does the job it is best at, and shapes the members accordingly: thin for tension, thicker for compression.

You can tell tension from compression in a model by imagining cutting the member. If you cut a member in tension, the two ends spring apart, because they were being pulled away from each other. If you cut a member in compression, the two ends press together or overlap, because they were being pushed towards each other. In a drawing, tension members are often marked with arrows pointing outward along the member, away from each other, and compression members with arrows pointing inward.

Tension and compression also appear together in a single bending member. When a beam bends under a load, the top surface is squashed and the bottom surface is stretched: compression on top, tension underneath. This is why a beam fails by cracking on its underside, and it is the clue to how I-beams and reinforced concrete work, which comes in a later session.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of tension and compression, which materials carry which, and how to tell them apart in a member.

## Subtopic: The Roof Truss and Its Members

A roof truss is a triangulated frame that carries the roof covering and transfers its weight, together with wind and any rain or hail load, to the walls. Trusses are placed in a row along the building, usually about a metre apart, and the roof sheeting or tiles rest on battens or purlins that run across them. Because a truss is made of triangles, it is stiff; because it is a frame, it is light; and because the forces in it are known, each member can be sized exactly.

The members have names. The two sloping members that form the roof surface are the rafters, also called top chords. They carry the roof load down to the walls and are in compression, being pushed inward by the weight above. The horizontal member across the bottom, joining the feet of the rafters, is the tie beam, or bottom chord. Its job is to stop the two rafters spreading apart and pushing the walls outward, so it is in tension, being pulled from both ends. Without the tie beam, a pitched roof would slowly flatten and shove the walls over.

Inside the triangle are the web members. A strut is a web member in compression, pushing two points apart to stop the rafters sagging. A tie is a web member in tension, pulling two points together to stop the tie beam sagging or a joint opening. The vertical member hanging from the apex to the middle of the tie beam is the post. Whether it is in tension or compression depends on the truss pattern, and that is the subject of the next section.

The forces run in a loop. Roof weight presses on the rafters, the rafters push outward at their feet, the tie beam pulls the feet back in, and the walls receive only a downward load at each end. The struts and ties divide the long rafters and tie beam into short lengths so that none of them bend. Every member is either pushing or pulling, and the truss as a whole is in balance.

The questions for this section are with you now: the names rafter, tie beam, strut and tie, and the force in each.

## Subtopic: King Post and Queen Post

The king post truss is the simplest standard pattern. It has two rafters, a tie beam, and a single vertical member, the king post, from the apex down to the centre of the tie beam. Two struts run from the foot of the king post up to the middle of each rafter. The rafters are in compression. The tie beam is in tension. The struts are in compression, holding the rafters up at mid-span. The king post is in tension: it hangs from the apex and holds up the centre of the tie beam, stopping it sagging under its own weight and the ceiling fixed to it. The name comes from the single central post, like a king standing alone. King post trusses suit spans of about five to eight metres, which is a typical house.

The queen post truss is used for wider spans. Instead of one central post, it has two vertical posts, the queen posts, placed a third of the way in from each end, with a short horizontal member called the straining beam between their tops. The rafters are in compression, the tie beam in tension, and the queen posts hang in tension from the rafters to hold up the tie beam at two points instead of one. The straining beam between the posts is in compression, keeping them apart. Struts from the feet of the posts support the rafters. Two posts, two support points, so the tie beam can be longer: queen post trusses span about eight to twelve metres, which suits halls and classrooms.

Modern prefabricated trusses often use more complex patterns, such as the fink truss with a W of web members, but they are all built from the same vocabulary of rafters in compression, a tie beam in tension, struts pushing and ties pulling. Learning king and queen post gives you the key to reading any of them.

In the PAT, a truss-shaped top to your frame is an excellent way to carry a mechanism: the apex is a natural place for an axle, and the triangulation keeps it stiff. Knowing which members pull and which push tells you where string is enough and where you need a dowel.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: assuming the post is in compression because it is vertical and "holds things up"; the king post hangs from the apex and is in tension. Two: thinking the tie beam carries the roof weight; it carries almost no vertical load and exists to stop the rafters spreading. Three: calling every web member a strut; struts push, ties pull, and the name tells you the force. Four: believing a wider span just needs a bigger king post; beyond about eight metres the pattern changes to queen post.

The questions for this section are with you now: the members of king and queen post trusses, the force in each, and the spans each suits.

## Subtopic: Pulled or Squashed

Every bar in a frame is doing one of two things. Pulled from both ends: that is tension, and it wants to stretch. Pushed from both ends: that is compression, and it wants to squash or bow.

A rope can be pulled but not pushed. A pile of bricks can be pushed but not pulled. Steel and timber can do both, but steel is best at pulling and timber and concrete are best at pushing.

Imagine cutting the bar. If the ends spring apart, it was in tension. If the ends press together, it was in compression.

Bend a beam and the top is squashed while the bottom is stretched. That is why beams crack underneath.

Keep the rope and the bricks in mind and try the questions for this part: pulled or pushed.

## Subtopic: The Triangle Over Your Head

A roof truss is the triangle of timber that holds up a roof. A row of them sits on the walls about a metre apart, with the roof sheets on top.

The two sloping pieces are the rafters. The roof pushes down on them, so they are squashed: compression.

The flat piece along the bottom is the tie beam. It stops the rafters' feet sliding apart and pushing the walls over. It is pulled: tension.

Inside the triangle, a strut pushes and a tie pulls. The name tells you the force.

The forces go round in a loop: roof pushes rafters, rafters push outward, tie beam pulls them back, walls get only a straight push down.

Hold the triangle over your head in mind and try this part's questions: rafters squashed, tie beam pulled.

## Subtopic: One Post or Two

The king post truss has one upright in the middle, hanging from the top down to the tie beam. It holds the tie beam up, so it is pulled: tension. Two struts push from its foot up to the rafters. Good for a house, about five to eight metres wide.

The queen post truss has two uprights, one third of the way in from each side, with a short beam between their tops. The two posts hang in tension and hold the tie beam up in two places. The short beam between them is squashed. Good for a hall, eight to twelve metres wide.

In your PAT, a truss top is a strong place for an axle, and knowing which members pull tells you where string is enough.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Tension pulls and stretches, compression pushes and squashes. In a truss the rafters and struts are in compression, the tie beam, ties and posts are in tension. A king post truss has one central post for house spans; a queen post truss has two posts and a straining beam for hall spans.

Hold the king and the two queens, and take the final questions of the lesson: one post or two.
