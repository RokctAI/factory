### subtopic_1 — Four Forces on a Member
**Assistant:** Shear and compression both involve pushing. How do I tell them apart on a bolt?

**Tutor:** Look at the direction of the push relative to the bolt's length, because that is the whole difference. In compression the pushes act along the length of the member, in at both ends, so the bolt would be squashed shorter; that happens if you stand a bolt on end and press down on its head, which is almost never how a bolt is loaded in a structure. In shear the pushes act across the bolt, at right angles to its length, and in opposite directions on either side of a plane through it. Picture two steel plates overlapping with a bolt through both. Pull the top plate to the left and the bottom plate to the right. Each plate pushes on the bolt sideways, the top plate pushing the upper part of the bolt left and the bottom plate pushing the lower part right, and the plane between the plates is where the bolt would be sliced through if it failed. The bolt is not getting shorter; it is being cut like a carrot under a knife. There is a second clue in how the fastener fails: a bolt that has failed in shear shows a clean slanted or flat cut face across its shank at the level where the plates met, while a column that has failed in compression is crushed or has bowed sideways. In your PAT model the same thinking applies to a dowel axle passing through two cardboard uprights: when the crank is turned and the gear resists, the two uprights push on the axle in opposite directions across it, so the axle at each hole is in shear and the cardboard around the hole is being pressed; a doubled layer of card there, or a bead glued on, spreads that shear over more material.

### subtopic_2 — Shear and Torsion in Real Structures
**Assistant:** Why is a hollow tube better than a solid rod against twisting? Surely the solid one has more material.

**Tutor:** It has more material, but most of that material is in the wrong place, because torsion is resisted by material far from the axis and hardly at all by material near it. When a shaft twists, the outer surface rotates furthest and is stretched and sheared most, while the centre line does not move at all; the material close to the centre is barely strained and therefore contributes almost nothing to resisting the twist. In a solid rod the core is dead weight as far as torsion is concerned. Take that core out and spread the same mass of material at a larger radius as a thin-walled tube, and every bit of it is now at the outer surface where the strain is greatest, so the tube resists twist several times better than the rod for the same weight. That is why the drive shaft of a bakkie, the steering column, a bicycle frame, scaffold poles and the mast of a yacht are all tubes, and why a rolled-up newspaper is so much harder to twist than the same paper flat. The same logic explains why torsion failures look the way they do: the outer fibres fail first, along the spiral line where they are most stretched, which is the slanted break you see on a twisted stick of chalk. There is a limit, since a very thin tube wall can crumple locally, so designers balance diameter and wall thickness, but within that limit the rule is simple: for twisting, push the material outward. For your PAT axle this means a hollow straw of large diameter may resist the twist from the crank better than a thin solid skewer, as long as the straw does not kink.

### subtopic_3 — Identifying Forces on a Drawing
**Assistant:** A beam just sits across two walls. How can it have shear, tension and compression all at once?

**Tutor:** Because a load in the middle of a beam does three different things to three different parts of it, and each part responds with the force that fits. Start with bending. The load pushes the middle of the beam down, so the beam curves, its top surface arcing into a shorter path and its bottom surface into a longer one. Shorter means squashed, so the top is in compression; longer means stretched, so the bottom is in tension. A beam that fails in bending cracks on the underside first, where the tension is, and that is why concrete beams have steel bars near the bottom. Now look at the ends. The whole load has to travel from the middle of the beam, where it was applied, to the walls, where it is carried. Just inside each wall, the part of the beam resting on the wall is pushed up by the wall while the part hanging out towards the middle is pulled down by the load. Those two opposite pushes, close together on either side of a vertical plane, are shear, and a beam that fails this way shows a diagonal crack near a support. The amounts vary along the beam: bending is greatest in the middle and zero at the ends, shear is greatest at the ends and zero in the middle under a central load. Engineers draw graphs of both to decide where to put material, which is the origin of the I-beam shape: wide flanges top and bottom for the compression and tension of bending, a thinner vertical web for the shear. In the PAT, if your gear axle rests across two cardboard uprights, the axle is a tiny beam with exactly these forces, plus torsion from the crank, and that is why axles need to be stiff as well as strong.
