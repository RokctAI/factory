### subtopic_1 — Members Under Tension and Compression
**Assistant:** Why is the king post in tension? It stands upright under the apex, so surely the roof is pressing down on it.

**Tutor:** It looks that way, but follow where the loads actually go and the picture flips. The roof load presses on the rafters, and the rafters carry it down their slope to the walls at each end, where the walls take it. At the apex the two rafters lean against each other and the load passes from one to the other; nothing at the apex needs to be held up from below. What does need help is the tie beam. It is a long horizontal timber, perhaps six metres, carrying its own weight plus a ceiling and perhaps a water tank, and left alone it would sag in the middle. The king post is fixed to the apex, where the two stiff rafters meet, and reaches down to the centre of the tie beam. It acts like a string hanging the middle of the tie beam from the apex: the tie beam pulls down on its bottom end, the apex holds its top end, and the post is stretched between them. That is tension. You can prove it with a model: build a truss from strips of card with a string for the king post, and it works perfectly, because string carries tension; replace the tie beam with string too and it still works; but replace a rafter with string and it collapses, because rafters are in compression and string cannot push. The struts that run from the foot of the king post up to the rafters are different; they push the rafters up at mid-span to stop them sagging, so they are in compression and must be stiff members. Vertical does not mean compression; the force in a member depends on what it is connected to and what is pulling or pushing at its ends.

### subtopic_2 — The Roof Truss and Its Members
**Assistant:** If the tie beam is only there to stop the rafters spreading, what happens in old buildings that have no tie beam at all?

**Tutor:** Exactly what you would predict, which is why such roofs are a well-known problem for people who repair old buildings. Without a tie beam the rafters still carry the roof load down their slope, but at the wall they push both downward and outward, and the outward push has nothing to resist it except the wall itself. Over years the walls are shoved outward at the top, cracks open along the eaves, the roof ridge drops as the rafters flatten, and in the worst cases the wall leans and fails. Builders have dealt with this in several ways. The oldest is simply to make the walls very thick and heavy, so that their weight holds them against the outward push, which is why medieval stone churches have massive walls and often external buttresses leaning against them. Another is to tie the two walls together with iron rods running right across the building at ceiling height, with decorative plates on the outside of the walls; those plates, often star or S shaped, are the ends of hidden tie rods added to stop spreading. A third is a collar, a horizontal member joining the two rafters partway up, which reduces the spread though less effectively than a tie at the feet. The modern truss solves it completely by building the tie beam in from the start, so that the walls receive only vertical load and can be as thin as a single brick or a timber frame. This is a nice example of what structural investigation is for: once you know which member carries which force, you can diagnose a failing building and choose the right repair.

### subtopic_3 — King Post and Queen Post
**Assistant:** Why not use a queen post truss everywhere, since it spans further? Would it not be stronger for a house too?

**Tutor:** It would be strong enough, but it would also be a waste, and good design uses just enough structure for the job and no more. A queen post truss has more members than a king post: two posts instead of one, a straining beam, and usually four struts instead of two, with more joints to cut and fasten. For a five-metre house span, the tie beam only needs support at one point to stop it sagging, so a single king post does the job and the extra members of a queen post would carry almost no load while costing timber, labour and weight on the walls. Span is the deciding factor because of bending. A tie beam held at its two ends and its centre has unsupported lengths of half the span; at six metres that is three metres, which pine can manage. At ten metres it would be five metres, and the tie beam would sag and the rafters would be so long that they would bow between their supports. Adding two posts instead of one cuts the unsupported lengths to a third of the span and gives the rafters an extra strut each, which is exactly what the longer span needs. Modern engineered trusses take this logic further with computer-designed web patterns that put a member precisely where the forces demand and nowhere else, and a truss factory will produce a different pattern for every span and roof pitch. For the PAT the same rule applies: brace where the forces are, not everywhere, and be ready to explain why each member of your frame is there.
