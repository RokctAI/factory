# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies structures that span: beams and I-beams, lintels, beam and column bridges, suspension and cable-stayed bridges, arches and cantilevers. The anchor is a journey down the N2: over the Van Stadens arch bridge, past the cable-stayed Nelson Mandela Bridge seen on television from Johannesburg, across the concrete beam-and-column overpasses at every interchange and finally the Bloukrans Bridge, the highest concrete arch in Africa, where bungee jumpers leap from the deck. The governing idea is that every spanning structure must carry load across a gap by converting it into tension and compression in members that lead the load to the supports, and that each type does this differently: a beam by bending, a cable by tension, an arch by compression and a cantilever by bending from a single fixed end. Describing how each type carries its load and naming an example of each are examinable.

## Subtopic: Beams, I-Beams and Lintels

The simplest way to span a gap is a beam: a straight member resting on supports at both ends. A lintel is a beam over a door or window opening, carrying the wall above it; in older houses it is a length of timber or a stone slab, in modern houses a precast concrete bar with steel inside. A beam and column bridge, the commonest type in the world, is a row of beams resting on columns or piers, with the road deck on top; almost every highway overpass is one, and long rivers are crossed by many spans in a row.

A beam carries load by bending. Load pushes down in the middle, the beam curves, its top is compressed and its bottom stretched, and the supports push up at the ends. The deeper the beam, the stiffer it is, because the top and bottom are further apart and resist bending with a longer lever arm. A plank laid flat bends easily; the same plank on its edge hardly bends at all. This is why floor joists stand on their narrow edge and why beams are tall rather than wide.

Since the bending forces live in the top and bottom of a beam and the middle does little except hold them apart and carry shear, engineers remove most of the middle. The result is the I-beam: a wide top flange in compression, a wide bottom flange in tension and a thin vertical web between them. An I-beam gives nearly the stiffness of a solid rectangular beam of the same depth with a fraction of the steel. Universal beams of this shape frame most steel buildings and carry most steel bridges. Hollow box sections and trusses apply the same idea.

Beams are limited by span. Double the span of a beam and the bending effect goes up four times, so beyond about 40 or 50 metres a plain beam becomes too deep and heavy to be practical, and engineers turn to the other spanning types.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a beam carries load, why depth matters, how the I-beam saves material, and what a lintel and a beam and column bridge are.

## Subtopic: Suspension and Cable-Stayed Bridges

Where a span is very long, the road is hung from cables. In a suspension bridge, two tall towers carry a pair of main cables that drape in a curve from anchorages on one shore, over the towers, to anchorages on the other. Vertical hanger cables drop from the main cables to the deck and hold it up. Every cable is in tension, which steel does superbly, so the deck can be hung across gaps of over a kilometre. The towers are in compression, pressed down by the main cables, and the anchorages must be massive blocks of concrete or rock to resist the enormous pull of the cables. The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco and the Akashi Kaikyo Bridge in Japan are suspension bridges; South Africa has few long ones because its rivers are narrow, but suspension footbridges cross many gorges.

A cable-stayed bridge also hangs its deck from cables, but the cables run straight from the deck to one or more towers, like the strings of a harp or a fan, with no draped main cable and no anchorages on the shore. Each stay is in tension and pulls part of the deck up towards the tower; the tower is in compression and carries the whole load down to its foundation. Because the stays pull inwards towards the tower as well as upwards, the deck is also compressed along its length. Cable-stayed bridges suit spans from about 200 to 1000 metres and are cheaper than suspension bridges for those lengths because they need no anchorages. The Nelson Mandela Bridge over the railway yards in Johannesburg is a cable-stayed bridge with two pylons, and so is the Arch Bridge over the N1 at Pretoria's Centurion interchange.

Both types show the designer's rule: use each material for what it does best. Steel cable is the strongest material by weight in tension, so it carries the deck; concrete or steel towers carry compression; and the deck itself is a stiffened beam or box that spreads traffic loads to the hangers. Wind is the great enemy of both, because a light deck hanging from cables can twist and flutter, so modern decks are shaped like aircraft wings to let the wind pass smoothly.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a suspension bridge and a cable-stayed bridge each carry load, which members are in tension and compression, and an example of each.

## Subtopic: Arches and Cantilevers

An arch spans a gap by compression alone. Its curve leads the load from the top of the arch down and outward to the supports at each end, called abutments, so that every part of the arch is squashed and none is stretched. This is why arches can be built of stone, brick or plain concrete, materials that are strong in compression but weak in tension, and why Roman arches two thousand years old still stand. The price is the outward thrust: the abutments must be heavy or braced into solid rock to stop the arch spreading, just as the tie beam stops a roof truss spreading. The Bloukrans Bridge carries the N2 over the Bloukrans River on a concrete arch 272 metres across and 216 metres above the river, and the Van Stadens Bridge near Port Elizabeth is another concrete arch. Railway viaducts and old stone bridges are rows of arches.

A cantilever is a beam fixed firmly at one end only, with the other end sticking out into space. A diving board, a balcony, a shop awning, the jib of a tower crane and the wing of an aeroplane are cantilevers. Because it has no support at the free end, a cantilever carries load by bending from its fixed end, and the bending is greatest at the root, where the beam enters the wall. Unlike a simple beam, a cantilever has tension on top and compression underneath, since the load bends it downward from a fixed root. Cantilever bridges extend two cantilevers out from piers towards each other and often drop a short beam between their tips; the Forth Bridge in Scotland is the classic, and many South African river bridges use balanced cantilevers built outwards from each pier during construction.

Each spanning type has a natural range. Beams for short spans, up to about 50 metres. Arches for medium spans where the ground is solid enough to take the thrust, up to about 300 metres in concrete and over 500 in steel. Cable-stayed bridges for 200 to 1000 metres. Suspension bridges for the longest spans, over 1000 metres. Cantilevers for balconies and roofs and for bridges where supports cannot be placed mid-river. The designer chooses by span, ground, materials, cost and appearance.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking an arch is just a curved beam; an arch is in compression throughout and needs abutments to resist its thrust, while a beam bends. Two: confusing suspension and cable-stayed bridges; suspension bridges drape a main cable between anchorages and hang the deck from it, cable-stayed bridges run each cable straight from tower to deck. Three: assuming a cantilever is supported at both ends; it is fixed at one end only, and the bending is greatest at the root. Four: believing a wider beam is stiffer than a deeper one; depth is what fights bending.

The questions for this section are with you now: how an arch and a cantilever carry load, the natural span range of each type, and the examples.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a plank, a rope and a stone archway — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Crossing the Gap With a Beam

The simplest way across a gap is a beam: a straight bar resting on two ends. A lintel is a beam over a door. A beam and column bridge is beams resting on pillars, like every highway overpass.

A beam works by bending. Push down in the middle and the top gets squashed, the bottom gets stretched. A deep beam bends less than a wide one: lay a plank flat and it bends, stand it on its edge and it does not.

Since the top and bottom do the work, take out most of the middle. That is the I-beam: a wide top, a wide bottom, a thin web between. Nearly as stiff, far less steel.

Beams only go so far. Double the gap and the bending gets four times worse, so beyond about 50 metres you need another idea.

Keep the plank on its edge in mind and try the questions for this part: beams bend, deep is stiff, I-beams save steel.

## Subtopic: Hanging the Road From Cables

For a huge gap, hang the road from cables. Cables only pull, and steel cable is the strongest thing there is for pulling.

A suspension bridge drapes two big cables over two towers, from one shore to the other, and hangs the road from them with vertical cables. The cables pull, the towers are squashed, and massive concrete blocks at each shore hold the cable ends.

A cable-stayed bridge runs cables straight from the road up to a tower, like the strings of a harp. No big draped cable, no blocks on the shore. The Nelson Mandela Bridge in Johannesburg is one.

Both use each material for what it is good at: cable pulls, tower pushes.

Hold the harp strings in mind and try this part's questions: suspension drapes, cable-stayed fans out.

## Subtopic: Curves and Diving Boards

An arch is a curve that is squashed all the way round. Load on top pushes down and out to the two ends, so nothing is stretched. That is why stone and brick arches stand for thousands of years. The ends must be heavy or sunk in rock to stop the arch spreading. The Bloukrans Bridge on the Garden Route is a concrete arch.

A cantilever is a beam fixed at one end only, sticking out into the air: a diving board, a balcony, a crane jib. It bends most where it enters the wall, and it is stretched on top and squashed underneath, the opposite of a normal beam.

Which to use? Short gap, beam. Medium gap on solid rock, arch. Long gap, cable-stayed. Very long gap, suspension. Balcony or awning, cantilever.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Beams span by bending and are stiff when deep, with the I-beam saving steel. Suspension bridges hang the deck from draped cables anchored on shore; cable-stayed bridges run cables straight from tower to deck. Arches span in pure compression and need heavy abutments. Cantilevers are fixed at one end and bend most at the root. Each type has a natural span.

Hold the archway and the diving board, and take the final questions of the lesson: naming the type and how it stands.
