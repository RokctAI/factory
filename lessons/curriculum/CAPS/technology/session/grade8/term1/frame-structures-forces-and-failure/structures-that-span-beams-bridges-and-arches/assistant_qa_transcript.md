### subtopic_1 — Beams, I-Beams and Lintels
**Assistant:** Why does standing a plank on its edge make it so much stiffer? It is the same piece of wood.

**Tutor:** Because stiffness against bending depends far more on how far the material is from the middle of the beam than on how much material there is, and turning the plank on edge moves the wood further from the middle. When a beam bends, the top is squashed and the bottom is stretched, while a line through the centre, the neutral axis, is neither. The material at the top and bottom does the resisting, and it resists more the further it is from that centre line, for two reasons: it is strained more for the same curve, and it acts on a longer lever arm about the centre. Lay a plank 200 mm wide and 25 mm thick flat, and its top and bottom surfaces are only 12.5 mm from the centre line; stand it on edge and they are 100 mm away. The result is dramatic: stiffness grows with the cube of the depth, so the plank on edge is about 8 times 8, or sixty-four times, stiffer than the plank flat. That is why floor joists are tall and narrow, why a ruler bends easily flat and hardly at all on edge, and why the I-beam exists. The I-beam takes the logic to its conclusion: keep wide flanges at the top and bottom where they do the most good, and thin the middle to a web that just holds them apart and carries the shear. For your PAT frame the lesson is direct. If a cardboard member must resist bending, fold it into a tall channel or a box rather than leaving it as a flat strip, and the same card becomes many times stiffer at no extra cost.

### subtopic_2 — Suspension and Cable-Stayed Bridges
**Assistant:** If arches are so strong and so old, why are modern bridges mostly beams and cables instead?

**Tutor:** Arches are superb when their conditions are met, but they make demands that many modern sites cannot satisfy, and beams and cables make fewer. An arch carries its load in compression down to the abutments, but it pushes outward at those abutments with a force often larger than the weight it carries, so the ground at both ends must be solid rock or must be given very heavy foundations. The Bloukrans and Van Stadens bridges are arches precisely because they cross deep gorges with rock walls to push against; a river in soft alluvial soil gives an arch nothing to lean on. Arches also need to be built over the gap before they can carry themselves, so a temporary framework, called centering, has to be erected first, which is expensive over a wide river or deep valley, though modern engineers sometimes build the two halves out as cantilevers and join them. And an arch's shape is set by the forces, so the deck must either sit on top of a rising curve or hang beneath it, which does not suit a flat road crossing a flat site. Beams and columns, by contrast, press only downward on their piers, can be built on almost any soil with piles, can be made in a factory and lifted into place, and keep the road flat, which is why every highway interchange uses them. Cables take over for very long spans where piers cannot be placed. So the arch has not been beaten; it has been given its proper place, deep gorges and rock, where it is still the most elegant and economical choice, as anyone standing on the Bloukrans deck can see.

### subtopic_3 — Arches and Cantilevers
**Assistant:** How does a cantilever balcony stay up when nothing holds its far end? It looks as if it should just tip over.

**Tutor:** It would tip if it were merely resting on the wall, and the whole secret of a cantilever is that it is not resting but fixed, clamped at its root so firmly that the wall can push up on it and also stop it rotating. Picture the balcony slab as a stiff bar with its inner end buried in the wall. The load on the balcony tries to swing the bar downward about the edge of the wall. To stop that, the wall pushes down on the part of the bar inside the wall and up on the part at the face, forming a pair of opposite pushes that create a turning effect equal and opposite to the load's. Inside the slab this appears as bending, greatest at the root and shrinking to nothing at the free tip, with the top surface stretched and the underside squashed, the reverse of a simple beam. That is why a reinforced concrete balcony has its steel bars near the top, not the bottom, and why cracks in a failing balcony appear on the top surface at the wall. For the wall to hold the root, the slab usually continues inside the building as part of the floor, so the floor's weight counterbalances the balcony like the long side of a seesaw, or the wall above clamps it down. The diving board is the same: its root is bolted under a heavy base, and the bending at that root is what you feel as the board flexes. Tower cranes balance their long cantilever jib with a short counter-jib carrying concrete blocks, a seesaw in the sky. In your PAT model a crank arm sticking out from the frame is a small cantilever, and it must be glued firmly along a length of the upright, not just at a point, or it will tear out at the root exactly as the theory predicts.
