### subtopic_1 — From Scenario to Design Brief
**Assistant:** Why must the brief not say how the device will work? Our team already knows we want to use a pulley.

**Tutor:** Because the brief is the question and the mechanism is an answer, and if you write the answer into the question you have stopped designing before you started. The point of the design step is to generate several possible solutions and choose between them against the specifications. A brief that says "a pulley-operated lifting device" rules out gears, levers and racks before anyone has sketched them, and the team never finds out whether one of those would have been lighter, stronger or easier to build in cardboard. The marking rubric reflects this: marks are given for a brief that defines the problem clearly and for a range of different initial ideas, and a team whose brief already names the solution loses on both counts because its "alternatives" are all versions of the same thing. There is also a practical risk. Your team may want a pulley now, but when you sketch it you may discover that a string winding on a bare axle slips, or that the frame must be very tall to get the lift height, and you may want to switch to a gear-driven design. If the brief said pulley, you would have to rewrite it, and your portfolio would show a brief that no longer matches the model. Keep the brief about the problem, write your preference for a pulley as one of the three idea sketches with its advantages noted, and let it win the comparison table honestly if it is the best. Then your design choice is justified by evidence, which is exactly what the examiner wants to see.

### subtopic_2 — Specifications and Constraints
**Assistant:** Is 'it must be safe' a specification or a constraint? It feels like it could be either.

**Tutor:** It is a specification, because it describes a property the finished device must have for its user, but as written it is a poor one, and sharpening it shows the difference between the two lists. Specifications come from the needs of the user and the purpose of the product: what it must do, how well, how safely, how it should look and feel. Constraints come from the situation the designer is working in: how much space, time, money and material there is, and what tools and skills are available. Safety belongs to the first group because it is about the user, so it is a specification. The problem with "it must be safe" is that it cannot be tested, so the evaluate step cannot say whether it was met. Ask what unsafe would look like for a hand-cranked lifting frame: fingers caught in gears, the load dropping when the crank is released, the frame toppling onto someone, sharp wire ends. Each of those becomes a testable specification: "all gears and the winding axle must be enclosed within the frame", "the load must stay up when the crank is released", "the frame must not topple when pushed sideways at the top with a force of one finger", "no wire ends may protrude". Now the evaluation can tick or cross each one. Some teams are confused because a constraint can also mention safety, for example "only tools approved by the teacher may be used", but that is a limit on how you work, not a property of the product, which is the test for telling them apart: specifications describe the product, constraints describe the conditions of designing and making it.

### subtopic_3 — Initial Idea Sketches
**Assistant:** How rough is too rough for an initial idea sketch? Mine look like scribbles compared with the pictures in the textbook.

**Tutor:** Rough is right, and the textbook pictures mislead because they were redrawn neatly for printing. An initial idea sketch has one job: to let someone else, and your future self, understand how the idea would work well enough to judge it against the specifications. It passes that test if the main shape is clear, the parts are labelled and the movement is shown with arrows, and it fails if a reader cannot tell what is what, however neat the lines are. So the question is not whether it is tidy but whether it is readable. Three habits make a rough sketch readable without slowing it down. Draw in 3D, even crudely, using the oblique method from Grade 7, because a flat side view hides how the frame and mechanism fit together. Make the sketch large enough, at least the size of your palm, so labels fit without crowding. And annotate as you draw: every time you add a part, write two or three words beside it and draw an arrow to it, so that nothing is left to guesswork. Lines may wobble, proportions may be off, and you may draw over an earlier line instead of erasing; none of that costs marks. What does cost marks is a single careful ruled drawing presented as "my ideas", because it shows no exploration, and a page of unlabelled doodles, because it shows no thinking. The examiners are looking for evidence of a mind at work on several possibilities, and a page of quick, labelled, honest sketches is exactly that evidence. Save neatness for the working drawing, where it belongs.
