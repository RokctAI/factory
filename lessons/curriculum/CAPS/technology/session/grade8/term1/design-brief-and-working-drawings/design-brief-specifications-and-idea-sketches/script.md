# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers writing a design brief with specifications and constraints and producing initial idea sketches for the PAT. The anchor is a team in a Grade 8 class in Welkom whose scenario is a hand-powered device to raise 20-litre water containers from a low collection point onto a stand, and who must turn that story into a brief, a list of musts and limits, and three sketched ideas before Friday. The governing idea is that a design brief states the problem to be solved, specifications state the requirements the solution must meet, constraints state the limits imposed on the designer, and initial sketches explore several possible solutions quickly so the best can be chosen with reasons. Writing a brief, distinguishing specifications from constraints and producing annotated idea sketches are examinable.

## Subtopic: From Scenario to Design Brief

A design brief is a short, clear statement of the problem a designer is going to solve. It is written from the scenario, which is the client's story, and it turns that story into a task. A good brief answers four questions: who the solution is for, what need it meets, what kind of product will meet it, and where or how it will be used. For the Welkom team, the scenario describes families collecting water at a communal point and struggling to lift full containers onto a stand. Their brief might read: "Design and make a model of a hand-operated lifting device, housed in a stable frame, that will enable one adult to raise a 20-litre water container from ground level onto a stand one metre high at a communal water point."

Notice what the brief does and does not do. It names the user, one adult. It names the need, raising a heavy container. It names the type of solution, a hand-operated lifting device in a frame. It names the setting, a communal water point. It does not say how the device will work; it does not mention gears, pulleys or cams. The brief defines the problem and leaves the solution open, because deciding the solution is the next step and a brief that already contains the answer stops the team from considering alternatives.

A brief is usually one or two sentences and begins with a verb such as "design", "design and make" or "develop". It is the sentence against which the finished model will be judged in the evaluate step, so every word must be defensible from the scenario. If the scenario does not say the device is hand operated, the team must decide that and be able to explain why: no electricity at the water point, cost, simplicity.

Teams often write the brief too vaguely, "make something to help with water", or too narrowly, "build a crane with a pulley". The test is to read it back against the scenario and ask whether a stranger could tell from the brief alone what problem is being solved, without being told how.

## Subtopic: Specifications and Constraints

Specifications are the requirements the finished solution must meet. They describe what it must do and what properties it must have, and each one can be tested. For the lifting device: it must lift a mass representing a 20-litre container, scaled for the model; it must raise the load through a height representing one metre; it must be operated by one person turning a crank; it must hold the load at the top while the container is moved onto the stand; the frame must stand without support and not topple when the crank is turned; the moving parts must be housed inside the frame so that fingers cannot be caught. Specifications usually start with "it must" and describe performance, safety, size, appearance, ergonomics and durability.

Constraints are the limits placed on the designer by the situation rather than by the user's needs. They are the boundaries within which the specifications must be met. For the PAT: the model must be no larger than a stated size, for example 300 mm by 300 mm by 400 mm; it must be made from the materials available, such as cardboard, dowels, string, wire, straws and glue; it must be finished within the two making lessons; the cost of materials must not exceed a set amount; it must be built using the tools in the classroom. Constraints usually mention size, materials, time, cost, tools and skills.

The two lists serve different jobs. Specifications are tested in the evaluate step: did the model lift the load, did it hold at the top, did it stand. Constraints are checked during design and making: is it within size, did we use only the allowed materials, did we finish in time. A specification not met means the solution does not do its job; a constraint not met means the solution is disqualified, however well it works.

Writing good specifications means making them measurable. "It must be strong" cannot be tested; "it must lift a 500 g mass without the frame bending visibly" can. "It must be safe" is vague; "all gears must be enclosed within the frame" is checkable. The marking rubric rewards specifications that can be tested and constraints that are complete.

## Subtopic: Initial Idea Sketches

Initial idea sketches are quick freehand drawings of possible solutions, made before any decision is taken. Their purpose is to get ideas out of heads and onto paper where they can be compared. Each team member produces at least two or three different ideas, drawn in 3D where possible so the shape is clear, with labels, called annotations, pointing to the main parts and explaining how each works. A sketch without annotations is a picture; a sketch with annotations is a design.

Speed matters more than neatness at this stage. Use a pencil, draw lightly, do not use a ruler, and do not erase; if an idea goes wrong, start another beside it. A good page of idea sketches has three or four different ideas, each a few centimetres across, each with five or six short labels: "crank here", "small gear drives big gear to slow the lift", "string winds on this axle", "wide base of double card", "load platform guided by two uprights". Arrows show movement. A sentence under each sketch says the main advantage and the main worry.

The ideas should be genuinely different, not three versions of the same thing. For the lifting device, one idea might wind a string on a crank axle over a pulley at the top of a tall frame; another might use a gear pair to drive a rack that raises a platform; a third might use a lever with the crank pulling its short end down. Different mechanisms, different frames. Only when the ideas are different can the comparison that follows be meaningful.

Choosing the best idea is done against the specifications and constraints. The team draws up a table with the ideas across the top and the specifications and constraints down the side, and marks each idea as meeting, partly meeting or failing each one. The idea that meets the most, especially the most important, is chosen, and the reasons are written down. Often the final design borrows a feature from a rejected idea, which is one more reason to sketch several.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing one idea neatly with a ruler and calling it the idea sketches; the step needs several rough alternatives. Two: sketches with no annotations, which cannot be evaluated because no one can tell how they work. Three: choosing the idea the loudest team member likes instead of the one that scores best against the specifications. Four: throwing away the rejected sketches; they are portfolio evidence and earn marks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose and features of idea sketches, why the ideas must differ, and how the best is chosen.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the water-lifting device for the Welkom team — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Saying the Problem in One Sentence

The scenario is the story. The design brief is the story turned into a job, in one or two sentences.

A brief answers four questions: who is it for, what do they need, what kind of thing will do it, where will it be used. The Welkom team: design and make a model of a hand-operated lifting device in a stable frame that lets one adult raise a 20-litre water container onto a stand one metre high at a communal water point.

The brief says what the problem is. It does not say how to solve it. No gears, no pulleys yet. If you put the answer in the brief, you stop yourself from finding a better one.

Keep the four questions in mind and try the questions for this part: what a brief says and what it leaves out.

## Subtopic: Musts and Limits

Specifications are the musts. It must lift the load. It must hold it at the top. One person must be able to turn the crank. The frame must not topple. The gears must be inside the frame. Each one can be tested later.

Constraints are the limits. No bigger than this size. Only these materials. Finished in two lessons. Costs no more than this. Only the tools in the classroom.

Musts come from the user's needs. Limits come from the situation. Miss a must and the device does not do its job. Break a limit and the device is out, however well it works.

Make the musts measurable. Not "it must be strong" but "it must lift 500 grams without bending".

Hold the musts and limits in mind and try this part's questions: which is which.

## Subtopic: Three Quick Ideas

Now draw. Each person sketches two or three different ideas, quickly, in pencil, no ruler, no rubbing out. Draw in 3D so the shape is clear.

Label each sketch: crank here, this gear drives that gear, string winds on this axle, wide base. Arrows for movement. One line underneath: the best thing and the worst thing about it.

Make the ideas really different: one with a pulley, one with gears, one with a lever. Three copies of one idea are not three ideas.

Then make a table: ideas along the top, musts and limits down the side, tick or cross each box. The idea with the most ticks wins, and you write down why. Keep the losing sketches; they earn marks too.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A design brief turns the scenario into a one-sentence problem: who, what need, what kind of thing, where, without saying how. Specifications are testable musts; constraints are limits on size, materials, time, cost and tools. Several quick annotated sketches of different ideas are compared against the list to choose the best, with reasons.

Hold the table of ticks, and take the final questions of the lesson: three quick ideas and how to choose.
