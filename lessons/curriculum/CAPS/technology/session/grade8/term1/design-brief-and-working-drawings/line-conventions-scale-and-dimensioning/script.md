# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the line conventions, scaling and dimensioning used on working drawings. The anchor is a drawing office at a steel fabricator in Germiston, where a drawing of a bracket produced in Johannesburg is sent to a workshop in Cape Town and comes back as a perfect steel part, because both ends understand the same conventions. The governing idea is that technical drawings use standard line types so that outlines, construction lines, hidden detail and centre lines are never confused, a stated scale so that sizes on paper relate exactly to real sizes, and dimensions in millimetres placed according to fixed rules so that nothing is left to guesswork. Naming the line types, converting sizes with a scale and dimensioning a drawing correctly are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Line Types and What They Mean

A working drawing uses several kinds of line, each with a fixed meaning. The outline, also called the object line, is a dark, continuous, fairly thick line that shows every edge of the object that can be seen from the viewing direction. It is the line the eye reads first, so it must be clear and confident.

The construction line is a very feint, thin continuous line used to set out the drawing: to position the views, to project heights and widths from one view to another, to find the centres of circles and to mark where edges will go before the outlines are drawn over them. Construction lines are drawn lightly enough that they almost disappear behind the outlines, and they are usually left on the drawing rather than erased, because they show how the drawing was built and let an examiner check the method.

The hidden detail line is a dashed line of medium thickness: short dashes with small gaps. It shows edges that exist but cannot be seen from the viewing direction because they are behind or inside the object, such as the hole for an axle seen from the side, or the back legs of a frame seen from the front. Without hidden detail, a single view could not show that a hole goes all the way through.

The centre line is a thin chain line, long dash, short dash, long dash, used to mark the centre of a circle or hole with two crossing centre lines, and to mark the axis of a cylinder or the centre of a symmetrical object. Centre lines locate features and are where dimensions to holes are measured from. Other lines that appear are the dimension line and projection line, thin continuous lines used for measurements, and the cutting plane and break lines used on more advanced drawings.

The rule is simple. Dark for what you can see, feint for guidance, dashed for what is hidden, chain for centres. Mixing them up, drawing a hidden hole as a solid outline, or leaving construction lines as dark as outlines, makes the drawing unreadable, because a maker cannot tell what is really there.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four main line types, how each is drawn, and what each means.

## Subtopic: Scale: Drawing Bigger and Smaller

Few objects fit the paper at their real size. A pylon 40 metres tall must be shrunk thousands of times to fit an A4 sheet; a gear tooth a few millimetres across must be enlarged to show its shape. Scale is the ratio between the size on the drawing and the size of the real object, and it is written on every working drawing so that anyone can convert back.

A scale is written as two numbers separated by a colon. The first number is the drawing, the second is the real object. A scale of 1:1 is full size: one millimetre on the drawing is one millimetre in reality. A scale of 1:2 is half size: one millimetre on paper is two millimetres in reality, so a 300 mm frame is drawn 150 mm long. A scale of 1:10 is a tenth: a 3 metre beam becomes 300 mm. These are reducing scales, used when the object is bigger than the paper. A scale of 2:1 is twice full size: two millimetres on paper for every real millimetre, so a 15 mm gear is drawn 30 mm across. A scale of 5:1 or 10:1 enlarges tiny parts. These are enlarging scales.

To convert a real size to a drawing size on a reducing scale, divide by the second number: at 1:5, a real 250 mm becomes 50 mm on paper. To convert a drawing size back to real, multiply: 50 mm on a 1:5 drawing is 250 mm real. On an enlarging scale it is the other way round: at 2:1, a real 15 mm becomes 30 mm on paper, and 30 mm on paper means 15 mm real. A useful check is that a reducing scale makes the drawing smaller than life and an enlarging scale bigger; if your answer goes the wrong way, you have divided when you should have multiplied.

Whatever the scale, the dimensions written on the drawing are always the real sizes, never the measured paper sizes. A frame drawn at 1:2 as 150 mm long is dimensioned 300. This is why the scale must be stated: the maker reads the real dimensions from the numbers and uses the scale only to understand the picture. The usual scales are 1:1, 1:2, 1:5, 1:10 and their enlarging opposites, chosen so the largest view fills the paper comfortably.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to read and write a scale, converting real sizes to drawing sizes and back, and what the written dimensions show.

## Subtopic: Dimensioning in Millimetres

Dimensioning is writing the sizes on a drawing so that the maker never has to measure the paper. In Technology, all dimensions are in millimetres, and the unit is not written after each number; a note on the drawing, "all dimensions in mm", or the convention itself makes it understood. Writing 300 means 300 mm. Using centimetres, or mixing units, is an error.

A dimension has three parts. The projection lines, also called extension lines, are thin continuous lines drawn outward from the two ends of the feature being measured, at right angles to it, leaving a small gap so they do not touch the outline. The dimension line is a thin continuous line between the projection lines, parallel to the feature, with an arrowhead at each end touching the projection lines. The dimension figure is the number, written above the dimension line near its middle, reading from the bottom or the right of the drawing, never upside down.

Rules make dimensions readable. Place dimensions outside the outline where possible, not inside the view. Keep dimension lines about 10 mm away from the outline and from each other. Do not let dimension lines cross each other or cross outlines. Dimension each size once only; a maker who finds the same length given twice, with different numbers, does not know which to trust. Give overall sizes, the total length, width and height, and then the sizes of the features inside them. Locate holes from their centre lines, and give a hole's diameter with the symbol for diameter, a circle with a slash, before the number. Give a radius with R before the number.

Dimensioning serves the maker, so think about what the maker needs to cut. For a cardboard frame upright 300 mm long and 20 mm wide with an axle hole 15 mm from the top, the maker needs 300, 20, the hole's diameter, and 15 from the top edge to the hole's centre line. Nothing else. Over-dimensioning clutters; under-dimensioning forces the maker to guess or to measure the paper, which a scaled drawing makes meaningless.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing "cm" or using centimetres; Technology drawings are in millimetres only. Two: dimensioning the paper size instead of the real size on a scaled drawing. Three: arrowheads that miss the projection lines or dimension lines that cross outlines, which make it unclear what is being measured. Four: leaving out the overall size, so the maker must add up a row of smaller dimensions and may add wrongly.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a dimension, the rules for placing dimensions, and what a maker needs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a drawing posted from Johannesburg to a workshop in Cape Town — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Four Kinds of Line

A working drawing uses different lines for different jobs, so the maker never has to guess.

Dark solid lines: the edges you can see. These are the outlines.

Very light thin lines: your guide lines, used to set out the drawing before the dark lines go on. Leave them on, lightly; they show how you worked.

Dashed lines: edges that are there but hidden, like a hole seen from the side or legs behind the front.

Thin long-dash short-dash lines: centre lines, crossing at the middle of a hole or down the middle of a rod.

Dark for seen, feint for guide, dashed for hidden, chain for centre.

Keep the four lines in mind and try the questions for this part: which line for which job.

## Subtopic: Shrinking and Growing on Paper

Most things do not fit the page at real size, so we shrink or grow them with a scale.

A scale is two numbers with a colon. First number is the paper, second is real life. 1:2 means half size: 300 mm real is drawn 150 mm. 1:10 means a tenth. 2:1 means twice as big: a 15 mm gear is drawn 30 mm.

Shrinking: divide the real size. Growing: multiply it. Going back from paper to real, do the opposite.

But the numbers you write on the drawing are always the real sizes. A frame drawn at half size is still labelled 300, not 150. That is why the scale is written on the drawing.

Hold the colon in mind and try this part's questions: shrinking and growing on paper.

## Subtopic: Writing the Sizes

Dimensions are the sizes written on the drawing, always in millimetres, with no unit after the number. 300 means 300 mm.

Each dimension has three parts: two thin lines sticking out from the ends of the thing you are measuring, a thin line between them with an arrowhead at each end, and the number written above the middle.

Rules: keep dimensions outside the drawing, about 10 mm away. Do not let them cross. Give each size once only. Always give the overall length, width and height. Measure holes from their centre lines and give their diameter.

Ask what the maker needs to cut, give exactly that, nothing more.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Dark outlines, feint construction lines, dashed hidden detail, chain centre lines. Scale is paper to real, shrink by dividing, grow by multiplying, but write real sizes. Dimensions in millimetres with projection lines, a dimension line with arrowheads and the number above, placed outside, once each, with overall sizes always given.
