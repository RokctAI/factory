### subtopic_1 — Choosing the View
**Assistant:** Why does Grade 8 only ask for one view? Surely one face cannot show the whole frame.

**Tutor:** One view cannot show everything, and that is exactly why the choice of view and the use of hidden detail matter so much at this stage. Grade 8 asks for a single view for two reasons. The first is skill-building: a full orthographic drawing with front, side and top views, all projected from each other and aligned, is a Grade 9 skill that only works if the draughtsperson can already produce one view with correct line types, scale and dimensions. Learning to do one face well, with construction lines, outlines, hidden detail, centre lines and a complete set of dimensions, is the foundation. The second reason is that for many simple structures one well-chosen view really does carry most of the information the maker needs. A frame that is the same shape all the way back, like a flat panel or a box frame of uniform depth, is fully described by its front view plus a single written depth dimension or a note, "all members 20 by 20 mm, frame 200 mm deep". Features that live behind the front face are shown with dashed lines. Where one view genuinely is not enough, for example if the crank handle sticks out sideways and its length matters, the convention is to add a note or a small supplementary sketch rather than to cram the information into the front view by cheating with 3D. The discipline of asking "what can this face not show, and how will I tell the maker?" is itself the lesson, and it prepares you for Grade 9, where the answer becomes "draw the other views".

### subtopic_2 — Setting Out and Drawing to Scale
**Assistant:** What if my frame is not symmetrical and has parts at different depths? How do I show that in a flat view?

**Tutor:** A flat view shows height and width directly, and it shows depth indirectly through three tools: hidden detail, notes and the choice of what to leave out. Anything behind the front face that cannot be seen from the front is drawn with dashed hidden detail lines in the position where it would appear if the front were transparent. If your frame has a rear brace running diagonally where the front has none, draw that brace dashed in the front view, and the maker knows there is a member there and where it goes, even though its depth position is not given numerically. Depth itself, how far back a part sits, is given by a note in words or by a small side sketch, for example "rear upright set back 180 mm from front" or "pulley bracket centred in depth". Where two different members overlap in the front view, such as a front upright and a rear upright that line up exactly, you draw them once as a solid outline, because the front one hides the rear one completely, and you note "front and rear uprights identical". If the structure is so irregular that notes become long and confusing, that is a signal that the object needs more than one view, and in Grade 8 the honest solution is a second small sketch of the side labelled "side view, not to scale, for information", kept separate from the dimensioned front view. The one thing to avoid is drawing depth into the front view by slanting lines, because that turns a working drawing into a 3D sketch that can no longer be measured; depth is told, not drawn, in a single view.

### subtopic_3 — Adding Line Types and Dimensions
**Assistant:** How do I know whether my drawing is good enough before the teacher marks it?

**Tutor:** Use the same two tests the examiner will use, and do them in this order. The first is the maker's test, which checks completeness. Hand the drawing to someone who has not seen your model, ideally a member of another team, and ask them to write down every part they would cut and the size of each, and to point to where every hole would be drilled. If they can do that from the drawing alone, your dimensions are complete; wherever they hesitate or ask a question, a dimension or a note is missing, and you add it. If they get a size wrong, check whether you dimensioned the paper size instead of the real size, which is the commonest reason. The second is the convention test, which checks correctness, and you can do it yourself with a checklist. Is there one view, flat, with no slanted depth lines? Is the scale written, and is it a standard one that fills the page sensibly? Are construction lines present but feint, and are outlines dark and continuous? Is every hidden feature dashed, and does every hole have crossing centre lines? Are all dimensions in millimetres with no unit, outside the view, with projection lines, dimension lines, arrowheads and the figure above, each size given once, and the overall width and height present? Is there a title block with title, scale, "all dimensions in mm", names and date? Tick each item honestly. Most lost marks in working drawings come from one of three slips: a missing overall dimension, a hidden feature drawn solid, and a title block left out, so check those three twice. A drawing that passes both tests is one another team could build from, which is the only definition of good enough that matters.
