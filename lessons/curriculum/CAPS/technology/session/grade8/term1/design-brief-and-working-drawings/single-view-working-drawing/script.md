# Part 1 — Expert

This session produces a single-view 2D working drawing of the PAT structure with dimensions, line types and scale. The anchor is the Welkom team's water-lifting frame, 400 mm tall, 300 mm wide and 200 mm deep, with a crank axle 50 mm from the top and a pulley at the apex, which must be drawn on an A4 sheet so the team can cut every part correctly in the making lesson. The governing idea is that a working drawing shows one face of the object, chosen to reveal the most information, drawn to a stated scale with construction lines, outlines, hidden detail and centre lines in their correct weights, and dimensioned in millimetres so that the maker needs nothing else. Choosing the view, setting out to scale, applying line conventions and dimensioning completely are examinable.

## Subtopic: Choosing the View

A single-view working drawing shows one face of the object exactly as it would appear if you looked straight at that face, with no perspective and no depth. It is a 2D drawing: height and width only. Its value is precision. Because the face is drawn flat, every length on it can be measured and dimensioned exactly, which a 3D oblique or isometric sketch cannot offer. Grade 8 asks for one view; Grade 9 adds the other views to make a full orthographic drawing.

The first decision is which face to draw. Choose the view that shows the most about the shape and the features that must be made: usually the front, the face a user would stand in front of. For the Welkom frame, the front view shows the overall height and width, the two uprights, the base, the cross-braces, the position of the crank axle hole and the pulley at the top. The side view would show the depth and the crank handle sticking out, but less of the mechanism. If important features are on different faces, choose the face with the most and note the rest in words or in a small second sketch.

Think about what the maker must cut. The maker needs the lengths and widths of the uprights, the base and the braces, the positions and diameters of the axle holes, and the overall size to check against the constraint. The chosen view must make all of these visible or show them as hidden detail. A view that hides the axle holes behind a solid panel is a poor choice, however pretty it looks.

The drawing is made on plain or squared paper, in pencil, with a ruler and set square, and on A4 in landscape or portrait depending on the shape of the frame. A tall frame fits portrait; a wide one fits landscape. Leave room around the view for dimensions, about 30 mm on each side, and room at the bottom for a title block with the drawing title, the team's names, the scale and the date.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a single-view drawing is, how to choose the view, and what the maker must be able to read from it.

## Subtopic: Setting Out and Drawing to Scale

Setting out begins with the scale. Measure the real overall size of the face: the Welkom frame's front is 400 mm high and 300 mm wide. An A4 sheet in portrait is 210 mm by 297 mm, and after leaving margins for dimensions the usable area is about 150 mm by 230 mm. A scale of 1:2 makes the frame 200 mm by 150 mm, which fits. A scale of 1:1 would not fit; 1:5 would make it 80 mm by 60 mm, too small to dimension clearly. Choose the largest standard scale that fits: here 1:2. Write it in the title block at once.

Next, draw the construction lines. With a hard pencil and a light hand, draw a feint horizontal line for the base and a feint vertical line for one side, positioned so the view will sit centrally. From these, measure the scaled overall width and height, 150 mm and 200 mm, and draw feint lines to make the overall rectangle. Inside it, set out the members: the uprights 20 mm wide in reality become 10 mm on the drawing; the base rail 30 mm deep becomes 15 mm; the cross-braces are drawn from corner to corner of the panels they stiffen. Locate the axle hole: 50 mm from the top in reality is 25 mm on the drawing, measured down the centre line of the frame. All of this is still feint, so mistakes can be adjusted without erasing.

Check the set-out before darkening. Measure the overall rectangle again. Check that both uprights are the same width and that the hole is on the centre line. Look at the whole drawing and ask whether it looks like the frame; a set-out error is cheap to fix now and expensive later.

Now darken the outlines. With a softer pencil or firmer pressure, go over every visible edge with a dark, continuous line: the outside of the frame, the inner edges of the uprights and base, the edges of the braces, the circle of the pulley at the apex. Use the ruler for every straight line and a compass or circle template for the holes and pulley. The construction lines stay beneath, feint, as evidence of the method.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing a scale that fits the paper, setting out with construction lines, and checking before darkening.

## Subtopic: Adding Line Types and Dimensions

With the outlines dark, add the remaining line types. The axle hole passes right through the uprights; in the front view it is seen as a circle, so draw it as a dark circle, but if any feature is behind the front face and cannot be seen, such as a rear brace hidden by a front panel or a dowel running from front to back, draw it with dashed hidden detail lines. Add centre lines: two thin chain lines crossing at the centre of each hole and of the pulley, extended a few millimetres beyond the circle, and a vertical chain line down the centre of the frame if it is symmetrical.

Then dimension. Start with the overall sizes, placed outside the view about 10 mm from the outline: 300 across the bottom for the width, 400 up one side for the height. Remember that these are the real sizes, not the 150 and 200 measured on the paper. Then the member sizes: 20 for the width of an upright, 30 for the depth of the base rail. Then the locations: 50 from the top edge to the centre line of the axle hole, and the hole's diameter with the diameter symbol, perhaps 6 for a 6 mm dowel. Each dimension has projection lines leaving a small gap from the outline, a dimension line with arrowheads touching the projection lines, and the figure above the line. Keep dimension lines from crossing. Give each size once.

Finish with the title block: a rectangle in the bottom right corner containing the title, "Water-lifting frame: front view", the scale "1:2", the note "all dimensions in mm", the team members' names and the date. The title block is what tells a stranger what they are looking at.

The final check is the maker's test: hand the drawing to a classmate and ask them to list the parts they would cut and the sizes they would cut them to. If they can do it without asking you anything, the drawing is complete. If they ask, add what they asked for.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: choosing a scale by guesswork so the view runs off the page or sits tiny in a corner; measure the paper and the object first. Two: drawing in 3D "to make it look nice"; a working drawing is a flat view so that it can be dimensioned. Three: dimensioning the paper sizes, 150 and 200, instead of the real 300 and 400. Four: leaving out the title block, so nobody knows the scale or what the drawing shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding hidden detail and centre lines, dimensioning in the right order, and completing the title block.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the water-lifting frame on an A4 sheet — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Which Face to Show

A single-view working drawing shows one face of your structure, flat, as if you stood right in front of it. No depth, no 3D. Just height and width.

Why flat? Because flat can be measured exactly, and the maker needs exact sizes.

Pick the face that shows the most: usually the front. For the water-lifting frame, the front shows the height, the width, the two uprights, the base, the braces, the axle hole and the pulley. That is nearly everything the maker needs to cut.

Use A4, pencil, ruler. Tall frame, hold the page upright. Wide frame, turn it sideways. Leave space round the edge for the measurements.

Keep the front of the frame in mind and try the questions for this part: which face to show and why flat.

## Subtopic: Light Lines First

First pick the scale. The frame front is 400 by 300 mm. A4 is 210 by 297. Half size, 1:2, makes it 200 by 150. That fits with room to spare. Write 1:2 in the corner now.

Now light lines. A faint line for the base, a faint line for one side. Measure 150 across and 200 up and draw the faint outer rectangle. Faint lines for the uprights, 10 mm wide on paper because they are 20 mm real. Faint braces corner to corner. The hole 25 mm down from the top on paper because it is 50 real.

Stop and check. Both uprights the same? Hole in the middle? Looks like the frame? Fix anything now, while it is faint.

Then press harder and go over every edge you can see with a dark line, ruler for straights, compass for circles. Leave the faint lines underneath.

Hold the faint lines first in mind and try this part's questions: scale, set out, check, darken.

## Subtopic: Finishing the Drawing

Now the special lines. Anything behind the front that you cannot see: dashed. Centre of every hole and the pulley: two thin dash-dot lines crossing.

Then the sizes. Outside the drawing, 10 mm away. The overall width, 300, along the bottom. The overall height, 400, up the side. Real sizes, not the paper sizes. Then the upright width, 20. Then 50 from the top to the hole's centre line, and the hole's diameter. Thin lines out, thin line between, arrowheads, number on top. Each size once. No crossing.

Last, the title block in the corner: what it is, the scale, "all dimensions in mm", your names, the date.

Then the test: give it to a friend and ask what they would cut. If they can tell you without asking, you are done.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Choose the face that shows the most. Choose the biggest standard scale that fits the paper. Set out in feint construction lines, check, then darken the outlines. Add dashed hidden detail and chain centre lines. Dimension in real millimetres, outside, once each, overall sizes first. Finish with a title block and the maker's test.

Hold the title block and the friend's test, and take the final questions of the lesson: finishing the drawing.
