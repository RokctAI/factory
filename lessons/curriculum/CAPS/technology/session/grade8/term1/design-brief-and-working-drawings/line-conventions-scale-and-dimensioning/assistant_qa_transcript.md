### subtopic_1 — The Line Types and What They Mean
**Assistant:** Why are construction lines left on the drawing? Surely a finished drawing should look clean.

**Tutor:** They are left on because a working drawing is not a picture to be admired but a record of a method, and the construction lines are the proof that the method was followed. When a draughtsperson sets out a drawing, the feint lines fix the positions of the views, carry heights and widths across from one view to another and locate the centres of holes. An examiner or a checker can look at those lines and see immediately whether the views line up, whether a height in the front view really was projected to the side view or just guessed, and whether a circle was drawn about a properly found centre. Erase them and all that evidence goes, leaving only a result that might be right by accident. There is a second reason, which is damage. Erasing on a drawing roughens the paper, smudges nearby outlines and often removes part of a line you wanted to keep, so professional drawing practice has always been to draw construction lines so lightly that they need no erasing; they sit there, faint and tidy, under the dark outlines, visible if you look for them and invisible if you do not. The skill being taught is pencil control: a hard pencil, a light hand and a steady ruler for the feint lines, then a softer pencil or more pressure for the outlines. In the PAT the rubric awards marks for correct use of line types, and a drawing with no construction lines at all does not demonstrate that you can use them, while one with construction lines as dark as the outlines shows that you cannot tell the two apart. Light enough to be ignored, present enough to be checked, is the standard.

### subtopic_2 — Scale: Drawing Bigger and Smaller
**Assistant:** If I draw at 1:2 and label the frame 300, won't the maker measure 150 on the paper and be confused?

**Tutor:** The maker should never measure the paper at all, and the whole system of dimensioning exists so that they do not have to. A paper drawing stretches with damp, shrinks when photocopied, is sometimes printed at a different size and is often read from a screen at some arbitrary zoom; a size measured from it with a ruler is unreliable to a few percent at best. The written dimension, by contrast, is exact and survives every copy. So the convention is absolute: the number on the drawing is the real size, and the scale is written in the title block only to tell the reader how the picture relates to reality, so they can judge proportions and recognise what they are looking at. A maker who sees 300 cuts 300 mm, full stop. If they want to check that the drawing is consistent, they may measure 150 on the paper, look at the scale 1:2, multiply and get 300, which confirms the drawing is correctly drawn, but the cutting is always done to the written figure. This also explains why an undimensioned drawing is nearly useless, however carefully it is drawn to scale, and why a dimension given twice with different numbers is dangerous: the maker has no way to decide which is true. Your job as the draughtsperson is therefore to draw the picture to scale so that it looks right and the views line up, and then to write every real size the maker needs once, clearly, in millimetres.

### subtopic_3 — Dimensioning in Millimetres
**Assistant:** How do I decide which dimensions to include? If I dimension everything the drawing becomes a mess.

**Tutor:** Imagine you are the maker, standing at the bench with the card, the ruler and the knife, and ask what measurements you would need to mark out and cut each part without ever having to deduce, add up or measure the drawing. Those, and only those, are the dimensions to include. Start with the overall sizes of the whole object: its total length, width and height, because the maker needs to know what size of card to start with and because an overall dimension lets them check the smaller ones. Then dimension each feature the maker must cut or drill: the length and width of each member, the diameter of each hole and the position of its centre from a clear edge or from another centre line. Stop there. Do not add dimensions that can be obtained from others already given; if the overall length is 300 and one part is 100, the remaining 200 is already known and dimensioning it as well creates the risk of three numbers that do not add up. Do not dimension the same feature in two views; choose the view that shows it most clearly and dimension it there once. Do not dimension hidden detail if the same feature is visible in another view. A quick test for a finished drawing is to cover the drawing with your hand and try to make the object from the numbers alone: if you cannot, a dimension is missing; if you find a number you never used, it is surplus. Clear, sufficient and not repeated is the standard, and a maker who receives such a drawing can build the part without a single phone call to the designer.
