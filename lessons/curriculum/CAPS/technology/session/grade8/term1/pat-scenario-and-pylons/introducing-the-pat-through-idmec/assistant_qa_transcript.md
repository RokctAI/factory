### subtopic_1 — The Term 1 PAT Scenario
**Assistant:** Why is the PAT done in teams if each of us gets our own mark?

**Tutor:** Because real technology is done in teams, and because a team can build something better than any one member could alone, while your own portfolio proves what you personally contributed and understood. Engineers, architects and designers almost never work alone: a bridge is designed by structural engineers, drawn by draughtspeople, costed by quantity surveyors and built by contractors, and each one must be able to explain their part. The PAT mirrors that. The team shares the investigation, agrees the brief, chooses between members' sketches and builds one model, so that the frame can be braced by one person while another cuts gears and a third makes the crank, and the model is finished in the time allowed. Your individual mark comes from the evidence in your own portfolio: your notes from the investigation, your own initial sketches, your contribution to the working drawing, your evaluation of the model and your part of the presentation. Teachers watch the making sessions as well, and a learner who sits back while others work has nothing to put in the portfolio. There is a fairness point too. The team chooses the best idea, which may not be yours, but your sketch still earns its marks for being a reasonable, well-drawn idea, and your evaluation can honestly say what you would have done differently. So the team is the way the model gets built; the portfolio is the way you get credit for what you learned building it.

### subtopic_2 — IDMEC Applied to the PAT
**Assistant:** What if our mechanism works perfectly but the frame falls over? Do we lose everything?

**Tutor:** No, and understanding why tells you how the marks are spread. The PAT is marked across all five IDMEC steps, and the make step is only one of them. If your investigation notes are thorough, your design brief fits the scenario, your working drawing is correct and your presentation is clear, you have already earned most of the available marks before the model is tested. A frame that falls over costs marks in the make step, where stability and build quality are assessed, but it can actually earn marks in the evaluate step, provided you deal with it honestly. An evaluation that says "the frame toppled when the crank was turned because the base was only 80 mm wide for a height of 300 mm, and we would widen the base to 150 mm and add a cross-brace at the bottom" shows exactly the understanding the rubric rewards; an evaluation that says "it worked well" when everyone saw it fall shows the opposite. Better still, if the frame falls in the making week, you still have time to fix it before evaluation, which is how real designers work: test early, fail small, improve. That is also why this course teaches the structures content in Weeks 4 and 5, before you design: a team that has studied pylons knows about wide bases, low centres of gravity and triangulation before it draws a single line. The team that loses the most is the one that hides a problem; the team that gains the most is the one that finds it, explains it and fixes it.

### subtopic_3 — Structural Components and Mechanisms Together
**Assistant:** How detailed does the scenario have to be in our design brief? Can we change the scenario to fit a mechanism we already want to build?

**Tutor:** You may interpret the scenario, but you may not replace it, and the difference decides whether your brief earns marks. The scenario is the client's problem; your brief is your statement of how you understand that problem and what you intend to do about it. Interpreting means making reasonable choices the scenario leaves open: if it says a community needs a hand-powered device to lift containers from a pit, you may decide the containers are 20-litre buckets, that the pit is one metre deep and that the device will be used by adults, and you may choose a crank driving a wheel and axle with a pulley because that suits lifting. Those are design decisions, and you should record the reasons. Replacing means ignoring the need: deciding to build a cam-driven nodding animal because you enjoyed the cam lesson, and then writing a brief about a "display device" that the scenario never asked for. That earns little, because the first thing the rubric asks is whether the brief addresses the scenario. The practical test is to write your brief as one or two sentences that name the user, the need and the device, then read the scenario again and check that every need in it is met by something in your brief. If you genuinely find the scenario allows more than one kind of device, choose the one your team can build well and say why in the design notes; a justified choice is a strength. If you want to use a particular mechanism, find the honest reason it suits the need, and if there is no honest reason, choose a different mechanism.
