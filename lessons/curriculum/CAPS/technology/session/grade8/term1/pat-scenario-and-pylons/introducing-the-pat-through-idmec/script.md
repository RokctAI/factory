# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Term 1 PAT through the IDMEC design process. The anchor is a community in the Eastern Cape that needs a small hand-operated device, such as a crank-driven lift for raising buckets from a storage pit or a model drawbridge for a school gate, in which a frame structure must support and protect a working mechanism. The governing idea is that the PAT is an IDMEC project: investigate existing structures and mechanisms, design a solution with a brief, sketches and working drawings, make a model safely, evaluate it against the specifications and communicate the result, with marks awarded at every step. Stating the PAT scenario, mapping IDMEC onto the term's weeks and explaining how structural components and mechanisms combine are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Term 1 PAT Scenario

A PAT is a Practical Assessment Task. It is the main piece of formal assessment in Technology each term, and it is practical: you investigate, design, make, evaluate and communicate a real solution to a stated problem, working in a team but with your own portfolio of evidence. The Term 1 PAT for Grade 8 asks each team to design and make a structure that houses a mechanism. The structure is a frame that must stand up, carry a load and keep its shape. The mechanism is a moving system, driven by a crank, that uses at least one of the mechanisms studied this term: gears, a cam, a wheel and axle, a pulley or a lever.

The scenario gives the problem a context. In a typical version, a rural community needs a hand-powered device for a job such as lifting water containers from a low platform, raising and lowering a barrier at a school entrance, or presenting a moving display at a community event. The device must be operated by one person turning a handle, must be housed in a stable frame that protects the moving parts, and must be built as a working scale model from materials such as cardboard, dowels, wire, straws, string and glue. Your teacher may use the textbook scenario or adapt it to your area.

The scenario matters because every later decision is judged against it. The design brief is written from it, the specifications come out of it, and the evaluation asks whether the model solves the problem the scenario set. A beautiful model that does not fit the scenario earns fewer marks than a plain one that does. So the first task of the PAT is to read the scenario carefully and underline the needs: who the user is, what the device must do, where it will be used and what limits apply.

The PAT runs from Week 4 to Week 9 of the term. Each week has a step of IDMEC attached to it, and each step produces evidence for the portfolio: notes, sketches, drawings, photographs of the model, an evaluation sheet and a presentation. The portfolio is what the marks are awarded on, so keep everything.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a PAT is, what the Term 1 PAT asks for, and why the scenario matters.

## Subtopic: IDMEC Applied to the PAT

Investigate comes first, in Weeks 4 and 5. The team studies existing structures that do a similar job, with the electrical pylon as the case study for how frames are made stiff with bracing and triangulation, and examines how members of a frame carry tension and compression, how beams, arches and cables span gaps, and how structures fail. The investigation also looks at existing mechanisms: how a hand winch, a drawbridge or a lift uses a crank, gears or pulleys. Evidence: notes, labelled sketches of existing designs and a list of their advantages and disadvantages.

Design comes next, in Week 6. The team writes a design brief from the scenario, lists specifications, which say what the device must do, and constraints, which say what limits apply, such as size, materials and time. Each member sketches initial ideas, the team chooses the best, and a single-view 2D working drawing is produced with line conventions, dimensions in millimetres and a scale. Evidence: the brief, the sketches, the chosen idea with reasons, and the working drawing.

Make comes in Week 7. The team builds the structure and the mechanism from the working drawing, using tools safely: cutting away from the body, using a cutting mat, wearing eye protection when cutting wire, keeping glue off skin. Evidence: the model and photographs of the stages.

Evaluate comes in Week 8. The team tests the model against each specification: does the frame stand, does it carry the load, does the mechanism move when the crank is turned, does it do the job the scenario asked? Strengths and weaknesses are recorded honestly and improvements proposed. Evidence: the completed evaluation sheet.

Communicate comes in Week 9. The team presents its plans, drawings, model and evaluation to the class. Evidence: the presentation and any posters or artist's impressions. Week 10 then adds the isometric drawing of the design as consolidation.

## Subtopic: Structural Components and Mechanisms Together

The PAT joins two strands of the subject that are usually taught separately. The structures strand supplies the frame: upright members called columns or posts, horizontal members called beams or ties, diagonal members called braces or struts, joined at the corners and, where necessary, triangulated so that the frame cannot rack sideways. The frame must be stable, which means a wide enough base and a low centre of gravity, and stiff, which means it does not bend or twist when the crank is turned. A frame that wobbles when the handle is turned will make the gears skip or the cam jam.

The mechanical systems strand supplies the moving part. A crank is the input, because one person turning a handle is the power source. From the crank, the motion can go to a gear pair to make the output slower and stronger or faster and weaker, to a cam to make something bob up and down, to a wheel and axle winding a string over a pulley to lift a load, or to a lever to tip a platform. The mechanism needs axles that run through holes in the frame members, so the frame must be designed with the axle positions in mind; the holes must be in line, otherwise the axle binds.

The two strands meet at the bearings, the points where the moving axles pass through the fixed frame. This is where most model failures happen: holes that are too tight and stop the axle, holes that are too loose and let it wobble, and frame members too thin to carry the load of the axle. Good teams design the bearings early, reinforce the frame around them with doubled cardboard or a glued washer, and make sure the axle is straight.

Thinking about the whole system is what the examiners look for. A systems diagram, input, process, output, helps: input is the person turning the crank, process is the mechanism inside the frame, output is the lifted bucket or the raised barrier. The frame is what makes the process possible. Teams that can explain their model in these terms score well in the communicate step.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: designing the mechanism first and squeezing a frame around it afterwards; the frame and mechanism must be designed together. Two: treating the PAT as a making project only; most marks are for investigation, design, evaluation and communication. Three: losing portfolio evidence; a sketch that is not in the portfolio earns nothing. Four: building a model that works but does not answer the scenario; the scenario is the question and the model is the answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a hand-cranked bucket lift for a village — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What You Are Going to Build

PAT stands for Practical Assessment Task. It is the big project of the term, and most of your term mark comes from it.

This term's PAT: build a frame that stands up and holds a moving mechanism. You turn a crank, and inside the frame gears, a cam, a pulley or a lever make something move: a bucket lifts, a barrier rises, a figure bobs.

The scenario gives the story: a community needs a hand-powered device for a job. Read it carefully and underline who needs it, what it must do and what the limits are. Everything you do later is judged against that story.

Keep the bucket lift in mind and try the questions for this part: what the PAT asks and why the scenario matters.

## Subtopic: Five Steps, One Project

The PAT follows IDMEC, one step per week or two.

Investigate, Weeks 4 and 5: look at pylons and bridges to see how frames are made strong, and look at winches and lifts to see how mechanisms work. Keep notes and sketches.

Design, Week 6: write the brief, list what it must do and what the limits are, sketch ideas, pick the best, draw it with measurements.

Make, Week 7: build it safely from the drawing.

Evaluate, Week 8: test it against the list. Be honest.

Communicate, Week 9: present drawings, model and evaluation to the class.

Everything goes in your portfolio. No evidence, no marks.

Hold the week-by-week plan in mind and try this part's questions: which step comes when.

## Subtopic: A Frame With Something Moving Inside

Your model has two halves. The frame: uprights, cross-pieces and diagonal braces so it cannot wobble, a wide base so it cannot tip. The mechanism: a crank you turn, then gears, a cam, a pulley or a lever, on axles that run through holes in the frame.

The two halves meet at the holes where the axles pass through. Too tight and the axle sticks; too loose and it wobbles; too thin and the frame bends. Plan those holes early and strengthen the cardboard around them.

Think of it as a system: input is you turning the crank, process is the mechanism inside, output is the bucket going up. The frame is what holds the process still.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Term 1 PAT is a frame structure that houses a crank-driven mechanism, solving a scenario for a community. It runs through IDMEC from Week 4 to Week 9: investigate pylons and mechanisms, design a brief and working drawing, make safely, evaluate against the specifications, communicate the result. The frame and mechanism are designed together and meet at the bearings, and all evidence goes into the portfolio.

Hold the crank and the frame, and take the final questions of the lesson: how the two halves fit together.
