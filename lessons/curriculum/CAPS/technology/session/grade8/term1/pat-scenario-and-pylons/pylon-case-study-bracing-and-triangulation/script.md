# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the Investigate step of the PAT: a case study of electrical pylon designs, their internal cross-bracing and triangulation, and the skill of evaluating complex structures. The anchor is the line of lattice pylons that carries 400 kilovolt power from the Mpumalanga power stations across the Highveld towards Gauteng, visible from the N4 and N12, each tower standing more than 40 metres tall on four slender legs. The governing idea is that a pylon is a frame structure made stiff by triangulation, so that every panel of the lattice is divided into triangles that cannot change shape, and stable by a wide base, and that evaluating such a structure means listing advantages and disadvantages against its purpose, materials, cost, safety and impact. Explaining why pylons are lattice frames, how cross-bracing stiffens them and how to evaluate complex structures are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Pylons Look the Way They Do

A pylon, or transmission tower, has one job: to hold high-voltage cables far enough above the ground, and far enough apart from each other, that they are safe, and to go on doing so through storms, heat and decades of weather. The conductors are heavy, several tonnes per span, and they pull on the tower from both sides; the wind pushes on the tower and on the cables; the tower's own weight presses down on its legs. The structure must resist all of these without bending, twisting or toppling, and it must do so as cheaply as possible, because a single transmission line may need a tower every 400 metres for hundreds of kilometres.

The answer engineers found is the lattice tower: a frame of steel angle sections bolted together into an open framework, tapering from a wide base to a narrow top, with cross-arms near the top to carry the insulators and conductors. The lattice is open because most of the material in a solid post would be doing nothing; the loads are carried by the four corner legs and the bracing between them, so everything else can be left out. This saves steel, saves weight, saves transport cost, and lets the wind blow through the tower instead of pushing against a solid wall. Galvanised steel resists rust for fifty years or more.

Different pylon shapes suit different jobs. Suspension towers, the common ones along a straight line, simply hold the cables up. Tension or angle towers, at corners and at the ends of lines, are much heavier because the cables pull them sideways. Some lines in flat open country use guyed towers, thin masts held up by steel cables anchored to the ground, which use even less steel. Monopole towers, single tubular steel poles, are used near towns where land is scarce and appearance matters. In South Africa the lattice tower dominates because steel is made locally, the designs are proven, and open country allows a wide base.

The PAT connects here directly. Your model frame must also carry a load, stay stiff while the crank is turned and stand stable on the desk, with as little material as possible. Studying how pylon designers solved the same problems with steel is the investigation that informs your design in cardboard and dowel.

The questions for this section are with you now: what loads a pylon must resist, why it is an open lattice, and how pylon designs differ.

## Subtopic: Cross-Bracing and Triangulation for Stiffness

Grade 7 established that a frame made of rectangles is not stiff: push on one corner and the rectangle racks into a parallelogram because its joints can hinge, even when the members themselves are strong. A triangle cannot do this. Its three sides fix its three angles, so the only way to change its shape is to stretch or crush a member, which takes a very large force. Dividing a frame into triangles is called triangulation, and it is the single idea that makes lattice pylons, roof trusses, bridge girders and crane jibs possible.

Look closely at a pylon and every panel between the corner legs is triangulated. The most common pattern is cross-bracing, also called X-bracing: two diagonal members crossing in each rectangular panel, dividing it into four triangles. When the wind pushes the tower sideways, one diagonal of each X goes into tension, being stretched, and the other into compression, being squashed; between them they hold the panel square. Other patterns are also used: a single diagonal in each panel, a zig-zag of diagonals running up the tower called a Warren pattern, or K-bracing where two diagonals meet at the midpoint of a leg. All do the same job: no panel is left as an unbraced rectangle.

The bracing also has a second role, which is to stop the long corner legs from buckling. A long thin steel angle under compression will bow sideways and fail at a load far below the strength of the steel itself, exactly as a plastic ruler bends when you push its ends together. The bracing members connect to the leg every metre or two and hold it straight, so that each short section between connections is stiff enough not to buckle. More bracing therefore allows thinner legs, which is why pylons can be so slender for their height.

Stiffness is not the same as stability. A pylon is stiff because of its triangulation; it is stable because its four legs stand on a base several metres wide, giving a low centre of gravity relative to that base, and because each leg is anchored into a concrete foundation that resists both pushing down and pulling up. In a gale the windward legs are pulled upwards while the leeward legs are pushed down, and the foundations must hold both.

The questions for this section are with you now: why triangles are stiff, how cross-bracing works when the wind blows, and how bracing stops buckling.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Complex Structures

Investigating structures means more than describing them. The Grade 8 skill is to evaluate: to weigh up a structure's advantages and disadvantages against its purpose, so that a designer can decide what to copy and what to avoid. A fair evaluation looks at several aspects: does the structure do its job, how much material and money does it need, how safe is it, how easy is it to build and maintain, how long will it last, and what effect does it have on people and the environment.

Take the lattice pylon. Advantages: very strong and stiff for its weight; uses the least steel for the height; lets wind through; can be made in parts, transported by truck and bolted together on remote sites; proven over a century; easy to inspect and repaint. Disadvantages: thousands of bolts to make and check; many surfaces to rust and to paint; climbing it for maintenance is dangerous; it is widely considered ugly in a landscape; it needs a large footprint of land; birds collide with it.

Compare the monopole. Advantages: small footprint, so it fits beside roads and in towns; neater appearance; few joints to inspect; quicker to erect with a crane. Disadvantages: much heavier steel per metre of height because a tube must resist bending on its own without triangulation; expensive to transport in long sections; needs a heavy foundation; limited height. The guyed mast uses the least material of all but needs a large area of land for its anchor cables, which get in the way of farming and can be struck by vehicles.

A good evaluation ends with a judgement tied to the purpose. For a 400 kilovolt line across open Highveld farmland, the lattice tower is the right choice because material cost and strength dominate and land is available. For a line along a city road, a monopole may be right despite its cost because land is not available. There is no single best structure; there is a best structure for a stated purpose and set of constraints.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: listing only advantages, which is advertising, not evaluation. Two: evaluating without stating the purpose, so that "heavy" is called a disadvantage when, for a tower that must resist a cable pulling sideways, weight may be wanted. Three: thinking more bracing is always better; every member costs steel, bolts and maintenance, and the designer uses just enough. Four: confusing stiffness and stability; a stiff tower on a narrow base still topples.

The questions for this section are with you now: the aspects used to evaluate a structure, the advantages and disadvantages of the lattice pylon, and how to make a judgement tied to purpose.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the steel towers along the highway — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Tower of Triangles

Those tall steel towers along the highway are pylons. Their job is to hold power cables high and apart, for fifty years, through every storm.

Why are they open frames and not solid posts? Because most of a solid post would be doing nothing. The loads run down the four corner legs and through the bracing. Leave the rest out: less steel, less weight, and the wind blows straight through.

Wide at the bottom, narrow at the top, arms near the top for the cables. Straight-line towers are light; corner towers are heavy because the cables pull sideways. Near towns a single pole is used because there is no space.

Your PAT frame has the same job: carry a load, stay stiff, stand up, use little material.

Keep the highway towers in mind and try the questions for this part: why pylons are open frames.

## Subtopic: Why the X Shapes Matter

A rectangle made of sticks folds flat when you push its corner. A triangle cannot fold. That is triangulation, from Grade 7.

Look at a pylon: every panel has an X in it. Two diagonals crossing make four triangles. When the wind pushes, one diagonal is stretched and the other is squashed, and together they hold the panel square. Some pylons use single diagonals or zig-zags instead. Same idea: no rectangle left unbraced.

The bracing also stops the long legs from bowing. A long thin stick pushed from both ends bends in the middle. Bracing holds it every metre or two so each short piece stays straight. That is why the legs can be so thin.

Stiff is not the same as stable. Stiff comes from the triangles. Stable comes from the wide base and the concrete feet.

Hold the X in mind and try this part's questions: triangles for stiffness, wide base for stability.

## Subtopic: Weighing Up Big Structures

Investigating means weighing up, not just describing. Ask: does it do the job, what does it cost, is it safe, is it easy to build and look after, how long will it last, what does it do to people and the land?

Lattice pylon: strong and light, lets wind through, bolted together on site, proven. But thousands of bolts, lots of rust, ugly, needs a lot of land, birds hit it.

Single pole: neat, small footprint, quick to put up. But heavy steel, costly to transport, big foundation, not very tall.

Then judge for the purpose. Open farmland: lattice wins on cost and strength. City road: the pole wins because there is no land. No best structure, only best for the job.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Pylons are open lattice frames because the loads run through the legs and bracing and everything else is left out. Cross-bracing divides every panel into triangles, one diagonal in tension and one in compression, and also stops the legs buckling. Stability comes from the wide base and foundations. To evaluate a structure, list advantages and disadvantages across purpose, cost, safety, maintenance and impact, then judge against the stated purpose.
