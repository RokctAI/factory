# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises mechanical advantage and introduces the idea that complex machines are built from simple mechanisms. The anchor is a worker in Durban loading a 200-litre drum onto a bakkie: lifting it straight up is impossible for one person, but rolling it up a plank, or using a hand winch, makes the job possible. The governing idea is that a machine gives a mechanical advantage when the load it moves is greater than the effort applied, expressed as the ratio load divided by effort, and that every complex machine, from a bicycle to a drilling rig, is a combination of the six simple machines. Defining mechanical advantage, naming the simple machines and identifying them inside a complex machine are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Mechanical Advantage Means

Mechanical advantage is the number of times a machine multiplies your force. If you push with an effort of 100 newtons and the machine moves a load of 400 newtons, the mechanical advantage is 400 divided by 100, which is 4. Grade 7 introduced this idea with levers; Grade 8 uses the same ratio for every mechanism and begins to calculate it. A mechanical advantage greater than 1 means the machine makes you stronger. A mechanical advantage of exactly 1 means it changes only the direction or position of the force. A mechanical advantage less than 1 means you must push harder than the load, but in exchange the load moves faster or further.

The price of a mechanical advantage is always distance. The worker rolling the drum up a plank pushes with much less force than the drum's weight, but pushes it along three metres of plank to raise it one metre. The hand winch turns many times to lift the drum a little. No machine gives something for nothing: force is traded for distance, or distance for force. This trade is the heart of mechanical systems and control, and it is why a designer must always ask what the user needs more, strength or speed.

A well-designed machine matches its mechanical advantage to the job. A pair of nail clippers needs a large mechanical advantage over a tiny distance. A bicycle in top gear needs a mechanical advantage below 1 so the wheel turns faster than the pedals. A car jack needs a very large mechanical advantage because the load is enormous and the distance small. Grade 8 asks you to recognise these choices and later, in Term 3, to calculate them for gears.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of mechanical advantage as a ratio, what values above, equal to and below 1 mean, and the trade between force and distance.

## Subtopic: Six Simple Machines

Engineers traditionally list six simple machines. The lever: a rigid bar turning about a pivot, in three classes as revised last session. The wheel and axle: a large wheel fixed to a smaller axle so that turning one turns the other, as in a steering wheel or a doorknob. The pulley: a wheel with a grooved rim carrying a rope, which changes the direction of a pull and, in combinations, multiplies force. The inclined plane: a sloping surface such as a ramp, which lets a load be raised with a smaller force over a longer distance. The wedge: two inclined planes back to back, which converts a push into a sideways splitting force, as in an axe or a knife. The screw: an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder, which converts turning into a strong straight push, as in a vice or a bottle cap.

Each simple machine gives a mechanical advantage in its own way. Levers and the wheel and axle use the ratio of two distances from a centre. Pulleys use the number of rope sections supporting the load. Inclined planes, wedges and screws use the ratio of the long sloping distance to the short vertical rise. Grade 8 concentrates on the wedge, the wheel and axle, gears, cams and cranks in Term 1, and returns to levers and gears with calculations in Term 3.

Gears are not one of the classical six, but they are a development of the wheel and axle: two wheels with teeth that mesh so that turning one turns the other. Because they cannot slip, gears transfer force exactly, and because they can be different sizes, they change speed and force in a controlled ratio. Cams and cranks are also developments of the wheel: a cam is a wheel with an off-centre shape that pushes a follower up and down, and a crank is an arm on an axle that turns rotary motion into back-and-forth motion. Together these make up the Grade 8 mechanisms toolkit.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming the six simple machines with an example of each, and how gears, cams and cranks relate to the wheel and axle.

## Subtopic: Complex Machines Combine Simple Ones

A complex machine is a combination of simple machines working together, so that the output of one becomes the input of the next. Take a bicycle. The pedals and crank arms are a wheel and axle. The chain and sprockets are gears connected at a distance. The brake levers are levers. The handlebars turning the front fork are a wheel and axle. The quick-release on the saddle is a screw. Nobody invented the bicycle from nothing; its designers assembled known simple machines into a chain of mechanisms.

A hand drill or egg beater shows the same thing more compactly. The handle is a crank. The crank turns a large gear which meshes with a small gear on the drill shaft, so the shaft spins faster than the handle: a mechanical advantage below 1, chosen because drilling needs speed. A can opener combines a lever (the handles), a wheel and axle (the turning knob), a gear (the toothed wheel that grips the rim) and a wedge (the cutting wheel). A pair of scissors is two levers; a pair of pruning shears adds a spring and a wedge-shaped blade.

Analysing a complex machine means tracing the path of the force from input to output and naming the simple machine at each stage. In a wind-up toy car, the key is a wheel and axle, it winds a spring that stores energy, the spring turns a gear train that drives the axle, and the wheels turn. In a bakkie's hand winch, the handle is a crank, it turns a small gear meshing with a large one for a big mechanical advantage, and the large gear winds a cable on a drum, which is a wheel and axle. The Grade 8 PAT asks you to design a structure that houses mechanisms, and that design will combine at least two of these.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a machine with a high mechanical advantage is always better; a bicycle in a very low gear is strong but painfully slow on the flat. Two: believing machines create energy; they only trade force for distance, and friction always takes a share. Three: calling gears a kind of pulley; gears mesh with teeth and cannot slip, pulleys use a belt or rope and can. Four: treating a complex machine as one thing; it is always several simple machines, and each can be analysed on its own.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying the simple machines inside a bicycle, a hand drill and a can opener, and tracing the force path through a complex machine.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a drum, a plank and a bicycle — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Small Push, Big Result

A machine gives a mechanical advantage when the load it moves is bigger than your push. Divide the load by your push and you get the number. Push 100, lift 400, mechanical advantage 4.

More than 1: the machine makes you stronger. Exactly 1: it only changes the direction. Less than 1: you push harder but the load moves faster.

There is always a price. Roll the drum up a long plank and the push is small but the distance is long. Force for distance, every time.

Keep the drum and the plank in mind and try the questions for this part: what the number means and what you trade for it.

## Subtopic: The Six Helpers

There are six simple machines. Lever: a bar on a pivot. Wheel and axle: a big wheel on a thin rod, like a doorknob. Pulley: a grooved wheel with a rope. Inclined plane: a ramp. Wedge: two ramps back to back, like an axe. Screw: a ramp wrapped round a rod, like a bottle cap.

Gears are wheels with teeth that lock together so they cannot slip. A cam is a wheel with a bump that pushes something up and down. A crank is an arm on an axle that turns round-and-round into back-and-forth. These three are the Grade 8 favourites.

Hold the doorknob and the axe in mind and try this part's questions: the six machines and their examples.

## Subtopic: Machines Inside Machines

A complex machine is just simple machines joined up. On a bicycle, the pedals are a wheel and axle, the chain and sprockets are gears, the brake handles are levers, the saddle clamp is a screw.

A hand drill: crank handle turns a big gear, big gear turns a small gear, the drill spins fast. A can opener: lever handles, a turning knob, a toothed wheel, a cutting wedge.

To understand any machine, follow the push from where it goes in to where it comes out and name each simple machine on the way.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. More than 1 makes you stronger, less than 1 makes you faster, and distance is always the price. The six simple machines are the lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge and screw. Gears, cams and cranks grow out of the wheel. Complex machines are teams of simple ones.

Hold the bicycle and the can opener, and take the final questions of the lesson: spotting the simple machines inside complex ones.
