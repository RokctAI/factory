# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces spur gears, their counter-rotation and the idler gear. The anchor is a hand-cranked mealie grinder on a farm stall outside Bethlehem and the gear train inside a wind-up torch, both of which pass turning motion from one shaft to another through teeth. The governing idea is that when two spur gears mesh, their teeth push against each other so that the gears must rotate in opposite directions, and that inserting a third gear, the idler, between a driver and a driven gear reverses the direction a second time, so that driver and driven gear turn the same way at the same speed if they are the same size. Naming the driver, driven and idler gears, predicting direction of rotation and explaining the purpose of an idler are examinable.

## Subtopic: Spur Gears That Mesh

A spur gear is a wheel with straight teeth cut around its rim, parallel to the axle. Two spur gears mesh when their teeth interlock so that a tooth on one gear sits in the gap between two teeth on the other. Because the teeth are locked together, the gears cannot slip, and turning one gear forces the other to turn. The gear that receives the effort, from a hand crank, a motor or a pedal, is called the driver gear. The gear that it turns is called the driven gear. Together, two or more meshing gears form a gear train.

Gears exist to transfer rotary motion from one shaft to another. In the mealie grinder, the handle turns a large driver gear on one shaft, and that gear meshes with a small driven gear on the grinding shaft, so the grinding plates spin quickly while the handle is turned slowly. In a wind-up torch, the crank drives a train of three or four gears that make the tiny generator spin very fast. In a car, gears carry the engine's turning to the wheels. In a clock, gears carry the turning of the spring to the hands.

For gears to mesh properly, their teeth must be the same size and shape, otherwise they jam or skip. A gear with 40 teeth can mesh with a gear of 10 teeth as long as each tooth is the same size, which means the 40-tooth gear is four times the diameter of the 10-tooth gear. The number of teeth, not the diameter, is what we count when we study a gear train, and the two are always in proportion.

The parts to name on any drawing are: the teeth, the axle or shaft at the centre, the driver gear, the driven gear and the direction arrow on each. Grade 8 asks you to draw these with a template or compass and to label them correctly.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a spur gear is, the words driver and driven, and why meshing gears cannot slip.

## Subtopic: Counter-Rotation

The first rule of gears is counter-rotation: two gears that mesh turn in opposite directions. The reason is in the teeth. Where the two gears touch, a tooth on the driver pushes a tooth on the driven gear. At the point of contact both teeth move the same way, say downwards. But a point on the rim of the driver moving downwards on the right-hand side of its centre means the driver is turning clockwise, while the same downward movement on the left-hand side of the driven gear's centre means the driven gear is turning anticlockwise. The teeth move together; the gears turn opposite ways.

You can feel this with two coins. Lay two coins flat on a desk, edges touching, and roll one clockwise with a finger. The other coin rolls anticlockwise. Gears are coins that cannot slip. Draw two meshing gears, put a clockwise arrow on the driver, and the arrow on the driven gear must be anticlockwise. Add a third gear meshing with the second and it turns clockwise again. In any straight train of gears, the direction alternates: clockwise, anticlockwise, clockwise, and so on.

Counter-rotation is sometimes exactly what the designer wants. In a hand mixer, two beaters turn opposite ways so that they fold the batter towards each other. In the wheels of a toy that must roll backwards when a lever is pulled forwards, a single pair of gears reverses the direction. The paper feed rollers of a printer counter-rotate to grip and pull the sheet.

Sometimes counter-rotation is a nuisance. If the grinding plate of the mealie grinder must turn the same way as the handle, a single pair of gears will not do. Then the designer needs a third gear.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rule of counter-rotation, why the teeth cause it, and the alternating pattern in a train.

## Subtopic: The Idler Gear

An idler gear is a gear placed between the driver and the driven gear. It meshes with both. Because each mesh reverses the direction, two reversals cancel: the driver turns clockwise, the idler turns anticlockwise, and the driven gear turns clockwise again, the same way as the driver. The idler's only job is to change direction back; it does not change the speed ratio between the driver and the driven gear. If the driver and driven gears have the same number of teeth, they turn at the same speed and in the same direction, in perfect synchronisation, whatever size the idler is.

The idler is called an idler because it does no work of its own: it is not fixed to a shaft that drives anything, it simply spins freely on its axle and passes the motion along. It can be any size. A small idler saves space; a large idler lets the designer place the driver and driven shafts further apart without making either of them bigger. Idlers also let designers reach around an obstacle, by placing the idler to one side so the train bends around a motor or a bracket.

Because the idler is in contact with two gears and is often small, its teeth wear faster than the others. Designers therefore often make the idler of a harder material, such as steel between two brass or plastic gears, so that the whole train lasts as long as its hardest-working part. This detail returns in Term 3 when the mine headgear gear train is designed.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking an idler speeds up or slows down the driven gear; it only changes direction, the ratio is set by the driver and driven gears alone. Two: believing meshing gears turn the same way because they are connected; connected by teeth means opposite, connected by a chain or belt means the same. Three: calling the driven gear the driver because it is bigger; the driver is the one the effort is applied to, whatever its size. Four: forgetting that direction alternates along a train, so that an even number of gears in a line gives opposite directions at the two ends and an odd number gives the same direction.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an idler does and does not change, why it may be made of harder material, and the odd-even rule for direction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two coins and a mealie grinder — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Wheels With Teeth

A spur gear is a wheel with teeth. When two gears are pushed together so the teeth lock, turning one turns the other, and they cannot slip.

The gear you turn is the driver. The gear it turns is the driven gear. Two or more gears together are a gear train.

In a mealie grinder, the handle turns a big gear, the big gear turns a small gear, and the grinding plate spins fast.

The teeth must be the same size to lock together. We count teeth, not centimetres.

Keep the grinder in mind and try the questions for this part: driver, driven, and teeth that lock.

## Subtopic: Opposite Ways Round

Here is the first rule of gears: two gears that mesh turn opposite ways.

Try it with two coins on a desk, edges touching. Roll one clockwise and the other rolls anticlockwise. Gears are coins that cannot slip.

In a row of gears the direction flips every time: clockwise, anticlockwise, clockwise.

Sometimes opposite is useful. A hand mixer's beaters turn towards each other. Printer rollers turn opposite ways to grip the paper.

Hold the two coins in mind and try this part's questions: which way the second gear turns.

## Subtopic: The Gear in the Middle

What if you need the last gear to turn the same way as the first? Put a third gear in the middle. That is the idler.

Driver clockwise, idler anticlockwise, driven clockwise again. Two flips cancel and the ends match.

The idler does not change speed. Only the driver and driven gears decide speed. The idler can be any size and only fixes direction.

Because the idler rubs against two gears, it wears faster, so it is often made of harder metal.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Spur gears are toothed wheels that mesh and cannot slip. The driver is turned by the effort, the driven gear is turned by the driver. Meshing gears counter-rotate, and direction alternates along a train. An idler between them makes driver and driven turn the same way without changing the speed ratio, and is often made harder because it wears.

Hold the gear in the middle, and take the final questions of the lesson: what the idler does and does not do.
