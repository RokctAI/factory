# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the wedge and the wheel and axle as sources of mechanical advantage. The anchor is a Saturday morning in a Free State township: a man splitting firewood with an axe, his daughter slicing bread with a kitchen knife, and a shopping trolley being pushed up a ramp outside the spaza shop. The governing idea is that a wedge is an inclined plane driven into a material so that a forward push becomes a much larger sideways force, while a wheel and axle is a lever that turns, with the mechanical advantage set by the ratio of the wheel radius to the axle radius. Explaining how a wedge multiplies force, how a thinner wedge gives a greater advantage, and how the wheel-to-axle ratio works are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Wedge as a Moving Inclined Plane

An inclined plane is a slope. Pushing a load up a slope needs less force than lifting it straight up, because the force is spread over the longer sloping distance. A wedge is the same slope turned into a tool and driven into something. Instead of the load moving up the slope, the slope moves into the load, and the forward push on the wedge becomes a sideways force that splits, lifts or holds.

An axe is a wedge. The head is thick at the back and thin at the edge, so it is two inclined planes meeting at the blade. When the axe is swung down into a log, the edge enters the wood and the widening sides push the two halves apart. The downward force of the swing is converted into a much greater sideways force on the fibres, and the log splits. The longer and thinner the wedge, the greater the mechanical advantage, because the same forward movement produces a smaller sideways movement with a larger force.

A knife blade works the same way on a smaller scale. A sharp blade is a very thin wedge; it concentrates the push on a tiny edge and parts the bread with little effort. A blunt knife is a thicker wedge and needs a harder push for the same cut. Chisels, nails, needles, ploughs and the bow of a ship are all wedges that part material as they move forward.

A door wedge uses the same geometry in reverse. Pushed under a door, its slope lifts the door slightly and jams it against the floor. Friction on the two faces then holds the wedge in place. The small push needed to slide it in becomes a large holding force, which is why a small block of rubber can stop a heavy door swinging in the wind.

The questions for this section are with you now: the wedge as a moving inclined plane, how an axe converts a downward swing into a sideways split, and why thin wedges give more advantage.

## Subtopic: The Wheel and Axle

A wheel and axle is a large wheel fixed rigidly to a smaller cylinder, the axle, so that they turn together. Because they turn together, a point on the rim of the wheel travels a much longer path in one turn than a point on the surface of the axle. That difference is where the mechanical advantage comes from. Turning the wheel with a small force moves the axle with a larger force, exactly as a long effort arm on a lever moves a short load arm. The mechanical advantage is the radius of the wheel divided by the radius of the axle.

A tap handle shows this clearly. The handle is the wheel, the spindle it turns is the axle. A gentle twist of the wide handle produces a strong turning force on the narrow spindle, enough to squash the washer and stop the water. A screwdriver is a wheel and axle: the fat handle is the wheel, the thin shaft is the axle, and a wide handle drives a screw more easily than a thin one. A steering wheel and a doorknob work the same way.

The machine can also be used the other way round. If the effort is applied to the axle, the rim of the wheel moves faster and further than the axle, with a mechanical advantage less than 1. This is what a bicycle does: the chain turns the rear axle through the sprocket, and the large wheel's rim moves fast over the road. Speed, not force, is the goal. A shopping trolley's wheels are wheels and axles that roll, replacing the sliding friction of dragging with the much smaller rolling friction of a wheel turning on a greased axle.

Without the wheel and axle there would be no vehicles, no mills, no winches and no gears, because gears are wheels and axles with teeth. Grade 7 met the pulley as a wheel and axle; Grade 8 adds gears, cams and cranks to the same family.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a wheel and axle multiplies force, the ratio that gives its mechanical advantage, and how the bicycle uses it for speed instead.

## Subtopic: Where Both Are Used

Designers choose a wedge when they need to split, cut, lift slightly or hold. Splitting: axes, log splitters and the wedge-shaped bits of a jackhammer. Cutting: knives, chisels, scissors blades and the cutters on garden shears. Lifting: the wedge under a cupboard to level it, or the inclined bar a mechanic slides under a car to lift it a little. Holding: door wedges, the tapered pegs that hold wooden furniture joints, and the wedge that locks an axe head to its handle.

Designers choose a wheel and axle when they need to turn something with more force, or turn something faster, or move a load with less friction. More force: taps, steering wheels, screwdrivers, door handles and the winding handle of a hand winch. More speed: bicycle wheels, the rollers of a conveyor belt and the spinning drum of a washing machine. Less friction: every trolley, wheelbarrow, cart and car wheel, which roll instead of dragging.

In the Grade 8 PAT, a structure that houses mechanisms, both machines appear. A wedge-shaped foot can hold the structure steady on an uneven desk. A wheel and axle, turned by a crank, can drive the moving part. Choosing which simple machine suits each job, and saying why, is part of the design justification that earns marks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a wedge pushes in the direction you hit it; the useful force is sideways, at right angles to the swing. Two: believing a blunt knife is safer because it is less sharp; a blunt knife needs more force and slips more easily. Three: thinking the axle does the hard work; the axle turns slowly with a large force, while the wheel is turned by a small force through a long distance. Four: calling any round thing a wheel and axle; it only counts if the wheel and axle are fixed together so that they turn as one.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching wedges and wheels and axles to jobs, and how each might be used in the PAT structure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an axe, a bread knife and a tap — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Ramp That Moves

A ramp lets you push a heavy thing up slowly with a small force. A wedge is a ramp that moves: instead of the load going up the ramp, the ramp goes into the load.

An axe is a wedge. Swing it down into a log and the thin edge goes in, then the thick sides push the two halves apart. Your downward swing becomes a big sideways push. Thin edge, big split.

A bread knife is a tiny wedge. Sharp and thin cuts easily; thick and blunt needs a hard push.

A door wedge slides under a door and jams it. A small push in becomes a strong hold.

Keep the axe in mind and try the questions for this part: a wedge is a moving ramp that turns a push into a sideways force.

## Subtopic: Big Wheel, Thin Rod

A wheel and axle is a big wheel stuck to a thin rod so that they turn together. Turn the big wheel gently and the thin rod turns with a strong force. That is a tap, a steering wheel, a doorknob, a screwdriver handle.

The rule: the bigger the wheel compared to the rod, the more it helps. Wheel radius divided by axle radius.

Turn it the other way round and you get speed instead. A bicycle chain turns the small axle, and the big wheel rim rushes over the road.

Wheels also stop things dragging. A trolley rolls instead of scraping.

Hold the tap and the trolley in mind and try this part's questions: big wheel, thin rod, and which way gives force or speed.

## Subtopic: Spotting Them Around You

Need to split, cut or hold? Use a wedge: axe, chisel, knife, door wedge, the peg in a chair joint.

Need to turn with more force? Use a wheel and axle: tap, handle, screwdriver, winch. Need speed? Drive the axle and let the wheel rim fly: bicycle wheel. Need less friction? Put it on wheels: trolley, wheelbarrow.

In your PAT structure, a wedge foot can keep it steady and a wheel and axle with a crank can make the moving part move.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A wedge is an inclined plane driven into a material; it turns a push into a bigger sideways force, and thinner means more advantage. A wheel and axle is a big wheel fixed to a thin axle; turning the wheel gives force at the axle, turning the axle gives speed at the wheel, and the mechanical advantage is wheel radius over axle radius.

Hold the knife and the doorknob, and take the final questions of the lesson: choosing the right machine for the job.
