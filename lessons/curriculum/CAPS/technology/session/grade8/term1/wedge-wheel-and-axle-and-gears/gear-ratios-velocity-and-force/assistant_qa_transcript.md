### subtopic_1 — Different Sizes, Different Speeds
**Assistant:** Why does a bigger gear turn more slowly? I keep picturing a bigger wheel covering more ground and going faster.

**Tutor:** Your picture is right for a wheel rolling on a road, and that is exactly why it misleads you here, because meshing gears are a different situation. A bicycle wheel's rim moves at road speed whatever its size, so a bigger wheel does cover more ground per turn and the bicycle goes faster for the same pedalling. But two meshing gears are locked together at their rims, so their rims move at the same speed, tooth for tooth; the question is not how far the rim moves but how many turns each centre must make for that rim movement. A big gear has a long rim, so one rim-length of movement is only a fraction of a turn for it, while the same rim-length is a whole turn or more for a small gear. Picture a small cog running around the inside of a bicycle chain: the cog spins many times while the chain moves once around. Picture a large chainring doing the same: it spins less than once. Rim speed equal, turning speed different, and the bigger gear turns more slowly. The force side follows from energy. The teeth push with the same force on each gear, because it is one push shared by two teeth, but that force acts on a longer arm on the big gear, so the big gear gets more turning force, just as a push on the end of a long spanner turns a nut harder than the same push on a short one. Slower and stronger go together because they come from the same long radius.

### subtopic_2 — Velocity Ratio and Force Ratio
**Assistant:** What does the 1 in a ratio like 1:3 stand for, and does it matter which number I write first?

**Tutor:** It matters a great deal, which is why the convention must be fixed before you start. In this course the velocity ratio is written as turns of the driven gear for one turn of the driver, and a quick way to get it is to divide the number of teeth on the driver by the number of teeth on the driven gear. A 10-tooth driver and a 30-tooth driven gear give 10 over 30, which is one third, written 1:3, meaning that for every one turn of the driver the driven gear makes a third of a turn, or equivalently the driver makes three turns while the driven gear makes one. The 1 therefore stands for one turn of the driven gear in the second reading, or one turn of the driver in the first, and the number alongside it tells you how many turns the other gear makes in the same time. Textbooks and engineers sometimes write the ratio the other way, as driven teeth over driver teeth, and call it the gear ratio rather than the velocity ratio; a 30-tooth gear driven by a 10-tooth gear then has a gear ratio of 3:1. Both are correct as long as the writer says what is meant, so in any answer state in words which gear turns faster and by how much: "the driven gear turns one third as fast as the driver". Words cannot be read backwards. The force ratio, which is the mechanical advantage, is the inverse of the velocity ratio, so the 1:3 velocity ratio is a 3:1 force ratio, and the test of a sensible answer is that a slower output always comes with a larger force.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing a Gear Ratio
**Assistant:** If my PAT mechanism needs to lift a heavy load slowly, how big a ratio should I aim for, and is there a downside to making it huge?

**Tutor:** Start from what the user of your model will do and what the load is. A hand crank turned comfortably by a learner makes perhaps one or two turns a second, and the load on a model lift might be a small box or a toy figure. If a direct drive with equal gears would make the lift shoot up too fast or need too hard a push, a ratio of 1:3 or 1:4 with a small driver and a large driven gear will slow the lift to a smooth climb and cut the effort on the crank to a third or a quarter. For a classroom model that is usually plenty, and it can be made with two gears from a construction kit, for example a 12-tooth driver meshing with a 36- or 48-tooth driven gear. Going much larger has real costs. First, speed: a 1:12 ratio means twelve turns of the crank for one turn of the output, so the lift would take so long that the audience at your presentation loses interest. Second, size: a single pair with a 1:12 ratio needs a driven gear twelve times the diameter of the driver, which will not fit inside a structure of the dimensions the brief allows, so you would need a compound train of two or three pairs, more axles, more bearings and more friction. Third, friction itself: every extra mesh loses some effort as heat and wear, so very long trains can waste a large part of the advantage they are supposed to give. Good design is choosing the smallest ratio that makes the job comfortable, then stating the tooth counts and the reason in your design justification; that reasoning is what earns the marks, not the size of the number.
