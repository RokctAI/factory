### subtopic_1 — Spur Gears That Mesh
**Assistant:** Why do the teeth make gears turn opposite ways? A chain on a bicycle connects two sprockets and they turn the same way.

**Tutor:** The difference is where the two wheels touch and which way the contact point moves relative to each centre. With meshing gears the rims touch directly, at a single point between the two centres. At that point the teeth move together, say downwards. Downward movement to the right of the driver's centre is clockwise rotation for the driver; the very same downward movement is to the left of the driven gear's centre, which is anticlockwise rotation for the driven gear. One shared movement, two opposite rotations, purely because the contact point lies between the centres. A chain or belt changes the geometry. The chain leaves the top of the front sprocket and arrives at the top of the rear sprocket, so the top of both wheels moves forward together and the bottom of both moves backward together. Now the shared movement is on the same side of both centres, so both sprockets turn the same way. You can prove this to yourself by crossing the chain into a figure of eight: the top of one wheel is then connected to the bottom of the other, and they turn in opposite directions, which is how some old factory belt drives were deliberately arranged. So the rule is not really about teeth or chains; it is about whether the connection is made between the centres, as with gears, or around the outside of both, as with an uncrossed chain. Designers use both to get the direction they need, and an idler gear is the toothed way of achieving what an uncrossed chain does naturally.

### subtopic_2 — Counter-Rotation
**Assistant:** If the idler does not change the speed, why not just make the driver and driven gears the same size and leave the idler out?

**Tutor:** Because without the idler the two gears would turn in opposite directions, and the whole point of adding it is to make them turn the same way; the speed ratio is a separate matter decided by the sizes of the driver and the driven gear. Think about what the designer is juggling. The size of the driver and the driven gear sets the speed and force ratio. The number of meshes between them sets the direction: an odd number of gears in the line gives the same direction at both ends, an even number gives opposite directions. If the design needs the output to turn the same way as the input at the same speed, as in the mealie grinder whose plate must follow the handle, two equal gears will not do because they counter-rotate, so a third gear must be inserted and that is the idler. The idler also solves a second, very practical problem, which is distance. Two meshing gears must have their shafts exactly one gear-radius-plus-one-gear-radius apart. If the output shaft needs to be further away than that, perhaps because a motor or a bearing is in the way, the designer either makes the gears enormous, which is heavy and expensive, or drops in an idler to bridge the gap. Since the idler can be any size without affecting the ratio, it can be chosen to fit the space exactly. In Term 3 the mine headgear design uses this freedom, and specifies that the idler should be of harder material because it is the gear that meshes on two sides and wears first.

### subtopic_3 — The Idler Gear
**Assistant:** How can I quickly work out the direction of the last gear in a long train without drawing every arrow?

**Tutor:** Count the gears in the line and use the odd-even rule. Each mesh reverses the direction, so in a straight train of gears the direction alternates: the first gear clockwise, the second anticlockwise, the third clockwise, and so on. If the train has an odd number of gears, the last gear turns the same way as the first; if it has an even number, the last gear turns the opposite way. A train of three, which is driver, idler and driven, has the last gear matching the first. A train of four has it opposite. This only counts gears that are each meshed to the next in a single line. Two complications are worth knowing. First, if two gears are fixed on the same shaft, a compound gear, they turn together and count as one gear for direction purposes, even though they change the speed ratio. Second, if the train branches, so that one gear meshes with two others, follow each branch separately from the gear where they split. In the exam a quick method is to put a small clockwise arrow on the driver, then write C, A, C, A along the train, one letter per gear, and read off the letter on the driven gear. It takes a few seconds and avoids the common slip of drawing a careful diagram and then reversing one arrow by mistake. The same letters also help you check a drawing: if two meshing gears in your sketch carry the same letter, something is wrong.
