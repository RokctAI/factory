### subtopic_1 — The Wedge as a Moving Inclined Plane
**Assistant:** Why does a thinner wedge give more mechanical advantage? I would have thought a thicker one would push the wood apart more.

**Tutor:** A thicker wedge does push the wood apart further, but that is exactly why it needs more force; the two things are the same trade of force for distance that runs through every machine. Think of the wedge as a ramp. When you drive it forward by one centimetre, the two faces move apart sideways by a distance that depends on the angle: a fat wedge spreads the wood by a lot for each centimetre forward, a thin wedge spreads it by very little. The work you put in is your push times the forward distance; the work that comes out is the splitting force times the sideways distance. Because the sideways distance is small for a thin wedge, the splitting force must be large to make the two amounts of work equal. In numbers, the mechanical advantage of a wedge is roughly its length divided by its thickness at the back, so a wedge ten centimetres long and two centimetres thick multiplies the force about five times, while one ten centimetres long and one centimetre thick multiplies it about ten times. There is a practical limit, which is why axes are not razor thin. A very thin blade bends, chips and gets stuck in the wood, and once the first split has opened, a fatter section is needed to lever the halves fully apart. Real axe heads are therefore thin at the edge to start the cut with great force and thicker behind to finish the split, two wedges in one tool, each matched to its part of the job.

### subtopic_2 — The Wheel and Axle
**Assistant:** If the wheel and axle is really a lever, where are the pivot, load and effort?

**Tutor:** Freeze the wheel at one instant and draw a line from the top of the rim, through the centre, to the bottom of the axle; that line is a lever. The pivot is the centre, the point everything turns about. The effort is your hand on the rim of the wheel, a long way from the centre, so the effort arm is the radius of the wheel. The load is whatever resists at the surface of the axle, such as the rope being wound on a winch drum or the washer being squashed in a tap, a short distance from the centre, so the load arm is the radius of the axle. Pivot in the middle, effort on one side and load on the other, which makes it a first-class lever, and like every lever its mechanical advantage is the effort arm divided by the load arm, here the wheel radius divided by the axle radius. The only difference from an ordinary lever is that the bar keeps turning instead of stopping, so you get a lever that works continuously through a full circle rather than one that has to be lifted and reset. That is why a winch can lift a load as high as its rope is long while a crowbar can only lift a slab a few centimetres before you must move the brick. Gears, which come next, are two of these rotating levers with their rims locked together by teeth, and in Term 3 you will calculate their mechanical advantage with the same ratio of radii, or equivalently of tooth counts.

### subtopic_3 — Where Both Are Used
**Assistant:** Why do shopping trolleys and wheelbarrows count as wheels and axles when nobody turns the wheel by hand?

**Tutor:** Because the wheel and axle does two separate jobs, and only one of them involves turning with your hand. The first job is the lever job we have discussed, multiplying force or speed by the ratio of radii, and that is what taps, screwdrivers and bicycle drives do. The second job is reducing friction, and that is what a trolley wheel does. When you drag a box across a floor, every point of the box's underside scrapes along the whole distance you move it, so sliding friction acts along the full path and wastes a great deal of your effort. Put the box on wheels and the only sliding that happens is the tiny movement of the axle inside its bearing, which has a very small radius and is usually greased. The rim rolls over the floor without scraping at all. Because the axle is thin compared with the wheel, the sliding distance at the bearing is small compared with the distance the trolley travels, so the frictional work is a small fraction of what dragging would cost. That is the same geometry as the lever job, the big wheel radius against the small axle radius, used to shrink friction rather than to multiply force. A loaded trolley that one person pushes easily on wheels would take several people to drag, and a wheelbarrow combines this rolling wheel with a second-class lever in its handles, which is why it has been the builder's favourite machine for centuries.
