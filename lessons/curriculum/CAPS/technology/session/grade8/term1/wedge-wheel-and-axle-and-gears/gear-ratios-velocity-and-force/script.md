# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies how gears of different sizes change the velocity ratio and, inversely, the force ratio. The anchor is a learner cycling to school in Polokwane, dropping into a low gear for the hill by the taxi rank and clicking up to a high gear on the flat road past the stadium. The governing idea is that when a small driver turns a large driven gear, the driven gear turns more slowly but with more force, and when a large driver turns a small driven gear, the driven gear turns faster but with less force; the velocity ratio is found from the tooth counts and the force ratio is its inverse. Reading a gear ratio from tooth counts, predicting which gear turns faster, and explaining the inverse relationship between speed and force are examinable.

## Subtopic: Different Sizes, Different Speeds

When two meshing gears have the same number of teeth, they turn at the same speed in opposite directions. When they have different numbers of teeth, their speeds differ, and the reason is simple: the teeth pass the contact point one at a time, so in the time the driver moves 10 teeth past the contact point, the driven gear must also move 10 teeth past it. If the driver has 10 teeth and the driven gear has 30, one full turn of the driver moves 10 teeth, which is only one third of the driven gear's 30. The driven gear turns a third of a revolution for every full turn of the driver: three times slower.

Turn it round. If the driver has 30 teeth and the driven gear has 10, one turn of the driver passes 30 teeth, which turns the 10-tooth driven gear three full times. The driven gear turns three times faster. The rule: a small driver turning a large driven gear slows the output down; a large driver turning a small driven gear speeds it up. The amount is set by the ratio of the tooth counts.

The bicycle shows both at once. The front chainring is the driver, turned by the pedals; the rear sprocket is the driven gear. In a low gear the chain sits on a small chainring and a large sprocket, so the wheel turns slowly for each pedal stroke and the hill feels easier. In a high gear the chain sits on a large chainring and a small sprocket, so the wheel turns many times for each pedal stroke and the bicycle flies along the flat. The gears on a bicycle are connected by a chain rather than meshing directly, so they turn the same way, but the speed rule is identical.

Because the diameter of a gear is proportional to its number of teeth, you can use diameters instead of tooth counts when a drawing gives no teeth. A gear of diameter 60 mm meshing with a gear of diameter 20 mm behaves exactly like a 30-tooth gear meshing with a 10-tooth gear.

The questions for this section are with you now: why tooth counts control speed, which gear turns faster in a pair, and how a bicycle uses both cases.

## Subtopic: Velocity Ratio and Force Ratio

The velocity ratio of a gear pair tells you how many times the driven gear turns for one turn of the driver. For a 10-tooth driver and a 30-tooth driven gear, the driven gear turns one third of a turn, so the velocity ratio is 1 to 3, often written 1:3, or as a fraction one third. For a 30-tooth driver and a 10-tooth driven gear, the velocity ratio is 3 to 1: the output turns three times for every input turn. The convention in Grade 8 is to read it as the number of driver teeth divided by the number of driven teeth, which gives the output speed as a multiple of the input speed.

The force ratio is the inverse. Gears cannot create energy, so whatever is gained in speed is lost in force, and whatever is gained in force is lost in speed. The 10-tooth driver turning the 30-tooth driven gear makes the output three times slower, and in exchange the output turns with three times the turning force. That is a mechanical advantage of 3. The 30-tooth driver turning the 10-tooth driven gear makes the output three times faster but with only a third of the turning force: a mechanical advantage of one third, which is less than 1.

Put the two together and the pattern is: velocity ratio up, force ratio down, and the two ratios multiply to 1. A gear pair with a velocity ratio of 1:4 has a force ratio of 4:1. In Term 3 this becomes a calculation; for now the task is to reason with it. If the driven gear has more teeth than the driver, the output is slower and stronger. If the driven gear has fewer teeth than the driver, the output is faster and weaker.

The same trade appears in every machine studied so far. A long lever arm, a long ramp and a large wheel on a small axle all buy force with distance. A gear train buys force with the number of turns. Nothing is free.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to state a velocity ratio from tooth counts, why the force ratio is its inverse, and which pair gives a mechanical advantage above 1.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Gear Ratio

A designer chooses a gear ratio by asking what the output must do. A hand drill must spin fast, so a large driver on the crank turns a small driven gear on the chuck, a velocity ratio above 1. A hand winch lifting a bakkie's spare wheel must pull hard, so a small driver on the handle turns a large driven gear on the cable drum, a velocity ratio below 1 and a force ratio well above 1. A clock needs the minute hand to turn exactly twelve times for every turn of the hour hand, so its gears are chosen for that exact ratio with no concern for force.

In the Grade 8 PAT, a structure housing a mechanism, the mechanism often includes a crank driving a gear pair. If the moving part must move slowly and smoothly, as in a model lift or a drawbridge, the designer uses a small driver and a large driven gear. If it must spin fast, as in a model fan or a windmill display, the designer uses a large driver and a small driven gear. Stating this choice in the design justification, with the tooth counts and the reason, is exactly what the marking rubric looks for.

Very large ratios are reached by compound gear trains, in which a large driven gear and a small gear share one shaft and the small gear drives the next large gear. Each stage multiplies the ratio, so three stages of 1:3 give 1:27. The gear train inside a wind-up torch or a toy car is compound. Grade 8 only requires simple trains, but recognising a compound train helps you read real machines.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a bigger driven gear makes the output faster because it is bigger; it makes the output slower, because it has more teeth to pass. Two: believing you can increase both speed and force with the right gears; one always falls as the other rises. Three: reading a ratio the wrong way round, so check: driven gear with more teeth means slower output. Four: confusing an idler with a ratio change; an idler changes direction only, however big it is.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing a ratio for speed or force, applying it to the PAT mechanism, and recognising a compound train.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a bicycle on a hill — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Big Gear, Small Gear

Two gears with the same number of teeth turn at the same speed. Make them different sizes and the speeds change.

Think of teeth as steps. In one turn, a 10-tooth gear takes 10 steps. Those 10 steps only move a 30-tooth gear a third of the way round. Small gear turning a big gear: the big one goes slower.

Swap them. One turn of the 30-tooth gear is 30 steps, which spins the 10-tooth gear round three times. Big gear turning a small gear: the small one goes faster.

On a bicycle, low gear is small chainring, big sprocket, slow wheel, easy hill. High gear is big chainring, small sprocket, fast wheel, flat road.

Keep the bicycle in mind and try the questions for this part: which gear turns faster.

## Subtopic: Faster or Stronger, Never Both

The velocity ratio says how many turns the driven gear makes for one turn of the driver. 10 teeth driving 30 teeth: a third of a turn, 1:3. 30 teeth driving 10 teeth: three turns, 3:1.

Now the catch. Gears do not give anything for free. Slower output means stronger output. Faster output means weaker output.

10 driving 30: three times slower, three times stronger. That is a mechanical advantage of 3. 30 driving 10: three times faster, a third of the force. Mechanical advantage of a third.

Faster or stronger, never both.

Hold the trade in mind and try this part's questions: speed up means force down.

## Subtopic: Picking the Right Pair

To pick a gear pair, ask what the job needs. Fast spinning, like a drill? Big driver, small driven. Hard pulling, like a winch lifting a wheel? Small driver, big driven.

For your PAT, a slow smooth lift wants a small driver and a big driven gear. A fast fan wants the opposite. Write down which you chose and why.

For huge ratios, engineers stack pairs: a big gear and a small gear share a shaft, and the small one drives the next big one. Three stages of 1:3 give 1:27.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Teeth pass one at a time, so tooth counts set the speed. Small driver, big driven: slower and stronger. Big driver, small driven: faster and weaker. Velocity ratio is driver teeth over driven teeth, and the force ratio is the inverse. Choose the pair to suit the job and say why.

Hold the drill and the winch, and take the final questions of the lesson: picking a pair for speed or for force.
