# Part 1 — Expert

This session consolidates the Term 1 work on mechanical systems, structures and design skills with line conventions. The anchor is a single object that contains the whole term: the finished PAT model standing on the desk, a triangulated frame with a crank, a gear pair and a pulley inside it, beside its working drawing, isometric drawing and artist's impression. The governing idea is that the term's content falls into three strands, mechanisms that give mechanical advantage and convert motion, structures that carry forces without failing, and the design process with its drawing conventions, and that the test will ask for definitions, examples, labelled sketches and short explanations from each strand. Recalling the key facts of each strand and demonstrating the drawing conventions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Mechanisms of the Term

Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. Above 1 the machine multiplies force; below 1 it multiplies speed or distance; the price of force is always distance. Complex machines are combinations of the simple machines: lever, wheel and axle, pulley, inclined plane, wedge and screw.

The wedge is an inclined plane driven into a material, turning a push into a larger sideways force; thinner wedges give more advantage; axes, knives and door wedges are examples. The wheel and axle is a large wheel fixed to a small axle; turning the wheel gives force at the axle with mechanical advantage equal to wheel radius over axle radius, and driving the axle gives speed at the rim; taps, screwdrivers, bicycles and trolleys are examples.

Gears are toothed wheels that mesh and cannot slip. Two meshing gears counter-rotate; an idler between driver and driven gear restores the same direction without changing the ratio, and is often of harder material because it wears. Different sizes change the speed: a small driver turning a large driven gear gives a slower, stronger output; a large driver turning a small driven gear gives a faster, weaker output. The velocity ratio is driver teeth over driven teeth, and the force ratio is its inverse: 10 teeth driving 30 gives a velocity ratio of 1:3 and a mechanical advantage of 3.

A cam converts rotary motion into reciprocating motion. The follower rests on the cam and rises and falls once per turn. An eccentric wheel, a disc on an off-centre shaft, gives smooth even motion with a stroke of twice the offset; a snail cam gives a slow rise and a sudden drop and turns one way only. A crank is an arm on an axle with a handle; it is a second-class lever bent into a circle, with the pivot at the axle centre, the effort at the handle and the load at the axle surface, so a longer arm gives more advantage. With a connecting rod and slider it converts rotary to reciprocating motion or back, with a stroke of twice the crank arm.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of mechanical advantage, the six simple machines, gear direction and ratio rules, and the motions of cams and cranks.

## Subtopic: Structures of the Term

A pylon is an open lattice frame because the loads run through its corner legs and bracing, so the rest of a solid post would be wasted; it is stiff because every panel is triangulated by cross-bracing, with one diagonal in tension and one in compression under wind, and stable because of its wide base and anchored foundations. Bracing also stops the long legs buckling. Evaluating any structure means listing advantages and disadvantages for its purpose and judging accordingly.

Members carry four forces. Tension pulls along the member and stretches it; compression pushes along it and shortens or buckles it; shear slices across it, as in bolts and scissors; torsion twists it, as in shafts and off-centre loads. In a roof truss, rafters and struts are in compression, the tie beam, ties and posts in tension; the king post truss has one central post for house spans, the queen post truss two posts and a straining beam for hall spans. Each force has an arrow symbol: out, in, opposite across, curved opposite.

Structures that span do so differently. A beam bends, compressed on top and stretched below, is stiffer when deeper, and becomes the I-beam when its middle is removed; lintels and beam and column bridges are beams. A suspension bridge hangs its deck from draped cables anchored on shore; a cable-stayed bridge runs stays straight from tower to deck. An arch spans in pure compression and needs heavy abutments. A cantilever is fixed at one end and bends most at its root.

Structures fail in three ways. Fracture, snapping or tearing, is a lack of strength, cured by more or better material and larger joints. Bending and buckling are a lack of stiffness, cured by depth, tubes, triangulation and bracing. Toppling is a lack of stability, cured by a wide base, a low centre of gravity and anchoring. The failure names the missing property, and the wrong cure fixes nothing.

The questions for this section are with you now: why pylons are lattices, the four forces and the truss members, the spanning types and the three failure modes.

## Subtopic: Design Skills and Drawing Conventions

The design process is IDMEC: investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate. The design brief is one or two sentences turning the scenario into a problem, naming the user, the need, the kind of product and the setting, without stating the solution. Specifications are testable requirements the product must meet; constraints are limits of size, materials, time, cost and tools. Several different annotated idea sketches are compared in a table against the specifications and constraints to choose the best, with reasons recorded. Making follows a cutting list and a sequence of manufacture with safe working practices. Evaluation tests each specification and records strengths, weaknesses with causes and improvements. Communication presents the whole story with drawings and model.

Drawing conventions are examinable in their own right. Line types: dark continuous outlines for visible edges; feint construction lines, left on; dashed hidden detail; thin chain centre lines. Scale is written drawing to real: 1:2 is half size, 2:1 double; divide to shrink, multiply to enlarge, and always write real sizes. Dimensions are in millimetres with no unit, placed outside the view with projection lines, a dimension line with arrowheads and the figure above, each size once, overall sizes always given, holes located from centre lines with diameters marked.

Three kinds of drawing serve three purposes. The single-view working drawing shows one face flat, to scale, with full dimensions, for the maker. The isometric drawing shows the object in 3D with a vertical axis and two axes at 30 degrees, true along the axes, with the main dimensions, to explain how the whole thing fits together. The artist's impression uses colour, shading from one light direction and texture to show what the finished object looks like, with no dimensions, for the client and the audience.

The test will typically ask you to: define mechanical advantage and calculate it from a simple ratio; name the simple machines in a complex machine; predict gear direction and compare speeds from tooth counts; sketch and label a cam or a crank; label the forces on the members of a truss or frame; name a bridge type and say how it carries load; name a failure mode and its cure; write a brief or sort specifications from constraints; and draw or correct a working drawing with the right lines, scale and dimensions. Practise each once with a pencil, not only by reading.

The error museum, four exhibits, chosen because they recur in tests. One: reading a gear ratio the wrong way; the gear with more teeth turns more slowly. Two: labelling a vertical truss post as compression; the king post is in tension. Three: dimensioning paper sizes on a scaled drawing; write real millimetres. Four: calling a stiff tower stable; stiffness is triangulation, stability is the base.

The questions for this section are with you now: the IDMEC steps and design documents, the line, scale and dimension rules, the three drawing types and the shape of the test.

## Subtopic: Gears, Cams and Cranks in a Minute

Look at the crank on your model and run the mechanisms in your head.

Mechanical advantage: load divided by push. More than 1, stronger; less than 1, faster; distance is always the price.

Wedge: a moving ramp; thin edge, big split. Wheel and axle: big wheel, thin rod; turn the wheel for force, drive the rod for speed.

Gears: meshing gears turn opposite ways; an idler in the middle puts them back in step without changing speed. Small driving big: slower and stronger. Big driving small: faster and weaker. 10 teeth driving 30: one third the speed, three times the force.

Cam: round and round becomes up and down. Off-centre disc: smooth. Snail: slow up, sudden drop, one way only. Crank: a second-class lever bent into a circle; longer arm, easier turn; add a rod and slider and it makes back-and-forth.

Keep the crank in your hand and try the questions for this part: gears, cams and cranks in a minute.

## Subtopic: Triangles, Trusses and Toppling

Now look at the frame.

Pylons are open frames because the legs do the work; X-braces make triangles so panels cannot rack; a wide base stops tipping.

Four forces: pull, push, slice, twist. Tension, compression, shear, torsion. In a truss, rafters and struts are pushed, the tie beam and the posts are pulled. King post for a house, queen post for a hall.

Spanning: beams bend and like to be deep; I-beams throw away the middle. Suspension bridges hang from draped cables; cable-stayed from straight ones. Arches are squashed all round. Cantilevers stick out from one end.

Three failures: snapped means not strong enough; bent or bowed means not stiff enough; fell over means not stable enough. Each has its own fix.

Hold the X-brace in mind and try this part's questions: triangles, trusses and toppling.

## Subtopic: The Drawings You Must Be Able to Do

Now look at the three drawings beside the model.

IDMEC ran the whole project: find out, plan, build, test, present. The brief said the problem in one sentence. Musts were tested; limits were kept. Three different sketches, a tick table, a winner with reasons.

Lines: dark for seen, feint for guides, dashed for hidden, dash-dot for centres. Scale: paper to real; 1:2 is half; write real sizes. Dimensions: millimetres, outside, arrowheads, number on top, once each, overall sizes always.

Three drawings: the flat working drawing for the maker, the isometric to show how it fits together, the coloured impression to show how it looks.

The test will ask you to calculate a ratio, name machines inside a machine, draw gear arrows, label a truss, name a bridge, name a failure and its cure, write a brief, and fix a working drawing. Practise each with a pencil.

Pocket summary of the term. Machines trade distance for force; gears, cams and cranks change speed and motion. Structures carry four forces, are stiffened by triangles, span by bending, pulling or pushing, and fail by snapping, bowing or tipping. Design runs through IDMEC and speaks the language of lines, scale and dimensions.

Hold the model and the three drawings, and take the final questions of the lesson: the drawings you must be able to do.
