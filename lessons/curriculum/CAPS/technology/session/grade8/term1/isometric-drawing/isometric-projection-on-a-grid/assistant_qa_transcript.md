### subtopic_1 — What Isometric Projection Is
**Assistant:** Why 30 degrees? Why not 45 like oblique, which I already know?

**Tutor:** Because 30 degrees is what you get when you look at a cube from the one direction in which all three of its visible faces are foreshortened equally, and that equal treatment is the whole point of isometric. Imagine holding a cube so that one corner points straight at your eye, with the three edges from that corner spread evenly around it. Seen that way, the three edges appear at 120 degrees to each other, which puts one vertical and the other two at 30 degrees above the horizontal, and all three appear the same length. Every face is tilted away from you by the same amount, so none dominates and none is distorted more than another. Oblique projection is different: it shows the front face flat and true, as if you were looking straight at it, and then fakes depth with lines at 45 degrees. The front face is undistorted, which is why oblique is quick and good for sketching, but the depth is a convention, not a view, and the object looks stretched; a cube drawn in oblique looks like a box longer than it is wide, and its depth lines cannot be trusted for measurement. Isometric costs a little more to learn, because no face is flat and circles become ellipses, but every edge along the three axes is true to scale, which is what makes it the standard for engineering drawings, assembly instructions and the artist's impressions of designers. Keep oblique for fast idea sketches and use isometric when the drawing must explain the whole object and carry dimensions, and never mix the two on one drawing.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing on the Isometric Grid
**Assistant:** Why can I not draw the diagonal brace at its real length? I know how long I cut it.

**Tutor:** Because the brace does not lie along any of the three isometric axes, and only lines along the axes are drawn at true scaled length; everything else is foreshortened by an amount that depends on its direction. Think about why axis edges are true. Isometric is set up so that the three directions of the cube are tilted equally away from the viewer, and the drawing simply uses a scale that makes those three directions come out at the right length. A diagonal brace across a side panel runs partly along the vertical axis and partly along the depth axis, at some angle between them, and when the two components are drawn in their isometric directions the resulting line is at a different angle and a different length from the real brace. If you measured the real brace, say 450 mm for a diagonal across a 400 by 200 panel, scaled it and drew that length from one corner, it would miss the opposite corner, and the drawing would show a brace that does not reach. The correct method is to locate both ends on the grid, the bottom of one upright and the top of the other, which you can do because those points are on axis edges, and then join them with a straight line whatever its apparent length. This is a general rule for all pictorial drawing: locate points using the axes, then join them. The same applies to the sloping rafters of a truss, the handle of a crank at an angle, or any edge of a shape that is not a box. It also explains why dimensions are placed only along axis directions; a dimension on the diagonal would be a lie about its length.

### subtopic_3 — Scale, Dimensions and the PAT Design
**Assistant:** Why do we need an isometric drawing when we already have the working drawing and the artist's impression?

**Tutor:** Because the three drawings answer three different questions, and a complete design portfolio needs all three answered. The working drawing answers "exactly how big is every part?" It is flat, dimensioned and precise, and the maker builds from it, but a person who has never seen the object struggles to picture it from a single flat view. The artist's impression answers "what will it look like and feel like?" It is coloured, shaded and textured, persuasive to a client, but it is not to scale and carries no dimensions, so nobody can measure anything on it. The isometric drawing sits between them and answers "how does the whole thing fit together?" It is a 3D picture, so anyone understands the shape at a glance, and it is to scale along its axes with the main dimensions, so it also tells the viewer how big the object is and how its parts relate. In industry the isometric is used for assembly instructions, for exploded views showing how parts go together, for piping and steelwork layouts and for explaining a design to people who cannot read orthographic drawings but need more than a pretty picture. For the PAT it does two jobs. In the portfolio it demonstrates a drawing skill the curriculum requires and ties the flat working drawing to the real three-dimensional frame. In the presentation it is the drawing the audience grasps first; you can point to a brace on the isometric and everyone sees where it is on the model, which is much harder with the flat view. Three drawings, three questions: how big, how it looks, how it fits together.
