# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches 3D isometric projection of the PAT design, drawn to scale on an isometric grid with dimensions. The anchor is the exploded assembly picture on the instruction sheet of a flat-pack bookshelf bought in Polokwane, which shows every panel in isometric so the buyer can see how they fit, compared with the Grade 7 oblique sketch of the rescue device. The governing idea is that isometric projection draws vertical edges vertically and all horizontal edges at 30 degrees to the horizontal, left or right, with every edge along those three axes drawn to true scaled length, so the drawing is both pictorial and measurable. Stating the rules of isometric projection, drawing a rectangular object on an isometric grid and adding scale and dimensions are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Isometric Projection Is

Grade 7 drew objects in oblique projection: the front face flat and true, with depth lines going back at 45 degrees. It is quick, but the front face dominates and the object looks stretched backwards. Isometric projection is the standard pictorial drawing in engineering because it treats all three directions equally. The word means equal measure. The three axes, height, width and depth, are drawn at 120 degrees to each other: the height axis is vertical, the width axis slopes up to the right at 30 degrees to the horizontal, and the depth axis slopes up to the left at 30 degrees.

Every edge of the object that runs along one of these axes is drawn parallel to that axis, at its true scaled length. A box 100 mm wide, 60 mm deep and 80 mm high is drawn with a vertical line of 80 mm for the front corner, a line of 100 mm sloping up to the right for the bottom front edge, and a line of 60 mm sloping up to the left for the bottom side edge, all at the chosen scale. Edges that do not lie along an axis, such as the diagonal of a brace, are not drawn to true length; they are found by locating their two ends on the axes and joining them.

No face of an isometric drawing is seen flat on. Each face is a parallelogram, and the drawing shows the top, the front and one side at once, so that the viewer understands the whole shape. Circles become ellipses, which is the one awkward part of isometric and why Grade 8 keeps to rectangular shapes and marks round holes with a simple ellipse or a centre cross.

Isometric is pictorial, so it is used for the artist's impression and for assembly instructions, but because its axis lengths are true it can also carry dimensions, which oblique cannot do reliably. That makes it the bridge between the sketch that explains and the working drawing that measures.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three axes and their angles, which edges are drawn true, and how isometric differs from oblique.

## Subtopic: Drawing on the Isometric Grid

Isometric grid paper makes the construction easy. The grid is a pattern of dots or fine lines at 30 degrees left, 30 degrees right and vertical, so that every edge along an axis is a line from dot to dot. Each step between dots is one unit, and the scale decides how many millimetres a unit stands for: at a scale where one grid unit is 10 mm, a 100 mm edge is ten grid units long.

Start with the lowest front corner of the object. From it, draw the vertical edge upwards for the height, the right-hand sloping edge for the width and the left-hand sloping edge for the depth, counting grid units for each. This gives three edges meeting at a corner, like the corner of a room seen from inside out. Complete the front face by drawing a vertical from the far end of the width edge and a parallel sloping line across the top. Complete the side face the same way from the depth edge. Finally, draw the top face by taking the two top edges back parallel to the width and depth axes until they meet. The box is done, and every line is on the grid.

Features are added by the same method. A hole on the front face is located by counting units along the width and up the height to its centre, marked with a small cross. A member of a frame is a long thin box, drawn with the same three-axis rules. A frame of several members is drawn by starting with the base rectangle as a flat parallelogram on the ground, raising verticals at each corner, and joining the tops. Diagonal braces are drawn last, by joining the two corner points they connect, since they are not on an axis.

Line conventions carry over. Construct feint, then darken the visible outlines. Hidden edges are usually left out of a pictorial drawing to keep it clear, or shown dashed if essential. Keep all verticals truly vertical and all sloping lines exactly on the 30 degree grid; a line that wanders off the grid is the commonest fault and makes the box look twisted.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the grid encodes the axes, the order of constructing a box, and how features and braces are added.

## Subtopic: Scale, Dimensions and the PAT Design

The Grade 8 task is an isometric drawing of the PAT design, to scale, with dimensions. Choose the scale first as for the working drawing: the Welkom frame is 400 high, 300 wide and 200 deep, and on A4 isometric paper with 10 mm grid units a scale of 1:2 gives 20 units high, 15 wide and 10 deep, which fits. Write the scale on the drawing.

Construct the frame from the base up. Draw the base rectangle, 15 units along the right axis and 10 along the left, as a parallelogram. Raise verticals of 20 units at the four corners for the uprights, giving each its width of 1 unit as a thin box. Join the tops with the top rails. Add the cross-rails and, last, the braces from corner to corner of each side panel. Locate the axle by counting down from the top on the two uprights it passes through and mark the hole centres; draw the axle as a line between them, projecting beyond the frame for the crank. Add the pulley as a small ellipse on the axle and the crank arm as a short line at the end.

Dimension the isometric drawing along its axes. Dimension lines in isometric are drawn parallel to the edge they measure, in the same plane as the face, so the width dimension slopes with the width axis and the height dimension is vertical. Give the overall width 300, depth 200 and height 400 in real millimetres, and the main features such as the axle height from the base. Fewer dimensions are given than on the working drawing, because the isometric explains while the working drawing specifies, but the overall sizes are always present.

The finished drawing joins the portfolio alongside the working drawing and the artist's impression, completing the set: the sketch that explored, the flat view that specified and the isometric that explains the whole design in one picture. In the presentation, the isometric is the drawing the audience understands fastest.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing the depth lines at 45 degrees from habit; that is oblique, and the two must not be mixed. Two: drawing diagonals to the length measured on the real brace; only axis edges are true length, so diagonals are found from their end points. Three: letting lines drift off the grid, which twists the box. Four: dimensioning paper lengths or leaving the scale off; the written sizes are real millimetres and the scale is stated.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing the scale for the PAT isometric, the order of constructing the frame, and how dimensions are placed on an isometric drawing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a flat-pack bookshelf instruction sheet and a sheet of dotted paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Axes, Two Slopes

In Grade 7 you drew in oblique: flat front, depth going back at 45 degrees. Today a new way: isometric.

Three directions. Up is straight up. Width slopes up to the right at 30 degrees. Depth slopes up to the left at 30 degrees. All three get equal treatment, which is what isometric means: equal measure.

Any edge that runs along one of those three directions is drawn at its true scaled length. A box 100 wide, 60 deep and 80 high is drawn with lines of exactly those scaled lengths along the three directions.

You see the top, the front and one side at once. Circles turn into ovals, so we stick to boxes and frames.

Because the lengths are true, you can put measurements on an isometric drawing. You cannot trust those on an oblique one.

Keep the three directions in mind and try the questions for this part: up, right-up, left-up.

## Subtopic: Following the Dots

Isometric grid paper has dots in exactly those three directions. Every edge is a line from dot to dot, and each dot step is one unit.

Start at the bottom front corner. Count up for the height. Count up-right for the width. Count up-left for the depth. Three lines meeting at a corner.

Finish the front face: a vertical at the far end of the width line, a sloping line across the top. Finish the side face the same way. Then the top: two lines back, parallel to width and depth, until they meet. One box.

Holes: count across and up to the centre, mark a cross. Frame members: thin boxes. Braces: last, by joining the two corners they connect, because they are not along a grid direction.

Faint first, then darken what you can see. Keep every line on the dots.

Hold the dots in mind and try this part's questions: following the dots to build a box.

## Subtopic: Drawing Your Own Frame

Now your frame. Pick a scale: 400 high, 300 wide, 200 deep at half size is 20, 15 and 10 units. Write the scale down.

Base first: a parallelogram, 15 up-right and 10 up-left. Verticals at the four corners, 20 units, one unit thick. Top rails joining them. Cross-rails. Braces last, corner to corner. Count down the uprights for the axle hole, draw the axle through and out to the crank, a small oval for the pulley.

Dimensions go along the direction they measure: width slopes with the width line, height is vertical. Overall 300, 200, 400 in real millimetres, plus the axle height. Scale written on.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Isometric uses a vertical axis and two axes at 30 degrees; edges along the axes are drawn true to scale, others are found from their ends. On grid paper, build from the bottom front corner, box by box, braces last. Dimension along the axes in real millimetres and state the scale.

Hold the three axes and the base parallelogram, and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing your own frame.
