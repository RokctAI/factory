# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the Evaluate and Communicate steps of the PAT: evaluating the model against its specifications, evaluating complex structures by listing advantages and disadvantages, and presenting plans and model to the class. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Pietermaritzburg on the last two Fridays of the project, where each team loads its frame in front of the others, fills in an evaluation sheet, and then stands up with its drawings and model for a five-minute presentation that ends in questions from the floor. The governing idea is that evaluation is a test against the team's own written specifications and constraints, recorded with evidence and followed by proposed improvements, that evaluating any structure means weighing advantages against disadvantages for its purpose, and that communication means presenting the whole IDMEC story clearly to an audience. Carrying out a specification-based evaluation, writing a balanced evaluation of a complex structure and planning a team presentation are examinable.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Against the Specifications

Evaluation in Technology has a precise meaning: comparing what was made with what was promised. The promises are the specifications and constraints written in the design step. Each one becomes a test. "It must lift a 500 g load to a height of 250 mm" is tested by hanging 500 g on the platform, turning the crank and measuring the lift with a ruler. "The frame must not topple when pushed sideways at the top" is tested by pushing. "The moving parts must be enclosed" is checked by looking. "It must be no larger than 300 by 300 by 400 mm" is checked with a tape measure. Each test gets a result, met, partly met or not met, and a short note of the evidence: "lifted 500 g to 240 mm; 10 mm short because the string slipped on the axle".

The evaluation sheet records all of this in a table: the specification or constraint, the test, the result and the evidence. Below the table come three paragraphs. Strengths: what the model does well, with reasons. Weaknesses: where it falls short, with the probable cause traced to a design or making decision, such as "the lift is slow because we chose a 1:4 gear ratio when 1:2 would have been enough". Improvements: specific changes that would fix the weaknesses, such as "glue a card collar to the axle so the string cannot slip, and change the driven gear from 48 to 24 teeth".

Honesty is the whole value of an evaluation. A sheet that reports every specification met when the class saw the string slip earns fewer marks than one that reports the slip, explains it and proposes the cure, because the second shows understanding and the first shows nothing. Examiners read evaluations for evidence of thinking, not for a good score. The rubric awards marks for evaluating objectively, fairly, accurately and in depth: objectively means by test, not opinion; fairly means strengths and weaknesses both; accurately means with measurements; in depth means tracing causes and proposing fixes.

Evaluation also looks back at the process. Did the team follow the sequence of manufacture? Did the working drawing have everything the maker needed? Did the brief match the scenario? A sentence on each closes the loop between the steps and shows that the team understands IDMEC as a cycle in which evaluation feeds the next design.

The questions for this section are with you now: how specifications become tests, what the evaluation sheet contains, and what objective, fair, accurate and in-depth mean.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Complex Structures Fairly

The second kind of evaluation in this term is the evaluation of existing complex structures, which began with the pylon case study and continues here with the structures the team investigated: bridges, towers, trusses and the frames of other teams. The method is the same as for the pylon. State the purpose of the structure. List its advantages: how well it serves that purpose, what it saves in material or cost, how safe and durable it is, how it looks, how it affects people and the environment. List its disadvantages under the same headings. Then judge: for this purpose, in this place, do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages, and what would you change?

Applied to other teams' models, this becomes peer evaluation, and it must be done respectfully and usefully. Watch the model being tested. Note what worked: a wide base that never wobbled, a smooth crank, a neat enclosure. Note what did not: a brace missing on one side so the frame racked, an axle that bound. For each point give the structural reason, using the vocabulary of the term: tension, compression, triangulation, buckling, stability, mechanical advantage. A peer evaluation that says "it looked nice" helps nobody; one that says "the single diagonal on the back panel left the front panel as an unbraced rectangle, so the frame racked when the crank was turned" teaches both teams.

The information gathered in the investigation also gets a final evaluation. Which of the structures studied gave the team ideas it used? The pylon's cross-bracing might appear in the frame's side panels; the king post truss might have suggested a triangular top for the pulley. Saying where ideas came from, and why they were adapted, shows that the investigation fed the design, which is what the rubric looks for.

The commonest fault in evaluating structures is bias. Teams praise their own model and criticise others', or they admire a famous bridge and refuse to see its costs. The cure is to use the same headings for every structure, to write at least two points under each heading, and to tie every judgement to the stated purpose. A fair evaluation of a rival team's frame reads the same whether or not you like the team.

The questions for this section are with you now: the method for evaluating a complex structure, how to give peer evaluation usefully, and how to avoid bias.

## Subtopic: Presenting Plans and Model

Communication is the final step, and in the PAT it means a short team presentation of the whole project to the class. The audience should leave understanding what the problem was, what the team designed, how it was built, how well it worked and what the team learned. The presentation therefore follows IDMEC: a sentence on the scenario and the brief; a minute on the investigation and where the ideas came from; the chosen design, shown on the working drawing and the artist's impression, with the reasons it was chosen over the alternatives; the making, with photographs and the sequence; the evaluation, with results against each specification; and the proposed improvements.

Every team member speaks. The work is divided so that each person presents a step, and each person is ready to answer questions on the whole project, because the examiner may ask anyone. The model is demonstrated live: the crank is turned, the load is lifted, and the audience sees it work or sees what goes wrong, which the speaker then explains calmly using the evaluation. A model that fails in front of the class is not a disaster if the team can say why and what they would change.

Visual material carries the message. The working drawing, the idea sketches, the evaluation table and the artist's impression are displayed large enough to be seen from the back of the room, either on a poster or held up and pointed to. Speakers point to the drawing when they talk about it; they do not read the poster aloud. Technical vocabulary is used correctly and explained in a phrase: "we triangulated the side panels, that is, we added diagonals to make triangles so the frame cannot rack".

Delivery matters less than clarity, but it matters. Stand where everyone can see the model, face the audience, speak slowly enough to be heard at the back, and keep to time, which is usually five minutes with two for questions. Rehearse once with a timer. Have one person responsible for the model, so that it is not knocked over while someone else is talking. End with the improvements, so the audience hears the team thinking forward, and then invite questions.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: presenting only the model and skipping the investigation and design, so the audience cannot tell why it looks the way it does. Two: reading the poster word for word with your back to the class. Three: one member doing all the talking while the others stand silent; marks are for individual contribution. Four: hiding a weakness the evaluation found, then having a questioner point it out; say it first, with the fix.

The questions for this section are with you now: the structure of the presentation, the role of visual material and the model, and the delivery rules.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the last two Fridays of the project — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Did It Do What We Said?

Evaluation means checking what you made against what you said it would do. Your musts and limits from Week 6 become tests.

"Must lift 500 g to 250 mm"? Hang 500 g, turn the crank, measure. "Must not topple when pushed"? Push it. "No bigger than 300 by 300 by 400"? Tape measure. Each one gets met, partly met or not met, and a note of what you saw.

Then three short paragraphs. Strengths: what worked and why. Weaknesses: what fell short and what caused it. Improvements: exactly what you would change.

Be honest. The class saw the string slip. Say so, say why, say the fix. That earns more than pretending.

Keep the tape measure and the 500 g in mind and try the questions for this part: did it do what we said?

## Subtopic: Good Points and Bad Points

You also evaluate the big structures you studied, and other teams' models, the way you did the pylon.

Say what it is for. List the good points: does the job, saves material, safe, lasts, looks fine, kind to people and the land. List the bad points under the same headings. Then judge for the purpose.

For another team's frame, say what worked and what did not, and give the reason in Technology words: "no diagonal on the front panel, so it racked". That helps them. "It looks nice" helps nobody.

Say where your own ideas came from: the pylon's X-braces, the truss's triangle.

Be fair: same headings for every structure, at least two points under each, and judge against the purpose, not against whether you like the team.

Hold the two columns in mind and try this part's questions: good points and bad points, fairly.

## Subtopic: Showing the Class

Now show the class. Five minutes, two for questions. Tell the whole story in order: the problem, what you found out, what you designed and why, how you built it, how it tested, what you would improve.

Everyone speaks. Everyone can answer questions on any part.

Show the model working. If it goes wrong, explain calmly why; you already know from your evaluation.

Hold up the drawing and the sketches big enough for the back row. Point at them; do not read them out. Use the proper words and explain each in a phrase.

Face the class, speak slowly, keep to time, rehearse once. One person guards the model. End with the improvements, then ask for questions.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Evaluate by turning each specification into a test, recording results and evidence, then writing strengths, weaknesses with causes and specific improvements, honestly. Evaluate other structures with the same headings, tied to purpose, without bias. Present the whole IDMEC story in order, with every member speaking, the model demonstrated and the drawings shown.

Hold the story in order and the model held steady, and take the final questions of the lesson: showing the class.
