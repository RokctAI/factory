### subtopic_1 — Evaluating Against the Specifications
**Assistant:** If our model met every specification, what do we write under weaknesses? Surely we do not have to invent problems.

**Tutor:** You do not invent problems, but you look harder, because a model that genuinely has no weaknesses has never been built, and an evaluation that finds none usually means the specifications were too easy or the testing too gentle. Start by pushing the tests further than the specification requires. If the brief said 500 g and the model lifted it, try 700 g and 1 kg and record where bending or slipping begins; the margin tells you how robust the design is and gives you a measured weakness, "bowed at 900 g", even if the specification was met. Then evaluate against the aspects of fitness for purpose from Grade 7 that your specifications may not have covered: cost, how much material was used and whether less would do; ergonomics, whether the crank is comfortable and at a sensible height for the user; aesthetics, whether the frame looks finished; safety beyond enclosure, such as whether the load can drop if the crank is released; durability, whether the glued joints would survive a week of use; environmental impact, whether the materials could be recycled. Almost every model has something to say under several of these. Then evaluate the process: was the drawing complete, did the sequence work, did the team run out of time at any stage? Finally, ask the audience; a question from the floor often reveals a weakness the team stopped seeing weeks ago. Writing "no weaknesses found" is read by examiners as shallow evaluation and loses the marks for depth; writing "all specifications met; under further testing the frame bowed at 900 g and the crank is too low for an adult user; we would raise the crank 100 mm and double the uprights" shows exactly the depth the rubric rewards.

### subtopic_2 — Evaluating Complex Structures Fairly
**Assistant:** How do I give a bad evaluation of another team's model without being rude or starting an argument?

**Tutor:** By evaluating the structure and not the people, and by using the method rather than your opinion, which is the same discipline engineers use when they review each other's designs. Three habits make peer evaluation useful and keep it civil. First, describe what you observed before you judge it: "when the crank was turned under load, the top of the frame moved about 20 mm sideways" is a fact nobody can argue with, whereas "the frame was wobbly" is an opinion that invites a defence. Second, give the structural reason in the shared vocabulary of the course: "the front panel has no diagonal, so it is an unbraced rectangle and racks". This turns the comment from a criticism into a piece of teaching that the other team can act on, and it shows your own understanding, which is what you are marked on. Third, pair every weakness with a strength observed with the same care: "the base was wide and the frame never tipped, even when pushed". A team hearing a fair balance of specific points, each with a reason, usually responds with interest rather than anger, and often the conversation produces ideas for both models. Avoid comparing their model with yours, which turns evaluation into competition, and avoid words like "bad", "wrong" or "obviously". If you are on the receiving end, the same rules apply in reverse: listen, write the point down, ask for the reason if none was given, and thank them, because a weakness found by a classmate on Friday is one you can fix before the examiner sees it. Fair evaluation is a skill, and it is tested on the pylon, on the bridges and on each other alike.

### subtopic_3 — Presenting Plans and Model
**Assistant:** What should we do if the model breaks during the live demonstration in the presentation?

**Tutor:** Stay calm, because a failure explained well in front of the class can earn as many marks as a success, and the examiner is watching how you respond at least as closely as what the model does. The first thing is practical: one team member, the model guardian, steadies the model and removes the load so nothing falls further, while the speaker keeps talking rather than freezing. The second thing is to name what happened in the vocabulary of the course: "the rear upright has buckled under the load; it was the thinnest member and it was in compression". That single sentence shows the audience that you understand the failure, which is the whole point of the evaluate step. The third thing is to connect it to your evaluation sheet. If you had already found this weakness during testing, say so and quote your proposed improvement, which demonstrates that your evaluation was honest and accurate. If it is new, say so too, and propose the fix on the spot: "we would double the rear uprights or add a mid-height brace, as we did on the front". Then move on with the presentation. Do not apologise repeatedly, do not blame a team member, and do not try to force the model to work again under load; a second collapse looks worse than the first. It helps to prepare for this before the day. Rehearse the demonstration with the real load several times so that you know the model's limits, and consider demonstrating at a load comfortably inside those limits while stating the maximum reached in testing. Engineers call this a safety margin, and explaining why you chose one is itself a mark of understanding.
