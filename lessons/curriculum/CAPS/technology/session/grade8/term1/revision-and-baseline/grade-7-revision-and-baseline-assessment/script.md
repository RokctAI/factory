# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Grade 7 Technology course as the foundation for Grade 8. The anchor is a single question that runs through the whole subject: how do people use knowledge, skills and resources to solve practical problems? In Grade 7 the answers came through the design process (investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate), through mechanical systems such as levers and hydraulics, through structures that resist forces, and through electrical systems that use magnetism. The baseline assessment in Week 1 tests whether those ideas are still in place. Recalling the IDMEC steps, naming the three lever classes with examples, explaining triangulation and drawing a simple circuit are the core skills that will be examined.

## Subtopic: What Grade 7 Technology Taught Us

Grade 7 Technology was organised around four mini-PATs, each one a design problem solved with the IDMEC process. In Term 1 the Jaws-of-Life rescue device introduced levers and syringe hydraulics. In Term 2 the cell-phone tower introduced frame structures, triangulation and stability. In Term 3 the scrap-metal crane introduced cranks, pulleys, simple circuits and the electromagnet. In Term 4 the emergency shelter and food project introduced processing of textiles and food. Every project followed the same five steps, and that pattern is the first thing Grade 8 assumes you remember.

Investigate means finding out about the problem, the users and existing solutions before you start sketching. Design means writing a design brief with specifications and constraints, sketching several ideas and choosing the best one. Make means building a model safely from a working drawing. Evaluate means testing the model against the specifications and judging it honestly. Communicate means presenting the drawings, the model and the evaluation so other people can understand the solution. The steps are not strictly one after another; a maker who finds a problem goes back to design.

The second strand of Grade 7 was graphical communication. You learnt that dark outlines show the edges you can see, feint construction lines guide the drawing, dashed lines show hidden detail, dimensions are written in millimetres and a scale such as 1:2 means the drawing is half the real size. You drew one face of an object as a 2D working drawing and the whole object in 3D oblique projection, with the depth lines slanting back at 45 degrees. Grade 8 adds isometric drawing to this toolkit, so the conventions must be solid.

The third strand was the impact of technology on society and the environment, from the cost of cable theft to indigenous building materials. Grade 8 extends that to plastic bags, mining and electricity supply, so the habit of asking who benefits, who is harmed and what the environment pays is a Grade 7 habit worth keeping.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five IDMEC steps, the four Grade 7 projects and the basic line conventions.

## Subtopic: Levers, Mechanical Advantage and Structures

Mechanical advantage was the big Grade 7 idea in mechanical systems. A machine gives a mechanical advantage when the load it moves is bigger than the effort you apply. Levers do this by the position of the pivot. A first-class lever has the pivot between the effort and the load, like a seesaw, a crowbar or scissors, and its mechanical advantage can be more than, equal to or less than 1 depending on where the pivot sits. A second-class lever has the load between the pivot and the effort, like a wheelbarrow or a nutcracker, and it always gives a mechanical advantage. A third-class lever has the effort between the pivot and the load, like tweezers, a fishing rod or a stapler, and it never gives a mechanical advantage but it gives speed and precision.

Hydraulics and pneumatics gave a mechanical advantage too. When a small syringe pushes liquid into a large syringe, the large plunger moves with more force but through a shorter distance. Liquids cannot be squashed, so hydraulics is firm; gases can be squashed, so pneumatics is springy. Grade 8 builds on this when it introduces the wedge, the wheel and axle, gears, cams and cranks, and asks you to calculate mechanical advantage as a ratio.

Structures were the Grade 7 Term 2 idea. A structure holds a load, spans a gap or encloses a space. Frame structures are made of members; shell structures are a thin skin; solid structures are a mass of material. A frame made of rectangles collapses sideways because the joints bend, and a diagonal member that turns each rectangle into two triangles stops that. That is triangulation, and it is the single most important Grade 7 structures idea for the Grade 8 pylon and headgear work. Stability came from a wide base, a low centre of gravity and a heavy bottom.

Electrical systems were Term 3. A simple circuit needs a cell, a conductor, a switch and an output such as a lamp or buzzer, drawn with standard symbols. A coil of wire around an iron nail becomes an electromagnet when current flows, and more turns or more cells make it stronger. Grade 8 Term 4 returns to circuits with resistors, Ohm's law and logic gates, so the circuit symbols must be remembered.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three lever classes, why triangulation works, and the parts of a simple circuit.

## Subtopic: The Baseline Assessment

A baseline assessment is not a test to pass or fail. Your teacher uses it in Week 1 to find out what the class remembers, so that the Grade 8 lessons can start at the right level and fill the right gaps. The marks usually do not count towards your term mark, but the information matters a great deal: it decides how much revision the class gets before new content starts.

Typical baseline questions ask you to list the IDMEC steps in order, to name the class of lever shown in a sketch, to identify the pivot, load and effort on a drawing, to say which syringe moves further in a hydraulic pair, to add one member to a rectangular frame to make it stiff, to draw the circuit symbols for a cell, a lamp and a switch, and to explain in one sentence what triangulation does. There is often a short sketching task too, such as drawing a box in oblique projection, and a question about the positive and negative impact of a product on society.

The best way to prepare is to say each idea aloud in your own words and to draw each one once. If you can explain to a friend why a wheelbarrow is a second-class lever by pointing to its pivot, load and effort, you will answer any lever question. If you can draw a rectangle, add a diagonal and say "two triangles cannot change shape", you understand triangulation. Short, correct, labelled sketches earn more than long paragraphs.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing the IDMEC steps in the wrong order, usually by putting make before design. Two: calling a wheelbarrow a first-class lever because it has a wheel; the pivot is at the wheel and the load sits between the pivot and your hands, which makes it second class. Three: thinking that a triangle is strong because of its shape alone; it is stiff because its three sides fix its three angles. Four: drawing a circuit with a gap in the wire and expecting the lamp to light; a circuit must be a complete loop.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a baseline assessment is for, the kinds of questions it asks and how to prepare.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a rescue tool, a cell-phone tower and a nail wrapped in wire — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Five Steps and Why They Matter

Last year you solved four problems: a rescue tool, a cell-phone tower, a scrap-metal crane and an emergency shelter. Every one of them used the same five steps.

Investigate: find out about the problem. Design: write the brief, sketch ideas, pick one. Make: build it safely. Evaluate: test it and be honest about it. Communicate: show and explain it. I-D-M-E-C.

You also learnt to draw properly. Dark lines for edges you can see. Feint lines to guide you. Dashed lines for hidden parts. Measurements in millimetres. A scale like 1:2 means half size.

Keep I-D-M-E-C in mind and try the questions for this part: the five steps and the drawing rules.

## Subtopic: Stronger Hands and Stiffer Frames

A machine makes you stronger when the load is bigger than your push. That is mechanical advantage.

Levers: find the pivot, the load and the effort. Pivot in the middle, like a seesaw, is first class. Load in the middle, like a wheelbarrow, is second class and always helps. Effort in the middle, like tweezers, is third class and never helps, but it is quick and neat.

Syringes: a small one pushing a big one gives a bigger push but a shorter move. Water does not squash, air does.

Frames: a rectangle folds flat; add a diagonal and it becomes two triangles that cannot fold. That is triangulation. Wide base and low weight keep a tower standing.

Circuits: cell, wire, switch, lamp, in a complete loop. Wire wrapped round a nail with current flowing is an electromagnet.

Hold the wheelbarrow and the diagonal brace in mind and try this part's questions: lever classes, triangulation and circuits.

## Subtopic: Showing What You Already Know

The baseline assessment is a check-up, not a final exam. It tells your teacher what the class remembers so that Grade 8 can start in the right place.

Expect short questions: list the five steps, name the lever class in a picture, label pivot, load and effort, fix a wobbly frame with one line, draw three circuit symbols, sketch a box in oblique.

Prepare by saying each idea out loud and drawing it once. A small labelled sketch beats a long paragraph.

Pocket summary of the lesson. IDMEC is investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate. Levers come in three classes by what sits in the middle. Hydraulics trade force for distance. Triangulation makes frames stiff. A circuit must be a complete loop. The baseline assessment finds gaps so they can be filled.

Hold the five letters and the three levers, and take the final questions of the lesson: what the assessment is for and how to show what you know.
