### subtopic_1 — From Investigation to Design Brief
**Assistant:** Why does it matter so much that the brief does not name the solution? The team already knows what they want to build.

**Tutor:** Because the moment the brief names the solution, every later step of the design process stops doing its job. The free-hand sketches next week are meant to develop two genuinely different ideas and compare them against the brief; if the brief already says "cardboard box", the second idea becomes a token and the comparison is theatre. The evaluation in Week 8 is meant to judge whether the solution reduced the harm; if the brief said "cardboard box", the evaluation can only ask whether the box is a good box, not whether a box was the right answer at all. And the team's own thinking narrows too early. In the taxi-rank case, the team that wrote "design a cardboard box" never considered that a wet cardboard box blocks a drain almost as well as polystyrene for several months, nor that the stall owners' real complaint was cost, nor that a deposit scheme with returnable containers might cut the waste to nearly nothing. The team that wrote the problem considered all three before choosing. There is a second, practical reason: the assessment rubric for the mini-PAT rewards a brief that states a problem and penalises one that states a solution, because the curriculum is teaching a process, not a product. If the team already has a favourite idea, the right place for it is the sketching stage, as one of the two ideas, where it has to earn its place against the other.

### subtopic_2 — Two Routes: Adapt the Material or Design the Product
**Assistant:** Our investigation was about cell phones and e-waste. We cannot adapt the material of a phone. Does that mean we can only take the second route?

**Tutor:** It means your harm sits mostly in the use and end-of-life stages, which is a strong signal that a product or system solution will be more effective than a material change, but it does not close the first route entirely, and it certainly does not limit you to one idea. Adapting the material at a phone's scale is beyond a classroom, but adapting the material of what surrounds the phone is not: a protective case made from a recycled or compostable material instead of virgin plastic is a material adaptation that reduces one small part of the phone's footprint, and a case that is designed to be kept across several phones changes the use stage too. On the second route the field is wide. The investigation presumably found that phones are discarded because batteries fail, screens crack and there is nowhere convenient to hand them in; each of those is a design brief. A drop-off box for a school or a spaza shop that keeps old phones out of household waste and feeds them to a licensed recycler is a product with a structure, a lock and a signage problem. A screen guard or a case shaped to protect the corners where screens crack extends the use stage. A repair-and-reuse stall layout is a system design. Your brief should name the harm, e-waste from discarded phones in the township, the users, the people who own them and the collectors, and the setting, and should then allow both a material idea and a product idea to be sketched and compared. The route is chosen by the evidence, not by what seems possible at first glance.

### subtopic_3 — Specifications and Constraints for the Solution
**Assistant:** How many specifications should we have, and how do we know when one is good enough to keep?

**Tutor:** Aim for between five and eight, which is enough to pin the solution down and few enough to test every one in the time you have. A specification earns its place by passing three tests. First, it must be measurable: a tester in Week 8 must be able to say yes or no, or write down a number, without arguing about what the words mean. "Holds 500 grams for 30 minutes without leaking" passes; "is strong and does not leak" fails until you add the mass and the time. Second, it must be traceable: you can point to the line in your investigation or your conversation with the users that it comes from. Twenty high comes from measuring the stall's storage box; thirty minutes comes from watching how long customers carry their food; if you cannot say where a number came from, you invented it, and an invented specification will either be ignored or will push the design in a direction nobody needs. Third, it must matter: if the solution failed this specification, would the user or the environment be worse off? If not, strike it. A good discipline is to write each specification on one line with its reason and its force in two more columns: "Stacks 20 high; storage box is 20 deep; compression, walls must not buckle." The forces column turns the specification list into a test plan for comparing materials and shapes, and it is also the line that shows the assessor you have connected the design to the forces topic, which the rubric looks for. Constraints follow the same rules but describe limits rather than performance, and there are usually fewer of them: cost, tools, time, safety, and the setting's lack of services.
