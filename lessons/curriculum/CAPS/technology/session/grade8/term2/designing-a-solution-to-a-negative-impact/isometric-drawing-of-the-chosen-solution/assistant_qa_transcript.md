### subtopic_1 — Isometric Projection Revisited
**Assistant:** Why do we draw the solution in isometric when we already have the free-hand sketch and will also draw a development?

**Tutor:** Because the three drawings do three different jobs, and the isometric is the bridge between the other two. The free-hand sketch records an idea at the speed of thought; it is roughly proportioned, has no scale, and its notes are the designer talking to the team. The development is a flat pattern for cutting and folding one part; it is exact but shows nothing of what the assembled object looks like or how the parts relate, and a development alone cannot be checked for whether the finished object is the right shape and size. The isometric sits between them: it is the first drawing to scale, so proportions are tested against real dimensions and errors in the sketch, a lid that would not fit or a rib in the wrong place, show up before anything is cut; it shows all three faces of the assembled object, so the team and the assessor can see what will be built; and its dimensions become the input to the development, since every fold line and flap length on the net comes from an edge on the isometric. In industry the equivalent is the general-arrangement drawing that sits above the detail drawings of each part. There is also an assessment reason: the curriculum requires an isometric drawing of the chosen solution as evidence of graphic communication, separately from the sketches and the working drawings, so the portfolio needs all three. If the solution is a system rather than an object, say a return crate plus signage, the isometric is drawn of its main physical component.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing the Chosen Solution Step by Step
**Assistant:** The container tapers so it can stack, which means almost none of its vertical edges are isometric. How do we draw that?

**Tutor:** You draw the two rectangles that the tapered sides connect, and let the slopes find themselves. A tapered container is a smaller rectangle at the base and a larger one at the rim, both horizontal, so both lie in isometric planes and both can be drawn exactly: the base from its length and width along the two 30-degree axes, and the rim the same, lifted up the vertical axis by the height and set outward on each side by half the difference in size. Once those two rectangles are on the grid, each sloping edge of the container is simply the straight line from a base corner to the rim corner above it. Those four lines are non-isometric, which only means you do not measure along them and you do not write dimensions on them; the dimensions that define them are the base size, the rim size and the height, all of which are isometric and all of which you do write. The same approach handles any object with sloping faces: a wedge, a hopper, a roof. Draw the isometric outlines at each end of the slope, then join. If the taper is small, as it is on a stackable food container, the difference between the base and rim rectangles may be a single grid unit or less, and at that point a note, "SIDES TAPER 5 MM OVER HEIGHT", is clearer than trying to draw it. Remember to carry the taper into the development next week, where it makes the side panels trapeziums rather than rectangles.

### subtopic_3 — Adding Dimensions, Notes and Non-Box Shapes
**Assistant:** Does the drawing have to be perfect? Our set square slips and the lines are not exactly 30 degrees.

**Tutor:** It has to be accurate enough that a maker working from it would build the right object, and it has to be honest, but it does not have to be perfect, and at Grade 8 the assessor is looking for understanding of the method more than for drafting polish. A line that is a degree off 30 does not change what gets built, because the maker reads the dimension figures, not the drawn lengths, and the figures are full-size millimetres. What does change what gets built is a wrong figure, a missing feature, or an edge drawn parallel to the wrong axis so that a slot ends up on the wrong face; those are errors of understanding and they are what you should check for. Practical steps help. Use the grid sheet rather than plain paper so the angles are given; hold the set square against a ruled horizontal or a T-square rather than free; draw construction lines so lightly that a slipped line can be erased without a trace; and darken outlines only after the whole shape is checked against the sketch. If a line does wander, correct it rather than starting over; a corrected drawing with clean final lines is normal practice. One more honest point: a drawing that is approximately right but fully dimensioned and noted is far more useful, and scores far better, than a beautifully ruled drawing with no dimensions, because the first can be built and the second cannot.
