### subtopic_1 — Why Two Ideas, and Why Free-Hand
**Assistant:** Our team's two ideas are both containers, one paperboard and one bagasse. Are those different enough?

**Tutor:** Probably not, and the test is to ask what the two sketches would teach you that one would not. Paperboard and bagasse are two candidate materials for the same idea, a disposable moulded container that replaces polystyrene like for like. Everything else, the shape, the way it is used, the way it is thrown away, the fact that it still ends up in the drain for some months, is identical, so the comparison table would show two columns of the same ticks and the choice would come down to a material test. That test is real and worth doing, but it belongs in the development of one idea, not in the comparison between two. A genuinely different second idea changes the route or the stage: it tackles the use stage with a returnable container, the end-of-life stage with a collection crate or a drain guard, or the system with a discount for customers who bring their own container. Choosing between a material change and a system change is a decision about what kind of solution the rank needs, and that is the decision the two sketches are meant to inform. There is a neat way to keep your material question alive: develop Idea A as the moulded disposable container and write "paperboard or bagasse, to be tested" against the material note, then develop a truly different Idea B. If A wins the comparison, the material test becomes the first task of the making stage; if B wins, the material question falls away, which is itself useful to know before you spend a week on it.

### subtopic_2 — Sketching Techniques That Explain an Idea
**Assistant:** Why does the comparison need a written table? Our team agreed in two minutes which idea was better.

**Tutor:** Two minutes of agreement usually means the team compared the ideas on one or two dimensions, typically the ones they found most exciting, and never looked at the rest. The table forces every specification and every constraint to be considered for both ideas, and it is in the neglected rows that the surprises live. In the taxi-rank case the returnable container looked like the clear winner in conversation, because it reduces waste almost to zero; it was only when the team filled in the constraint rows that they confronted the fact that the rank has no water and the stall owners have no time to wash, which is fatal to the idea as sketched. Without the table that would have surfaced in Week 7 with the model half built. The table also records the reasoning for people who were not in the conversation: your teacher, the assessor, the class you present to in Week 9, and your own team a month later when someone asks why you did not go with the returnable box. And it protects the team from its own hierarchy; the quietest member's objection has the same weight in a table cell as the loudest member's enthusiasm. The rubric asks for an evaluation of the two ideas against the specifications, and a filled table with a paragraph of reasons beneath it is the most direct evidence you can give. Two minutes of agreement is a fine place to start; the table is where you check whether the agreement survives contact with the brief.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing the Two Ideas Against the Brief
**Assistant:** What if neither idea meets all the specifications? Do we have to start again?

**Tutor:** No; it is normal, and it is what the specifications are for. A specification list is a set of targets, and at the sketch stage a good list is demanding enough that no first idea meets every line. What you do with the gaps is the design work. First, sort them. Some gaps are "needs testing": the paperboard container probably holds hot gravy for 30 minutes, but nobody knows until a sample is tried, so that becomes the first test of the making stage. Some gaps are "fixable by a change in the idea": the ribbed base that lets it stack twenty high was added to the sketch precisely because a plain base would not. Some gaps are "fixable by borrowing": the returnable idea's return crate can be added to the paperboard idea so that containers are collected for composting rather than dropped. And some gaps are "fatal in this setting": needing washing water at a rank with no tap cannot be fixed by the team, and an idea with a fatal gap is set aside however attractive its other rows look. Second, look at the specifications themselves; if a specification turns out to be unreachable by any idea you can imagine, ask whether it was traceable to a real need or was invented, and if invented, strike it with a note saying why. The team chooses the idea with the fewest fatal gaps and the most fixable ones, records the fixes as changes to the sketch, and carries the "needs testing" items into the plan for making. Starting again is only right when both ideas have fatal gaps, and even then the brief and the specifications stand; only the ideas are replaced.
