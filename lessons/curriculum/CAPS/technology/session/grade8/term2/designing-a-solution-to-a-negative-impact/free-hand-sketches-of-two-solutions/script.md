# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers producing free-hand sketches of two possible solutions to the negative impact identified, and choosing between them. The anchor is a double page in a Grade 8 learner's portfolio in Rustenburg: on the left a sketched take-away container pressed from recycled paperboard, on the right a sketched returnable container with a deposit tag, each with notes, and a comparison table ruled underneath.

## Subtopic: Why Two Ideas, and Why Free-Hand

The design process asks for two possible solutions before one is chosen, and the reason is simple: the first idea a team has is rarely the best, and a second idea developed with equal care gives a real comparison instead of a formality. The two ideas should be genuinely different, not two colours of the same object. In the taxi-rank case, one team's ideas are a container of a different material and a returnable container with a deposit; another team's are a drain guard and a stall-side collection crate. Two sizes of cardboard box are not two ideas.

Free-hand means drawn without instruments: no ruler, no compass, no template, only pencil and eraser. This is deliberate. At the idea stage the drawing has to keep up with thinking, and a ruler slows the hand and makes the designer commit to measurements before the idea deserves them. Free-hand sketches are also honest about their status: a quick, slightly uneven drawing invites criticism and change, whereas a ruled drawing looks finished and discourages it. Working drawings with instruments come later, after the choice is made, and the isometric drawing of next session will use a grid; this session is deliberately rough.

A sketch is a communication, not a picture. Its job is to let someone who was not in the team's conversation understand the idea: what it is, roughly how big, what it is made of, how it is used, how its parts fit, and how it reduces the harm. A sketch with no words fails this test almost every time, so annotation, the labels and notes around the drawing, is half the work. Each sketch should be headed with a name for the idea and a one-line statement of how it answers the brief.

The questions for this section are with you now: why two different ideas are required, why the sketches are free-hand, and what a sketch must communicate.

## Subtopic: Sketching Techniques That Explain an Idea

Several techniques make a free-hand sketch readable, and a good portfolio page uses most of them. Start with a light construction: a box or a few guide lines in faint pencil to fix the proportions, then the outline of the object drawn more firmly over it. Draw the object in three dimensions where possible, a rough oblique or isometric view, so that depth is visible; a flat elevation alone hides how the thing works. Add a second, smaller view where the main view cannot show something, a top view of a lid, a side view of a hinge.

Use an exploded view to show how parts fit together: draw each part slightly separated along the line on which it would be assembled, so the lid floats above the box and the deposit tag floats beside its slot. Use a cut-away or section where the inside matters: cut the container in half in the drawing to show the double wall that keeps the gravy hot, or the rib that stiffens the base against stacking. Draw a detail, a small enlarged sketch of one corner or fold, where the main view is too small to show it.

Annotate everything that matters. Label the parts. Write the material against each part: "pressed recycled paperboard, 1.5 mm", "polypropylene, reused". Write approximate overall dimensions. Note the forces where they act, in the language of the last three weeks: "walls in compression when stacked, ribbed to resist buckling", "lid in bending, corrugated board on edge", "fork puncture here, double layer". Write how the user handles it, how it is cleaned or disposed of, and how it reduces the harm named in the brief. Arrows connect notes to the parts they describe. Shade lightly to show curved surfaces and to separate the object from its background, but shading is the least important technique; a clear, labelled line drawing beats a beautifully shaded picture with no notes.

The questions for this section are with you now: the sequence of construction lines and outline, exploded and cut-away views, and what must be annotated.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Two Ideas Against the Brief

With two sketched ideas on the page, the team compares them against the brief, and the comparison is written down, not just talked about. A table does it: the specifications and constraints from last session down the side, the two ideas across the top, and in each cell a judgement, met, partly met or not met, with a word of reason. Idea A, the paperboard container: holds a hot meal for 30 minutes, probably, needs testing; stacks twenty high, yes if the base is ribbed; cost, about forty cents more, within the limit; breaks down in a year, yes; needs no bins or water, yes; accepted by owners, likely, it works the same as polystyrene. Idea B, the returnable container: holds the meal, yes, it is rigid plastic; stacks, yes; cost, higher per container but reused fifty times, so lower per meal if returns are high; breaks down, no, but is not thrown away if the deposit works; needs no bins, no, it needs a return point and washing water the rank does not have; accepted, uncertain, customers may not come back for a deposit.

The table makes the decision honest. It shows that Idea B reduces the harm more if it works but depends on a return system and water that the setting lacks, while Idea A reduces the harm less but fits the setting as it is. The team chooses with reasons, and the reasons are written beneath the table: which idea meets more of the specifications, which is more likely to be accepted, which can be modelled and tested in the time available, and whether anything from the rejected idea can be borrowed, perhaps a return crate for the paperboard containers so they are composted together rather than dropped.

Choosing is not the end of the rejected idea. It stays in the portfolio, because the assessment rewards the comparison, and because a design that was considered and set aside with reasons is evidence of good thinking. The chosen idea goes forward to the isometric drawing next session and the model in the weeks after, carrying its annotations as the first list of what to make and what to test.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: two ideas that are the same idea with a different colour or size. Two: sketches without words, which communicate nothing about material, forces or use. Three: choosing by vote or by taste instead of against the specifications. Four: throwing away the rejected sketch, which is evidence the assessor needs and may hold a feature worth borrowing.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a comparison table is built, what the taxi-rank comparison showed, and why the rejected idea stays in the portfolio.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a pencil, two sketches and a table ruled underneath — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Different Answers on Paper

Two ideas, not one. Your first idea is hardly ever your best, and a second one done properly gives you a real choice. They must be truly different: a paperboard box and a returnable box with a deposit, yes; a big box and a small box, no.

Free-hand: pencil and eraser, no ruler. At this stage the drawing must keep up with your thinking, and a ruler slows you down and makes you commit to sizes you do not know yet. A rough sketch also invites people to criticise it, which is what you want now. Ruled drawings come later.

A sketch is a message, not a picture. A stranger must be able to see what it is, how big, what it is made of, how it is used, how the parts fit, and how it cuts the harm. No words, no message. Give each idea a name and one line saying how it answers the brief.

## Subtopic: Lines, Labels and Little Pictures

How to sketch so people understand. Faint box first for proportions, then the outline firmer on top. Draw it in 3D, a rough tilted view, so you can see depth. Add a small second view where the main one hides something, the top of a lid, the side of a hinge.

Exploded view: pull the parts apart along the line they go together, lid floating above the box, tag floating beside its slot. Cut-away: slice it in half on paper to show the double wall or the rib inside. Detail: a little zoomed-in drawing of a corner or fold.

Then write. Name every part. Material next to each: "recycled paperboard 1.5 mm". Rough sizes. The forces where they act: "walls squashed when stacked, ribbed so they do not buckle", "lid bends, corrugated on edge", "fork stabs here, double layer". How it is used, cleaned, thrown away, and how it cuts the harm. Arrows from notes to parts. Light shading last, and least important.

Hold the floating lid in mind and try this part's questions: lines, labels and little pictures.

## Subtopic: Pick One, and Say Why

Now compare, in writing. Rule a table: your rules down the side, Idea A and Idea B across the top, and in each box met, partly or not, with a word why. Paperboard box: holds hot food 30 minutes, probably, test it; stacks twenty, yes with a ribbed base; cost forty cents extra, fine; breaks down in a year, yes; no bins or water needed, yes; owners will accept, likely. Returnable box: holds food, yes; stacks, yes; cost per meal low if it comes back fifty times; does not break down but is not thrown away if the deposit works; needs a return point and washing water the rank has not got; customers coming back, uncertain.

The table shows the truth: B cuts more harm if it works but needs things the rank lacks; A cuts less harm but works today. Choose, and write the reasons under the table. Borrow from the loser if you can: maybe a return crate for the paperboard boxes.

Keep the rejected sketch. It proves you compared, and it may hold a good part.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Two truly different ideas, drawn free-hand so thinking stays fast. Construction lines, 3D view, exploded and cut-away views, and labels for parts, materials, sizes, forces and use. Compare both against every rule in a table, choose with written reasons, borrow what you can, and keep both sketches.

Hold the table with its ticks, and take the final questions of the lesson: pick one, and say why.
