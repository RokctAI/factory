# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers adapting a material or designing a product to reduce the negative impact of a technology identified in the investigation. The anchor is a Grade 8 team in Mthatha whose investigation found that polystyrene takeaway containers from the taxi-rank food stalls end up in the stormwater drains, and who must now write the brief for what they will design to reduce that harm.

## Subtopic: From Investigation to Design Brief

The investigation produced a finding: a named product, the stage of its life where the harm is worst, who is harmed, and evidence for it. A design brief turns that finding into a task. The brief is a short statement of the problem to be solved, written so that any competent designer could pick it up and understand what is wanted without being told how to do it. It names the problem, the people affected, the setting in which the solution must work, and the purpose of the solution, and it leaves the form of the solution open.

A weak brief states a solution: "design a cardboard takeaway box". A strong brief states a problem: "food stalls at the taxi rank serve meals in polystyrene containers that are thrown into the street, block the stormwater drains and never break down; design a way of serving take-away meals at the rank that does not leave persistent waste in the drains and that stall owners and customers will accept." The second version lets the team consider a different material, a returnable container, a collection system or a change in how food is served, and judges all of them by the same purpose.

The brief draws directly on the investigation. The harm it names is the one the evidence showed to be worst, not the one that seemed most obvious at the start. The user it names is the real user: the stall owner who buys the containers, the customer who eats on the move, the municipal worker who clears the drain. The setting is the real setting: an outdoor rank with no bins, no running water and a tight margin on every meal. Writing those three into the brief keeps the design honest later, when a clever solution turns out to need a dishwasher, a bin, or ten rand more per meal.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a design brief is, the difference between a brief that states a problem and one that states a solution, and the three things from the investigation that the brief must carry.

## Subtopic: Two Routes: Adapt the Material or Design the Product

The curriculum offers two routes to the solution, and the brief should say which the team is taking, or that it will explore both before choosing. The first route is to adapt a material: keep the product essentially as it is, but change what it is made of, or treat or combine the material, so that the same product does less harm. Replacing polystyrene with moulded bagasse or pressed recycled paperboard is adapting the material; so is making a plastic bag thicker so it is reused rather than discarded, replacing a laminated noodle wrapper with a single recyclable plastic, or using plywood from certified plantations instead of hardwood from indigenous forest. The forces topic of the last three weeks is the toolkit for this route: the new material must still carry the loads the product sees, and a container must resist the compression of stacking, the bending of a full lid, the shear of a plastic fork and the softening of hot gravy, so the designer tests candidate materials for the forces that matter before choosing.

The second route is to design a product: change the thing itself, or add a new product to the system, so that the impact falls. A returnable, deposit-backed container that is collected and washed; a stacking drain guard that catches containers before they enter the pipe; a folding reusable food carrier a regular customer keeps in a bag; a vending rack that sells meals in the customer's own container at a discount. These change the design, the use, or the end-of-life stage rather than the material, and they draw on the structures and mechanisms knowledge of Term 1 as much as on this term's materials.

Choosing between the routes depends on where the investigation found the harm. If the harm sits in the raw-material or disposal stage of a product that is otherwise fine, adapting the material is usually the direct answer. If the harm sits in how the product is used or in the system around it, a new product or a changed design is needed, and a change of material alone will not fix it; a biodegradable container still blocks a drain for months. Many strong solutions combine the two, a paperboard container plus a return scheme, and the brief can allow that. What the brief must not do is fix on one solution before the sketching stage, which is exactly where two different ideas will be developed and compared.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two routes and an example of each, how the forces topic supports adapting a material, and how the stage where the harm sits guides the choice of route.

## Subtopic: Specifications and Constraints for the Solution

Below the brief sit the specifications and constraints, the measurable requirements the solution must meet. Specifications describe what the solution must do and be: it must hold a 500 gram meal with gravy for at least 30 minutes without leaking or softening; it must stack at least twenty high in the stall's storage box; it must be carried in one hand; it must be made of a material that breaks down within a year in a landfill or that can be sold to a local recycler; it must keep the food at least as hot as the current container does. Constraints describe the limits the solution must stay within: it must cost the stall owner no more than fifty cents more per meal than polystyrene; it must be made with tools available to the team, card, knives, glue, a hot wire cutter; it must need no electricity, water supply or bins at the rank; it must be ready as a model within three weeks; it must be safe for hot food.

Good specifications are measurable, so that the evaluation in Week 8 can test them with a yes or a no: "must hold 500 grams for 30 minutes" can be tested; "must be strong" cannot. They flow from the users and the setting in the brief, and each one should be traceable to a reason: twenty high because that is the storage box; fifty cents because that is the margin the stall owners reported. Specifications that no one can trace to a need are decoration and should be struck out.

The specifications also carry the forces knowledge into the design. Stacking twenty high means the base and walls are in compression and must not buckle; a full lid means bending and a lid of corrugated board on edge beats flat card; a fork means puncture and shear; a hot meal means the material must keep its stiffness when warm and wet. Listing these forces beside each specification tells the team what to test when they compare candidate materials and shapes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a brief that names the solution, which forbids the comparison of two ideas that comes next. Two: specifications that cannot be measured, so the evaluation becomes opinion. Three: constraints that forget the setting, so the solution needs a bin, a tap or a socket the rank does not have. Four: choosing a material for its environmental story without testing it against the forces the product actually carries.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between a specification and a constraint, why both must be measurable and traceable, and how the forces on the product appear in the specifications.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a polystyrene box in a drain and a one-page brief — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Turning a Problem into a Brief

Your investigation found the harm. Now write it up as a job for a designer: a design brief. Short, and it says the problem, who is hurt, where it has to work and what the fix is for. It does not say what the fix is.

Weak brief: "design a cardboard takeaway box." That has already decided. Strong brief: "food stalls at the rank serve meals in polystyrene boxes that end up blocking the drains; design a way of serving take-aways at the rank that leaves no lasting waste and that stall owners and customers will accept." Now you could change the material, lend out containers, add a collection, or change how food is served, and judge every idea against the same purpose.

Three things come straight from the investigation: the worst harm the evidence showed, the real users, and the real place, an outdoor rank with no bins, no tap, and thin profits.

Hold the box in the drain in mind and try the questions for this part: turning a problem into a brief.

## Subtopic: Fix the Stuff or Fix the Thing

Two roads. Road one: fix the stuff. Keep the product, change what it is made of. Bagasse or pressed recycled board instead of polystyrene; a thicker bag that gets reused; a single-plastic wrapper instead of a laminated one. The last three weeks are your toolbox here: the new material must still take the squash of stacking, the bend of a full lid, the stab of a fork, and hot gravy.

Road two: fix the thing. Change the product or add one to the system. A returnable container with a deposit; a drain guard that catches boxes; a fold-up carrier regular customers keep; a rack that sells meals cheaper in your own container. These change the design, the use, or the end of life. Term 1's structures and mechanisms help here.

Which road? Look where the harm sits. Harm in raw materials or disposal of an otherwise fine product: fix the stuff. Harm in how it is used or in the system around it: fix the thing. A biodegradable box still blocks a drain for months. Many good answers use both. Do not pick one solution yet; next week you sketch two.

Hold the two roads in mind and try this part's questions: fix the stuff or fix the thing.

## Subtopic: Must Do, Must Not, Can Only

Under the brief go the rules. Specifications: what it must do and be. Hold a 500 gram meal with gravy for 30 minutes without leaking. Stack twenty high. Carry in one hand. Break down in a year or sell to a recycler. Keep food as hot as polystyrene does. Constraints: what it must stay inside. No more than fifty cents extra per meal. Made with card, knives, glue and a hot wire. No electricity, water or bins at the rank. Model ready in three weeks. Safe for hot food.

Make every rule measurable so Week 8 can test it yes or no. "Must be strong" fails; "holds 500 grams for 30 minutes" passes. And every rule has a reason: twenty high because that is the storage box; fifty cents because that is the stall's margin. No reason, strike it out.

Each rule hides a force. Stacking is compression, do not buckle. A full lid is bending, corrugated board on edge. A fork is puncture and shear. Hot gravy means it must stay stiff warm and wet. Write the force next to the rule; that is your test list.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A brief states the problem, users, setting and purpose, never the solution. Adapt the material when the harm sits in raw materials or disposal; design a product when the harm sits in use or the system; often do both. Specifications say what it must do, constraints what it must stay inside, all measurable, all with a reason, each tied to a force.

Hold the fifty cents, and take the final questions of the lesson: must do, must not, can only.
