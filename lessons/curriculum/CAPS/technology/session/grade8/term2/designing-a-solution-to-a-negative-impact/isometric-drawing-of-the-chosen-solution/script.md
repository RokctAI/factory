# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing the chosen solution in three-dimensional isometric projection. The anchor is a Grade 8 learner in East London at a desk with an isometric grid sheet, a 30-60-90 set square and a pencil, turning the annotated free-hand sketch of a ribbed paperboard take-away container into a dimensioned isometric drawing that the team can cut and fold from.

## Subtopic: Isometric Projection Revisited

Isometric projection is a way of drawing a three-dimensional object on flat paper so that all three dimensions, length, width and height, can be seen and measured at once. The object is tilted so that three of its edges, the three isometric axes, meet at the front corner: one vertical, and two sloping away at 30 degrees above the horizontal on either side. Any line parallel to one of these axes is an isometric line and is drawn at its true scaled length; lines that are not parallel to an axis are non-isometric and must be found from their endpoints rather than measured directly.

The isometric grid makes this fast. Its lines run vertically and at 30 degrees left and right, so every isometric edge follows a grid line and every scaled length is a count of grid units. A 30-60-90 set square on a T-square or a ruled horizontal does the same job on plain paper. Each grid triangle is the same size, so one unit on the grid represents a chosen real length; choosing that scale is the first decision of the drawing. For a take-away container 180 millimetres long, 120 wide and 60 deep on a grid with 10 millimetre units, a scale of 1 to 2 gives a drawing 9 by 6 by 3 units, which fits the sheet and leaves room for notes; a drain guard a metre long needs 1 to 10.

Isometric differs from the oblique sketching of the free-hand session in two ways that matter for building: all three faces are equally foreshortened, so none is distorted more than another, and because the axis lengths are true to scale, a maker can take a measurement off any isometric edge. It differs from the orthographic working drawings of Term 1 in showing all three faces in one view, which is why it is used to explain a shape, while the single-view working drawing is used to manufacture a flat part. For a folded paperboard container both will be made: the isometric this week to show the assembled solution, and the development, the flattened net, for cutting.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three isometric axes and their angles, what makes a line isometric or non-isometric, and how the scale is chosen.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Chosen Solution Step by Step

The method that works for almost any object is to draw its bounding box first and carve the object out of it. Step one: from the annotated sketch, write down the overall length, width and height and the scale, and convert each to grid units. Step two: choose which corner faces the viewer; the drawing should show the most informative faces, usually the front, the top and the side with the most detail, so a container is drawn with its lid visible and its handle side forward. Step three: draw the bounding box lightly: the vertical axis from the front corner, the two 30-degree axes left and right, then the parallel edges to close the box, nine lines in all, all on grid lines.

Step four: carve. Features are located by measuring along isometric edges from the box corners. The rib across the base is a line parallel to the width axis, so many units in from the front; the slot for the deposit tag is a small rectangle marked off along the lid's edges; the lip around the top is a second, slightly smaller box drawn inside the first and joined to it. Material thickness is shown by drawing the inner outline of walls set in by the thickness, scaled, which on a small drawing may be a single line's width and is then shown only by a note. Step five: darken the outline of the object and erase or leave faint the construction lines of the box that are not edges of the object. Step six: check against the sketch that every feature noted there appears, that proportions match, and that no edge has drifted off its grid line.

Hidden detail, the parts of the object behind the visible faces, is not normally shown in isometric; the view is chosen so that what matters is visible, and a second isometric from the other side, or a cut-away, shows the rest. A cut-away in isometric is drawn by removing a quarter of the bounding box along two isometric planes and drawing the inner structure in the opening, which is how the double wall of a hot-food container is shown. An exploded isometric separates the parts along isometric axes, the lid lifted straight up the vertical axis by a few units, which shows assembly far better than a single closed view.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six steps from bounding box to finished outline, how features are located along isometric edges, and how cut-away and exploded isometrics are made.

## Subtopic: Adding Dimensions, Notes and Non-Box Shapes

A drawing the team can build from carries dimensions and notes, following the conventions from Term 1. Dimensions in isometric are placed along isometric lines, with extension lines and dimension lines parallel to the edge being measured and the figure written in millimetres without the unit, in full-size values, not drawing-size, so the maker never has to multiply by the scale. Only the dimensions needed to make the object are given, each once: overall length, width and height, the position and size of each feature, and material thickness as a note. The scale is written in the title block, as "SCALE 1:2", together with the title of the drawing, the team's name and the date.

Circles and curves are the usual difficulty. A circle on an isometric face appears as an ellipse, because the face is tilted. The reliable method is the four-centre approximation: draw the isometric square that would contain the circle, mark the midpoints of its four sides, and draw four arcs with a compass from the obtuse-angle corners and from two points on the long diagonal, so that the arcs meet smoothly at the midpoints. On a grid sheet, a freehand ellipse that touches the four midpoints is acceptable at Grade 8. A cylinder, such as a cup-shaped container or the tube of a drain guard, is two ellipses joined by two vertical or isometric lines. Sloping faces, such as the tapered sides of a stackable container, are non-isometric: find the top and bottom edges by measurement along isometric lines, then join their endpoints; the slope itself is never measured on the drawing.

Notes finish the drawing. The material of each part, the fold and glue lines of a paperboard part, the forces that governed a feature, "rib resists buckling when stacked twenty high", and any instruction for making go in short notes with leader lines to the feature, in capital letters, outside the outline. The drawing and the development are the maker's instructions for Weeks 7 and 8, so a feature that is not drawn or noted will not be built.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: axes drawn at angles other than 30 degrees, so the drawing is neither isometric nor measurable. Two: measuring a non-isometric line directly on the drawing instead of finding its endpoints along isometric lines. Three: writing drawing-size dimensions instead of full-size ones, so the model comes out at half scale. Four: drawing a circle as a circle on a tilted face instead of an ellipse.

The questions for this section are with you now: how dimensions are placed and which values are written, the four-centre ellipse, and how sloping faces are found.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a grid sheet and a container coming out of a box of lines — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Thirty Degrees Each Way

Isometric is the drawing that shows length, width and height at once and lets you measure all three. Three lines meet at the front corner: one straight up, two sloping away at 30 degrees left and right. Any edge parallel to one of those is measured at true scale. Any edge that is not, a slope or a diagonal, you find from its two ends; you never measure it.

The grid does the angles for you: vertical lines and 30-degree lines both ways, all the same size, so a scaled length is just a count of triangles. First choose the scale. A container 180 by 120 by 60 millimetres on a 10 millimetre grid at 1 to 2 is 9 by 6 by 3 units; a metre-long drain guard needs 1 to 10.

Why isometric and not the quick tilted sketch from last week? Because all three faces are squashed the same amount and every edge is true to scale, so the maker can measure off it. And unlike Term 1's single-view working drawing, it shows the whole shape. For a folded container you will have both: the isometric to show the thing, the development to cut it.

Hold the three axes in mind and try the questions for this part: thirty degrees each way.

## Subtopic: Box First, Then Carve

The method: box first, then carve. Write down length, width, height and scale from your sketch; turn them into grid units. Decide which corner faces you, so the most useful faces show, the lid and the handle side. Draw the box faintly: up, left at 30, right at 30, then the parallel edges to close it. Nine lines.

Now carve. Find every feature by counting along the box edges. The rib across the base: so many units in from the front, parallel to the width. The slot in the lid: a small rectangle marked off the lid edges. The lip around the top: a smaller box inside the big one. Wall thickness: an inner outline set in by the thickness, or just a note if it is too thin to draw. Darken the real edges, leave the box lines faint. Check against the sketch that nothing is missing.

You do not draw what is hidden behind. Instead, draw a second view from the other side, or a cut-away: remove a quarter of the box and draw the inside, the double wall. Or an exploded isometric: lift the lid straight up the vertical axis a few units so people see how it goes together.

Hold the faint box in mind and try this part's questions: box first, then carve.

## Subtopic: Numbers, Notes and Curves

Now make it buildable. Dimensions along isometric lines, with extension lines and dimension lines parallel to the edge, numbers in millimetres, and always the real size, never the drawing size, so nobody has to multiply. Only the sizes needed, each once: overall, each feature, wall thickness as a note. Title block: title, SCALE 1:2, team, date.

Circles turn into ellipses on a tilted face. Draw the isometric square that holds the circle, mark the middle of each side, and sweep an ellipse through the four middles; four compass arcs if you want it neat. A cylinder is two ellipses joined by two lines. Sloping sides are not isometric: find the top edge and the bottom edge along isometric lines, then join their ends.

Notes in capitals with leader lines: material, fold and glue lines, the force behind a feature, "RIB RESISTS BUCKLING, STACKED 20 HIGH". If it is not drawn or noted, it will not get built.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Three axes, one up and two at 30 degrees; measure only along them. Choose a scale, draw the bounding box, carve the features by counting along edges, darken the real edges. Show the inside with a cut-away or exploded view. Dimension in full-size millimetres along isometric lines, draw circles as ellipses, find slopes from their ends, and note materials and forces.

Hold the ellipse in its square, and take the final questions of the lesson: numbers, notes and curves.
