### subtopic_1 — What Packaging Is For
**Assistant:** Why are eggs packed in those grey pulp trays and not in a strong plastic box, if protection is all that matters?

**Tutor:** Because protecting an egg is not the same as surrounding it with something strong, and the pulp tray is a clever answer to what an egg actually needs. An eggshell is a thin shell structure, very strong against even pressure along its long axis, which is why you can squeeze an egg end to end in your palm without breaking it, but weak against a point load or a knock on its side. The danger to eggs in transit is therefore not crushing from above but eggs knocking each other, rolling about, or taking a sharp blow. The moulded pulp tray meets this exactly. Each egg sits in its own cup, pointed end down, supported at its strong ends and kept a few millimetres from its neighbours so they cannot touch. The pulp itself is slightly soft and fibrous, so it cushions a knock by crushing a little rather than transmitting it, the way a hard plastic box would. The cups and the ridges between them give the tray stiffness, so a stack of trays carries the load down through the pulp walls, not through the eggs. A rigid plastic box can be made to do this too, and clear plastic egg boxes exist, but they cost more, cushion less, need separate inserts to hold the eggs apart and persist as waste, whereas the pulp tray is made from the lowest grade of recycled paper, is cheap, breathes so the eggs do not sweat, and composts or recycles afterwards. So the pulp tray wins on protection, cost and end of life at once. The lesson is the one this session keeps repeating: find out precisely what the product needs, in this case support at the ends, separation and cushioning, and then choose the material whose properties give exactly that.

### subtopic_2 — The Product Determines the Design
**Assistant:** Chips bags are mostly air. Isn't that just the manufacturer cheating us with a bigger package?

**Tutor:** It looks like it, but the gas in a chip bag is doing two protection jobs, and a bag with no air would deliver a packet of crumbs that went stale in a week. The first job is cushioning. Chips are brittle and snap under even small loads, and bags are packed into cases, stacked on pallets, thrown into trolleys and squashed in shopping bags. The inflated bag acts like a pillow: pressure on the outside is spread through the gas rather than passing from chip to chip, so the chips arrive whole. The second job is preservation. The gas is not ordinary air but nitrogen, which carries no oxygen and no moisture. Oxygen makes the oil in fried chips go rancid, and moisture makes them soft, so flushing the bag with nitrogen before sealing keeps them crisp for months. The bag material completes the system: a plastic film laminated with a thin layer of aluminium, which blocks light, oxygen and water vapour almost completely and heat-seals tightly. All of this is a genuine design response to the product's needs: fragile, spoils in air and light, must survive rough handling and sell for a long shelf life. Where the criticism bites is in degree. Some bags are inflated more than protection needs because a bigger bag looks like more on the shelf, and consumer regulations in several countries limit the ratio of empty space to product for this reason. The honest design uses enough gas to protect and no more, and declares the mass clearly on the front. There is also the cost at the end: the foil-plastic laminate that preserves the chips so well is almost impossible to recycle, which is the trade-off the next generation of packaging designers is trying to solve.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing the Packaging Material
**Assistant:** How do I decide between a paperboard carton and a plastic film bag for the Grade 8 task? Both seem to work for most things.

**Tutor:** Run both through the product's list of needs and the material's list of properties, and let the mismatches decide, because the right answer is whichever material fails fewer of the needs that matter most. Start with the needs in order of importance. If the product is crushable, such as a cupcake or a clay pot, stiffness is near the top, and a film bag has none; the carton wins at once, perhaps with a cushioning insert. If the product must be kept dry or airtight, such as biscuits or a powder, barrier is near the top, and plain paperboard has none; the film bag or a lined carton wins. If the product is hard and robust, such as pencils or soap, neither stiffness nor barrier matters much, and the decision passes to the next needs: display and information favour a carton, because board prints beautifully and stands up on a shelf, while cost and compactness favour a film sleeve. Then weigh the end of life. A plain paperboard carton is recycled almost everywhere in South Africa; a film bag usually is not, so where the two are otherwise close, the carton is the kinder choice. Finally consider making it: in class you can cut, fold and glue a carton from a development you draw yourself, whereas sealing a film bag needs a heat sealer, which is a constraint from Term 1. Write the comparison as a small table, needs down the side, the two materials across the top, ticks and crosses in the cells, and a sentence underneath stating the choice and the deciding need. That table is the justification the examiner is looking for, and it is far more convincing than "I chose card because it looks nice". If the product has two strong needs that point to different materials, consider combining: a carton with a film window, or a card sleeve around a sealed bag, naming the trade-off in recyclability that the combination brings.
