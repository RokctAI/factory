# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the drawing of the development, or net, of an opened container. The anchor is a packaging factory in Pinetown where a single flat sheet of printed board is die-cut, creased and folded into a toothpaste carton in under a second, and a Grade 8 learner carefully slitting open an empty cereal box to see the same flat shape by hand. The governing idea is that every container made from sheet material is a development, a 2D layout of all its faces connected along fold lines, drawn with outlines for cuts, dashed or chain lines for folds and added tabs for gluing, and that the accuracy of the development decides whether the box closes. Defining a development, drawing the net of a rectangular box with correct line types and adding tabs and lids are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Development Is

Almost every box, carton, tray and envelope begins life flat. Packaging is made from sheet material, cardboard, paperboard, plastic sheet or thin metal, and the only way to make a hollow 3D shape from a sheet is to cut out a flat pattern and fold it. That flat pattern is the development of the container: all of its faces drawn at true size, side by side, joined along the edges that will be folded, so that the whole thing can be cut out in one piece, creased and assembled.

The word development comes from technical drawing, where it means unfolding a surface onto a plane. The word net is used in mathematics for the same idea. A cube has six square faces, so its development is six squares joined edge to edge in an arrangement that folds up without any face overlapping or any gap. The best-known arrangement is a cross: four squares in a column with one square on each side of the second square. There are eleven different arrangements of six squares that fold into a cube and many more that do not, so a development must be checked by folding.

Opening a real box is the fastest way to understand the idea. Take an empty cereal carton, find the glued seam along one vertical edge, slit it carefully and unfold. The box lies flat as a single piece: the four side panels in a row, the top and bottom flaps attached above and below them, and a narrow glue tab along one edge where the seam was. Every fold in the box is a crease in the flat shape, and every edge that was not folded is a cut edge. The printing, which looked continuous on the box, is laid out in panels that match the faces.

Developments matter for two reasons in this term. They are how packaging is designed and made, so a packaging designer must draw them before anything is cut. And they are a drawing skill in their own right: a development is a 2D working drawing of a 3D object, drawn to scale with dimensions and line conventions, so everything from Term 1 applies.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a development is, why packaging is made from flat sheet, and what you see when you open a box.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Development of a Box

Drawing the development of a rectangular box begins with its three dimensions: length, width and height. Suppose a box is 80 mm long, 40 mm wide and 120 mm high. It has six faces: two of 80 by 120, two of 40 by 120, and two of 80 by 40 for the top and bottom.

Lay out the four sides first, in a row, as they wrap around the box: a side of 80 by 120, then an end of 40 by 120, then the other side of 80 by 120, then the other end of 40 by 120. They share their vertical edges, which will be folds. Draw this row with feint construction lines, measuring each width along the bottom line and projecting the common height of 120 upward. Then add the top and bottom: the 80 by 40 top panel attached to the upper edge of one 80 mm side, and the 80 by 40 bottom panel attached to the lower edge of the same or the other 80 mm side. Fold it up in your head, or in paper: the four sides wrap into a tube, the top and bottom close it.

Line conventions carry straight over from Term 1 with one addition. Cut lines, the outside edge of the whole development, are dark continuous outlines. Fold lines, the edges shared between faces, are drawn as dashed lines or as thin chain lines, and are often labelled "fold". Construction lines stay feint. Dimensions are added in millimetres, as on any working drawing: the 80, 40 and 120 for the panels, and the total length and height of the flat shape so the maker knows what sheet to start with. The scale is stated.

Check the development before cutting by the two rules: every face must appear exactly once at true size, and every pair of faces that share a fold in the development must be faces that meet along that edge in the box. A common fault is to attach the top panel to an end rather than a side, which makes it 40 wide when it needs to be 80; another is to leave the row of sides in the wrong order so that two 80 mm sides are next to each other, which cannot wrap into a box.

The questions for this section are with you now: laying out the four sides and the two ends, the line types for cuts and folds, and the two checks.

## Subtopic: Tabs, Folds and Other Shapes

A development that is only the six faces will fold into a box but will not stay closed. Real packaging adds tabs and flaps. A glue tab is a narrow strip, typically 10 to 15 mm wide, attached along the free vertical edge of the last side panel; when the sides wrap around, the tab tucks inside and is glued to the first panel to make the tube. Top and bottom closures can be glue flaps, extra panels attached to the ends that fold in and are glued to the top and bottom; or tuck-in flaps, a lid panel with a tongue that slides into a slot; or crash-lock bases that interlock without glue. The cereal box uses a glued tube with tuck-in top flaps and glued bottom flaps.

Tabs and flaps are drawn as part of the development, attached along the correct edge, with their own fold line shown dashed and their corners usually cut at an angle so that they do not catch when folding. A tab's width is noted, and the drawing may label each flap: "glue tab", "tuck flap", "dust flap". The small dust flaps at the sides of a lid keep the contents clean and give the lid something to tuck against.

Other shapes follow the same logic. A tray, an open box without a lid, has a base with four sides attached along its edges, each side with a tab at each end that folds round and glues to the next side. A triangular prism has three rectangular sides in a row and two triangles on the ends. A cylinder's development is a rectangle for the curved surface, whose length equals the circumference of the circle, plus two circles for the ends; a pyramid's is a base with triangles attached to each edge. In each case the rule is the same: unfold the faces along their shared edges, keep every face at true size, and add tabs where the shape must be held together.

Packaging designers also think about the sheet. A development is laid out so that as many as possible fit on a standard sheet with the least waste, called nesting, and so that the grain or flute direction of the board runs the right way for strength. The flat shape is die-cut with sharp blades for the cut lines and blunt rules that crease the fold lines, all in one press, which is why fold lines must be drawn exactly where they are wanted.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing folds as dark outlines, so the maker cannot tell a fold from a cut. Two: forgetting the glue tab, so the box has no seam to hold it closed. Three: attaching a flap to an edge of the wrong length. Four: drawing a face twice or missing one; count the faces, six for a box.

The questions for this section are with you now: the kinds of tabs and flaps, developments of other shapes, and how the drawing is used in manufacture.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an empty cereal box and a pair of scissors — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Unfolding the Box

Every box starts flat. You cannot make a hollow shape from a sheet of card without cutting out a flat pattern and folding it. That flat pattern is the development, or net.

Take an empty cereal box. Find the glued edge, slit it, unfold. It lies flat in one piece: four sides in a row, the top and bottom flaps stuck above and below, and a thin tab where the glue was. Every crease was a fold; every other edge was a cut.

A cube's development is six squares joined up. The famous one is a cross. There are eleven that work and plenty that do not, so you always test by folding.

A development is really a working drawing of a 3D thing laid flat, so all the Term 1 rules apply.

Keep the opened cereal box in mind and try the questions for this part: unfolding the box.

## Subtopic: Six Squares in a Cross

Say the box is 80 long, 40 wide, 120 high. Six faces: two of 80 by 120, two of 40 by 120, two of 80 by 40.

Draw the four sides in a row, in the order they go round the box: side, end, side, end. 80, 40, 80, 40 along the bottom, all 120 high. The lines between them are folds.

Stick the top, 80 by 40, on the top edge of an 80 side. Stick the bottom on the bottom edge of an 80 side. Fold it up in your head: the sides wrap round, the top and bottom close it.

Lines: outside edge is a dark cut line. Folds are dashed. Construction feint. Sizes in millimetres and the scale written.

Two checks: every face once, at the right size; and faces that share a fold on the paper must be neighbours on the box.

Hold the row of four in mind and try this part's questions: six faces in a cross.

## Subtopic: Glue Flaps and Lids

Six faces fold into a box, but nothing holds it shut. Add a glue tab, a strip 10 to 15 mm wide down the free edge of the last side, to glue inside the first side. Add flaps on the top and bottom: glue flaps, or a lid with a tongue that tucks into a slot, with little dust flaps at the sides. Cut the corners of tabs at an angle so they do not snag.

Other shapes work the same way. A tray is a base with four sides and little tabs. A triangular prism is three rectangles and two triangles. A cylinder is a rectangle as long as the circle's circumference plus two circles.

In the factory, the flat shapes are packed onto a big sheet with as little waste as possible, then cut and creased in one press. So fold lines must be drawn exactly right.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A development is the flat pattern of all a container's faces joined along fold lines. Draw the sides in a row, add top and bottom to the right edges, cuts dark, folds dashed, sizes in millimetres. Add a glue tab and flaps so it stays closed, and check that every face appears once and neighbours match.

Hold the glue tab and the tuck flap, and take the final questions of the lesson: glue flaps and lids.
