### subtopic_1 — What a Development Is
**Assistant:** Why are there eleven nets for a cube? I thought a cube only unfolds one way.

**Tutor:** A cube unfolds in many ways because you choose which seven of its twelve edges to cut and which five to leave as folds, and different choices give different flat shapes. Think of the six faces as rooms and the twelve edges as doors between them. For the net to be one connected piece that lies flat without overlapping, you must keep exactly five doors as folds, enough to join six faces in a chain or tree with no loops, and cut the other seven. There are many ways to pick five such edges, and when you lay out the results and ignore ones that are the same shape turned over or rotated, exactly eleven different flat shapes remain, all of which fold back into a cube. The cross with four squares in a line and one on each side of the second is just the most familiar. A row of four squares with the extra two attached on opposite sides at different positions also works; a zig-zag of six does not; a row of five does not, because the fifth would overlap when folded. This matters for packaging in two practical ways. First, different nets waste different amounts of sheet when nested side by side, so designers choose the net that packs most tightly on the board. Second, different nets put the glue seam and the folds in different places, which affects how strong the box is and how it opens. For your Grade 8 drawing the safe habit is to use the row-of-four layout with top and bottom attached to a long side, because it is easy to check and easy to fold, but understanding that other nets exist helps you spot an arrangement that cannot work, such as two top faces on the same side.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing the Development of a Box
**Assistant:** Why must the cylinder's rectangle be exactly the circumference long? Could I just leave it a bit longer to be safe?

**Tutor:** You could, but then it is no longer a development of the cylinder; it is a development with a built-in overlap, and in packaging that overlap must be designed on purpose as a glue tab, not left as an accident. The curved surface of a cylinder is a rectangle wrapped round, and when it is wrapped exactly once its two short edges meet. The length of the rectangle is therefore the distance round the circle, the circumference, which is pi times the diameter, so a tin 60 mm across needs a rectangle just over 188 mm long. If the rectangle is longer, the edges overlap when wrapped; if shorter, there is a gap. An overlap is actually how real cylindrical packaging is made: a crisp tube or a cardboard core has a seam where one edge is glued over the other, and the designer adds a tab of known width beyond the true circumference for exactly this purpose, drawn on the development with a fold line between the true surface and the tab. The difference between adding a labelled tab and just making the rectangle a bit longer is the difference between a design and a guess: the tab tells the maker where the glue goes and how wide the seam is, and the true circumference tells them where the printing must stop so the pattern lines up round the tin. The same logic applies to every development. Every face is drawn at true size, every tab is drawn and labelled as a tab, and nothing is left vague for the maker to sort out, because a die-cutting press cannot sort anything out; it cuts exactly what is on the drawing.

### subtopic_3 — Tabs, Folds and Other Shapes
**Assistant:** Why do the corners of the glue tabs get cut at an angle? It looks like extra work.

**Tutor:** It is a small cut that saves a lot of trouble, and once you have folded a few boxes you see why. A tab is a strip attached to one edge of a panel, and when the panel folds along that edge, the ends of the tab swing round inside the box. If the tab's corners are square, each corner is a sharp point that extends the full width of the tab right to the fold line of the neighbouring panel, where it collides with the flap or tab folding in from that panel. The two pieces of card jam against each other, the fold does not close cleanly, the corner bulges and the glued seam may be pulled open. Trimming each corner at about forty-five degrees removes the material that would collide, so the tabs slide past one another and the box folds flat and square. The angled corner also makes the tab easier to tuck in by hand or machine, since a point finds its way into a gap more readily than a square end. Packaging designers call this corner relief or chamfering, and you will see it on almost every carton you open: every flap and tab has its corners nicked. There is a second, structural reason. A square corner of card is a stress point where the board can tear or buckle as it is forced round the fold; an angled corner spreads the stress. On your drawing the angled corners are shown as short dark cut lines across the corners of each tab, and the detail is a sign to the examiner that you have understood how the development will actually be folded rather than only how it looks flat.
