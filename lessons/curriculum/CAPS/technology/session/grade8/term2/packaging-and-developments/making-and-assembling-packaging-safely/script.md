# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers making and assembling packaging safely from a drawn development. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Bloemfontein, each learner with a sheet of recycled board, a steel ruler, a craft knife, a scoring tool and glue, turning the development of a cupcake box into the real thing in a double lesson, with the cupcakes arriving from Consumer Studies to test it. The governing idea is that a development is transferred accurately to the card, cut on the cut lines and scored on the fold lines, folded in a fixed sequence and glued at the tabs, with safe handling of tools throughout, and that the finished package is tested against its functions with the product inside. Transferring and cutting a blank, scoring and folding correctly, gluing seams and evaluating the package are examinable.

## Subtopic: From Development to Flat Blank

The first step is to get the development from the drawing onto the card accurately. Two methods are used. The first is to mark out directly on the card with a pencil, set square and ruler, measuring each dimension from the drawing and drawing the cut lines and fold lines lightly; this is accurate but slow. The second is to make a template: draw the development full size on thin card or stiff paper, cut it out, lay it on the packaging board and trace round the outside, then mark the fold lines by pricking through the template's corners with a pin and joining the pricks with a ruler. The template is faster when several identical blanks are needed, which is how factories think, since they make thousands from one die.

Before marking out, the grain or flute direction of the board is checked. Paperboard folds cleanly along the grain and tends to crack across it; corrugated board folds easily parallel to its flutes and resists folding across them. The development is placed so that the most important folds, usually the long vertical ones, run along the grain or flutes. For a box that must stand up to stacking, the flutes run vertically in the side panels.

The blank is then cut on the cut lines only. The outside edge of the development is cut with a craft knife against a steel ruler on a cutting mat, cutting away from the body in several light passes rather than one hard one, with the free hand well back from the blade. Internal cuts, such as the slot for a tuck flap or a window, are made from the inside outward. Corners of tabs are trimmed at an angle. Fold lines are not cut; they are left for scoring. A blank with a fold line accidentally cut is scrap, which is why fold lines are drawn dashed and cut lines dark on the development, and why the maker checks which is which before the knife touches the card.

Safety follows the Term 1 rules exactly: mat under, steel ruler beside, blade away, retract when not cutting, hair tied back, tools in a tray, report any cut.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two methods of transferring a development, why grain direction matters, and how cut lines and fold lines are treated differently.

## Subtopic: Scoring, Folding and Gluing

A fold in card does not bend sharply by itself; it bows in a soft curve and cracks the surface. Scoring solves this. A score is a compressed or lightly cut line along the fold that weakens the card there so it bends exactly on that line. For paperboard, the score is made by pressing a blunt tool, a bone folder, the back of a butter knife, an empty ballpoint pen or a scoring wheel, firmly along a ruler on the fold line. This crushes the fibres without cutting them, and the card folds crisply away from the scored side. For thick corrugated board, a half-cut score with a knife set to cut only the top liner and flutes, leaving the bottom liner intact, may be needed, or the flutes can be crushed with a blunt tool. The score is always made on the outside of the fold for a crease that bends inward, which is the usual case for a box.

Folding follows a sequence. All scores are made first, while the blank is flat. Then each fold is bent gently to about 90 degrees and back, to set the crease, before assembly begins. The side panels are folded round to form the tube, the glue tab is folded in, and the tube is held square while the tab is glued. Only when the tube is set are the base flaps folded and glued, and the lid left to be tucked or glued after the product is inside. Folding in the wrong order, such as gluing the base before the tube is square, locks in a skew that cannot be corrected.

Gluing a seam well is a matter of amount, position and time. PVA glue is spread thinly and evenly over the whole tab with a brush or a scrap of card, not squeezed in a blob, which oozes out and warps the card. The tab is pressed against the inside of the mating panel, the joint is checked square, and it is held under pressure, with fingers, clips or a weight, until the glue grabs, a minute or two for PVA. Hot glue grabs instantly but is thick and can leave a ridge; double-sided tape is quick and clean for light cartons. Glue goes on the tab, not on the printed face, and any squeeze-out is wiped at once.

A clean seam is the sign of care. The edges of the panels meet exactly, the tab is hidden inside, no glue shows, and the box stands square on the desk. A skew box, a visible tab or glue smears are not cosmetic faults only: a skew box does not stack, and a tab that lifts means the seam will fail in use.

The questions for this section are with you now: how and why folds are scored, the order of folding and gluing, and how a seam is glued cleanly.

## Subtopic: Checking the Finished Package

The finished package is evaluated against the functions it was designed for, with the real product inside. Protection: put the product in, close the package, and give it the handling it will meet, a shake, a short drop onto the desk, a stack of books on top. Does the product survive? Containment: does everything stay inside with the lid closed; does a liquid or powder leak? Information: is there space for the name, contents and date, and can it be read? Convenience: can it be opened and closed easily and more than once if it should; can it be carried? Promotion: does it look as intended and show the product where a window was planned? Each function gets a short note, as in the Term 1 evaluation sheet.

The construction is also checked. Are all folds on their lines and crisp? Is the box square, measured by equal diagonals on the top? Does the lid tuck or close without forcing? Are the seams secure when pulled gently? Are there glue marks or cut marks on the outside? Is the package the size the development specified, measured with a ruler? Any fault is traced to its cause: a skew box to the order of gluing, a cracked fold to scoring on the wrong side or across the grain, a lid that will not tuck to a slot cut too short.

Improvements are proposed from the faults, and if time allows a second blank is made with them. Common improvements are a wider glue tab for a stronger seam, a deeper slot, a card insert to stop the product sliding, a score on the opposite side where a fold cracked, and better nesting on the sheet to waste less card.

The package is then compared with the material and environmental choices made in the design. Was the material the right one: did the card go soft, was the window needed? Could less material have done the job? Can the package be recycled as made, or did the window film and glue spoil that? These questions link the making back to the term's theme.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: cutting the fold lines; a scored fold bends, a cut fold falls apart. Two: scoring on the inside of the fold, which makes the card crack on the outside. Three: gluing with a blob instead of a thin spread, so glue squeezes out and the card warps. Four: calling the box finished without putting the product in and testing.

The questions for this section are with you now: testing the package against each function, checking the construction and tracing faults, and the environmental questions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a sheet of card, a knife and a cupcake — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Marking Out and Cutting

Get the drawing onto the card. Either measure every line from your development and draw it lightly on the card, or draw the development on thin card first, cut that out as a template, trace round it, and prick through the corners to mark the folds. Templates are faster if you need more than one.

Check the grain first. Card folds cleanly along its grain and cracks across it; corrugated folds along the flutes. Put your long folds along the grain.

Cut the outside edge only. Steel ruler, cutting mat, light passes, hand well back, knife away from you. Cut inside slots from the inside out. Nick the tab corners. Do not cut the fold lines; dashed means fold, dark means cut. Check before the knife touches.

Keep the dashed lines in mind and try the questions for this part: marking out and cutting.

## Subtopic: Crisp Folds and Clean Seams

Card does not fold sharply on its own. It bows and cracks. So you score the fold first: press a blunt tool, a bone folder, the back of a butter knife or an empty ballpoint pen, hard along a ruler on the fold line. That crushes the fibres and the card folds crisply away from the score. For thick corrugated, cut just the top layer or crush the flutes.

Score everything while the blank is flat. Bend each fold to a right angle and back to set it. Then wrap the sides into a tube, fold the tab inside, hold it square and glue the tab. Let it set. Then the base flaps. The lid last, after the product goes in. Glue the base before the tube is square and the skew is locked in for good.

Glue: a thin even layer on the whole tab, not a blob. Press, check square, hold for a minute or two. Wipe any squeeze-out at once. Tab inside, no glue showing, edges meeting, box standing square.

Hold the butter knife in mind and try this part's questions: crisp folds and clean seams.

## Subtopic: Does It Do Its Job?

Now test it, with the real product inside. Protect: shake it, drop it a little, pile books on it. Did the product survive? Contain: anything fall out or leak? Inform: room for the name and date? Convenient: opens, closes, carries? Promote: looks as planned, window shows the product?

Then check the build. Folds on the lines and crisp? Box square, diagonals equal? Lid closes without forcing? Seams hold when pulled? No glue or cut marks outside? Right size?

Trace each fault to its cause: skew from gluing in the wrong order, a cracked fold from scoring the wrong side, a lid that will not tuck from a short slot. Fix it on a second blank if you can.

Last, ask the term's question: was it the right material, could less have done, can it be recycled as made?

Pocket summary of the lesson. Transfer the development by marking out or a template, with folds along the grain. Cut only the cut lines, safely. Score every fold, set it, then tube, tab, base, lid in order with thin even glue. Test with the product against every function, check the build, trace faults and judge the material.

Hold the squashed cupcake that did not get squashed, and take the final questions of the lesson: does it do its job?
