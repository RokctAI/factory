# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the design of packaging for different purposes, with the principle that the product determines the design and the properties of the packaging material. The anchor is the shelves of a spaza shop in Khayelitsha: eggs in a moulded pulp tray, maize meal in a woven polypropylene sack, cough syrup in a brown glass bottle inside a printed carton, chips in a foil-lined bag and a cell phone in a rigid box with foam inserts. The governing idea is that packaging has several functions, protection, containment, information, convenience and promotion, and that the designer works out which functions matter most for a particular product and then chooses a shape and a material whose properties meet them. Listing the functions of packaging, explaining how a product determines its packaging and justifying a material choice are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Packaging Is For

Packaging is the material that surrounds a product from the factory to the moment it is used. It has five jobs, and every package does some or all of them. Protection: keeping the product safe from knocks, crushing, moisture, light, air, dirt, insects and tampering. Containment: holding a quantity of loose or liquid product together so it can be moved, such as a litre of milk or two kilograms of rice. Information: carrying the name, contents, mass, ingredients, instructions, expiry date, barcode and warnings that the law and the buyer require. Convenience: making the product easy to carry, open, store, pour, reseal and dispose of. Promotion: attracting the eye on a crowded shelf and telling the buyer why to choose this product, through colour, shape, pictures and words.

Different products rank these jobs differently. For eggs, protection is almost everything: the tray exists to stop them breaking. For maize meal, containment and convenience matter most: a sack that holds 10 kilograms, can be carried on a shoulder and does not split. For cough syrup, protection from light and children, accurate information and tamper evidence lead. For chips, protection from air and moisture keeps them crisp, and promotion sells them. For a cell phone, protection from shock and promotion of a premium product together justify an expensive box.

Packaging also has levels. Primary packaging touches the product: the bottle, the bag, the tray. Secondary packaging groups primary packages for sale or display: the carton around the bottle, the shrink-wrap around six cans. Tertiary packaging moves goods in bulk: the corrugated case holding twenty-four cartons, the pallet and its wrapping. Each level is designed for its own job, and Grade 8 concentrates on primary and secondary packaging.

Packaging has costs as well as functions. It uses material and energy, it adds mass to be transported, and it becomes waste the moment the product is opened, which is why last week's lessons on plastics, recycling and biodegradability bear directly on packaging design. The best package does its jobs with the least material and the kindest end of life.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five functions of packaging, how different products rank them, and the three levels.

## Subtopic: The Product Determines the Design

The design process for packaging starts with the product, not with the box. The designer asks a set of questions about what is inside. How fragile is it, and in which direction: an egg cracks under a point load but survives even pressure, a biscuit snaps when bent, a phone screen shatters when dropped flat. How heavy is it, which decides the strength of the container and the handles. Is it a liquid, a powder, a solid or many small pieces, which decides whether the package must be leak-proof, dust-tight or simply enclosing. Does it spoil: food needs protection from air, moisture, light and germs, and often a date; a spanner does not. Is it dangerous or must it be kept from children: medicines and chemicals need child-resistant and tamper-evident closures. How will it be stored and displayed: stacked on a pallet, hung on a hook, stood on a shelf, kept in a fridge. How will it be used: all at once, or opened and resealed many times.

The answers fix the shape. Eggs sit in individual cups that hold each egg at its strong ends and keep them apart. Maize meal fills a sack whose shape follows the contents and whose top is stitched or heat-sealed. Cough syrup goes in a bottle with a narrow neck for controlled pouring and a child-resistant cap, inside a rectangular carton that stacks and carries printing. Chips need a sealed, light-blocking bag inflated with nitrogen so the chips do not crush. A phone sits in a snug cavity cut in foam or moulded pulp inside a rigid box, so it cannot move.

Shape also follows handling and display. Rectangular boxes stack without gaps and fit pallets; cylinders roll and waste space but suit liquids; a hanging hole at the top of a blister card lets small goods hang on a hook. Size follows the quantity sold, and a package visibly larger than its contents misleads the buyer and wastes material, which regulations and customers increasingly punish.

In the Grade 8 task you will design packaging for a given product, such as a single cupcake, a set of six pencils, a bar of soap or a small clay pot made in Creative Arts, and the first page of the design is this analysis: what the product needs, in order of importance, and what shape follows from it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the questions to ask about a product, how the answers decide the shape, and the Grade 8 packaging task.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Packaging Material

Once the functions and shape are clear, the designer chooses the material by matching its properties to the product's needs. Properties to consider include strength, stiffness, weight, resistance to moisture and grease, barrier to air and light, transparency, ability to be printed, ability to be formed into the shape, cost, and what happens at the end of life.

Paperboard and corrugated board are stiff, light, cheap, easy to print and fold, and recyclable, but they soften when wet and give no barrier to air; they suit dry goods, cartons and shipping cases, and can be coated or lined for moisture. Moulded pulp cushions and insulates, uses waste paper and composts, but it is not strong in tension; it suits eggs, fruit and inserts. Plastic films, polythene and polypropylene, are flexible, waterproof, sealable and cheap, with good barrier when metallised, but they are hard to recycle and persist; they suit bags, wraps and sachets. Rigid plastics such as PET and HDPE are tough, light, transparent or opaque and recyclable where systems exist; they suit bottles and tubs. Glass is inert, gives a total barrier, is transparent and endlessly recyclable, but heavy and breakable; it suits medicines, preserves and premium drinks. Metal, in tins and foil, gives a total barrier, strength and heat resistance and recycles well, but is heavier and costlier; it suits canned food and foil-lined bags. Woven polypropylene sacks are strong, breathable and reusable; they suit grain and meal. Foam cushions but is bulky and rarely recycled.

The choice is a trade-off written out. For a cupcake, the product is soft, must not be crushed, is eaten within a day and sells on appearance: a paperboard box with a window of clear film, a card insert to hold the base, no barrier needed, recyclable except the window. For a bar of soap, the product is solid and scented, may sweat in heat and sells on promotion: a printed paperboard carton, perhaps with a thin inner wrap. For six pencils, the product is long, hard and sold on display: a paperboard sleeve or a blister card to hang on a hook. Each choice names the property that met the need, and that justification is what the marking rubric rewards.

Environmental thinking from earlier in the term sits inside every choice: use the least material that does the job, prefer one material over laminates so the package can be recycled, choose recycled or renewable content, and avoid packaging that outlives its product by centuries when it need not.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: designing the box first and squeezing the product in; the product decides. Two: choosing a material for looks alone; the properties must meet the needs. Three: over-packaging, which wastes material and misleads buyers. Four: ignoring the end of life; a laminate of paper, foil and plastic may do the job and be impossible to recycle.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties to match, the strengths and weaknesses of the common materials, and justifying a material choice for a given product.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the shelves of a spaza shop — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Protect, Contain, Inform, Sell

Packaging has five jobs. Protect the product from knocks, wet, light, air and dirt. Contain it, so a litre of milk or a kilo of rice stays together. Inform: name, contents, mass, date, barcode, warnings. Make it convenient to carry, open, pour and reseal. Promote it, so it catches your eye on the shelf.

Different products care about different jobs. Eggs: protection. Maize meal: containment and carrying. Cough syrup: protection from light and children, and clear information. Chips: keeping air out, and selling. A phone: shock protection and looking expensive.

Three levels: the bottle itself, the carton round it, the big box that carries twenty-four cartons.

And packaging is waste the moment you open it, so do the jobs with the least material.

Keep the spaza shelf in mind and try the questions for this part: protect, contain, inform, sell.

## Subtopic: Eggs Are Not Bricks

Start with what is inside, never with the box.

Ask: how fragile, and which way? Eggs crack from a point but take even pressure. How heavy? Liquid, powder or solid? Does it spoil? Is it dangerous or must children be kept out? How is it stored and shown: stacked, hung, in the fridge? Used all at once or opened many times?

The answers decide the shape. Eggs in cups that hold the strong ends and keep them apart. Meal in a sack that follows its shape. Syrup in a narrow-necked bottle with a child cap inside a carton that stacks. Chips in a sealed, puffed-up bag. A phone in a snug cut-out so it cannot move.

Boxes stack; cylinders roll and waste space; a hole at the top lets it hang. The size matches the amount inside.

Your task: design packaging for a product like a cupcake, six pencils or a bar of soap, starting with this list of needs.

Hold the egg and the brick in mind and try this part's questions: eggs are not bricks.

## Subtopic: Matching Material to Product

Now pick the material by matching its properties to the needs.

Cardboard: stiff, light, cheap, prints and folds well, recycles; goes soft when wet, lets air through. Dry goods, cartons, boxes. Moulded pulp: cushions, composts, weak in tension. Eggs and inserts. Plastic film: flexible, waterproof, seals, cheap; hard to recycle. Bags and wraps. Rigid plastic: tough, light, clear, recyclable where collected. Bottles and tubs. Glass: total barrier, clear, recycles for ever; heavy, breaks. Medicine and premium drinks. Metal: total barrier, strong; heavier, dearer. Tins and foil. Woven polypropylene: strong, breathes, reusable. Sacks.

A cupcake: soft, must not crush, eaten today, sells on looks. Card box with a clear window and an insert. Soap: solid, scented, sells on the shelf. Printed card carton. Six pencils: long, hard, hung on a hook. Card sleeve or blister card.

Each time, say which property met which need. Use the least material, one material rather than a sandwich, recycled where you can.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Packaging protects, contains, informs, makes convenient and promotes. The product's fragility, weight, state, spoilage, danger, storage and use decide the shape. Material properties are matched to the needs and the choice is justified, with the least material and the kindest end of life.

Hold the cupcake box, and take the final questions of the lesson: matching material to product.
