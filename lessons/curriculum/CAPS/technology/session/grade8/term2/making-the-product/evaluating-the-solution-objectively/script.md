# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers evaluating the solution's effectiveness objectively, fairly, accurately and in depth. The anchor is a Grade 8 team in Welkom with their finished paperboard container on the bench, a kitchen scale, a jug of hot water, a stopwatch, a stack of twenty identical containers borrowed from the other teams, and the specification table from Week 6 ruled into a results sheet.

## Subtopic: What Evaluation Is and Why It Must Be Objective

Evaluation is the judgement of how well a solution meets its brief, made on evidence rather than on hope. It is not the same as the testing done during making, which checks that parts fit; it stands back from the finished model and asks whether the thing that was made does what the brief asked, how well, and what should change. The curriculum asks for evaluation that is objective, fair, accurate and in depth, and each of those words rules out a common failing.

Objective means based on observation and measurement that anyone could repeat, not on the team's feelings about its own work. A team that has spent two weeks on a model wants it to succeed, and that wish bends judgement unless the evaluation is designed to resist it: tests are set before they are run, results are recorded as they happen, and a verdict follows from the results rather than being written first. Fair means the solution is judged against the brief and specifications it was designed to, not against a different standard invented afterwards, and that strengths and weaknesses get equal attention. Accurate means the measurements are real, taken with the right instrument, repeated, and reported as numbers with units: not "it held the food fine" but "held 520 grams of water at 70 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes; base softened but did not leak". In depth means the evaluation goes beyond the model to the solution as a whole: whether it would reduce the harm in the real setting, what the users would say, what its own impacts are, and what the team would do next.

Evaluation also serves the people who were not in the room. The presentation in Week 9 will be judged partly on it, the assessor marks it on the rubric, and a real client would decide from it whether to fund the product. A report that admits failures and explains them is more credible to all three than one that claims success everywhere.

The questions for this section are with you now: how evaluation differs from testing during making, and what objective, fair, accurate and in depth each rule out.

## Subtopic: Testing Against the Specifications

The core of the evaluation is a test for each specification and constraint, run on the model and written up in a results table with four columns: the requirement, the test, the result and the verdict. The requirements come straight from Week 6, which is why they were made measurable then.

Holds a 500 gram meal with gravy for 30 minutes without leaking: fill with 500 grams of water at the temperature of a hot meal, measured with the scale and a thermometer, set on paper towel, start the stopwatch, check at 10, 20 and 30 minutes for damp patches on the towel and for softening of the base, and record what was seen at each check. Stacks twenty high: fill one container with its load, stack twenty empties on it, or the equivalent mass in books, and measure any crushing of the walls with a ruler; repeat on a second container. Carried in one hand: three different people carry the loaded container twenty metres and report. Breaks down within a year or is recyclable: this cannot be tested in a lesson, so the evidence is the material's documented behaviour and a note that it was not tested directly. Cost no more than fifty cents over polystyrene: price the materials for one container from the quantities used and divide; note that a factory price would differ. Needs no bins, water or electricity: a reasoned check against the design. Accepted by owners and customers: show the model to at least three people who buy or sell at a rank and record what they say, favourable and unfavourable.

Every result is written with its number and unit, how many times it was repeated, and anything unusual. A failed test is recorded as plainly as a passed one, with the observation that explains it: "walls buckled under 14 containers; rib was glued to the base only and did not reach the walls". That observation is worth more than the pass, because it is the start of the recommendation. The verdict column says met, partly met or not met, and the table ends with a count. The tests are also judged for fairness: was the model loaded the way a real container would be, was the comparison with polystyrene made on the same test, were the three people asked a neutral question or led to the answer the team wanted?

The questions for this section are with you now: the four columns of the results table, how three of the specifications are tested, and how a failed test is recorded.

## Subtopic: Fair, Accurate and In Depth: Judging the Impact

The specifications say whether the model works as an object. The brief asked for more: a solution that reduces a negative impact for particular people in a particular place. The in-depth part of the evaluation returns to that. Does it reduce the harm? Estimate it. If every stall at the rank switched, how many polystyrene containers a day would no longer enter the drains, and what would replace them; is paperboard in a drain better, and for how long does it stay there before it breaks down; would the return crate, if the team borrowed one, actually collect the containers, and who would empty it? Does it keep the benefit? The customer still gets a hot meal to carry; the owner still serves quickly; the margin is reduced by forty cents; is that acceptable, and who said so? Is it practical in this setting? No water, no power, no bins: the solution was designed around those, but the evaluation says so and says what would break it, for example a rainy season that softens stored board.

Then the solution's own impacts. Every product has a footprint, including the one designed to reduce another's. Where does the paperboard come from; is the recycled content real; what energy does pressing use; what happens to the containers at end of life if the compost scheme does not exist yet? The evaluation names these honestly and judges whether the net effect is still a reduction. A solution that moves harm from one place to another is not a solution, and saying so is the mark of depth.

The evaluation closes with recommendations: specific, numbered changes the team would make with more time, each tied to a result. "Extend the rib to the walls so it carries the stacking load; re-test to twenty." "Line the base with a thin wax coating and re-run the 30-minute test." "Ask ten customers, not three, with a neutral question." Recommendations are the bridge to the presentation and the evidence that the team understands its own work.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing the verdict first and testing afterwards. Two: reporting feelings instead of numbers: "it worked well" is not a result. Three: evaluating only the object and never asking whether the harm in the brief is actually reduced. Four: hiding a failed test, which the assessor can usually see and which throws away the best recommendation.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three questions that return to the brief, why the solution's own impact is assessed, and what makes a good recommendation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a jug of hot water, a kitchen scale and a results table — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Does It Work? Prove It

Evaluation is deciding how well the thing you made does what the brief asked, using evidence, not hope. It is not the fit-checking you did while building; it stands back from the finished model and asks: does it do the job, how well, and what should change?

Four words. Objective: measured and seen, something anyone could repeat; set the tests before you run them, write results as they happen, decide the verdict at the end, not the start. Fair: judge it against its own brief and rules, not a new standard, and give weaknesses the same space as strengths. Accurate: real numbers with units, with the right instrument, repeated. Not "it held fine" but "520 grams at 70 degrees for 30 minutes; base went soft, did not leak". In depth: go past the object to the whole solution, the real place, the users, its own footprint, and what you would do next.

A report that admits what failed and explains why is believed. One that says everything worked is not.

Hold the four words in mind and try the questions for this part: does it work? Prove it.

## Subtopic: Test Every Rule, Write Every Number

Test every rule from Week 6. Four columns: the rule, the test, the result, met or not.

Holds 500 grams for 30 minutes: 500 grams of hot water on the scale, thermometer, sit it on paper towel, stopwatch, check at 10, 20, 30 for damp and for softening. Stacks twenty: load one, pile twenty on it or the same mass in books, measure any crushing, do it twice. One hand: three people carry it twenty metres. Breaks down in a year: cannot test here; cite the material and say so. Fifty cents: price your materials per container. No water or power: reason it through. Owners and customers accept it: show it to three real people and write down the good and the bad they say.

Every result gets a number, a unit, how many repeats, and anything odd. A fail is written as plainly as a pass, with the reason: "walls buckled at 14 containers; the rib only touched the base". That line is the start of your best recommendation. Count the mets at the bottom. And check your tests were fair: real loading, same test for polystyrene, a neutral question to the three people.

Hold the buckling at 14 in mind and try this part's questions: test every rule, write every number.

## Subtopic: Honest About the Harm

The rules test the object. The brief asked for less harm. So go back. Does it cut the harm? Estimate: if every stall switched, how many polystyrene boxes a day stay out of the drains; is paperboard in a drain better, and for how long; would the return crate actually be used, and who empties it? Does it keep the benefit? Hot meal, quick service, forty cents less margin; who says that is fine? Does it work here, with no water, power or bins; what would break it, like rain softening stored board?

Then be honest about your own thing's footprint. Where does the board come from, is the recycled content real, what energy does pressing use, what happens if the compost scheme never arrives? Harm moved somewhere else is not harm removed.

Finish with numbered fixes, each tied to a result: extend the rib to the walls and re-test; wax the base and re-run 30 minutes; ask ten customers, not three.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Evaluate on evidence: objective, fair, accurate, in depth. Test every rule with numbers in a four-column table, record fails plainly with their cause. Return to the brief: less harm, same benefit, works here. Name your own solution's footprint. End with specific recommendations.

Hold the numbered fixes, and take the final questions of the lesson: honest about the harm.
