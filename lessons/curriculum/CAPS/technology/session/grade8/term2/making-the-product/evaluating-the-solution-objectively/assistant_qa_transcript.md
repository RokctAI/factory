### subtopic_1 — What Evaluation Is and Why It Must Be Objective
**Assistant:** If our model failed two of the seven specifications, will we get a lower mark than a team whose model passed everything?

**Tutor:** Not if your evaluation is better, and it very likely will be. The rubric for this mini-PAT marks the evaluation on how objective, fair, accurate and in-depth it is, not on how many tests the model passed, and a results table with two honest fails, each with a measured number and an observation that explains it, scores higher on every one of those four words than a table of seven passes with no numbers. Consider what the two fails give you. "Walls buckled at 14 containers; rib reached the base only" leads directly to a recommendation, extend the rib to the walls, and connects the evaluation back to the forces topic, which the assessor is looking for. "Base softened at 20 minutes, no leak" leads to a wax lining or a thicker board and to the honest statement that the real material was not tested. A team whose model passed everything has either a very simple model, specifications that were too easy, which the assessor will notice, or tests that were not run hard enough. In real engineering, a prototype that passes every test on the first try is treated with suspicion, not celebration, because it usually means the tests did not probe the design. What does lose marks is a fail that is hidden, explained away without evidence, or recorded without a recommendation. Record the fails, explain them, propose the fix, and if there is time re-test after the fix and record that too; a before-and-after result is the strongest evidence of understanding a portfolio can hold.

### subtopic_2 — Testing Against the Specifications
**Assistant:** How can we evaluate whether the harm is reduced when we only have a model and cannot put it into the taxi rank?

**Tutor:** By estimating with the evidence you already gathered, stating your assumptions, and being clear about what is an estimate and what is a measurement. Your investigation in Week 4 counted containers at the rank or in the drain and found out roughly how many meals the stalls sell a day; those numbers are the base. If the rank serves 400 meals a day in polystyrene and your container replaced all of them, 400 polystyrene containers a day, 2 000 a week, would stop entering the drains; that is arithmetic from your own data. Then ask what replaces them: 400 paperboard containers, of which some fraction still gets dropped. If the return crate collects half, 200 a day go to compost and 200 still reach the street, but paperboard softens and breaks up in weeks rather than persisting for decades, so the drain-blocking harm is reduced in two ways, in number and in duration. Write that chain of reasoning with each assumption labelled: "assumes all stalls switch", "assumes 50 percent return, untested". Then do the small real tests you can: put a scrap of your board in a jar of water for a week and record what happens to it next to a scrap of polystyrene; that is a direct measurement of the persistence claim. Ask three stall owners whether they would switch at forty cents more and record the answer; that tests the "all stalls switch" assumption. The result is an evaluation that says "estimated reduction of up to 2 000 persistent containers a week, depending on uptake, which three of three owners said they would consider at this price", which is objective, honest about its limits and far more convincing than "it will help the environment".

### subtopic_3 — Fair, Accurate and In Depth: Judging the Impact
**Assistant:** Why should we criticise our own solution's footprint? It makes our product look worse.

**Tutor:** Because the brief asked you to reduce a negative impact, and you cannot know whether you did without counting the impact of what you added. A solution that cuts polystyrene in the drains but uses virgin paperboard from a plantation that replaced grassland, bleached with chemicals and trucked 600 kilometres, may still be a net improvement, but it may not be, and the only way to find out is to look. That is what "in depth" means in the rubric, and it is what distinguishes a designer from a salesperson. It also protects your presentation: the audience in Week 9 and the assessor will ask about the footprint, and a team that has already measured and named it answers calmly, while a team that has not is caught out. Looking at your own footprint usually improves the product rather than condemning it. Teams that ask where the board comes from discover that a recycled-content board is available locally; teams that ask what happens at end of life design the return crate or choose an unbleached board that composts faster; teams that ask about energy discover that the pressing is the smallest part and stop worrying about it. The honest comparison to make is between the harm of the old product and the harm of the new one over the same period, with numbers where you have them and labelled estimates where you do not. If the new product wins, say so with the evidence; if it only wins under certain conditions, say what they are and make them recommendations; if it does not win, you have discovered something important and the recommendation is a different route, which is a legitimate and valuable outcome of a design process.
