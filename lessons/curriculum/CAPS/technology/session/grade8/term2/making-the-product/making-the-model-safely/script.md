# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers making the model, prototype or product of the chosen solution safely. The anchor is a Technology room in Pietermaritzburg at the start of a double period: cutting mats on every desk, a hot wire cutter at the teacher's bench, a tray of craft knives signed out by name, and each team's isometric drawing and development pinned above its workspace.

## Subtopic: Planning the Make: Sequence, Materials and Tools

Making begins before anything is cut, with a plan. The plan lists every step in the order it will happen, who does it, what material and tool it needs, and how long it should take. For a ribbed paperboard take-away container the steps might be: transfer the development to the board; score the fold lines; cut the outline; cut the slot; fold and check the fit dry; glue the tabs; fit the rib; test the lid; finish and label. For a drain guard made of wire mesh and a timber frame they are different, but the logic is the same: parts are made before they are assembled, and nothing is glued until the dry fit is right.

The plan is written against the isometric drawing and the development, which are the maker's instructions, and against the specifications, so that each step that affects a specification is marked for a check: after the rib is fitted, stack-test the base; after gluing, fill with water or sand to the 500 gram mark and look for leaks. Materials are listed with quantities and a little spare, since a first cut is often wasted; tools are listed so that they can be signed out together. Time is divided over the two weeks of making with a margin at the end, because evaluation in the second week needs a finished object to test.

A prototype is not the final product. It is made at the scale and in the materials that answer the questions the team has, which may mean full-size in the real material for a container, so that it can hold food and be stacked, but a 1 to 10 scale model in card for a drain guard, so that its shape and fit can be judged without a metre of steel mesh. The plan states what the model is for and what it will not test, and that statement is carried into the evaluation.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a making plan contains, how it links to the drawings and the specifications, and the difference between a prototype and a final product.

## Subtopic: Safe Use of Tools and Materials

Safety in making is a set of habits, not a poster, and the habits are learned by using them on every cut. For cutting tools: a craft knife is held like a pen with the blade angled low and drawn towards the body only along a steel rule, never towards the hand that holds the rule, and the fingers of that hand are kept on top of the rule, well away from its edge; a fresh blade is safer than a dull one because it needs less force and does not slip; cuts are made in several light passes rather than one heavy one; the knife is closed or capped the moment it is set down and never passed open to another person; scissors are passed handles first; and every cut is made on a cutting mat, never on the desk or a lap. Blades go in the sharps tin, never the bin.

For hot tools: the hot wire cutter for foam and the glue gun are used only at the station set up for them, with the cable routed away from the walkway; the hot parts are never touched to find out if they are hot; a glue gun is rested on its stand with the nozzle down on a scrap tile; hot glue burns and sticks, so it is never wiped off skin but cooled under running water first; and foam is cut with a hot wire only in a ventilated room because the fumes are harmful. For adhesives and finishes: solvent glues and spray paints are used with windows open, away from the hot wire, and with hands washed afterwards; PVA and glue sticks are the default for paper and board because they are safe and strong enough.

For the room: bags under desks, walkways clear, one person at a time at the hot station, long hair tied back and sleeves rolled when working with the hot wire or a drill, eye protection whenever wire, foam or wood is cut or when anything brittle could snap, and everything cleaned and returned at the end so that the next class finds the room safe. Any cut, burn or splash is reported to the teacher at once, however small. A learner who is unsure how to use a tool asks before trying it; that is competence, not weakness.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rules for craft knives, the rules for hot tools and adhesives, and the room habits that keep everyone safe.

## Subtopic: Marking Out, Cutting, Forming and Joining

Good technique makes the model match the drawing. Marking out transfers the development onto the material: measurements are taken from the drawing's full-size dimensions, marked with a sharp pencil and a steel rule from a straight reference edge, and checked twice before cutting, since a line is cheap to redraw and a cut is not. Right angles are set with a set square; long lines are ruled against the steel rule, not a plastic one, which the knife will shave. Fold lines are marked differently from cut lines so that no one cuts a fold.

Cutting follows the rules above. Straight cuts are made along the rule in light passes; curves are cut free-hand in short strokes, turning the board rather than the knife; internal slots are cut from the corners inward so the corners stay crisp. Scoring is a shallow cut or a firm press with a blunt tool along a fold line on the outside of the fold, so the board folds cleanly without cracking its face; corrugated board scores best across the flutes with a blunt tool and along the flutes with a shallow knife cut. Forming is bending, curving or shaping the material: card is curved by drawing it over a table edge; wire is bent round a former with pliers, never fingers; thin plastic is formed warm over a mould with the hot air gun used by the teacher.

Joining chooses the method for the materials and the forces. Glued tabs with PVA, pressed and left clamped under a book, for paperboard; hot glue for a quick strong joint on foam or mixed materials, remembering that it is bulky and visible; slots and tabs for joints that must come apart; staples, split pins or wire for mesh and card; small screws, pre-drilled, for timber frames. A joint that will carry a load in the model is placed and shaped with the forces topic in mind: a glued tab under shear is long and wide; a corner under bending gets a triangular gusset; a joint that must resist pulling apart is mechanical, not glued.

The record of the make runs alongside: a photograph at each main step, a note of what went wrong and how it was fixed, and a note of any change from the drawing, which the evaluation will need. Changes are normal; unrecorded changes are the problem.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: cutting before the plan and the dry fit, so a glued part is wrong and the material is wasted. Two: drawing the knife towards the hand on the rule. Three: cutting fold lines instead of scoring them, so the box has four loose panels. Four: changing the design during the make and not writing it down, so the evaluation and the presentation describe an object that no longer exists.

The questions for this section are with you now: marking out from full-size dimensions, scoring versus cutting, choosing a joining method for the forces, and recording the make.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a cutting mat, a knife signed out in your name and a half-folded box — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Plan Before You Cut

Before you cut anything, write the plan. Every step in order: copy the net onto the board, score the folds, cut the outline, cut the slot, fold and test dry, glue the tabs, fit the rib, test the lid, finish and label. Who does each step, which tool, how long. Parts first, then put them together, and never glue until the dry fit is right.

Tie the plan to your rules. After the rib goes in, stack-test. After gluing, fill to 500 grams and watch for leaks. List materials with a little spare, because first cuts go wrong. Split the time over two weeks and leave a gap at the end, because the second week has to test a finished thing.

Your model is not the shop product. Make it at the size and in the material that answers your questions: full size in real board for a food box; 1 to 10 in card for a metre-long drain guard. Write down what the model will and will not test.

Hold the step list in mind and try the questions for this part: plan before you cut.

## Subtopic: Sharp Things, Hot Things, Fingers

Safety is habits, used on every cut. Knife: hold it like a pen, blade low, cut along a steel rule towards you, never towards the hand holding the rule, fingers on top of the rule. Fresh blade, several light passes, cap it the second you put it down, never hand it to someone open. Scissors handles first. Always on a mat. Old blades in the sharps tin.

Hot things: hot wire and glue gun only at their station, cable out of the walkway. Never touch to test. Glue gun on its stand, nozzle on a tile. Hot glue on skin: cold water, do not wipe. Hot wire only with windows open; the fumes are bad. Solvent glue and spray paint: windows open, away from the hot wire, wash hands. PVA and glue sticks are your default.

Room: bags under desks, paths clear, one person at the hot station, hair tied, sleeves up, eyes covered when cutting wire, foam or wood. Clean up fully. Report every cut and burn, however small. If you do not know how to use a tool, ask. Asking is what competent people do.

Hold the fingers on top of the rule in mind and try this part's questions: sharp things, hot things, fingers.

## Subtopic: Measure, Cut, Fold, Glue

Technique. Mark out from the real-size numbers on the drawing with a sharp pencil and a steel rule from one straight edge; check twice, a line is cheap, a cut is not. Set square for right angles. Mark fold lines differently from cut lines so nobody cuts a fold.

Cut straight along the steel rule in light passes. Curves free-hand in short strokes, turning the board not the knife. Slots from the corners inward. Score fold lines, a shallow cut or a firm press with a blunt tool on the outside of the fold, so the board bends without cracking. Form by curving card over a table edge, bending wire round a former with pliers, warming thin plastic over a mould with the teacher's heat gun.

Join for the material and the force. PVA tabs clamped under a book for board. Hot glue for quick strong joints, but it is bulky. Slots and tabs if it must come apart. Staples, split pins, wire for mesh. Pre-drilled screws for timber. A glued tab under shear is long and wide; a corner that bends gets a triangular gusset; a joint that gets pulled apart is mechanical, not glue.

Photograph each main step. Write what went wrong and how you fixed it. Write every change from the drawing. Changes are normal; unwritten changes are the problem.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plan every step against the drawings and the rules, dry fit before glue, and say what the model will test. Knives along the rule away from fingers, hot tools at their station, eyes covered, everything reported. Mark out from full-size numbers, score folds, cut in light passes, join for the force, and record every step and change.

Hold the photo of the half-built box, and take the final questions of the lesson: measure, cut, fold, glue.
