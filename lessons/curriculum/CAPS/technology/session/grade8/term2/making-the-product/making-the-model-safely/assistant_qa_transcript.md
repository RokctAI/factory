### subtopic_1 — Planning the Make: Sequence, Materials and Tools
**Assistant:** Why is the knife drawn towards the body? It feels like the opposite of safe.

**Tutor:** Because the direction of the cut is not where the danger is; the danger is the hand that is not holding the knife, and the rule is built around protecting that hand. When you cut along a steel rule you hold the rule down with one hand and draw the knife with the other, and the blade can only hurt you if it leaves the rule's edge and meets flesh. If you push the knife away from you, your body weight goes behind it, the blade digs in, it is hard to stop, and if it jumps the rule it travels fast into whatever is ahead, which on a crowded desk is often another learner's hand. Drawing the knife towards you with the blade angled low gives the wrist full control, the cut is made by the blade's edge and not by force, and if the blade does slip it slips along the rule's line, away from the hand on the rule, and loses its energy into the mat. The rule that actually protects your fingers is the second one: the hand on the rule has its fingers flat on top of the rule, never curled over its edge where the blade runs, and the cut is always made on the far side of the rule from those fingers. Combine the two and a slipping blade has nowhere to go but the mat. The feeling that "towards me" is wrong comes from kitchen knives, which are used differently; in cutting board and paper along a rule, the controlled draw stroke is the safe one, and it is how every professional cutter works.

### subtopic_2 — Safe Use of Tools and Materials
**Assistant:** Our model is made of corrugated card and the real product would be pressed paperboard. Does that make the model useless as a test?

**Tutor:** It makes it a test of some things and not others, and a good team says which before it tests. Corrugated card and pressed paperboard differ in thickness, in stiffness, in how they fold, and in how they behave when wet and hot, so the card model cannot tell you whether the real container would hold gravy for 30 minutes without softening, and you should not claim it does. But a great deal of what the specifications ask does not depend on the exact material. Whether the shape stacks twenty high without the walls buckling depends on the geometry and the rib, which the card model tests well; whether the lid fits and closes, whether one hand can carry it, whether the slot takes the deposit tag, whether the development folds up without gaps, and whether the overall size is right for a stall's portion are all answered by the card model. The honest approach is to list the specifications, mark each one as "tested by this model", "partly tested" or "not tested, material differs", and for the untested ones, do a small separate test on a sample of the real material, a square of paperboard with hot water on it for 30 minutes, which costs nothing and answers the question directly. This is exactly what industrial prototyping does: a foam model tests shape and handling, a rig tests the mechanism, a material sample tests the material, and no single prototype tests everything. Writing down what your model is for is part of the evaluation mark, and it is what separates a careful engineer from someone who built a box.

### subtopic_3 — Marking Out, Cutting, Forming and Joining
**Assistant:** What do we do when the model does not go together the way the drawing said?

**Tutor:** First, stop gluing; a dry-fit problem is cheap and a glued one is not. Then find out which of three things has happened, because the fix is different for each. One: the drawing is right and the making is wrong, a part cut short, a fold scored on the wrong side, a tab mirrored. Check the part against its dimensions on the drawing; if it is out, re-make that part from the spare material you planned for and carry on. Two: the making is right and the drawing is wrong, a flap that was never long enough, two features that occupy the same space, a lid dimension that ignored the wall thickness. This is common and it is what prototypes are for. Decide the correction with the team, mark it on the drawing in a different colour with the date, and make the corrected part; the drawing and the model must agree at the end, so the drawing is updated, not just the object. Three: both are right and the material behaves differently from what you assumed, the board cracks on the fold, the glue will not hold the plastic, the mesh will not bend to the radius. That is a finding about the material and it goes straight into the evaluation, with a decision: change the technique, score deeper, use a different adhesive, or change the material and record why. In every case, write it in the making record with a photograph, because the evaluation asks what went wrong and how it was solved, and the presentation in Week 9 is far stronger for a team that can say "the lid did not clear the rib; we moved the rib 10 millimetres and here is the drawing we changed" than for one that pretends everything went to plan.
