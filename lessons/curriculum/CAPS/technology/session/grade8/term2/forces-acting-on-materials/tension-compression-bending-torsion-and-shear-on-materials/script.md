# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the five forces that act on materials, tension, compression, bending, torsion and shear, and how different materials respond to each. The anchor is a Grade 8 workshop bench in Kimberley on which a learner has laid out a length of garden wire, a stick of blackboard chalk, a wooden ruler, a strip of corrugated card and a pair of scissors, each one about to demonstrate a different force.

## Subtopic: Five Forces and What They Do to a Material

A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons, and when it acts on a material it tries to change the material's shape. Five kinds of loading matter in Technology. Tension pulls a material so that it stretches and its particles are drawn apart; the garden wire holding up a washing line is in tension. Compression pushes a material so that it shortens and its particles are pressed together; the brick at the bottom of a wall is in compression. Bending happens when a force acts across a member that is supported at its ends or at one end, so that one side of the member stretches and the other side squashes at the same time; a shelf sagging under books is bending. Torsion is twisting, when forces turn one end of a member relative to the other; a screwdriver shaft or a wet towel being wrung out is in torsion. Shear is a pair of forces acting in opposite directions across a material, trying to slide one part past the other; scissors cutting paper, or a bolt holding two plates that are pulled in opposite directions, show shear.

Bending is really tension and compression together. If you draw a line along the middle of a ruler and then bend it, the outer curve gets longer, which is tension, and the inner curve gets shorter, which is compression, while the centre line, called the neutral axis, stays the same length. This is why a beam fails first on its outer surface and why the material in the middle of a solid beam does very little work, an idea that will matter enormously when we look at I-beams.

Torsion is also made of shear. When a shaft twists, every layer slides slightly past the layer next to it, so the material is in shear all around its outside, with the surface doing the most work and the centre almost none.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five forces, an everyday example of each, and why bending and torsion are built from the simpler forces.

## Subtopic: How Materials Respond: Strength, Stiffness and Failure

Materials respond to forces in characteristic ways, and engineers use precise words for the response. Strength is the greatest force, or more exactly stress, a material can carry before it breaks; it must always be named with the force, because tensile strength, compressive strength and shear strength of one material can be very different. Stiffness is how much a material resists being deformed while the force is applied; steel is stiff, rubber is not, and both can be strong. Elasticity is the ability to return to the original shape when the force is removed, up to a limit called the elastic limit; beyond it the material is permanently deformed, which is plasticity. Brittleness describes a material that breaks with little or no plastic deformation and little warning, like glass, chalk and cast iron. Toughness is the opposite, the ability to absorb energy and deform a great deal before breaking, like mild steel, leather and most plastics. Hardness is resistance to scratching and denting at the surface.

The contrast that matters most this term is between concrete and steel. Concrete is very strong in compression and very weak in tension, roughly one tenth as strong, and it is brittle, so a plain concrete beam cracks on its stretched underside at a small load. Steel is strong and tough in both tension and compression. Timber is strong along its grain and weak across it, and splits along the grain under shear. Glass is strong in compression and hard but brittle. Plastics vary: polythene is tough and flexible, polystyrene foam crushes, acrylic shatters.

Failure follows from the mismatch between force and material. A material loaded in a way it resists badly fails first at the weakest point, often at a notch, a hole, a knot or a scratch, because the force concentrates there; this is why a small nick in a cable or a crack in a windscreen spreads. Fatigue is failure after many repeated loads each smaller than the breaking load, which is how a paper clip bent back and forth snaps, and how aircraft parts and bicycle frames are designed to be checked.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meanings of strength, stiffness, elasticity, brittleness and toughness, and the contrasting responses of concrete, steel and timber.

## Subtopic: Testing Forces in the Classroom

Each force can be shown on the bench with household materials, and a good test isolates one force at a time. Tension: hang increasing masses from a thread, a strip of plastic bag and a strand of wool until each breaks, record the breaking mass and note how much each stretched first; the bag stretches a long way and is tough, the thread snaps suddenly. Compression: stand a chalk stick, a wooden dowel and a drinking straw upright and press down with a book; the straw buckles sideways, the chalk crushes, the dowel resists, showing that slender members fail by buckling before they crush. Bending: support a ruler, a strip of card and a strip of corrugated card between two bricks and load the middle with coins; the card sags and creases on its underside, the corrugated card holds much more because its ribs put material far from the neutral axis. Torsion: twist a chalk stick between your fingers until it breaks and look at the fracture, which runs at an angle around the stick, the signature of shear produced by twisting; twist a plastic straw and it kinks instead. Shear: cut card with scissors, or load a paper strip pinned between two pieces of card so the pin tries to slide through the paper.

Fair testing means changing one variable at a time. Specimens being compared must have the same length, width and thickness, be loaded at the same point and rate, and be tested more than once, because a single result may be an accident of a flaw in that specimen. Results go in a table with the force, the material, the dimensions, the breaking load and a note on how it failed, suddenly or gradually, at the middle or at a flaw.

Safety is simple and firm: eye protection when anything brittle may snap, no fingers under a loaded beam, and loads built up gradually so that nothing falls from height. The chalk and the straw are chosen precisely because they fail safely.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a material is "strong" without naming the force; concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension. Two: confusing stiffness with strength; a stiff ruler can snap, a floppy bag can carry a load. Three: testing specimens of different sizes and comparing the results. Four: forgetting that the slender straw buckled rather than crushed, and so calling it weak in compression when the real cause was slenderness.

The questions for this section are with you now: a classroom test for each force, the rules of fair testing, and the signature fracture of torsion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a bench, a stick of chalk and a wet towel — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pull, Squash, Bend, Twist, Cut

Five things a force can do to a piece of material. Pull it: tension, the washing-line wire. Squash it: compression, the bottom brick in a wall. Bend it: a shelf sagging under books. Twist it: torsion, wringing out a wet towel. Cut it: shear, two forces sliding one part past the other, scissors on paper.

Bending is a trick: it is pulling and squashing at the same time. Bend a ruler. The outside curve stretches, the inside curve squashes, and the line down the middle stays the same length. That middle line is called the neutral axis, and it does almost no work. Remember that for I-beams.

Twisting is a trick too: it is shear all round the outside of the shaft. The skin does the work, the core does nearly nothing.

Hold the bent ruler in mind and try the questions for this part: pull, squash, bend, twist, cut.

## Subtopic: Strong Is Not One Thing

"Strong" is not one thing. Strength is how much force before it breaks, and it must say which force: concrete is strong when squashed and weak when pulled, about ten times weaker. Stiffness is how much it bends while the force is on; steel is stiff, rubber is not. Elastic means it springs back; past the elastic limit it stays bent. Brittle means it snaps with no warning: glass, chalk. Tough means it bends and absorbs a lot before breaking: mild steel, leather. Hard means it resists scratches.

So steel is strong both ways and tough. Concrete is strong squashed, weak pulled, brittle. Wood is strong along the grain, weak across it, and splits along the grain. Glass is hard but brittle.

Things break at their weakest spot, a notch, a hole, a knot, a scratch, because the force piles up there. And they break from being bent back and forth many times, which is fatigue, the paper-clip snap.

Hold the paper clip in mind and try this part's questions: strong is not one thing.

## Subtopic: Try It on the Bench

Try each force on the bench. Hang masses from thread, a bag strip and wool until they break; the bag stretches far, the thread snaps. Press a book onto a chalk stick, a dowel and a straw; the straw buckles sideways, the chalk crushes, the dowel holds. Rest a ruler, a card strip and corrugated card between two bricks and pile coins in the middle; the card creases underneath, the corrugated card holds far more. Twist chalk until it breaks and look: the break runs at a slant, the sign of shear. Cut card with scissors: shear.

Test fairly. Same size pieces, same place to load, same speed, more than one try. Write the force, material, size, breaking load and how it broke.

Safety: eyes covered when something brittle may snap, no fingers under a loaded beam, add weight slowly.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Tension pulls, compression squashes, bending stretches one side and squashes the other, torsion twists and is shear all round, shear slides. Strength must name the force; stiffness, elasticity, brittleness and toughness are different things. Concrete is strong squashed and weak pulled; steel is strong both ways; wood depends on grain. Test one force at a time, fairly and safely.

Hold the slanted chalk break, and take the final questions of the lesson: try it on the bench.
