# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers adapting materials to withstand forces, through the case studies of reinforced concrete and plywood. The anchor is a building site in Soweto where a Grade 8 class watches a floor slab being cast: a lattice of ribbed steel bars sits on small plastic spacers inside timber shuttering, and the shuttering itself is made of plywood sheets stamped with a stack of thin layers at their cut edges.

## Subtopic: Why Materials Are Adapted: Composites

Every material has forces it resists well and forces it resists badly, and when the badly-resisted force cannot be avoided, designers adapt the material rather than abandon it. The usual way is to make a composite: two or more materials, or layers of one material, combined so that the whole has properties no part has alone. The principle is to place each component where the force it resists best will act.

Composites are ancient. Mud bricks were reinforced with straw to stop them cracking as they dried; the straw fibres carried tension the mud could not. Bows were laminated from wood, horn and sinew, with horn on the compressed belly and sinew on the stretched back. Daub walls in rural South Africa are mud over a woven lath of sticks, the sticks carrying tension and bending. Today's examples are the same idea with industrial materials: steel in concrete, wood veneer crossed with wood veneer, glass fibre in resin for a boat hull, carbon fibre in resin for a bicycle frame.

Adapting a material is not only about combining. A material may be shaped so that it uses its strength better, which is next session's topic of metal sections; it may be treated, as when steel is hardened by heating and quenching or timber is pressure-treated against rot; or it may be made in a new form, as when wood chips are pressed into chipboard. The common question is always the same: which force will act here, and how does this material fail under it?

The questions for this section are with you now: what a composite is, the principle of placing each material where its strength is needed, and three examples from before the industrial age.

## Subtopic: Reinforced Concrete: Steel Where the Tension Is

Concrete is a mixture of cement, sand, stone and water that sets into an artificial rock. It is cheap, can be poured into any shape, resists fire and weather, and is very strong in compression; a cube of good concrete carries roughly 25 to 40 newtons on every square millimetre before crushing. But in tension it carries only about one tenth of that and fails brittlely by cracking. A beam of plain concrete bends under load, its underside stretches, a crack opens at the bottom and runs upward, and the beam breaks suddenly at a load far below its compressive capacity.

Reinforced concrete solves this by placing steel bars where the tension will be. Steel has a tensile strength around ten to fifteen times that of concrete's compressive strength and is tough, so a small amount of steel carries all the tension a beam needs. In a simply supported beam the bottom is in tension, so the main bars run along the bottom with a few centimetres of concrete below them as cover; the concrete above the neutral axis carries the compression. Where a beam passes over a column and bends the other way, the top is in tension and the bars are placed near the top. Vertical or looped bars called stirrups wrap around the main bars to resist shear near the supports and to hold the cage together. In a column the bars run vertically, and the concrete carries most of the compression while the steel adds strength and stops the column failing suddenly.

The two materials cooperate because of three properties. The ribbed surface of the bars grips the concrete so the two cannot slide past each other. Steel and concrete expand by almost exactly the same amount when heated, so temperature changes do not crack them apart. And the alkaline concrete protects the steel from rusting, provided the cover is thick enough and the concrete is not cracked wide open; rusting steel swells and spalls the concrete off, which is the commonest way reinforced concrete fails in old buildings. Pre-stressed concrete goes a step further: the steel is pulled tight before the concrete sets, so the concrete is squeezed even before load arrives and never goes into tension at all.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plain concrete fails, where the bars go in a beam and over a column, and the three properties that let steel and concrete work together.

## Subtopic: Plywood: Grain Crossed Against Grain

Timber is strong in tension and compression along the grain, because the wood fibres run that way, but weak across the grain, where it splits, and it warps, shrinks and swells differently along and across the grain as it dries or dampens. A wide board of solid wood is therefore weak and unstable in one direction. Plywood adapts timber by slicing a log into thin sheets called veneers, a millimetre or three thick, and gluing an odd number of them, three, five, seven or more, with the grain of each layer at right angles to the layer below. The outside layers have their grain running the same way, which is why the number is odd.

The result is a sheet that is nearly as strong across as along, because in every direction half the layers have their grain running that way. It does not split, because a split in one layer runs into the crossed grain of the next and stops. It barely warps or shrinks, because each layer's tendency to move across its grain is restrained by its neighbours. And it uses the log efficiently, since veneers are peeled from the whole log instead of being sawn with waste. Plywood is used for shuttering on building sites, for floors and roofs, for furniture, boats, skateboards and the models in this course, and in a thick version for the decks of lorries.

Plywood's weakness is the glue. Interior plywood uses a glue that softens when wet, so it delaminates outdoors; exterior and marine plywood use waterproof glues. It is also weak at its edges, where screws can split the outer veneer, so fixings are kept away from the edge and holes are drilled first. Related engineered boards use the same idea in different ways: chipboard and MDF press fibres or chips with resin into a uniform board that has no grain at all but is weaker and swells badly when wet; laminated veneer lumber keeps all the grain running one way for beams; and glulam beams glue solid planks together to make members longer and deeper than any tree.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting the steel in the middle of a concrete beam, where the neutral axis is; the steel must be near the stretched face. Two: thinking the steel carries the compression; the concrete does, and the steel carries the tension. Three: gluing plywood layers with all the grain the same way, which is just a thick board that splits. Four: using interior plywood for a model that will get wet, and watching it delaminate.

The questions for this section are with you now: how plywood is made, the three weaknesses of solid timber it solves, and why the number of layers is odd.

## Subtopic: Two Weak Things Make One Strong Thing

Every material has a force it hates. When you cannot avoid that force, you adapt the material. The usual trick is a composite: put two materials together so that each covers the other's weakness, and put each one exactly where the force it is good at will act.

People did this long before factories. Straw in mud bricks stops them cracking. Sticks woven inside a mud wall carry the pull. Bows were wood, horn and sinew glued in layers. Today it is steel in concrete, wood glued crosswise, glass fibre in a boat.

Adapting can also mean shaping a material cleverly, or heating steel to harden it, or pressing wood chips into board. The question is always the same: which force acts here, and how does this material break under it?

Hold the straw in the mud brick in mind and try the questions for this part: two weak things make one strong thing.

## Subtopic: Steel Bars in the Right Place

Concrete is cheap, pours into any shape, shrugs off fire and rain, and is very strong when squashed. Pull it and it is about ten times weaker and cracks without warning. Bend a plain concrete beam and the underside stretches, a crack opens at the bottom and the beam snaps.

So put steel where the pull is. In a beam resting on two supports, the bottom stretches, so the main bars go near the bottom with a little concrete under them for cover. Over a column the beam bends the other way, so the bars go near the top. Loops called stirrups wrap round the bars near the ends to take shear and hold the cage. The concrete above takes the squash; the steel below takes the pull.

They work together for three reasons. The bars are ribbed, so they grip. Steel and concrete expand the same amount when hot, so they do not crack apart. The concrete shields the steel from rust, as long as the cover is thick and uncracked; rusting steel swells and pops the concrete off.

Hold the cage with the bars at the bottom in mind and try this part's questions: steel bars in the right place.

## Subtopic: Sheets Glued Crosswise

Wood is strong along the grain, weak across it, splits along it, and warps as it dries. Plywood fixes all of that. Peel a log into thin sheets, then glue an odd number of them together with each layer's grain at right angles to the one below. Three, five, seven layers; odd, so both outside faces run the same way.

Now it is nearly as strong across as along, because half the layers always run your way. It does not split, because a split hits crossed grain and stops. It stays flat. And the whole log is used. Shuttering, floors, furniture, boats, skateboards, your model.

Its weak spot is the glue: interior plywood falls apart when wet, so use exterior or marine glue outside. Its edges split if you screw too close; drill first. Chipboard and MDF press chips and fibres with resin into boards with no grain; cheaper, weaker, swell when wet.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A composite puts each material where the force it resists best will act. Concrete carries compression and steel, placed near the stretched face, carries tension; ribs, matching expansion and rust protection make them a team. Plywood glues an odd number of veneers with crossed grain so it is strong both ways, does not split and stays flat.

Hold the stripy edge of the plywood sheet, and take the final questions of the lesson: sheets glued crosswise.
