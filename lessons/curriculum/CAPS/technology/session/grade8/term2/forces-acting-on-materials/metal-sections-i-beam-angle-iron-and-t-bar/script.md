# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers selecting metal sections, the I-beam, angle iron and T-bar, to withstand forces while saving material. The anchor is a steel merchant's yard in Germiston where a Grade 8 class walks between racks of lengths stacked by shape, each rack labelled with a letter that is also a cross-section: I, L, T, C, and round and square tubes.

## Subtopic: Shape Carries Load: Why Sections Exist

A member's ability to carry load depends not only on how much material it contains but on where that material is placed in its cross-section. The clearest demonstration is a steel ruler. Laid flat across two supports it sags under a few coins; stood on edge it carries a heavy book without visible bending. The amount of steel is identical. What changed is the depth of the section in the direction of bending.

The reason comes from last week's neutral axis. In bending, the fibres furthest from the neutral axis are stretched or squashed the most and carry the most load, while material near the neutral axis is almost idle. A deep section places material far from the neutral axis, where it works hard; a shallow section leaves it near the axis, where it does little. Doubling the depth of a rectangular beam makes it about four times as stiff in bending and twice as strong; doubling the width only doubles both. Depth is therefore the cheapest way to strength, which is why floor joists stand on edge and never lie flat.

The same logic says that the material near the neutral axis can be removed with little loss. A solid rectangular steel bar has most of its weight in the middle doing almost nothing. If that middle is thinned to a web and the material concentrated into two flanges at the top and bottom, the member keeps most of its bending strength at a fraction of the weight and cost. That is the I-beam, and the same reasoning produces the hollow tube, the channel and the corrugated sheet. Shaping a section is adapting a material to withstand forces, exactly as reinforcing concrete is, and the payoff is measured in saved steel: a rolled I-section can carry the same bending load as a solid bar of three or four times its mass.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a ruler on edge is stiffer than one laid flat, the role of the neutral axis, and why material in the middle of a beam can be removed.

## Subtopic: The I-Beam, the Angle and the T-Bar

Steel is hot-rolled into standard sections, each a long member with the same cross-section along its whole length, and each named by the letter its cross-section resembles. Three appear everywhere in South African construction and in this course's design work.

The I-beam, also called a universal beam or H-section depending on its proportions, has a vertical web joining two horizontal flanges. It is the section for bending about one axis: the flanges, far from the neutral axis, carry tension and compression, and the web carries shear and keeps the flanges apart. It is the standard for floor beams, bridge girders, crane rails and the lintels over wide openings. Its weakness is bending about its other axis and torsion, because the thin flanges and web twist easily; long I-beams are therefore braced sideways.

Angle iron, the L-section, has two legs at right angles, usually of equal length. It is stiff in two directions at once, is easy to bolt or weld along either leg, and fits into corners. It is the section for frames, brackets, gates, bracing members in trusses and the legs of water-tank stands. Its weakness is that its centre of mass sits inside the corner, so a single angle twists and bends unevenly under heavy load; angles are often paired back to back.

The T-bar, the T-section, has one flange and a stem. It behaves like half an I-beam and is used where one face must be flat, as a lintel, a stiffener welded onto a plate, a rail, or a member whose flange carries a floor or a sheet. In reinforced concrete the same idea appears as the T-beam, where a floor slab acts as the flange of the beam below it.

Other common sections follow the same logic. The channel, the C-section, is an I-beam with one side of the flanges removed, used for purlins and door frames. The hollow square and round tubes carry compression and torsion best of all, because their material is evenly far from the centre in every direction, which is why scaffolding, bicycle frames and the braces of the Term 1 pylon are tubes. Flat bar and round bar are the plain sections used where bending is small, for ties in tension and for welding into other shapes.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of an I-beam and the forces each carries, where angle iron and T-bar are used, and why tubes resist torsion so well.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Section for a Force and Saving Material

Selecting a section is a design decision made in three steps. First name the force: is the member mainly in bending, in tension, in compression, in torsion, or in a combination? Second match the section to the force: bending about one axis wants depth in that direction, so an I-beam or T-bar; compression wants material far from the centre in all directions so the member does not buckle, so a tube or a pair of angles; torsion wants a closed tube; pure tension needs no particular shape, so a flat or round bar, chosen only for its area and for how it will be fixed at the ends. Third check the connections and the practical constraints: can the section be bolted or welded where it meets the others, does it fit the space, is it a standard size the merchant stocks, and does it leave a flat face where a sheet or a floor must sit?

Saving material is the second half of the decision. Steel is sold by mass, and the shaped section wins because it concentrates material where the force is. A gate frame made of 40 by 40 angle iron weighs a fraction of one made of solid 40 by 40 bar and is nearly as stiff. A warehouse roof carried on I-beams instead of solid bars would otherwise be impossibly heavy and expensive. The saving continues into transport, foundations and the energy used to make the steel, so a well-chosen section is also an environmental decision, which connects this topic to the term's theme of reducing the negative impact of technology.

The principle applies to every material, not only steel. A cardboard box is strong because its corrugated board is a tiny I-beam repeated a thousand times. A bicycle frame of thin tubes outperforms a solid rod of the same mass. Aluminium window frames are hollow extrusions. And in the models you will build this term, a strut folded from card into an L or a square tube will carry many times what the same card carries flat. Shape is free strength.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: choosing a section by its size alone, without naming the force it must carry. Two: laying an I-beam on its side, so the flanges are in the plane of bending and the depth is lost. Three: using a single thin angle for a long compression member and watching it twist and buckle. Four: assuming the heaviest section is the strongest, when a lighter but deeper section would carry more.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three steps of selecting a section, the match between each force and its best section, and how shaped sections save material.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a steel ruler and a rack of beams in a merchant's yard — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Flat Beats Nothing, On Edge Beats Flat

Take a steel ruler. Lay it flat across two books and it sags under a few coins. Stand it on edge and it holds a heavy book. Same steel. What changed? Depth in the direction of bending.

Why: when a beam bends, the top and bottom do all the work, stretched and squashed, and the middle line does almost nothing. A deep shape puts the metal far from the middle, where it works. Double the depth and you get about four times the stiffness; double the width and you only get twice.

So the metal in the middle is mostly wasted. Thin it to a web, pile the metal into a top and bottom flange, and you have an I-beam: nearly as strong, a third of the weight. The same trick gives you hollow tubes and corrugated card. Shape is free strength.

Hold the ruler on its edge in mind and try the questions for this part: flat beats nothing, on edge beats flat.

## Subtopic: Three Shapes You See on Every Site

Steel is rolled into long shapes named by the letter they look like end-on.

I-beam: a web standing up between two flanges. Flanges take the stretch and squash, web takes the shear and holds them apart. Floor beams, bridge girders, lintels over wide doors. Weak if bent sideways or twisted, so long ones are braced.

Angle iron, the L: two legs at right angles. Stiff two ways, easy to bolt or weld, fits in corners. Gates, frames, brackets, truss bracing, tank-stand legs. A single angle twists under big loads, so they are often paired back to back.

T-bar: one flange and a stem, half an I-beam. Where one face must be flat: lintels, stiffeners, rails. A concrete floor slab with its beam is a giant T.

Also: the channel, a C; and hollow square and round tubes, best for squashing and twisting because the metal is spread evenly far from the centre. Scaffolding and bicycle frames are tubes.

Hold the three letters in mind and try this part's questions: three shapes you see on every site.

## Subtopic: Pick the Shape for the Job

Choosing a section, three steps. Name the force: bend, pull, squash, twist, or a mix. Match the shape: bending wants depth, an I or T; squashing wants metal spread all round so it does not buckle, a tube or paired angles; twisting wants a closed tube; pure pulling needs only enough area, a flat or round bar. Check the fit: can you bolt or weld it, does it fit the space, does the merchant stock it, does it give a flat face where you need one?

Then save material. Steel is sold by the kilogram, and the shaped section puts the metal only where the force is. An angle-iron gate weighs a fraction of a solid-bar gate and is nearly as stiff. Less steel means less transport, smaller foundations, less energy, which is this term's theme of reducing harm.

It works in every material: a cardboard box is thousands of tiny I-beams; a card strut folded into an L or a tube carries many times what flat card does.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Depth beats width because the fibres far from the neutral axis do the work. I-beams for bending, angles for frames and corners, T-bars where one face must be flat, tubes for compression and torsion. Name the force, match the shape, check the fit, and save steel.

Hold the folded card strut, and take the final questions of the lesson: pick the shape for the job.
