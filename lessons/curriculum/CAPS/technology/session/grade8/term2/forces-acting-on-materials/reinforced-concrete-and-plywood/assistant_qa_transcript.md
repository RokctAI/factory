### subtopic_1 — Why Materials Are Adapted: Composites
**Assistant:** Why not just make the whole beam out of steel, if steel is strong in both tension and compression?

**Tutor:** Steel beams are used, and in many buildings they are the right choice, but reinforced concrete wins on several counts for ordinary buildings in South Africa. Cost is the first: steel is many times more expensive per kilogram than concrete, and a concrete beam uses a small amount of steel exactly where it is needed and cheap concrete everywhere else, so it carries the same load for far less money. Fire is the second: a bare steel beam loses most of its strength at around 500 degrees Celsius and sags in a building fire, while the concrete around reinforcing steel insulates it for an hour or more, which is why fire regulations favour concrete for floors and stairs. Shape is the third: concrete is poured, so a beam, a column, a slab and a staircase can be cast as one continuous piece with no joints, in any shape the shuttering allows, whereas steel comes in standard sections that must be cut, welded and bolted. Durability and maintenance are the fourth: steel must be painted and inspected against rust, while concrete with sound cover protects its own steel for decades. Steel's advantages are speed, lightness and long spans, which is why factories, warehouses and tall towers often use it. The engineer's job is to choose, and the choice usually comes down to money, fire and span.

### subtopic_2 — Reinforced Concrete: Steel Where the Tension Is
**Assistant:** If the concrete in the bottom of the beam is in tension and cracks anyway, what is it doing there? Why not leave it out?

**Tutor:** It is true that below the neutral axis the concrete does little to carry load; in fact engineers assume it has cracked and ignore its tensile strength when they design the beam. But it does four other jobs that cannot be left out. It holds the steel bars in position and bonds to their ribs along their whole length, so that the force in the steel is transferred into the compression zone above through the concrete; without that bond the bars would simply pull through. It provides the cover that protects the steel from rust and from fire; exposed bars in a damp climate rust within a few years, swell, and burst the surrounding concrete. It carries shear, the force that tries to slide the ends of the beam past the supports, together with the stirrups. And it gives the beam its stiffness, so that the floor does not sag and bounce under footsteps even when it is strong enough not to break. The cracks that do form in service are hairline and harmless, held tight by the steel, which is why you see fine lines on the underside of old concrete bridges that are perfectly safe. Where designers do want to remove idle concrete, they use a T-beam or a ribbed slab, which keeps a thin web around the bars and a wide flange on top for compression; that is exactly the logic of the next session on sections.

### subtopic_3 — Plywood: Grain Crossed Against Grain
**Assistant:** Plywood is made of wood, so how can it be stronger than the solid wood it came from?

**Tutor:** It is not stronger than solid wood along the grain, and a good plywood sheet carries somewhat less along its face grain than a solid board of the same thickness would, because half its layers are running the wrong way for that direction. What it does is trade a little of the best-direction strength for a very large gain in the worst direction and in stability. Solid wood across the grain is weak, perhaps one twentieth of its strength along the grain, and it splits at a knock. Plywood across the grain has half its layers running that way and is roughly as strong as along; the sheet's weakest direction has been raised twenty-fold while its strongest has been lowered a little. For most uses, a floor, a shelf, a boat hull, a shuttering panel, loads come from several directions and a split is the usual failure, so the trade is overwhelmingly worth it. The other gain is that a sheet of plywood can be a metre wide and flat, which solid wood cannot be without warping and cupping. Engineers call this isotropy, similar properties in every direction, and plywood is the simplest way to make an anisotropic material, one with a strong direction and a weak one, behave almost isotropically. The same idea drives carbon fibre layups in bicycles and aircraft, where fibre layers are laid at different angles for the same reason.
