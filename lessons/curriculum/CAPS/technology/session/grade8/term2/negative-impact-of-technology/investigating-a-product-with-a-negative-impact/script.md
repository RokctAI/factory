# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating a technological product with a negative impact on society or the environment and discussing solutions that counteract that impact. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Polokwane standing around a table of ordinary objects, a disposable nappy, a cell phone, a two-litre cold-drink bottle, a packet of two-minute noodles, a car tyre and a cigarette lighter, each team choosing one to investigate for the rest of the term. The governing idea is that every technological product carries costs alongside its benefits, that a fair investigation traces those costs through the product's life from raw material to disposal and across people, society and the environment, and that solutions are found by attacking the harm at the stage where it arises. Carrying out a structured investigation of a product's impact and proposing and comparing counteracting solutions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Technology Has Costs as Well as Benefits

Technology exists to solve problems, and the products of this term, plastics, packaging, recycled board, have all solved real ones. But every product also uses resources, consumes energy, produces waste and changes how people live, and some of those changes are harmful. The negative impact of technology is the set of costs a product imposes that its designers did not intend or did not weigh: pollution, waste, damage to health, loss of jobs or skills, injustice in who bears the costs, and effects on the climate.

Impacts are usually sorted into three groups. Impact on the environment: the raw materials taken from mines, forests and oil wells; the energy and water used in manufacture; the pollution released into air, water and soil; the waste left when the product is finished with; the effect on plants, animals and climate. Impact on society: who gains and who loses, including jobs created and destroyed, health effects on users and on the communities near factories and dumps, changes to how people live, and whether costs fall unfairly on the poor. Impact on the individual: safety, health, dependence, cost and convenience for the person using the product.

A fair assessment holds benefits and costs together. The cell phone connects millions of South Africans who never had a landline, lets small traders take payments and lets a learner look up a word, which is an enormous positive impact; it also contains metals mined in conflict zones, uses rare materials, is replaced every two or three years and becomes toxic electronic waste that is mostly not recycled. The disposable nappy frees parents from hours of washing and keeps babies drier and healthier; it also uses plastics and wood pulp, takes centuries to break down and fills landfills at a rate of thousands of tonnes a day. Neither product is simply good or bad; each has a balance that a technologist must understand before trying to improve it.

The question that starts every investigation is therefore not "is this product bad?" but "where in this product's life does it do harm, to whom, and how much?" Only when that is answered can a solution be aimed at the right place.

The questions for this section are with you now: what negative impact means, the three groups of impact, and why benefits and costs are weighed together.

## Subtopic: Investigating One Product in Depth

The investigation follows a method so that nothing is missed and the findings can be compared. First, choose and describe the product: what it is, what it is made of, who uses it and what need it meets. Second, trace its life cycle in stages: raw materials, manufacture, transport and sale, use, and disposal. Third, at each stage, ask the three impact questions: what does this do to the environment, to society and to the individual? Fourth, gather evidence for each answer, from the product and its packaging, from observation of how it is used and discarded in your own area, from books, reliable websites and news reports, and from interviews with users or people who deal with the waste. Fifth, decide which impacts are the most serious, by how many people or how much environment they affect, how long they last and whether they can be reversed. Sixth, record all of this in a table and a short written summary.

Take the two-litre cold-drink bottle. Raw material: PET plastic made from oil, non-renewable, with carbon dioxide released in refining. Manufacture: energy to mould bottles and to make the sugary drink; water use at the bottling plant. Transport: heavy liquid trucked across the country, fuel and emissions. Use: the drink itself contributes to obesity and tooth decay, a health impact on individuals; the bottle is convenient and resealable, a benefit. Disposal: South Africa recycles a growing share of PET through buy-back centres, which supports waste pickers, a social benefit, but much is still littered, blocking drains and reaching the sea, and a PET bottle in a dump persists for centuries. Weighing it, the most serious impacts are the health effect of the contents and the litter and landfill burden of the bottle.

Evidence turns opinion into findings. Counting the bottles in a stretch of river bank after a weekend, photographing a dump site, reading the recycling rate published by the industry body, asking a buy-back centre what they pay per kilogram, and checking the sugar content on the label are all evidence a Grade 8 team can collect in a week. Each piece is recorded with its source and date.

The method applies to any product. For the cell phone the heaviest impacts cluster at raw materials and disposal; for the nappy at disposal; for the tyre at disposal and at the dust and noise of use; for the noodle packet at the health effect of the contents and the unrecyclable laminated wrapper. The table shows at a glance where the harm is concentrated, which is exactly what the next step needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six steps of the investigation, the life-cycle stages, and the cold-drink bottle as a worked example.

## Subtopic: Discussing Solutions That Counteract the Harm

Once the investigation shows where a product's harm is concentrated, the class discusses solutions. A solution that counteracts a negative impact is any change that reduces the harm while keeping as much of the benefit as possible. Solutions come in types, and knowing the types helps the discussion produce more than one idea.

Change the material: replace the harmful material with one that does the same job with less damage, such as a bagasse box for polystyrene or a bioplastic that composts where composting exists. Change the design: redesign the product so that it uses less material, lasts longer, can be repaired, or comes apart for recycling, such as a phone with a replaceable battery or a bottle with a tethered cap that is not lost as litter. Change the use: reuse the product many times, as with refillable bottles and washable nappies, or share it. Change the end of life: build collection and recycling systems, deposit schemes that pay for returned bottles, or take-back schemes for electronics. Change the rules: regulations, levies and bans, as South Africa did with plastic bags. Change behaviour: education and campaigns that persuade users to recycle, to buy less or to choose better. Most real improvements combine several.

A class discussion works when it is structured. Each team presents its product and the stage where its harm is worst. The class proposes solutions, one per type if possible, and for each asks three questions: does it reduce the harm, does it keep the benefit, and is it practical here, in cost, in the systems that exist and in what people will actually do. A deposit scheme for bottles reduces litter and keeps the drink, and it works in several countries, but it needs shops to handle returns; a ban on cold drinks would remove the harm and the benefit together and would not be accepted. Writing the solutions on the board in a table with the three questions as columns makes the comparison visible and fair.

The outcome of this session is a decision: each team chooses one product, records its investigation, and selects the negative impact it will design against in the coming weeks, when the term's second mini-PAT asks you to adapt a material or design a product that reduces that impact. The investigation table and the discussion notes go into the portfolio as the Investigate step.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: investigating only the disposal stage; harm may be worst at raw materials or in use. Two: condemning a product without weighing its benefits; the solution must keep the benefit. Three: proposing a single solution; the types give at least six directions. Four: ignoring practicality; a solution that nobody will use counteracts nothing.

The questions for this section are with you now: the types of solution, the three questions for judging a solution, and how the discussion feeds the mini-PAT.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a table of ordinary objects and a class arguing about them — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Every Gain Has a Price

Every product solves a problem and makes a new one. Plastic saved lives and filled the oceans. That is the negative impact of technology: the costs nobody meant to cause.

Sort the costs into three piles. Environment: what it takes from the earth, what it pollutes, what it leaves behind, what it does to the climate. Society: who gains, who loses, whose jobs, whose health, who gets stuck with the waste. The individual: is it safe, healthy, affordable for the person using it.

Hold the good and the bad together. A cell phone connects millions and lets a trader take payments; it also uses mined metals and becomes toxic waste every two years. A disposable nappy saves hours of washing; it also sits in a dump for centuries. Not good, not bad: a balance.

So the first question is never "is it bad?" It is "where does it do harm, to whom, how much?"

Keep the phone and the nappy in mind and try the questions for this part: every gain has a price.

## Subtopic: Picking Apart One Product

Investigate one product with six steps. Say what it is, what it is made of, who uses it. Follow its life: raw materials, making, transport, use, throwing away. At each stage ask: environment, society, individual? Collect evidence: look at the product, watch how it is used and dumped near you, read reliable sources, ask people. Decide which harms are biggest and last longest. Put it all in a table.

The two-litre cold drink: made from oil; moulded and filled with energy and water; trucked heavy; the drink rots teeth and adds weight, though the bottle is handy; some is recycled, which pays waste pickers, but much ends up in drains and dumps for centuries. Biggest harms: the sugar inside and the litter outside.

Evidence you can get in a week: count bottles on a river bank, photograph a dump, read the recycling rate, ask a buy-back centre the price per kilo, read the sugar on the label. Write where each fact came from.

Hold the river bank count in mind and try this part's questions: picking apart one product.

## Subtopic: What Could Be Done About It

Now, what could be done? A solution cuts the harm and keeps the benefit. There are six kinds.

Change the material. Change the design so it uses less, lasts longer, can be fixed or taken apart. Change the use: refill, reuse, share. Change the end: collection, recycling, deposits for returned bottles. Change the rules: levies, bans. Change behaviour: campaigns, education.

Discuss each one with three questions. Does it cut the harm? Does it keep the benefit? Will it actually work here, in cost and in what people will do? A bottle deposit: yes, yes, if shops take returns. Banning cold drinks: cuts the harm, kills the benefit, nobody would accept it.

Then each team chooses its product and the one harm it will design against for the rest of the term.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Products have costs to the environment, society and individuals as well as benefits. Investigate one product through its life cycle with evidence and find where the harm is worst. Solutions change the material, design, use, end of life, rules or behaviour, and are judged on cutting harm, keeping benefit and being practical.

Hold the six kinds of fix, and take the final questions of the lesson: what could be done about it.
