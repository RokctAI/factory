### subtopic_1 — Technology Has Costs as Well as Benefits
**Assistant:** Isn't it unfair to blame a product like a nappy for harm when parents have no real choice?

**Tutor:** It would be unfair to blame the parents, and a good investigation does not; it examines the product and the system around it, which is a different thing. The disposable nappy delivers a real and large benefit: it saves carers, mostly women, hours of washing every day, it keeps babies drier and reduces rashes and infections, and in homes without running water it may be the only hygienic option. Any honest investigation records that benefit first. The negative impact is also real: a baby uses several thousand nappies before being trained, each a mix of wood pulp, plastics and absorbent gel that persists in a landfill for centuries, and in many areas the nappies are not even reaching landfill but are dumped in rivers and open spaces, where they are a health hazard. The point of the investigation is not to decide who is guilty but to find where in the product's life the harm arises and who is best placed to reduce it. Here the harm is at disposal, and the people best placed are not the parents but the manufacturers, municipalities and designers: nappies could be made with compostable materials, collection schemes could be set up, as some municipalities have tried, and alternatives such as modern washable nappies could be made affordable and supported with laundering services where water is available. Blaming users for a problem designed into a product is a common mistake, and it leads to solutions that do not work, such as campaigns telling poor families to buy more expensive products. The technologist's habit is to ask what the design and the system could do differently, and to keep the benefit for the people who depend on it while removing as much of the cost as possible.

### subtopic_2 — Investigating One Product in Depth
**Assistant:** How do we know which impact is the most serious when they are so different? Tooth decay and ocean plastic cannot be compared.

**Tutor:** They cannot be measured on one scale, which is why the method asks for a judgement rather than a calculation, and gives criteria to make that judgement defensible. Four criteria are commonly used. How many people, or how much of the environment, are affected: a harm to millions outweighs a harm to a few. How severe the harm is: death and permanent disability weigh more than inconvenience. How long it lasts: a plastic bottle persisting for centuries is a longer harm than a smell that disperses in a day. Whether it can be reversed: a species driven extinct or a river poisoned for decades is worse than a recoverable loss. Applying these to the cold-drink bottle, the health impact of sugary drinks affects millions of South Africans, contributes to diabetes and obesity that are severe and long lasting, and is partly reversible by changing diet, so it scores high; the litter and landfill impact affects whole river systems and the sea, is moderately severe to wildlife and drains, lasts for centuries and is barely reversible, so it also scores high. Both deserve attention, and a team might honestly choose either, as long as it states its reasons. What the criteria rule out is the shallow answer, such as choosing the impact that is most visible or most talked about without asking how large or lasting it is. Different teams will weigh the criteria differently, and a class discussion that compares their reasoning is more valuable than agreement. In the exam, a question about seriousness is answered by naming the criteria and applying them, not by asserting a verdict, and marks go to the reasoning.

### subtopic_3 — Discussing Solutions That Counteract the Harm
**Assistant:** Why does the discussion insist on a solution being practical here? A good idea is a good idea.

**Tutor:** Because an idea that cannot be carried out in the place where the harm occurs counteracts nothing, and technology is judged by what happens, not by what sounds good. Consider three solutions to bottle litter that are good ideas somewhere. Compostable bottles are a good idea where industrial composting exists, but South Africa has almost none, so a compostable bottle here would end up in the same drain as a PET one and would also contaminate the PET recycling stream that does exist. A deposit scheme, where shoppers pay extra and get it back on returning the bottle, works superbly in Germany and several other countries, but here it needs shops to accept returns, a way to handle the deposits and protection against fraud, so it is a real option that would need to be designed for local conditions, which is itself a technology task. A campaign urging people to recycle is cheap, but in a township with no kerbside collection and a buy-back centre an hour's walk away, it asks people to do what the system makes almost impossible, so it changes little. Practicality has three parts: cost, whether the money exists and who pays; systems, whether the collection, processing or enforcement needed is actually in place or could be built; and behaviour, whether people will do what the solution requires given their circumstances. A solution that fails any one of these in the place it is meant for is not yet a solution, however elegant. This is why the mini-PAT asks you to design for a specific negative impact in your own context, and why the best designs in past years have been small, local and workable, such as a collection point at the school or a product made from the local waste stream, rather than grand schemes that would need a government to adopt them.
