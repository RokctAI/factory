### subtopic_1 — What a Design Presentation Must Do
**Assistant:** Why should we spend the longest part of the presentation on the evaluation, including the failures? Surely the model is the exciting part.

**Tutor:** The model is what the audience looks at, but the evaluation is what they decide on, and the rubric weights it accordingly. A model shows that the team can make something; the evaluation shows that the team knows whether the thing works, how well, in what conditions, and what to do next, which is the knowledge a client is paying for. A funder who is shown a beautiful container and told "it works" has learned nothing they can act on; one who is told "holds 520 grams of hot water for 30 minutes with the base softening but not leaking, stacks to fourteen before the walls buckle because the rib only reaches the base, which we would fix by extending it, and three of three stall owners said they would consider it at forty cents more" can decide whether to fund the next prototype. The failures are the most valuable part of that account because they are the parts where the team learned something that nobody knew before the model was built; the passes mostly confirm what the sketches already suggested. Giving the failures time also changes the question period. If the team has already said "the walls buckled at fourteen and here is why", the inevitable question about stacking becomes a chance to show the fix rather than an ambush. And there is a simple assessment reason: the rubric asks whether the team evaluated objectively, fairly, accurately and in depth, and the only way to show depth in five minutes is to spend ninety seconds on it. Hold the model up during that ninety seconds so the exciting object and the serious thinking arrive together.

### subtopic_2 — Structuring the Presentation Around the Design Process
**Assistant:** One member of our team is very shy and does not want to speak. Can the rest of us cover their part?

**Tutor:** You can arrange the presentation so that the shy member's part is small and well supported, but every member should speak, for two reasons that are in their interest as well as the team's. The first is assessment: the rubric assesses each learner's communication, and a member who does not speak cannot be marked on it; the mark for communication is theirs to lose, not the team's to protect. The second is that the fear is almost always of improvising in front of people, and a design presentation has very little improvising in it. Give the shy member the part they know best from having done it, say the making, because first-hand knowledge is the strongest cure for nerves; give them the model to hold, because hands that are holding something do not shake as visibly and the audience looks at the object rather than the speaker; write their forty-five seconds on a card in short lines; and rehearse their part more times than anyone else's, first with the team only, then with one other team. Plan the handover so that a confident member introduces them, "Thabo built the model; he will show you what changed", which gets them the first second for free. In the questions, agree beforehand that questions about the make go to them, but that a teammate may add a sentence if they stall, and that "I would need to check that" is a complete answer. Most shy learners find that the second rehearsal is far easier than the first and the real presentation easier than either, because by then the words belong to them. A team that covers for a member denies them that.

### subtopic_3 — Delivering It: Visuals, Voice and Questions
**Assistant:** What if the stall owner or the teacher asks a question we have no answer to?

**Tutor:** Then you give the honest answer, which is a real answer and is marked as one: you say that you do not know, you say what you would do to find out, and if you can, you say what you expect and why. "We did not test how the board behaves in the rainy season. We would store a stack of containers in a damp room for a week and re-run the stacking test. We expect the walls to soften, so a thin wax coating might be needed, which would add to the cost." That answer shows the questioner three things a guess cannot: that you understood the question, that you know how your own methods extend to it, and that you can reason about the likely result from what you do know. It also keeps your credibility for the answers you are sure of; a team that is caught guessing one number is doubted on every number afterwards. Some questions are really suggestions in disguise, "have you thought of using banana leaves?", and the right response is to treat them as a recommendation: "we had not; it would be worth testing against the 30-minute specification." Some questions are outside the brief, and you may say so politely: "our brief was the rank; a school tuck shop would have bins and water, so the returnable container might win there." What you never do is argue, bluff, or let the most confident member invent a figure. Write down any question you could not answer; if the portfolio is still open, add a line to the recommendations, which turns a hard moment in the presentation into evidence of depth.
