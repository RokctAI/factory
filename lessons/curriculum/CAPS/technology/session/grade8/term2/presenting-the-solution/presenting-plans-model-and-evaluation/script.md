# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers presenting the plans, model and evaluation of the solution. The anchor is a Grade 8 classroom in Nelspruit rearranged for presentations: a table at the front with a team's paperboard container and return crate, their isometric drawing and development taped to the board behind, the specification table on a flip chart, and three rows of classmates, a teacher with a rubric and a visiting stall owner from the local rank.

## Subtopic: What a Design Presentation Must Do

A design presentation has one purpose: to let people who did not do the work understand the problem, judge the evidence and decide whether the solution deserves support. Everything in it serves that purpose. It is not a performance of how hard the team worked, nor a sales pitch that hides the weaknesses; it is the portfolio spoken aloud, in order, with the model in hand.

Three audiences are in the room at once, and a good presentation speaks to all of them. The assessor is marking against the rubric and needs to see each step of the design process named and evidenced: the investigation and its findings, the brief and specifications, the two ideas and the choice, the drawings, the making and the evaluation. The class is learning from the team's experience and needs to understand what was tried and what happened, including what went wrong. The client, real or imagined, in this case a stall owner, needs to know what the solution would cost, what it would change for them, and why they should trust it. The presentation that works gives each of them what they need without repeating itself: the process steps carry the evidence, the honest account of problems teaches the class, and the numbers and the model persuade the client.

What makes an audience trust a presentation is evidence, specificity and candour. Evidence: the count from the drain, the photograph of the dump, the mass on the scale, the result in the table. Specificity: "forty cents more per meal" rather than "a bit more expensive"; "buckled at fourteen" rather than "quite strong". Candour: the test that failed and what the team would do about it, said before anyone asks. A team that names its own weaknesses controls the conversation about them; a team that hides them loses the room the moment a question exposes them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of a design presentation, the three audiences and what each needs, and the three things that earn trust.

## Subtopic: Structuring the Presentation Around the Design Process

The structure is the design process itself, which is the structure the audience already expects and the one the rubric follows. Five minutes divides roughly like this. Thirty seconds: the problem, told as a story from the investigation. Not "our topic was negative impact" but "the stalls at the rank sell about 400 meals a day in polystyrene, and we counted 63 containers in one storm drain on a Monday morning; they do not break down, and the municipality clears that drain every month." One slide or one photograph, the drain.

Forty-five seconds: the brief and the specifications. The brief read aloud in its problem form, then the four or five specifications that mattered most, each with its reason: holds a hot meal 30 minutes, because that is how long customers carry it; stacks twenty, because that is the storage box; fifty cents, because that is the margin. The specification table on the flip chart, not read in full.

Sixty seconds: the two ideas and the choice. Both sketches held up, named in a sentence each, then the comparison in two sentences: the returnable container cut more waste but needed water the rank does not have; the paperboard container works today and borrowed the return crate. This is where the class learns most, so the reasoning is given, not just the result.

Forty-five seconds: the drawings and the make. The isometric and the development on the board, pointed to, with one sentence each on what they are for; then the model handed to the front row, and two sentences on the making: what was planned, what changed and why, with one photograph of the moment it went wrong and the moment it was fixed.

Ninety seconds: the evaluation. The results table, with the passes stated briefly and the fails stated fully with their cause and the recommendation that follows from each. Then the return to the brief: the estimated reduction in containers reaching the drain, with its assumptions, the benefit kept, the footprint of the new material named. Thirty seconds: the conclusion and recommendations, numbered, then "we are happy to take questions."

Each team member speaks, and the handovers are planned; a member who built the model talks about the make, a member who ran the tests talks about the evaluation, because first-hand accounts are the most convincing. Notes are on cards, not read from a page, and the model, the drawings and the table do the remembering.

## Subtopic: Delivering It: Visuals, Voice and Questions

Visuals carry a design presentation because the work was visual. The model is the first visual: it is held up at the start so the audience knows what the talk is about, passed round during the making section, and held again during the evaluation while the tests are described, so that "buckled at fourteen" is said with a finger on the rib. The drawings are the second: the isometric to show the shape, the development to show how it is made, each pointed to and named, never described in words that the drawing already shows. Tables and photographs are the third: the specification table and the results table on a flip chart or slide in large text, no more than seven rows visible at once, the fails highlighted; two or three photographs, the drain, the failed stack, the fixed rib. A slide with a paragraph of text is read by the audience instead of listened to and is not used.

Voice and body are simple matters of practice. Stand so the audience can see the model and the drawings, not in front of them. Speak to the back row at the speed of normal conversation, and pause at each handover. Hold the model steady and point with the other hand. Look at the audience, not the floor or the board. Rehearse the whole presentation twice with a timer, once in front of another team, who ask the questions they would ask the real team; cut whatever makes it run over, because a presentation that is stopped at five minutes loses its evaluation, which is the part that scores most.

Questions are where the presentation is won. Listen to the whole question, repeat it in a few words so everyone heard it, answer the question that was asked, briefly, with evidence, and if the team does not know, say so and say how it would find out: "we did not test that; we would put a scrap of the board in water for a week and compare it with polystyrene." Never argue with the questioner, never guess a number, and never let one member answer everything; the member who did that part answers. The stall owner's question, "would I really pay forty cents more?", is answered with the three owners' responses from the evaluation, not with hope.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reading the portfolio aloud, page by page, instead of telling the story of the problem and its solution. Two: hiding the failed tests, which a single question exposes. Three: slides full of text that the audience reads instead of listening. Four: one member speaking for the team, so the assessor cannot see that the others understand the work.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the model and drawings are used during the talk, the rules for voice and rehearsal, and how questions are answered.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a model on a table, a drawing on the board and a stall owner in the third row — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Tell the Story of the Problem

A design presentation has one job: let people who were not there understand the problem, weigh the evidence and decide whether your solution deserves backing. It is your portfolio said out loud, in order, with the model in your hand. Not a show of how hard you worked. Not an advert that hides the cracks.

Three audiences are in the room. The assessor wants every step named and proved: investigate, brief, two ideas, drawings, make, evaluate. The class wants to learn what you tried and what happened, including what broke. The client, here a stall owner, wants cost, what changes for them, and why to trust you. One talk can feed all three: the steps carry the proof, the honest problems teach the class, the numbers and the model convince the client.

Trust comes from three things. Evidence: the count in the drain, the number on the scale. Exact words: "forty cents more", not "a bit more"; "buckled at fourteen", not "quite strong". Honesty: say the test that failed, and your fix, before anyone asks. Name your own weakness and you control the conversation about it.

Hold the stall owner in the third row in mind and try the questions for this part: tell the story of the problem.

## Subtopic: Investigate, Design, Make, Evaluate, in Five Minutes

Structure it as the design process, which is what the rubric follows. Five minutes.

Thirty seconds, the problem as a story: "400 meals a day in polystyrene, 63 containers in one drain on a Monday, cleared every month." One photo. Forty-five seconds, the brief in its problem form and the four rules that mattered, each with its reason. Sixty seconds, both sketches held up, one sentence each, then why you chose: the returnable box cut more waste but needed water the rank has not got; the paperboard box works now and borrowed the crate. Forty-five seconds, drawings on the board, one sentence each, model passed to the front row, the moment it went wrong and the fix, with a photo. Ninety seconds, evaluation: passes briefly, fails fully with cause and fix, then back to the brief, less harm by how much, benefit kept, your own footprint. Thirty seconds, numbered recommendations and "questions?"

Everyone speaks. The person who built talks about building; the person who tested talks about testing. Cards, not pages. The model and the table remember for you.

Hold the 90 seconds for evaluation in mind and try this part's questions: investigate, design, make, evaluate, in five minutes.

## Subtopic: Hold Up the Model and Answer Back

Visuals do the work. Hold up the model at the start so people know what you are talking about; pass it round during making; hold it again during evaluation with your finger on the rib when you say "buckled at fourteen". Point to the drawings, name them, do not describe what they already show. Tables big, seven rows at most, fails highlighted. Two or three photos. No slides full of text; people read instead of listen.

Stand where people can see the model and the drawings. Talk to the back row at normal speed, pause at handovers, hold the model steady, look at people. Rehearse twice with a timer, once in front of another team who ask you hard questions. Cut anything that runs you over, because if you are stopped at five minutes you lose the evaluation, which scores most.

Questions win it. Hear the whole question, repeat it short, answer that question with evidence, briefly. Do not know? Say so and say how you would find out. Never argue, never guess a number, and the person who did that part answers. "Would I really pay forty cents more?" gets the three owners' answers, not hope.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The presentation lets outsiders understand, judge and decide; it serves the assessor, the class and the client at once. Structure it as the design process in five minutes with the evaluation longest. Let the model, drawings and tables carry it, rehearse with a timer, and answer questions with evidence and honesty.

Hold the finger on the rib, and take the final questions of the lesson: hold up the model and answer back.
