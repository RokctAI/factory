# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies environmentally friendly and biodegradable materials as a positive impact of technology. The anchor is a street vendor in Durban who switched from polystyrene takeaway boxes to boxes moulded from sugar-cane fibre, bagasse, after the municipality began charging for landfill waste, and the compost heap behind her stall where the new boxes disappear in a season. The governing idea is that a biodegradable material can be broken down by living organisms into water, carbon dioxide and compost within a reasonable time, that an environmentally friendly material also considers renewable sources, low energy and pollution in manufacture and safe disposal, and that technologists now design materials with their whole life cycle in mind. Defining biodegradable, giving examples of environmentally friendly materials and comparing materials across their life cycle are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Biodegradable Means

Biodegradable means able to be broken down by living things, mainly bacteria and fungi, into simple natural substances: water, carbon dioxide, minerals and the dark organic matter called compost. A banana peel, a cotton shirt, a wooden spoon and a paper bag are biodegradable because they are made of the carbon compounds that microbes have evolved to eat. Left in damp soil, they vanish in weeks to a few years and feed the plants that grow there.

Most plastics are not biodegradable. Polythene, polystyrene and PVC are made of long chains of carbon atoms arranged in ways that no natural organism has learned to digest. Buried, they stay intact for hundreds of years. In sunlight they break into smaller and smaller fragments, called microplastics, but the fragments are still plastic and are now found in rivers, seas, fish and even drinking water. This is the central problem the term's negative-impact lessons will return to.

The word biodegradable is often misused, so three conditions matter. Time: a material that breaks down in a hundred years is not usefully biodegradable; standards usually require most of it to decompose within months in a composting environment. Conditions: some materials labelled biodegradable only break down in industrial composters at high temperature, not in a home heap or a landfill, so the label must say where. Residue: the material must break down into harmless substances, not into toxic fragments or microplastics; a plastic that merely crumbles is called degradable or oxo-degradable, which is not the same thing.

Compostable is a stricter term: the material not only biodegrades but does so within a set time in a compost heap and leaves compost that is safe for growing food. Paper, cardboard, bagasse, cotton and untreated wood are compostable. A good technologist checks the claim on the label against these conditions before calling a material green.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of biodegradable, why most plastics are not, and the three conditions that make the word meaningful.

## Subtopic: Environmentally Friendly Materials

Environmentally friendly is a wider idea than biodegradable. It asks about the whole life of a material: where the raw material comes from, how much energy and pollution its manufacture causes, how long the product lasts, and what happens at the end. A material can score well on one and badly on another, so each must be checked.

Renewable sources are those that grow back: plant fibres, timber from managed plantations, starch from maize or cassava, sugar-cane waste. Non-renewable sources, oil, coal, metal ores, are used up for ever. Bagasse packaging, bamboo cutlery, paper bags and cotton shopping bags come from renewable sources. Bioplastics are plastics made from plant starch or plant oils rather than petroleum; some, such as PLA made from maize starch, are compostable in industrial composters, while others are chemically identical to oil-based plastics and are not biodegradable at all, though they still save oil.

Low-impact manufacture means less energy, less water and less pollution in making the material. Recycled paper uses far less energy and water than paper from fresh wood pulp and spares trees. Recycled aluminium uses about five percent of the energy of new aluminium. Rammed earth and adobe blocks for building use local soil and sun instead of fired brick and cement, which release large amounts of carbon dioxide.

Durability and reuse count too. A thick cotton bag used five hundred times is kinder than five hundred paper bags, even though paper is compostable, because making the paper costs trees, energy and water each time. Glass bottles that are returned and refilled beat both plastic and single-use glass. The greenest material is often the one that is used longest.

Safe disposal closes the loop. Materials that compost return to the soil; materials that can be recycled become new products; materials that do neither go to landfill or, worse, into rivers and the sea. Designers now talk of a circular approach, in which the end of one product is the start of another, instead of a straight line from mine or well to dump.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four stages of a material's life, examples of renewable and recycled materials, and why durability matters.

## Subtopic: Choosing Materials for the Environment

Comparing two materials for their environmental impact is a skill the term tests directly. Take the Durban vendor's choice between polystyrene and bagasse boxes. Source: polystyrene comes from oil, non-renewable; bagasse is the fibre left after sugar cane is crushed, a renewable waste product. Manufacture: polystyrene needs petroleum refining and chemical processing; bagasse is pulped, moulded and dried with modest energy. Use: both keep food hot and clean for one meal, though bagasse is less insulating. Disposal: polystyrene does not biodegrade, blows into drains and rivers and breaks into fragments; bagasse composts within months. On three of four stages bagasse wins clearly, and the fourth is a draw, so the switch was environmentally sound. Cost was higher, which is a constraint the vendor had to weigh.

Not every comparison is so clear. A paper bag against a thin polythene bag: paper is renewable and compostable but needs more energy and water to make and is heavier to transport, while polythene is light and cheap but persists for centuries. If each is used once, the paper bag's disposal advantage usually wins; if the polythene bag is reused many times as a bin liner, the balance shifts. The honest answer depends on how the product is used, which is why good evaluations state their assumptions.

Several questions guide any comparison. Is the source renewable or recycled? Does manufacture use little energy and cause little pollution? Will the product last and be reused? Can it be composted or recycled at the end? Does it release harmful substances at any stage? Scoring each material against these and adding up gives a fair verdict and shows the examiner the reasoning rather than a guess.

South Africa has its own developments. Packaging from sugar-cane bagasse is made in KwaZulu-Natal. Sisal and hemp fibres are being used in composites. Recycled PET bottles are spun into polyester fibre for clothing and insulation. Paper and cardboard recycling rates are among the highest in Africa. Each of these is technology responding to the harm earlier technology caused, which is the positive impact this week celebrates.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: trusting the word biodegradable on a label without asking how long, where and into what. Two: thinking paper is always greener than plastic; it depends on use and reuse. Three: assuming bioplastic means compostable; some bioplastics are identical to oil plastics. Four: judging a material on one stage only; the whole life cycle counts.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing polystyrene and bagasse across the life cycle, why paper versus plastic is not simple, and the guiding questions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a vendor's takeaway boxes and a compost heap — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Rotting Is a Good Thing

Biodegradable means living things can eat it and turn it back into water, carbon dioxide and compost. A banana peel, a paper bag, a cotton shirt, a wooden spoon: bacteria and fungi break them down in weeks or months.

Most plastics are not biodegradable. Nothing alive can digest them. Buried, they last hundreds of years. In the sun they crumble into tiny plastic bits that end up in rivers, fish and water. Still plastic, just smaller.

Three questions before you believe the word on a label. How long does it take? Where does it break down, a home compost heap or only a special factory? What is left, harmless compost or plastic dust?

Compostable is the strict word: breaks down in a compost heap in months and leaves soil you can grow food in.

Keep the banana peel and the plastic bottle in mind and try the questions for this part: rotting is a good thing.

## Subtopic: Kinder Materials

Environmentally friendly is bigger than biodegradable. Ask four things about a material's whole life.

Where does it come from? Renewable things grow back: plants, plantation timber, sugar-cane waste. Oil, coal and ores do not.

How is it made? Recycled paper and recycled aluminium take far less energy and water than new. Mud bricks dried in the sun beat fired bricks and cement.

How long is it used? A cotton bag used five hundred times beats five hundred paper bags. The greenest material is often the one you use longest.

What happens at the end? Compost, recycle, or landfill and the sea. Good designers plan for the end to be a new beginning.

Hold the four questions in mind and try this part's questions: kinder materials across their whole life.

## Subtopic: Picking the Greener Option

Compare two materials by running them through the four questions. The vendor's boxes: polystyrene comes from oil, needs chemical factories, is used once, and never rots. Bagasse comes from sugar-cane waste, is pulped and dried, is used once, and composts in months. Bagasse wins three of four. It cost more; that was the constraint.

Paper versus thin plastic bag is harder. Paper rots but takes more energy and water to make. Plastic is light but lasts for centuries. Used once, paper wins. Reused many times, plastic catches up. Say what you are assuming.

South Africa makes bagasse packaging in KwaZulu-Natal, spins old PET bottles into polyester, and recycles more paper than most of Africa.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Biodegradable means living things break it down into harmless compost within months, and the word only means something if you know how long, where and into what. Environmentally friendly looks at source, manufacture, use and disposal. Compare materials across all four stages, state your assumptions and judge.

Hold the vendor's two boxes, and take the final questions of the lesson: picking the greener option.
