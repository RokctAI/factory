# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the positive impact of technology through the replacement of natural materials by new or improved materials. The anchor is a kitchen in Mamelodi across three generations: a grandmother's clay water pot, enamel bowl and wooden spoon beside her granddaughter's plastic bucket, stainless steel pot and silicone spatula. The governing idea is that natural materials have properties that limit them, such as rotting, cracking, weight or scarcity, and that technologists have developed new materials, or improved natural ones, with properties better suited to particular jobs, so that products became lighter, stronger, cheaper, safer and longer-lasting. Naming natural materials and their limits, naming the materials that replaced them and explaining the benefits are examinable.

## Subtopic: Natural Materials and Their Limits

A natural material is one taken from plants, animals or the earth and used with little change: timber from trees, stone from quarries, clay from riverbanks, leather and wool from animals, cotton and sisal from plants, natural rubber from the latex of rubber trees. For most of human history these were the only materials available, and people used them with great skill, building homes from mud brick and thatch, carrying water in clay pots and animal skins, making rope from sisal and clothing from cotton, wool and hide.

Every natural material has properties that suit some jobs and limit others. Timber is strong and easy to work, but it rots when wet, burns, warps as it dries and is eaten by termites. Clay pots are cheap and keep water cool, but they are heavy and shatter when dropped. Leather is tough and flexible, but it stiffens and cracks if it dries out and rots if it stays wet. Cotton is comfortable and absorbs sweat, but it holds water, dries slowly and weakens when wet. Thatch is cool and locally available, but it leaks, harbours insects and catches fire. Natural rubber is elastic, but it goes sticky in heat and brittle in cold. Stone is durable, but heavy and slow to shape.

Natural materials also vary from piece to piece. No two planks or hides are the same, so a maker cannot be sure of the strength of the next one, and products made from them cannot easily be made identical in a factory. Supply is limited by what grows or can be dug: a drought reduces cotton, a forest runs out of hardwood, a quarry is exhausted.

These limits are the reason technologists looked for alternatives. The questions they asked were practical: can we find or make a material that does not rot, is lighter, does not break when dropped, is the same every time and can be made in any quantity? The answers, over the last two centuries, changed almost every object in the house.

## Subtopic: New and Improved Materials

New materials are those made by chemical or industrial processes rather than taken directly from nature. Plastics are the most visible. Polythene, used for buckets, bags and bottles, is light, does not rot or rust, does not shatter, is cheap and can be moulded into any shape. PVC replaced metal and clay for water pipes because it does not corrode, is light to transport and joins easily. Polystyrene replaced wood shavings and straw for packaging. Nylon and polyester replaced cotton, sisal and silk in ropes, fishing nets, sails and clothing because they are stronger when wet, dry quickly and resist rot. Synthetic rubber replaced natural rubber in tyres because it is more consistent and stands up to heat and oil.

Improved materials are natural materials that have been treated or combined to overcome their limits. Steel is iron improved by controlling its carbon content and alloying it with other metals; stainless steel adds chromium so it does not rust, and it replaced enamel and tin in pots and cutlery. Concrete is a mixture of cement, sand, stone and water that sets like artificial rock and can be poured into any shape, replacing cut stone and brick in foundations, bridges and tall buildings. Plywood is timber improved by gluing thin layers with the grain crossed, so it does not warp or split like a solid plank. Treated timber is soaked with preservatives against rot and termites. Glass fibre and carbon fibre composites combine fine fibres with a plastic resin to make materials stronger than steel for their weight, used in boats, helmets and sports equipment.

Many replacements combine both routes. A modern roof uses galvanised steel sheet, iron improved by a zinc coating, instead of thatch or clay tiles. A modern water tank is polythene instead of galvanised iron, which rusted, instead of clay, which cracked. A modern school chair is a steel frame with a polypropylene seat instead of solid timber.

The pattern is the same each time. A natural material's limit, rot, rust, weight, breakage, variation or scarcity, is identified, and a new or improved material is chosen or designed whose properties remove that limit while keeping the useful ones.

## Subtopic: Why the Change Was Positive

The positive impact of these replacements can be measured in lives changed. Clean water: PVC pipes and polythene tanks brought piped water to townships and villages where clay and iron systems would have cracked, rusted and cost too much. Health: plastic syringes, gloves and blood bags made single-use sterile equipment possible, and nylon sutures replaced catgut. Safety: polycarbonate helmets, laminated windscreens and glass fibre hard hats protect heads that natural materials could not. Housing: concrete, steel and fibre cement allowed millions of homes to be built quickly and to last, where mud brick and thatch needed constant repair. Food: plastic and foil packaging keeps food fresh for weeks rather than days and cut waste and disease.

The benefits usually fall into a short list. Lighter: a 20 litre polythene bucket weighs a fraction of a clay pot and can be carried further. Stronger or more durable: a steel beam carries more than a timber one of the same size and does not rot. Cheaper: mass-produced plastics and steel cost less per item than hand-worked natural materials, so more people can afford them. Safer: materials that do not shatter, burn or harbour germs reduce injury and illness. More consistent: factory materials are the same every time, so products can be made identical and reliable. More available: materials made from oil, ore and sand are not limited by harvest or season.

This is the positive side of the term's story. The negative side, pollution, waste, the use of non-renewable oil and the loss of traditional skills, is the subject of the coming weeks, and the term asks you to hold both in view. A technologist's job is to weigh them, and to design materials and products that keep the benefits while reducing the harm, which is what biodegradable plastics and recycled paper try to do.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking natural always means better; a clay pot that shatters and a thatch roof that burns are real limits. Two: thinking new always means plastic; steel, concrete and plywood are improved natural materials. Three: confusing the material with the product; the bucket is the product, polythene is the material. Four: listing only the benefits; the next lessons show that every new material also has costs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a grandmother's kitchen and her granddaughter's kitchen — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What People Used Before

Natural materials come straight from plants, animals or the ground: wood, stone, clay, leather, wool, cotton, sisal, natural rubber. For thousands of years they were all people had.

Each one has a weak spot. Wood rots, burns and gets eaten by termites. Clay pots are heavy and smash when dropped. Leather cracks if dry and rots if wet. Cotton holds water and dries slowly. Thatch leaks and catches fire. Natural rubber goes sticky in heat.

They are also never the same twice, and you can only have as much as grows or can be dug.

Those weak spots are why people went looking for something better.

Keep the clay pot in mind and try the questions for this part: natural materials and what they cannot do.

## Subtopic: The Materials That Took Over

New materials are made in factories by chemistry. Plastics: polythene buckets and bottles that do not rot, rust or smash. PVC water pipes that do not corrode. Nylon and polyester ropes and clothes that stay strong when wet and dry fast. Synthetic rubber tyres that stand heat.

Improved materials are natural ones made better. Steel is iron with carbon controlled; stainless steel adds chromium so it does not rust. Concrete is cement, sand and stone that sets like rock in any shape. Plywood is thin wood layers glued with the grain crossed so it does not warp. Treated timber is soaked so it does not rot. Glass fibre is fine threads in resin, stronger than steel for its weight.

Each one fixes a weak spot while keeping what was good.

Hold the plastic bucket and the steel pot in mind and try this part's questions: new and improved materials and what they replaced.

## Subtopic: Better Lives From Better Materials

Why was this positive? Lighter: a plastic bucket instead of a clay pot. Stronger and longer lasting: steel instead of timber. Cheaper: factory-made, so more people can afford it. Safer: helmets that do not crack, syringes used once. The same every time, so products are reliable. Always available, not waiting for the harvest.

Clean piped water, safe hospitals, quick strong houses, food that keeps: new materials did all of that.

But every new material also has a cost, and that is the rest of the term: plastic waste, oil, pollution. A technologist weighs both.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Natural materials have limits: rot, weight, breakage, variation, scarcity. New materials such as plastics and synthetic fibres, and improved materials such as steel, concrete and plywood, remove those limits. The benefits are lighter, stronger, cheaper, safer, more consistent and more available products, and the costs come next.
