# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study on recycling waste paper and cardboard into packaging products. The anchor is a waste picker in Johannesburg pulling a trolley stacked two metres high with flattened boxes to a buy-back centre in Booysens, from where bales go to a mill near Springs and return within weeks as new corrugated board. The governing idea is that paper fibre can be recovered by pulping, cleaned and reformed into new board several times before the fibres become too short, that recycled fibre supplies most of South Africa's packaging board, and that the process saves trees, water, energy and landfill space while depending on collection systems and having limits of fibre quality and contamination. Describing the recycling process, naming recycled packaging products and evaluating the benefits and limits are examinable.

## Subtopic: From Waste Paper to New Board

Paper is a mat of plant fibres, mostly cellulose from wood, pressed and dried. Because the fibres are only tangled and bonded, not melted together like plastic, they can be separated again with water and reused. That single property makes paper one of the most recyclable materials in the world.

The process begins with collection. Households, offices, schools and shops set aside paper and cardboard; waste pickers and collection companies gather it, sort it into grades such as cardboard, newspaper and white office paper, and compress it into bales. At the mill, bales are fed into a large vat called a pulper, where water and agitation break the paper back into a slurry of fibres. Staples, tape, plastic and other contaminants are removed by screens and by spinning the slurry so that heavy objects sink and light ones float. For white paper, the ink is removed in a de-inking stage using soap-like chemicals and air bubbles that lift the ink away; for brown board, ink is simply mixed in, which is why recycled board is grey-brown.

The cleaned pulp is then spread as a thin, wet layer on a moving wire mesh, where water drains through, pressed between rollers to squeeze out more water and bond the fibres, and dried on heated cylinders. The result is a continuous sheet of paper or board, wound onto reels. Several layers can be pressed together to make thick board, and a fluted middle layer glued between two flat liners makes corrugated board, the material of brown boxes.

Each time paper is recycled, the fibres are shortened and weakened a little by the pulping and pressing. After about five to seven cycles they become too short to hold together and are lost as sludge, so a proportion of fresh fibre from plantation timber or sugar-cane bagasse is added to keep the board strong. Recycling therefore extends the life of fibre many times but cannot replace new fibre entirely.

The questions for this section are with you now: why paper can be recycled, the stages from collection to new board, and why fresh fibre must be added.

## Subtopic: Packaging Products Made From Recycled Paper

Recycled paper fibre goes mainly into packaging, because packaging tolerates the grey-brown colour and the slightly lower strength of recycled fibre, and because packaging is where most paper is used. Corrugated boxes are the largest product: the brown cartons that carry groceries, appliances and online orders are made with a high proportion of recycled fibre in their liners and especially in the fluted middle. Boxes are typically used once, flattened and recycled again, so the loop turns quickly.

Moulded pulp products are made by sucking pulp onto a shaped mesh mould and drying it, giving the grey egg trays, fruit trays, cup carriers from takeaway shops and the protective inserts in boxes of electronics. These products use low-grade waste paper, including newspaper, that would otherwise be hard to recycle, and they are themselves recyclable and compostable. Folding cartons, the printed boxes for cereal, toothpaste and medicines, are made from recycled board with a thin coating of white fibre or clay on the outside so that they print well. Paper bags, wrapping paper, cores for toilet rolls and tape, and the grey backing board of notepads are all recycled products.

The design of these products uses what the term has taught about structures. Corrugated board is an I-beam in paper: two flat liners held apart by a fluted web, giving stiffness far beyond that of a flat sheet of the same weight. Egg trays use the shell shape of each cup to resist crushing. Cardboard boxes are braced by their flaps and corners. A packaging designer chooses the flute size and the number of layers to match the load, exactly as a structural engineer chooses a beam.

South Africa's paper recycling rate is around 70 percent for recoverable paper, among the highest in the world, and more than a million tonnes are recovered each year. Mills in Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga turn it into board; the industry employs tens of thousands, including an estimated 60 000 to 90 000 informal waste pickers who collect a large share of the material.

## Subtopic: Benefits, Limits and the Recycling System

Recycling paper has clear benefits. Each tonne of recycled paper saves roughly 17 trees worth of plantation timber, about 26 000 litres of water, and 60 to 70 percent of the energy needed to make paper from fresh wood pulp. It keeps about three cubic metres of waste out of landfill, where paper would decompose and release methane, a strong greenhouse gas. It supports jobs across collection, sorting, transport and milling, and gives an income to waste pickers who would otherwise have none. And it reduces the pressure to plant more timber plantations, which use land and water.

The limits are also real. Fibre shortens with each cycle, so recycling can never be a closed loop and fresh fibre is always needed. Contamination is the great enemy: food-soiled pizza boxes, wax-coated boxes, laminated juice cartons, paper mixed with plastic or glass, and wet paper all reduce the value of a bale or make it unusable, which is why sorting at source matters. De-inking produces a sludge that must be disposed of. Collection depends on people separating waste and on a system to carry it, and in many areas there is no kerbside collection, so the burden falls on waste pickers working in dangerous conditions for low pay. Recycled board is weaker and greyer than virgin board, so it is not suitable for every use. And transport of bulky bales uses fuel.

Evaluating the case fairly, the benefits outweigh the limits by a wide margin for paper, which is why paper recycling is one of the success stories of South African waste management, in contrast with plastics, where recycling rates remain low. The lesson for a technologist is that recycling works best when the material separates cleanly, when there is a steady market for the product, and when collection is organised; packaging board meets all three.

Improving the system is a design problem. Better separation at home and school raises fibre quality. Designing packaging for recycling, by avoiding laminates and wax and using water-based inks, keeps more of it in the loop. Formal recognition and fair pay for waste pickers would improve collection and lives at once. Each of these is a recommendation a Grade 8 report could make.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking paper can be recycled for ever; fibres wear out after five to seven cycles. Two: putting greasy or waxed boxes in the paper bin; they contaminate the bale. Three: assuming recycled always means weaker and worse; corrugated board is engineered to be strong enough for its job. Four: forgetting the people; the system runs on waste pickers whose work is often unrecognised.

The questions for this section are with you now: the benefits in trees, water, energy and landfill, the limits of fibre and contamination, and how the system could be improved.

## Subtopic: Old Paper, New Paper

Paper is a mat of plant fibres pressed and dried. The fibres are only tangled, not melted, so water can pull them apart and let you use them again.

Collect it: homes, shops, offices, waste pickers with trolleys. Sort it: cardboard, newspaper, white paper. Bale it. At the mill, tip it into a huge vat of water, the pulper, and stir it back into fibre soup. Screen out staples, tape and plastic. For white paper, float the ink off with bubbles.

Spread the clean pulp on a moving mesh, let the water drain, squeeze it between rollers, dry it on hot cylinders. Out comes a long sheet of new paper or board. Glue a wavy layer between two flat ones and you have the brown board of boxes.

Each trip through shortens the fibres a bit. After five to seven trips they are too short, so some fresh fibre is always mixed in.

## Subtopic: Boxes, Egg Trays and Cup Holders

Most recycled paper becomes packaging, because packaging does not mind being grey-brown.

Brown corrugated boxes: the biggest product by far. Used once, flattened, recycled again.

Egg trays, fruit trays, cup carriers and the grey inserts in electronics boxes: pulp sucked onto a shaped mesh and dried. They use the lowest grade of waste paper.

Cereal and toothpaste cartons: recycled board with a thin white skin to print on. Paper bags, toilet-roll cores, notepad backs.

Look at the structures inside them. Corrugated board is an I-beam in paper: two flat skins held apart by a wavy web. Egg trays are little shells. Boxes are braced by their flaps.

South Africa recycles about 70 percent of its paper, among the best in the world, with the help of tens of thousands of waste pickers.

## Subtopic: Why It Is Worth It and Where It Stops

Is it worth it? A tonne of recycled paper saves about 17 trees, 26 000 litres of water and two thirds of the energy, and keeps three cubic metres out of landfill where it would rot and give off methane. It gives work to tens of thousands of people.

Where does it stop? Fibres wear out, so fresh fibre is always needed. Greasy pizza boxes, waxed boxes, juice cartons and wet paper spoil a bale. De-inking leaves a sludge. Many places have no collection, so waste pickers do the hard, dangerous work for little pay. Recycled board is a bit weaker and greyer.

Still, for paper the good far outweighs the bad, unlike plastic where recycling is still rare. It works because paper separates cleanly, there is a steady market for board, and collection is organised.

To make it better: sort at home and school, design packaging without wax and laminates, and pay waste pickers fairly.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Paper fibres can be re-pulped and reformed five to seven times. Recycled fibre becomes corrugated boxes, moulded trays, cartons and bags. Recycling saves trees, water, energy and landfill and creates jobs, but needs clean sorting, fresh fibre and a collection system.
