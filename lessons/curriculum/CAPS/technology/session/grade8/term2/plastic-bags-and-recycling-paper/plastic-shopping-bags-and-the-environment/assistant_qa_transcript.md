### subtopic_1 — The Problem With Plastic Shopping Bags
**Assistant:** If the 2003 law did not reduce total plastic, was it a failure?

**Tutor:** It depends on what the law was for, and an honest report separates the aims it met from the aims it missed. The regulations were introduced mainly to deal with litter, the bags on fences and in drains, and on that measure they succeeded for years: thicker bags blew less, tore less and were reused more, consumption dropped sharply at first, and the visible pollution that embarrassed the country decreased. They also made recycling more viable, because a thicker bag contains enough material to be worth collecting, and they changed habits, since millions of people began carrying bags to the shops for the first time. Where the law fell short was in its longer-term effects. The charge of a few cents was never raised with inflation, so it stopped influencing behaviour and bag use climbed back towards earlier levels; the levy was collected but much of it went into general government funds rather than the recycling and clean-up it was promised for; and because each bag now contained more polythene, the total mass of plastic entering landfills may not have fallen even when the number of bags did. Critics also point out that the thicker bags still end up in rivers and the sea. A fair conclusion is therefore mixed: a partial success on litter and habits, a disappointment on total plastic and on the use of the money. That is a more useful finding than either "it worked" or "it failed", because it points to specific recommendations, such as raising the charge, ring-fencing the levy for recycling and promoting reusable bags, and because it teaches the general lesson that regulation changes behaviour only as long as the incentive stays strong.

### subtopic_2 — South Africa's Response: Thicker Bags and the Levy
**Assistant:** Why did Kenya and Rwanda ban bags completely when South Africa only made them thicker and charged for them?

**Tutor:** Different countries weighed the same trade-offs differently, and both approaches are legitimate technological and political choices with their own costs. A complete ban removes the product and therefore the litter at a stroke, which is why Rwanda, which banned plastic bags in 2008, has some of the cleanest streets in Africa, and why Kenya's 2017 ban, with heavy fines and even prison for manufacturers and sellers, cut the bags clogging Nairobi's drains dramatically. The costs of a ban are also real. Thousands of jobs in bag manufacturing are lost, informal traders who sold goods in bags need alternatives they can afford, shoppers must bring or buy reusable bags, and enforcement against smuggled bags takes police time. South Africa in 2003 chose a middle path that kept the plastics industry and its jobs, raised money through the levy and relied on price and thickness to change behaviour. That approach was gentler on industry and shoppers but, as the results showed, weaker on total plastic over time. Several points for a report follow. First, the same problem admits several solutions, and the choice depends on priorities: jobs, cleanliness, cost to the poor, enforcement capacity. Second, outcomes should be compared with evidence: litter levels, bag consumption, employment, cost of alternatives. Third, a recommendation for South Africa might borrow from both: keep the industry but raise the charge sharply and enforce it, or ban only the thinnest bags while subsidising woven reusable bags for low-income shoppers. Showing that you have considered the other approach, and why a country might choose it, is what lifts a report from description to evaluation.

### subtopic_3 — Writing a Case-Study Report
**Assistant:** How do I write findings when my sources disagree about the numbers?

**Tutor:** You report the disagreement, because an honest statement of uncertain evidence is itself a finding and is worth more than a confident number that may be wrong. Figures about plastic bags vary widely: estimates of South Africa's pre-2003 consumption range from several billion to over eight billion bags a year, and claims about the drop afterwards range from 50 to 90 percent, depending on who measured, when and how. When you meet this, do four things. Give the range rather than picking one number: "estimates of use before 2003 range from about five to eight billion bags a year". Name the sources and their dates, since an industry body, a government department and an environmental group may each measure differently, and a 2004 figure describes a different situation from a 2015 one. Say which figure you consider more reliable and why, for example because it comes from measured sales rather than a survey. And draw only the conclusion the range supports: whatever the exact figure, all sources agree that use fell sharply at first and rose again later, so that conclusion is safe even though the precise percentages are not. This is how scientists and engineers handle data in the real world, where exact numbers are rare and ranges are normal, and examiners reward it because it shows you understand that evidence has quality as well as quantity. The one thing never to do is to quote the number that best suits your argument and hide the others; a reader who later finds the other figures will distrust everything else you wrote. In the discussion section you can then note that better data collection would itself be a useful recommendation.
