# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study on the impact of plastic shopping bags on the environment and the response of thicker and biodegradable bags, with a written report as the product. The anchor is a fence line beside the N1 outside Beaufort West in the late 1990s, so thickly draped with wind-blown bags that travellers called them the national flower, and the same fence after the 2003 regulations that banned thin bags and introduced a levy. The governing idea is that a product can have a large negative environmental impact because of how it is used and discarded rather than because of its material alone, that regulation and redesign can reduce that impact, and that an investigation report presents findings on both sides with evidence before making a recommendation. Explaining the bag problem, describing South Africa's response and structuring a case-study report are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Problem With Plastic Shopping Bags

The plastic shopping bag is a triumph of materials technology and a textbook environmental problem at the same time. It is made of high-density polythene, a few grams of material blown into a thin film, so light and cheap that shops gave them away by the million. A single bag can carry many times its own weight, is waterproof and costs a fraction of a cent. Before 2003 South Africans used an estimated eight billion a year.

The problem comes from three properties working together. The bags are nearly free, so they are treated as rubbish the moment the shopping is unpacked. They are very light, so they blow out of bins, dumps and vehicles and travel for kilometres on the wind until they catch on a fence or a tree or wash into a river. And they are not biodegradable, so once they land they stay: tangled on fences across the Karoo, choking storm-water drains in Johannesburg and causing flooding, drifting in rivers to the sea where turtles and seabirds swallow them, mistaking them for jellyfish or food. Cattle and goats in rural areas die with stomachs full of plastic. Thin bags also tear easily, so a torn bag is thrown away even sooner.

Landfills filled with bags that will persist for centuries, but the visible problem was the litter. Thin bags blown across the veld became so common that they were nicknamed the national flower, and the sight shamed a country that was promoting tourism. The costs were real: municipal clean-up budgets, blocked drains, dead livestock, polluted beaches and rivers, and a growing load of microplastics as bags fragmented in the sun.

The case study matters because it shows that the impact of a product depends on its whole system, not just its material. Polythene in a water pipe is a benefit for fifty years. Polythene in a bag used for twenty minutes and discarded is a disaster. Same material, different use, different impact.

The questions for this section are with you now: what plastic bags are made of, the three properties that create the problem, and the harms they cause.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Response: Thicker Bags and the Levy

In May 2003 South Africa introduced the Plastic Bag Regulations under the Environment Conservation Act, one of the first such laws in the world. Two measures worked together. First, bags thinner than 30 micrometres were banned for shopping use, and bags had to be at least 24 micrometres thick with limits on printing. The thinking was that a thicker bag is stronger, so it is less likely to tear and more likely to be reused several times, and it is heavier, so it blows less easily. Second, shops were no longer allowed to give bags away; customers had to pay for them, and a levy on each bag went to government, with the intention of funding recycling and clean-up.

The results were mixed, and that is exactly what makes it a good case study. In the first years bag use fell sharply, by estimates of up to 80 percent, as people brought their own bags or reused the thicker ones. Litter on fences and in drains visibly decreased. Recycling of thicker bags became more viable because there was more material per bag. But over time the price of a bag, a few cents, stopped deterring shoppers; usage climbed back; the levy money was not always spent on recycling as promised; and thicker bags, when they are discarded, contain more plastic than the thin ones did. Critics argue the regulation reduced litter but not the total plastic.

The second response was material redesign. Some retailers introduced bags labelled biodegradable or compostable, made from starch-based bioplastics or from polythene with additives meant to make it break down. The lessons of last week apply: starch bags break down only in industrial composting, which South Africa largely lacks; oxo-degradable additives make the bag fragment into microplastic rather than disappear, and several countries have banned them; and biodegradable bags contaminate the recycling of ordinary polythene. Paper bags returned in some shops, with their own costs in trees, energy and water. Reusable woven polypropylene bags, used dozens of times, have become the main alternative.

The lesson for a technologist is that there is no single fix. Regulation changes behaviour for a while; redesign changes materials but introduces new problems; and the most effective measure, reuse, depends on people. Several other African countries, Kenya and Rwanda among them, later went further and banned plastic shopping bags completely, with strong penalties, and saw cleaner streets, though with costs to shoppers and manufacturers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two measures of 2003, the results and their limits, and the alternatives and their problems.

## Subtopic: Writing a Case-Study Report

A case-study report is a short written document that presents an investigation so that someone else can understand the problem, the evidence and what the writer recommends. The Grade 8 report follows a fixed structure, and marks are given for each part.

Title and introduction: name the case and state the question in one or two sentences, for example "This report investigates the environmental impact of plastic shopping bags in South Africa and whether thicker and biodegradable bags have reduced it." Background: describe the product, its material and how it is used, with figures where you have them, such as the estimated eight billion bags a year before 2003. Findings: the evidence, organised under headings, on the harm caused, the responses made and their results, with both sides given; a finding that thicker bags cut litter sits next to a finding that total plastic may not have fallen. Discussion: what the findings mean, where the evidence is uncertain, and how the alternatives compare, using the life-cycle thinking of last week. Conclusion and recommendation: a clear answer to the question and one or two practical recommendations, such as enforcing reuse through a higher levy, investing the levy in recycling, or promoting woven reusable bags. Sources: where the information came from.

Good reports use evidence rather than opinion. "Bags are terrible" is an opinion; "bags blocked storm-water drains in Johannesburg and contributed to flooding" is a finding that can be checked. Numbers are given with their source and their date, because figures change. Both sides appear, since a report that mentions only harms, or only successes, is advertising. Paragraphs are short and headed, so the reader can find each part.

The report can include a simple table comparing thin bags, thick bags, biodegradable bags, paper bags and woven reusable bags across source, manufacture, use and disposal, which is the life-cycle comparison in a form the reader can take in at a glance. A labelled sketch of a bag caught on a fence, or a bar chart of bag use before and after 2003, strengthens the findings.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing a story instead of a report; use headings and findings, not a narrative. Two: giving opinions without evidence; every claim needs a source or an observation. Three: one-sided reporting; the levy's successes and failures both belong. Four: a recommendation that does not follow from the findings; if the findings show reuse works best, recommend reuse.

The questions for this section are with you now: the sections of a report, the difference between evidence and opinion, and how to make a recommendation follow from findings.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a fence full of bags beside the N1 — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Why the Bag Became a Problem

A plastic shopping bag is a few grams of polythene blown into a thin film. It is light, waterproof, strong for its weight and costs almost nothing. Before 2003 South Africans used about eight billion a year.

Three things made it a problem. It was free, so people threw it away at once. It was light, so it blew out of bins and across the veld for kilometres. And it never rots, so wherever it landed, it stayed.

Bags on fences, bags in drains causing floods, bags in rivers and the sea where turtles eat them, bags in the stomachs of cattle and goats. So many bags on the fences of the Karoo that people called them the national flower.

The same polythene in a water pipe is a benefit for fifty years. In a bag used for twenty minutes it is a disaster. The use decides the impact.

Keep the fence in mind and try the questions for this part: why the bag became a problem.

## Subtopic: What the Country Did About It

In 2003 South Africa made a law. Thin bags were banned; a bag had to be at least 24 micrometres thick, so it would not tear and could be reused. And shops had to charge for bags, with a levy going to government for recycling.

At first it worked well. Bag use dropped by as much as 80 percent. Fences and drains got cleaner. But the price was only a few cents, so people stopped noticing it, use crept back up, and the levy money did not always go to recycling. Thicker bags thrown away contain more plastic, not less.

Then came new materials. Starch bags that only break down in a special composting factory. Bags with additives that just crumble into plastic dust. Paper bags, with their cost in trees and water. The one that really works is the woven bag you use dozens of times.

Kenya and Rwanda banned plastic bags completely.

Hold the 2003 law in mind and try this part's questions: what the country did and how well it worked.

## Subtopic: Putting the Report Together

Now write it up. A report has fixed parts.

Title and introduction: what you investigated, in a sentence. Background: the bag, its material, how many. Findings: the evidence under headings, harms, responses, results, both sides. Discussion: what it means and how the alternatives compare. Conclusion and recommendation: your answer and one or two practical steps. Sources: where you found it.

Evidence, not opinion. "Bags are bad" is an opinion. "Bags blocked drains in Johannesburg and caused floods" is a finding. Give numbers with their source and date.

Add a table comparing thin, thick, biodegradable, paper and woven bags across source, making, use and disposal. Add a sketch or a chart.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plastic bags became a problem because they are free, light and never rot. South Africa's 2003 law banned thin bags and charged for the rest; litter fell but use crept back. Biodegradable bags have their own problems; reusable bags work best. A report has an introduction, background, findings on both sides, discussion, recommendation and sources.

Hold the headings, and take the final questions of the lesson: putting the report together.
