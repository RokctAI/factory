### subtopic_1 — Developments: Nets of Containers Revisited
**Assistant:** In the exam, how do I tell quickly whether a net will fold into a cube or a box?

**Tutor:** Use two checks that take a few seconds each and catch nearly every wrong net. The first is the count: a closed box has six faces, so a net with five or seven rectangles is wrong before you fold anything, and an open tray has five. The second is the fold test, done in your head or with a pencil as the base. Pick one face as the base and imagine the faces attached to its edges folding up to become the four sides; any face attached beyond one of those sides folds over to become the lid or the opposite side. The net fails if two faces are forced to occupy the same position, for example a row of four squares with two more squares attached to the same side of the row, which puts both extra squares on the same face when folded, or if a position is never filled. There are eleven distinct nets of a cube, and all of them share the feature that no more than four squares are in a straight line and the remaining two are on opposite sides of that line or arranged so they do not collide; a row of five or six squares can never fold into a cube. For a rectangular box the same logic applies with the extra condition that edge lengths match: a side that attaches to the long edge of the base must itself be the length of that edge, and the lid must match the base. If a question gives four candidate nets, eliminate by count first, then by collision, then by edge length, and the survivor is the answer. When you must draw a net yourself, build it from the base outward as the lesson showed and you cannot produce a collision, because every face is placed where it will fold to.

### subtopic_2 — Forces on Materials and the Shapes That Resist Them
**Assistant:** A question shows a bicycle and asks for the force on the chain, the pedal crank and the frame tubes. How do I avoid mixing them up?

**Tutor:** Ask, for each part, what the load is trying to do to it, and describe that before you name the force; the name follows from the description almost automatically. The chain is pulled by the rider's effort on one side and resisted by the back wheel on the other; a chain cannot push, bend or twist in any useful way, so it is in tension, and the same is true of any cable, rope, string or wire, which is why "bending" is never the answer for a chain. The pedal crank is a lever: the foot pushes down on its end and the other end is fixed to the spindle, so the crank arm is loaded across its length and bends, while the spindle it turns is twisted, torsion; if the question asks about the crank arm, answer bending, and if it asks about the axle or spindle, answer torsion. The frame tubes depend on which tube and which load. The seat tube carries the rider's weight down to the pedals and is mostly in compression; the down tube and top tube form a triangle with it and are in a mix of compression and tension depending on the load, which is why they are tubes, equally stiff in every direction, and why the frame is triangulated, as in the Term 1 pylon. A good answer names the force and gives the one-line reason: "chain: tension, because it is pulled and cannot push; crank arm: bending, because the foot loads it across its length; spindle: torsion, because the crank turns it; seat tube: compression, because the rider's weight presses down it." That form earns the mark for the force and the mark for the reason, and it protects you from the trap of writing the first force that comes to mind.

### subtopic_3 — Impact of Materials on the Environment: Weighing Both Sides
**Assistant:** For the impact question, how much should I write, and what if I only know negatives about the material?

**Tutor:** Write to the marks and the command word: a two-mark "name" wants two items, a four-mark "describe" wants about four points, and a six-mark "evaluate" or "discuss" wants both sides and a judgement, which is where the four-part paragraph fits. Do not write a page; a six-mark answer is five or six sentences, each carrying a point. If you only know negatives, find the positive by asking the question the term kept asking: what problem did this material solve, and for whom? Every material that is widely used solves something, or nobody would buy it. Polystyrene is light, cheap, insulating and clean; plastic bags are waterproof, strong for their mass and nearly free; cement makes houses that do not wash away; PET bottles are light, unbreakable and keep drinks sealed. State that benefit in one sentence, honestly, because the examiner is testing whether you can weigh, not whether you disapprove. Then give the negative at its life-cycle stage, raw material, manufacture, use or disposal, which shows you understand where the harm sits, and finish with a solution of the type the term taught: change the material, redesign the product, change how it is used, improve collection or recycling, regulate, or educate. If the question is about a material you have not studied, apply the same frame; it works for anything from a car tyre to a cell phone. A balanced answer with one point each side and a fix will usually score more than a long one-sided list, because the rubric awards marks for the structure of the judgement, not for the number of complaints.
