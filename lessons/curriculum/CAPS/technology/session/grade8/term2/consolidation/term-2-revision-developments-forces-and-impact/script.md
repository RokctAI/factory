# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers revision of the term's three threads: practising developments, types of forces, and the negative impact of materials on the environment. The anchor is a Grade 8 class in Bloemfontein in the week before the mid-year examination, with a cereal box flattened on each desk, a bent ruler, a stick of chalk and the term's portfolio open at the specification table.

## Subtopic: Developments: Nets of Containers Revisited

A development, or net, is the shape a container makes when it is unfolded and laid flat, drawn so that it can be cut out, folded along the marked lines and glued into the solid. It is the drawing that connects the term's packaging work to its design project, and it is examined because it tests three skills at once: visualising a solid in three dimensions, measuring and laying out accurately with instruments, and knowing the conventions of fold lines, cut lines and glue flaps.

The method for a rectangular box with a lid is fixed. Draw the base to its true length and width. Attach the four sides to the four edges of the base, each side rising to the true height; opposite sides are equal, and a side's width is the edge of the base it is attached to. Attach the lid to the top edge of one side, usually the back, to its true length and width, so that it folds over to close. Add glue flaps to the edges that must be joined when folded: one flap on the free vertical edge of each pair of adjacent sides, and a tuck flap on the lid; flaps are drawn as trapeziums, slightly shorter than the edge so they do not foul the corners. Mark fold lines as thin dashed or chain lines and cut lines as thick continuous lines, and label glue flaps. Check by counting faces, six for a closed box, and by confirming that every edge that meets another edge when folded has the same length.

Common examination variants are the open tray, which is a base with four sides and no lid, so the net is a cross; the box with a tuck-in lid, where the lid gains a flap of about one third of the box height; and the triangular prism, where the net is a row of three rectangles with a triangle attached above and below one of them. A hexagonal pencil box is a row of six rectangles with hexagons attached. The examiner may give the solid as an isometric drawing with dimensions and ask for the net to scale, or give the net and ask for the solid, or ask which of several nets folds into a given box. For the last, the test is to mentally fold each net and reject any in which two faces land on the same place or a face is missing.

The questions for this section are with you now: the method for the net of a lidded box, the conventions for folds, cuts and flaps, and how to check a net before cutting.

## Subtopic: Forces on Materials and the Shapes That Resist Them

The forces thread runs from the member to the section. The five forces: tension stretches, compression shortens, bending stretches one face and shortens the other about a neutral axis, torsion twists and is shear around the surface, and shear slides one part past another. An examination item typically shows a loaded structure, a shelf bracket, a swing, a signpost, a bicycle pedal, and asks which force acts on a named part. The method is to ask what the load is trying to do to that part: the swing's chains are pulled, tension; the seat is loaded across its span, bending; the top bar's fixings try to slide, shear; the pedal spindle is turned, torsion; the post of a signpost carries the sign's weight, compression, and the wind's push, bending.

Materials respond with strength named by the force, stiffness, elasticity, brittleness and toughness, and the key contrast is concrete, strong in compression and weak in tension, against steel, strong in both. Two composites adapt materials to forces: reinforced concrete puts steel near the stretched face of a beam, near the bottom between supports and near the top over a column, with stirrups for shear and cover to protect the steel; plywood crosses the grain of an odd number of veneers so the sheet is strong in both directions and does not split. Three sections shape a material to the force while saving mass: the I-beam for bending about one axis, with flanges far from the neutral axis; angle iron for frames, corners and bracing; the T-bar where one face must be flat; and tubes for compression and torsion, because their material is far from the centre in all directions.

A typical two-part item gives a short beam spanning an opening and asks, first, which parts are in tension and compression, answer the bottom in tension and the top in compression between supports, and second, which section would carry the load with the least steel and why, answer the I-beam, because its flanges sit where the bending stress is greatest and its web carries the shear, so most of its mass works. A variant asks why a ruler on edge is stiffer than one laid flat, and the answer is depth: material further from the neutral axis resists bending more, so the same mass placed deeper is stiffer.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying the force on a named part of a loaded structure, where the steel goes in reinforced concrete, and matching a section to a force.

## Subtopic: Impact of Materials on the Environment: Weighing Both Sides

The impact thread asks for balance, and the examination rewards answers that weigh both sides with evidence. The positive impact of materials: new or improved materials replaced natural ones that were scarce, slow to grow or harmful, plastics for ivory, horn and tortoiseshell, synthetic rubber for the tree, stainless steel for utensils that once rusted, aluminium and fibreglass for weight; environmentally friendly and biodegradable materials, bagasse and paper pulp for polystyrene, bioplastics, recycled paperboard, aim to deliver the same service with less lasting harm. The negative impact: non-renewable raw materials from oil and mines, energy and emissions in manufacture, waste that persists, with plastic shopping bags as the case study, thin bags blowing into fences and rivers, the thicker bag and levy that followed, and the recycling and waste-picker economy that has grown around PET and paper.

An examination paragraph on the impact of a material scores when it does four things. It names the material and its use. It gives at least one positive impact with a reason or example. It gives at least one negative impact with a reason or example, located at a life-cycle stage, raw material, manufacture, use or disposal. And it reaches a judgement or proposes a solution: reduce, reuse, recycle, redesign, replace, regulate. "Polystyrene take-away containers are light, cheap and keep food hot, which helps stall owners and customers; they are made from oil, are rarely recycled and persist for decades in drains and rivers; a pressed paperboard container with a return crate would keep the benefit and remove the persistent waste" is a complete answer in three sentences.

The negative-impact investigation and the design project give the examples: the stage where the harm was worst, the six solution types, and the specification-based evaluation. Expect a question that gives a short case, a new product or material, and asks for its likely negative impact and a solution that counteracts it; answer with the life-cycle stages and the solution types from the term.

Examination habits for this paper. Read the command word: name, list, describe, explain, compare, evaluate each ask for a different amount; draw means with instruments unless the question says sketch; labels and units are marks. For a development, use a ruler and set square, mark folds and cuts differently, and label flaps. For a force question, name the force and say what the load is doing to the part. For an impact question, write both sides and a solution. Leave no question blank; a part answer earns part marks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: a net with a face missing or two faces that fold onto the same place. Two: naming "bending" for a chain or cable, which can only be in tension. Three: an impact answer that is all negative or all positive. Four: sketching free-hand when the question said draw, and losing the accuracy marks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four parts of a balanced impact paragraph, the plastic-bag case study, and the examination habits that save marks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a flattened cereal box, a bent ruler and an exam paper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Unfold the Box

A development is a box unfolded flat so you can cut it, fold it and glue it. It is in the exam because it tests three things: seeing a solid in your head, measuring and ruling accurately, and knowing the drawing rules.

A lidded box: draw the base true size. Hang the four sides off its four edges, each as tall as the box; a side is as wide as the edge it hangs from. Hang the lid off the top of the back side. Add glue flaps, slightly shorter trapeziums, on the free edge of sides that must join, and a tuck flap on the lid. Thin dashed lines for folds, thick lines for cuts, label the flaps. Check: six faces, and every pair of edges that meet when folded are the same length.

Variants: an open tray is a cross. A triangular prism is three rectangles in a row with a triangle above and below one of them. A hexagonal pencil box is six rectangles with hexagons. If asked which net folds into a box, fold each in your head and throw out any where two faces land in the same place or one is missing.

Hold the flattened cereal box in mind and try the questions for this part: unfold the box.

## Subtopic: Five Forces, Three Letters, Two Composites

Five forces: pull, squash, bend, twist, cut. The exam shows a loaded thing and names a part. Ask what the load is doing to that part. Swing chains: pulled, tension. Seat: loaded across its span, bending. Pedal spindle: turned, torsion. Signpost: holds weight, compression, and the wind pushes it, bending. A bolt between two plates pulled apart: shear.

Strong must say which force. Concrete: strong squashed, weak pulled. Steel: strong both. Two composites: reinforced concrete puts steel near the stretched face, bottom of a beam between walls, top over a column, with stirrups and cover; plywood crosses an odd number of layers so it is strong both ways and never splits.

Three letters and a tube: I-beam for bending, flanges far from the middle; angle iron for frames and corners; T-bar where one face must be flat; tubes for squashing and twisting. The classic two-parter: a beam over a doorway. Bottom pulled, top squashed. Best section with least steel: the I-beam, because its flanges sit where the bending is biggest and its web takes the shear. Why a ruler on edge is stiffer: depth, material further from the middle fights bending harder.

Hold the swing in mind and try this part's questions: five forces, three letters, two composites.

## Subtopic: Good, Bad and What to Do

Impact questions want both sides. Good: new materials replaced scarce or harmful natural ones, plastic for ivory and horn, stainless steel for rusting iron, and greener materials like bagasse, bioplastics and recycled board aim for the same service with less lasting harm. Bad: oil and mines for raw materials, energy and emissions to make, waste that will not go away. Case study: thin plastic bags in fences and rivers, then the thicker bag and the levy, and the recycling and waste-picker economy around PET and paper.

A full-mark paragraph does four things: names the material and its use; one good with a reason; one bad with a reason and the life stage it sits at; a judgement or a fix, reduce, reuse, recycle, redesign, replace, regulate. Three sentences can do it: polystyrene is light, cheap and keeps food hot; it is made from oil, rarely recycled and sits in drains for decades; a paperboard box with a return crate keeps the benefit and removes the lasting waste.

Exam habits: read the command word; draw means instruments; labels and units are marks; nets with a ruler, folds and cuts marked differently, flaps labelled; forces named with what the load is doing; impact answers with both sides and a fix; never leave a blank.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A net is the box unfolded, drawn with instruments, folds dashed, cuts solid, flaps labelled, six faces checked. Five forces found by asking what the load does to the part; steel where concrete is pulled, plywood crossed, I-beams for bending, tubes for squashing and twisting. Impact answers name the material, one good, one bad with its life stage, and a fix.

Hold the three-sentence paragraph, and take the final questions of the lesson: good, bad and what to do.
