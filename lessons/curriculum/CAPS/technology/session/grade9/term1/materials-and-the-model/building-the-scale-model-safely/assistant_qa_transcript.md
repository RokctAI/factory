### subtopic_1 — Model Materials, Tools and the Conversion Table
**Assistant:** Why 1:20 for the model when the drawing is at 1:50? Would one scale not be simpler?

**Tutor:** One scale would be simpler in one way and worse in every other, because the drawing and the model have different jobs and different limits. The drawing's job is to fit a 12 metre structure on an A3 sheet with room for three views, dimensions and a title block, and 1:50 does that: 240 millimetres for the plan. A model at 1:50 would be 240 millimetres long, with risers of 3 millimetres and rail posts 18 millimetres tall, which is too small to cut accurately from card, too small for a glue joint to hold without swamping the part, and too small for a tender board to see across a table. The model's job is to let people see and handle the design, so it wants to be as large as the materials and the base board allow, and 1:20 gives a ramp of 360, risers of 7.5 that can be cut and checked with a steel rule, and rails of 45 that can be made from 3 millimetre dowel without looking like tree trunks. At 1:10 the model would be 1.2 metres long, too big for the bench and the car home. So each representation uses the largest standard scale that suits its medium, and the two are linked by the one set of real dimensions written on the drawing. That is why the conversion table matters: it is where 7 200 becomes 360 for the model, just as it became 144 for the drawing, and a table checked by two people is the safeguard against the single slip that builds a wrong ramp.

### subtopic_2 — Safe Working Practices with Knives, Glue and Dust
**Assistant:** Why is a blunt blade more dangerous than a sharp one? Surely sharp cuts deeper.

**Tutor:** A sharp blade cuts deeper into the card, which is what you want, and a blunt blade cuts deeper into you, which is the problem. A sharp craft knife slices mounting board with light pressure and in a straight line, following the rule, so the hand stays relaxed and the blade stays where it is aimed. A blunt blade drags; it does not bite into the surface, so the cutter pushes harder to make it cut, and a hard push is a loaded spring: the moment the blade finally breaks through, or skips off a hard spot in the card, all that force is released sideways, the blade jumps the rule and travels wherever the push was going, often towards the fingers holding the rule or off the edge of the mat into a leg. Blunt blades also tear rather than cut, leaving ragged edges that have to be recut or sanded, and they tempt people into one heavy pass instead of several light ones. The rule, therefore, is to change the blade the moment it starts to drag, which on card is more often than people expect, and to dispose of the old blade in the blade tin. The same principle applies to the saw: a sharp saw cuts dowel with its own weight and a blunt one needs force, and force is what causes slips. It is also why the knife is used in several light passes: each pass needs little pressure, so if the blade does wander it wanders slowly and shallowly. Careful and sharp are the same thing in a Technology room.

### subtopic_3 — Building Neatly to the Drawing and Checking as You Go
**Assistant:** How neat does the model have to be? Surely the ideas matter more than the glue.

**Tutor:** The ideas matter more, but the model is how the tender board, and the marker, see the ideas, and an untidy model hides good ideas as effectively as a wrong one. Picture two models of the same design on the board's table. One has clean edges, square posts, rails at an even height and a surface that reads as brushed concrete; the board sees a ramp at 1 in 12 with proper landings and rails, and talks about the design. The other has torn card edges, blobs of hot glue at every joint, three posts leaning and a rail that dips in the middle; the board sees the glue and asks whether the real ramp would be built with the same care, and the design never gets discussed. Neatness in a model is evidence of control, and control is what a client is buying. There is a second reason that matters for the marks: the model is checked against the specifications, and an untidy model cannot be checked. If the risers are rough and uneven, nobody can tell whether the design had equal risers; if the rails wander, nobody can measure 45. So neatness is not decoration added at the end; it is accuracy made visible. The habits that produce it are cheap: a sharp blade, parts cut slightly oversize and sanded to the line, glue spread thinly with a scrap of card, joints taped square until dry, and paint applied in thin coats. None of those take longer than doing the job badly and redoing it, and together they turn a reasonable design into a convincing one.
