# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the properties of construction materials, density, hardness, stiffness, flexibility, corrosion resistance and corrosion prevention, as they bear on the choice of materials for the stairs and ramp. The anchor is a tray of samples on a bench in a Technology room in East London: a block of concrete, an offcut of mild steel tube with rust blooming at one end, a galvanised bracket with its grey crystalline coat, a piece of stainless steel, a length of pine, a strip of aluminium and a lump of rubber. The governing idea is that each property is a separate, measurable thing, that a material can score high on one and low on another, and that choosing a material means matching its properties to the job of the part: dense and stiff for a slab, hard for a step nosing, corrosion resistant for anything steel outdoors. Defining the properties and using them to justify material choices is examinable.

## Subtopic: Density, Hardness and Stiffness

Density is mass per unit volume, usually in kilograms per cubic metre. Concrete is about 2 400, steel about 7 800, aluminium about 2 700, pine about 500, and water 1 000, which is why pine floats and the others sink. Density matters in two ways for the ramp. A dense material means a heavy structure: the concrete slab's 3 tonnes of dead load, which the ground must carry, comes straight from concrete's density. And density affects cost and handling: a steel rail is heavier to lift into place than an aluminium one of the same size, though steel is cheaper per metre and stiffer.

Hardness is resistance to scratching, denting and wear. Hardened steel, glass and ceramic tiles are hard; mild steel is moderately hard; aluminium, copper and most timber are soft and dent easily; rubber and plastics are softer still. Hardness matters where surfaces are rubbed or struck: the nosing of a step, which thousands of shoes scrape every day, needs a hard material or a hard insert, and a soft timber step edge rounds off within a year. Hardness is tested by pressing a hard point into the surface and measuring the dent, or by trying to scratch one material with another.

Stiffness is resistance to bending or stretching under load; a stiff material deflects little. Steel and concrete are stiff; aluminium is about a third as stiff as steel for the same shape, which is why aluminium parts are made thicker; timber is less stiff again; rubber is not stiff at all. Stiffness is separate from strength: a rubber band is strong for its weight but not stiff, and a biscuit is stiff but not strong. For the ramp, stiffness decides how much a step or a landing slab sags under a crowd and how much a handrail flexes when leaned on; a rail that bends noticeably feels unsafe even if it is nowhere near breaking, which is why handrail tube is specified by its wall thickness as well as its diameter.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of density, hardness and stiffness with typical values or examples, and why stiffness is not the same as strength.

## Subtopic: Flexibility, Brittleness and Toughness

Flexibility is the ability to bend without breaking and return to shape; it is the opposite of stiffness, not of strength. Rubber, thin steel strip, nylon and green timber are flexible. Flexibility is wanted where a part must give and recover: a rubber mat at the ramp's foot, a fishing rod, a car spring. It is unwanted in a handrail or a step.

Brittleness is the tendency to break suddenly with little bending and no warning, usually along a crack: glass, cast iron, ceramic, chalk, unreinforced concrete and many hard plastics are brittle. Toughness is the opposite, the ability to absorb energy from a blow or a shock by deforming before breaking: mild steel, which dents rather than shatters, most timbers, nylon and rubber are tough. Hardness and brittleness often go together, since the treatments that make steel very hard also make it brittle, and a designer must choose: a hard nosing resists wear but may chip, a tough one dents but stays whole.

These properties explain the material choices on the stairs and ramp. Concrete is dense, stiff, hard enough for a walking surface, and brittle, so it is reinforced with tough steel mesh and given slightly rounded nosings to avoid chipping. Mild steel for the rails is stiff, tough and moderately hard, so it takes a knock from a trolley without shattering. Timber for a handrail cap is tough and warm but soft, so it will wear and must be sealed. A rubber strip at the join between ramp and paving is flexible and tough and takes up movement. No material is best; each is chosen for the properties the part needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meanings of flexibility, brittleness and toughness, which materials show each, and why hardness and brittleness often go together.

## Subtopic: Corrosion: How Metals Rot and How to Stop It

Corrosion is the slow destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with its surroundings. For iron and steel the reaction is rusting: iron, oxygen from the air and water combine to form iron oxide, the orange-brown flaky rust, which is weaker than the metal, takes up more space so it lifts paint and cracks concrete around embedded steel, and keeps going as long as air and water reach fresh metal. Salt, as in sea air at East London or Durban, speeds rusting greatly. Aluminium corrodes too, but its oxide forms a thin hard skin that protects the metal beneath, which is why aluminium stays dull grey rather than flaking away. Copper turns green for the same protective reason. Stainless steel contains chromium, which forms a similar protective skin, so it barely corrodes at all, at a much higher price.

Corrosion prevention works by keeping air and water away from the metal or by using a metal that protects itself. Painting puts a barrier on the surface; it is cheap and any colour, but it scratches, and rust starts at every scratch, so painted steel must be repainted every few years. Galvanising dips the steel in molten zinc, which bonds as a grey crystalline coat; zinc corrodes in preference to steel, so even if the coat is scratched the zinc around the scratch protects the exposed steel, and a galvanised rail lasts twenty years or more outdoors without attention. Electroplating deposits a thin coat of chromium, nickel or zinc by electricity; it is bright and thin and suits indoor fittings. Oiling or greasing protects moving parts. Stainless steel needs no coating but costs several times more than mild steel.

Design also prevents corrosion: shaping a rail so water drains off rather than pooling, capping the open ends of tubes so rain cannot collect inside, keeping dissimilar metals apart since steel in contact with copper corrodes faster, and embedding steel posts in concrete above the level where puddles form. For the stairs and ramp, galvanised mild steel for rails and posts, with capped ends and a drained base, is the standard specification, and the analysis of rusted rails in week 4 showed what happens without it. The error museum, four exhibits. One: specifying bare mild steel outdoors because it is cheap; it rusts within a season. Two: thinking paint alone is permanent; it needs maintenance. Three: confusing stiffness with strength or hardness with toughness. Four: leaving tube ends open so they fill with water and rust from the inside.

The questions for this section are with you now: what rusting is and what speeds it, how painting and galvanising differ, and the design measures that reduce corrosion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tray of samples: concrete, rusty steel, galvanised steel, pine, aluminium, rubber — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Heavy, Hard or Stiff: Three Different Things

Density is how heavy something is for its size. Steel 7 800, concrete 2 400, aluminium 2 700, pine 500, water 1 000. Dense means heavy. The slab weighs 3 tonnes because concrete is dense.

Hardness is how well a surface resists scratches and dents. Hard steel and glass are hard. Aluminium, timber and rubber are soft. Step edges need hard material because shoes scrape them all day.

Stiffness is how little something bends under load. Steel and concrete are stiff. Aluminium bends three times more. Rubber bends completely. Stiff is not the same as strong: a rubber band is strong but not stiff.

Keep the three words apart and try the questions for this part: heavy, hard or stiff.

## Subtopic: Bends, Snaps or Takes a Knock

Flexible means it bends and springs back. Rubber, thin steel strip, a fishing rod. Good for a mat, bad for a rail.

Brittle means it snaps suddenly with no warning. Glass, chalk, cast iron, plain concrete. Tough means it takes a knock and dents instead of shattering. Mild steel, timber, nylon, rubber.

Very hard materials are often brittle. A hard step edge resists wear but may chip. A tough one dents but stays whole. You choose.

Concrete is dense, stiff, hard enough, but brittle, so it gets steel mesh inside and rounded edges. Mild steel rails are stiff and tough. Timber handrail caps are warm but soft.

Hold snap versus dent in mind and try this part's questions: bends, snaps or takes a knock.

## Subtopic: Rust and Its Enemies

Rust is iron plus air plus water making iron oxide, the orange flaky stuff. It is weaker than steel, it swells and lifts paint, and it keeps going. Salt air makes it faster.

Stop it by keeping air and water off. Paint: cheap, any colour, but every scratch starts rust, so repaint every few years. Galvanise: dip in molten zinc; the zinc protects even around a scratch, lasts twenty years outdoors. Stainless steel protects itself but costs a lot.

Design helps: let water drain, cap tube ends so they do not fill with rain, keep steel away from copper, keep posts out of puddles.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Density is mass for size, hardness resists scratching, stiffness resists bending, flexibility bends and recovers, brittle snaps and tough dents. Rust is iron reacting with air and water, faster with salt; paint is a barrier that needs renewing, galvanising protects even when scratched, stainless steel protects itself, and good design keeps water away. Galvanised steel rails with capped ends are the standard choice.
