### subtopic_1 — Static Loads: Weight That Stays Put
**Assistant:** If a learner weighs the same whether standing or jumping, how can the jump push harder on the step?

**Tutor:** Because the step does not feel the learner's weight; it feels the force needed to hold or stop the learner, and stopping something that is moving takes more force than holding something that is still. A learner standing on a step is at rest, so the step pushes up with exactly the learner's weight, about 500 newtons for 50 kilograms, and nothing more. A learner who jumps from two steps up arrives at the lower step moving downwards at a couple of metres per second, and the step must bring that motion to a stop. The shorter the stopping distance and time, the larger the force: a hard concrete tread stops the learner in a few centimetres and a few hundredths of a second, which means the force during the landing is several times the weight, perhaps 1 500 to 2 000 newtons, before it drops back to 500 once the learner stands still. You can feel this yourself: step off a kerb and your knees barely notice; jump off a wall and your knees and feet protest, although you weigh the same in both cases. Engineers call this an impact load and allow for it by multiplying the expected static load by a factor, and by making treads and landings thick and reinforced so that a sharp blow does not crack them. The same reasoning applies to a trolley dropped on the landing or a wheelchair dropping off a badly made join: the mass is the same, but the stopping is sudden, and the structure feels the suddenness.

### subtopic_2 — Dynamic Loads: Forces That Move and Change
**Assistant:** Why does a load that repeats cause damage if each single load is safe?

**Tutor:** Because materials remember. Every time a load is applied, a slab bends a tiny amount and tiny cracks at the surface or at a weak point open slightly; when the load is removed they close, but not quite perfectly, and the next application opens them a fraction further. One application does nothing you could measure. Ten thousand applications, which is roughly the number of times a school staircase is trodden in a single term, can grow a hairline crack into a visible one, and a visible crack lets in water, which in winter freezes and expands in colder parts of the country, and in all parts carries dirt and rusts any steel it reaches. This slow growth of damage under repeated loads is called fatigue, and it is why a bridge or an aircraft can fail under a load it has carried safely a million times before. Steel welds are especially prone to it, which is why a handrail that has been leaned on every day for ten years can break at a weld under a push no greater than any of the previous pushes. Designers respond in three ways: they keep the stress under each application well below the level at which cracks start to grow, they reinforce concrete with mesh so that any crack is held tight, and they detail the vulnerable points, step nosings, rail welds and post fixings, so that they can be inspected and replaced. For the PAT, the practical lesson is that the stairs and ramp are designed not for the heaviest day but for twenty years of ordinary days, and that durability, which appears in the design considerations, is partly this question of fatigue.

### subtopic_3 — Even and Uneven Loads on Stairs, Ramps and Landings
**Assistant:** Why would a wheelchair be a worse load for the slab than a crowd? The crowd weighs far more.

**Tutor:** Because the crowd spreads its weight and the wheelchair concentrates it, and a slab fails where pressure is highest, not where total weight is highest. A landing full of standing learners might carry 1 500 kilograms, but that weight arrives through hundreds of shoes, each spreading its share over a couple of hundred square centimetres, so the pressure on any one spot of concrete is modest and the whole slab shares the work. A wheelchair with its user weighs perhaps 120 kilograms, far less, but it stands on four hard wheels whose contact patches are each only a few square centimetres, so the pressure under each wheel is many times higher than under any shoe, like the difference between a flat palm and a fingertip pressing on your arm with the same force. On a slab sitting fully on good compacted fill, that high local pressure is still carried easily, because the fill directly beneath pushes back. The danger arrives when the slab is unsupported somewhere, over a hollow where fill has washed out or at an edge, so that it has to act as a beam, and then a point load in the wrong place bends it far more than the same weight spread out would. This is why the working drawing specifies compacted fill and mesh reinforcement, why the kerb is cast as part of the slab, and why the surface finish must resist wear from hard wheels. It is also why a building's floor is often rated for both a distributed load, so many kilograms per square metre, and a separate point load, so many kilograms on a small area: the two can fail the structure in different ways.
