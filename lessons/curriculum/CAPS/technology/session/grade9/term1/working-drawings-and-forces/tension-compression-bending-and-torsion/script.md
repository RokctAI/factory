# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the strength of materials under forces: tension, compression, bending of beams and torsion resisted by cross-bracing, applied to the stairs and ramp. The anchor is a workbench in a Technology room in Durban holding a length of foam beam, a steel rule, a piece of string and a cardboard box frame, each about to be pulled, pushed, bent or twisted in turn, showing what the concrete, steel and timber of the real structure will experience. The governing idea is that every force on a structure resolves, inside the material, into tension or compression, that bending is the two together on opposite faces of a beam, that torsion is a twist that frames resist poorly unless they are braced diagonally, and that knowing which face is in tension tells the designer where steel reinforcement must go. These ideas are examinable and explain the detailing of the slab, the steps and the rails on the working drawing.

## Subtopic: Tension and Compression: Pulling and Pushing Members

Tension is a pulling force that tries to make a member longer. A rope holding a bucket, the cable of a crane, the string of a bow and the steel reinforcing bar in the bottom of a concrete beam are all in tension. Materials that are strong in tension are those whose particles hold tightly together along their length: steel, which can be pulled with enormous force before it stretches and snaps; nylon rope; timber along the grain. Materials weak in tension pull apart easily: concrete, brick, stone, cast iron and glass crack under a pull a tenth of what they can carry in compression, which is why you never see a concrete rope.

Compression is a pushing force that tries to make a member shorter. The legs of a table, the columns of a building, the wall under a window and the stone of an arch are in compression. Materials strong in compression resist being squashed: concrete, brick, stone, cast iron and timber across its grain. Steel is strong in compression too, but a long thin steel member in compression has a different problem: it buckles, bowing sideways and collapsing long before the material itself is crushed, which is why compression members are kept short and thick or are braced along their length.

Testing the two is simple. Pull a strip of paper lengthwise and it holds a surprising load; push its ends together and it folds instantly, because paper has no compressive stiffness. Push on a chalk stick end to end and it holds; pull it and it snaps at the first flaw. In the stairs and ramp, the concrete slab and steps are compression members carrying weight down into the ground, the rail posts are columns, and the handrail between two posts, when someone leans on it, is partly in tension. The design puts each material where its strength lies.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of tension and compression, which materials are strong in each, and why long thin compression members buckle.

## Subtopic: Bending in Beams: Both at Once

A beam is a member that carries a load across a gap and is supported at its ends or along part of its length: a plank across a stream, a lintel over a door, a step cantilevered from a wall, or the ramp slab over a hollow where the fill has washed away. A load on a beam makes it bend, and bending is tension and compression at the same time in the same member. Picture a plank across two bricks with a person standing in the middle: the plank sags, its top surface is squeezed shorter, which is compression, and its bottom surface is stretched longer, which is tension. Along the centre of the plank's thickness there is a line, the neutral axis, that is neither stretched nor squeezed.

The bending is greatest where the beam is furthest from its supports with the most load, at the middle of a simply supported beam, and at the support of a cantilever that is fixed at one end, where the top is in tension and the bottom in compression, the reverse of the simple beam. A foam beam drawn with a grid of lines shows this clearly when bent: the lines on the top close up, the lines on the bottom open out, and the lines in the middle stay the same.

This is the key to reinforced concrete. Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension, so a plain concrete beam cracks on its tension face at a small load. Steel is strong in tension. Put steel bars or mesh in the concrete near the face that will be in tension, the bottom of a slab on fill or a simply supported step, the top of a cantilevered step near the wall, and the concrete carries the compression while the steel carries the tension, each doing what it is good at. That is why the working drawing's section shows mesh low in the ramp slab, and why a cantilevered step would have its bars near the top. Making a beam deeper is the other way to resist bending: a plank on edge is far stiffer than the same plank flat, because the top and bottom faces are further apart and the lever arm between the compression and the tension is longer.

The questions for this section are with you now: what happens to the top and bottom of a loaded beam, where bending is greatest, and why steel goes near the tension face of concrete.

## Subtopic: Torsion and Cross-Bracing

Torsion is a twisting force, applied as a turning effect about a member's long axis. Wringing out a cloth, turning a screwdriver, and the twist on a long handrail when someone grabs one end and swings are torsion. A member in torsion has shear stress spiralling round it, with the surface stressed most and the centre least, which is why a hollow tube resists torsion almost as well as a solid bar of the same diameter and far better for its weight; it is also why steel handrails are tubes. Open shapes, angles and channels, twist easily; closed tubes and boxes twist little.

Frames are weak in torsion unless braced. A rectangular frame of four members pinned at the corners, like a gate or the side of a card box, can be pushed out of square into a parallelogram with almost no effort, and if one face is pushed and the opposite face held, the whole frame twists. Adding a diagonal member turns each rectangle into two triangles, and a triangle cannot change shape without changing the length of a side: push it and one diagonal goes into compression or the other into tension, and the frame holds. This is cross-bracing, and it is the standard answer to racking and torsion in frames, from pylons and roof trusses to the X braces on the back of a bookshelf.

On the stairs and ramp, torsion appears in the handrail. A rail on posts along a ramp is a long frame; someone leaning hard at one end or a crowd pushing sideways twists the whole run and tries to rock the posts. The remedy is a diagonal brace between post and rail at each end and at the landing, or a knee brace, which triangulates the frame, together with posts set deep enough into the concrete to resist rotation at the base. A steel model of a rail with and without a diagonal, twisted by hand, makes the difference obvious. The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting the steel in the middle of the slab, where the neutral axis does no work; it belongs near the tension face. Two: thinking concrete is simply strong; it is strong in compression and weak in tension. Three: using a solid bar where a tube would be lighter and stiffer in torsion. Four: a rectangular frame with no diagonal, which racks and twists.

The questions for this section are with you now: what torsion is and why tubes resist it well, why a rectangle racks and a triangle does not, and where bracing goes on the handrail.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a strip of paper, a stick of chalk and a foam beam — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Stretched or Squashed

Tension is pulling. It tries to make a thing longer. Rope, cable, the steel inside a concrete beam. Steel loves tension. Concrete, brick and glass hate it; they crack.

Compression is pushing. It tries to make a thing shorter. Table legs, columns, walls, arches. Concrete, brick and stone love compression. Steel is fine too, but a long thin steel piece bows out sideways and buckles.

Try it: pull a paper strip, it holds; push it, it folds. Push a chalk stick, it holds; pull it, it snaps.

Put each material where its strength is. Concrete for the slab and steps that are pushed on. Steel where things are pulled.

Keep the paper and the chalk in mind and try the questions for this part: stretched or squashed.

## Subtopic: The Top Squashes, the Bottom Stretches

A beam carries a load across a gap. Put a plank on two bricks and stand on the middle. It sags. The top gets squeezed: compression. The bottom gets stretched: tension. The middle line does neither.

Bending is worst in the middle of a plank on two supports, and at the wall for a step sticking out of a wall, where it is the top that stretches.

Concrete cracks when stretched. So put steel on the stretched side: mesh low in the ramp slab, bars at the top of a step that sticks out of a wall. Concrete does the squeezing, steel does the stretching.

A plank on its edge is much stiffer than a plank flat. Deeper beams bend less.

Hold the plank on two bricks in mind and try this part's questions: top squashed, bottom stretched.

## Subtopic: Twist, and the Diagonal That Stops It

Torsion is twisting. Wringing a cloth. Grabbing one end of a long rail and swinging. A tube resists twisting almost as well as a solid bar and weighs much less, which is why rails are tubes.

A rectangle of four sticks pinned at the corners folds into a parallelogram with no effort. Add one diagonal and it becomes two triangles. A triangle cannot change shape without a side changing length. It holds. That is cross-bracing.

On the ramp, a crowd pushing the rail twists the whole run. Put a diagonal between post and rail at the ends and the landing, and set the posts deep.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Tension pulls, compression pushes; steel is strong in tension, concrete in compression. A loaded beam is compressed on top and stretched underneath, so reinforcing steel goes near the tension face and deeper beams bend less. Torsion twists; tubes resist it well and a diagonal brace turns a rectangle into triangles that cannot rack.

Hold the diagonal stick, and take the final questions of the lesson: twist, and what stops it.
