# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the working drawing of the chosen design in first angle orthographic projection and the flow chart for making the model, the last tasks of the Design stage of PAT 1 in week 6. The anchor is a team in the Eastern Cape whose Final Idea sketch shows a ramp doubling back with a 1 500 landing and a flight of four steps beside the door, now to be converted into a drawing a builder could use and a plan a model-maker can follow. The governing idea is that a working drawing is the single authoritative description of the design, drawn to the conventions learnt earlier in the term and checked against every specification, and that a flow chart turns the making into an ordered sequence with checks, so that the Make stage is executed rather than improvised. Both documents are assessed as part of Design.

## Subtopic: From Final Idea to Working Drawing

The working drawing is drawn from the Final Idea sketch using the method from earlier in the term: choose the views, choose the scale, convert the sizes in a table, set out the front view, project the plan below and the end view to the opposite side, add hidden detail, correct the line types, dimension once each in real sizes, complete the title block. For the doubled-back ramp the plan becomes the most informative view, because it shows the two legs of the ramp, the turning landing and the stairs in relation to the door and the site boundary; the elevation shows the step profile and the ramp's rise along each leg; a section through the ramp on a chain line in the plan shows its construction, the fill, the slab and the kerb.

The scale is chosen for the sheet: at 1:50 a plan 12 metres by 4 metres becomes 240 by 80 millimetres, which fits A3 comfortably with the elevation beneath and the section beside. Every dimension the builder needs appears once: in the plan the leg lengths, the landing size, the widths, the position relative to the door; in the elevation the risers as 4 at 150 equals 600, the treads, the rail height, the ramp rise per leg; in the section the slab thickness, the kerb height, the rail post fixing. The title block carries the project, the team, the scale, the first angle symbol, millimetres and the date.

The working drawing has a second purpose beyond building: it is the drawing the tender board will see in week 10, so it must be clean, with two clear line weights, lettering that can be read at arm's length and no construction lines so heavy that they compete with the outlines. A model-maker will also use it, scaling the sizes again to the model's scale, so the dimensions must be complete and consistent.

The questions for this section are with you now: the order of producing the working drawing, which view carries the most information for a turning ramp, and what the title block must contain.

## Subtopic: Checking the Working Drawing Against the Brief

Once drawn, the working drawing is checked against the brief, specification by specification, and the check is recorded. Gradient: read the ramp length per leg and the rise per leg from the dimensions, divide, confirm 1 in 12 or gentler for each leg. Width: read 1 200 or more for the ramp and stairs. Risers: read the riser dimension, multiply by the count, confirm 600, confirm all equal. Treads: 250 or more. Rails: shown on both sides in plan and at 900 to 1 000 in elevation, extending 300 beyond the ends. Landings: 1 200 or more at top, bottom and the turn, level, the top landing clear of the door swing which is drawn as an arc in the plan. Kerb: 75 shown in the section. Non-slip: a note on the drawing, brushed finish. Fits the site: the overall plan dimensions within 12 by 4 and clear of the path.

Any failure is corrected on the drawing, not excused. A ramp leg that comes out at 1 in 11 is lengthened; a landing that would push the stairs into the path is moved. This check is the Evaluate stage working inside Design, and the record of it, a copy of the specification list with ticks and the dimension that proves each tick, goes into the PAT file. It is also what will make the week 8 evaluation of the model straightforward: if the drawing meets the brief and the model follows the drawing, the model meets the brief.

A second check is the drawing's own consistency: do widths in the plan sit under the same widths in the elevation, do the heights in the elevation agree with the section, does every dimension appear exactly once, and are the three line weights distinct. A classmate who has not seen the design is the best checker; if they can read the riser, the gradient and the landing size without asking, the drawing works.

The questions for this section are with you now: how each specification is checked against the dimensions, what is done about a failure, and how the drawing's consistency is checked.

## Subtopic: The Flow Chart: Planning the Making in Order

A flow chart is a diagram of a process as a sequence of steps, each in a box, joined by arrows that show the order, with diamond-shaped boxes where a decision is made and the path splits. In Technology it is used to plan the Make stage so that nothing is forgotten, nothing is done before the thing it depends on, and quality is checked at the right moments. The standard symbols are few: an oval for start and end, a rectangle for an action, a diamond for a yes or no decision with two labelled arrows leaving it, and arrows for the flow.

For the stairs and ramp model, the chart might run: Start. Gather materials and tools: card, balsa, dowel for rails, glue, cutting mat, craft knife, ruler, PVA, paint. Convert working drawing sizes to model scale, say 1:20, in a table. Mark out the base board with the plan. Cut the ramp legs and landing from card to the scaled sizes. Check: are the leg lengths and rise correct for 1 in 12? If no, recut; if yes, continue. Assemble the ramp legs and landing on the base with supports. Cut and assemble four steps with equal risers. Check: are all risers equal? If no, adjust; if yes, continue. Fit the kerb along the ramp edges. Cut dowel rail posts to scaled 900 plus fixing depth and fit at scaled 1 500 spacing. Fit handrails along the posts on both sides. Paint or finish surfaces, texturing the walking surfaces. Final check against the specification list. End.

The flow chart does three things for the team. It splits the two weeks of making into tasks that can be shared and scheduled. It puts the checks where errors are cheapest to fix, after cutting and before gluing. And it shows the marker that the making was planned. A flow chart also needs the sequence to respect dependencies: rails cannot be fitted before posts, posts before the ramp, the ramp before the base is marked out. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a flow chart with no decision diamonds, so nothing is ever checked. Two: steps out of order, painting before the rails are fitted and then gluing onto paint. Three: a working drawing with a dimension missing, discovered only when the model-maker needs it. Four: skipping the specification check on the drawing, so a 1 in 11 ramp is built faithfully into the model.

The questions for this section are with you now: the flow chart symbols, the sequence of making the model, and where the checks are placed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Final Idea sketch on the left and a clean A3 sheet on the right — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Drawing the Builder Uses

The working drawing is the real drawing of your final design. Same method as before: views, scale, table, front view, plan below, end view opposite, hidden detail, line types, dimensions, title block.

For a ramp that turns, the plan shows the most: two legs, the landing, the stairs, the door, the site edge. The elevation shows the steps and the rise. A section shows the slab, the fill and the kerb.

At 1:50 the 12 by 4 site is 240 by 80 on paper. Fits A3. Every size once. Title block with scale, symbol, millimetres.

Make it clean. The tender board will see it. The model-maker will use it.

Keep the clean A3 in mind and try the questions for this part: the drawing the builder uses.

## Subtopic: Does the Drawing Keep Every Promise?

Now check the drawing against your specifications, one by one. Gradient: length over rise for each leg, 12 or more. Width 1 200. Four risers of 150, all equal. Treads 250. Rails both sides at 900, sticking out 300. Landings 1 200, flat, top one clear of the door arc. Kerb 75 in the section. Note saying brushed finish. Fits inside 12 by 4.

If something fails, fix the drawing. Do not excuse it. Write the check in the file with the dimension that proves each tick.

Then check the drawing against itself: views lined up, every size once, three line weights. Ask a classmate to read the riser and the gradient without help.

Hold the tick list in mind and try this part's questions: keeping every promise.

## Subtopic: Boxes and Arrows for Building

A flow chart plans the making. Oval for start and end. Rectangle for a job. Diamond for a yes or no check with two arrows out. Arrows for order.

Start. Gather tools and card. Convert sizes to 1:20. Mark the base. Cut ramp legs and landing. Check the gradient: no, recut; yes, go on. Build the ramp. Cut and build four equal steps. Check the risers. Fit the kerb. Fit posts, then rails. Texture and paint. Final check. End.

Checks go after cutting and before gluing, where fixing is cheap. Posts before rails, ramp before posts, base before ramp.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The working drawing is the final design in first angle projection with every size once and a clean title block, checked specification by specification with the check recorded. The flow chart plans the making as boxes and arrows with decision diamonds for checks, in an order that respects what depends on what.

Hold the diamond and its two arrows, and take the final questions of the lesson: boxes and arrows for building.
