### subtopic_1 — From Final Idea to Working Drawing
**Assistant:** Why draw a section? The plan and elevation already show the ramp.

**Tutor:** They show its shape, but not what it is made of inside, and the inside is what the builder has to construct and the tender board has to pay for. A plan shows the ramp from above: its route, width, landing and kerb line. An elevation shows its slope and the rails. Neither shows how thick the concrete slab is, what lies under it, how the kerb is formed, how deep the rail posts are set, or whether there is a retaining wall holding back the fill at the open edge. A section answers all of that in one view. You imagine slicing the ramp across its width along a chain line marked in the plan with arrows and letters, and you draw what the cut face would show: the slab 150 thick with its mesh, the compacted fill below it, the block retaining wall at the edge, the 75 kerb cast on top, the rail post set 300 into the concrete, the brushed surface indicated by a note. The section is where the material quantities for the budget come from, since slab thickness times area gives concrete volume, and it is where the model-maker learns how the ramp must be supported. Sections are drawn by architects and engineers for every structure that has an inside, which is all of them; a wall section, a stair section and a foundation section are standard sheets in any building set. For the PAT, one section through the ramp and one through the stairs are enough, and they lift the working drawing from a picture of the design to a description of how to build it.

### subtopic_2 — Checking the Working Drawing Against the Brief
**Assistant:** How do I know what scale to use for the model, and does the working drawing change?

**Tutor:** The working drawing does not change; it stays at 1:50 and states real sizes, and the model is simply another reader of it, like the builder. The model scale is chosen by the same logic as the drawing scale: the largest standard scale at which the whole thing fits the base board and the materials available. A structure 12 by 4 metres at 1:20 becomes 600 by 200 millimetres, which suits a base board of about 700 by 300, and at that scale a 150 riser is 7.5 millimetres, a 1 200 width is 60, a rail at 900 is 45 high, all of which can be cut from card and dowel with reasonable accuracy. At 1:50 the model would be 240 by 80 and the risers 3 millimetres, too fiddly; at 1:10 it would be 1.2 metres long and need a table to itself. So 1:20 is the usual choice for this PAT, and the first box in the flow chart after gathering materials is to convert every dimension on the working drawing to model size in a table, dividing by 20, exactly as the drawing conversion divided by 50 earlier. That table is kept with the model and checked against the drawing, because a slip there, a leg cut to 300 instead of 360, builds a 1 in 10 ramp into the model and fails a specification the drawing had met. The point to hold on to is that there is one set of true sizes, on the working drawing, and two scaled copies of them, the drawing itself at 1:50 and the model at 1:20, and the dimensions written on the drawing are the real ones that both copies are derived from.

### subtopic_3 — The Flow Chart: Planning the Making in Order
**Assistant:** Where exactly should the decision diamonds go? I could check after every step.

**Tutor:** You could, and the chart would be twice as long and nobody would follow it; the skill is to put a check where an error is likely and where fixing it is still cheap. Two rules find those places. First, check after any step that fixes a size that a specification depends on and before the step that makes it permanent. Cutting the ramp legs fixes the gradient; gluing them down makes it permanent; so the diamond, is the leg length right for 1 in 12, goes between cutting and gluing, because a wrongly cut card leg costs a minute to recut and a wrongly glued one costs the ramp. Cutting the steps fixes the risers; assembling them makes them permanent; so the diamond, are all risers equal, goes between. Second, check before any step that cannot be undone or that depends on many earlier steps being right: before painting, which hides joints and cannot be reversed, and at the end, where the whole model is held against the specification list for the final time. Three or four diamonds are usually enough for this model: gradient after cutting the legs, risers after cutting the steps, rail height after cutting the posts, and the final specification check before the end oval. Each diamond has two arrows, yes continuing down the chart and no looping back to the step that must be redone, and that no loop is the important part: it tells the maker what to do when the check fails instead of leaving them to improvise. Real production flow charts in factories follow the same rule, inspecting where defects are caught cheaply, and the habit of asking where would a mistake hurt most is one the PAT is trying to build.
