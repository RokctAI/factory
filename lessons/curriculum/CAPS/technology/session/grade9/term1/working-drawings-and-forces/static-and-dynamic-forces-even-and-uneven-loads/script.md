# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers static and dynamic forces and even and uneven loads as they act on structures, applied to the stairs and ramp. The anchor is a school hall entrance in Soweto at the final bell, when three hundred learners pour down four steps in two minutes while a wheelchair user waits on the landing for the rush to pass, and the question of what the concrete and the rails feel in that moment compared with the quiet of the night before. The governing idea is that a structure carries two kinds of force, static loads that are constant and dynamic loads that move or change, that dynamic loads can be several times more severe than the same weight at rest, and that the way a load is distributed, evenly across a surface or concentrated at a point, decides where a structure is stressed most. These ideas are examinable and inform the slab thickness, the foundations and the rail design in the working drawing.

## Subtopic: Static Loads: Weight That Stays Put

A static load is a force that does not change with time. The largest static load on any structure is usually its own weight, called the dead load: for the ramp, the concrete slab, the fill beneath it, the kerb and the rails, all pressing down on the foundation and the ground every hour of every day. A ramp 7 200 long, 1 200 wide and 150 thick holds about 1.3 cubic metres of concrete, and concrete weighs about 2 400 kilograms per cubic metre, so the slab alone presses down with roughly 3 100 kilograms of weight, before any fill or any person stands on it.

Other static loads come and go but, while present, are steady: a wheelchair and its user parked on the landing, a delivery trolley left on the ramp, a stack of chairs waiting by the door. These are live loads, loads from use, but they are static in the sense that while they are there they do not move or change. Engineers design for the heaviest static live load that could reasonably occur, such as a landing packed with standing people at a prize-giving, which for a public building is taken as several hundred kilograms per square metre.

Static loads are predictable, which makes them the easier part of design. The weight of concrete is known, the area of the slab is on the drawing, so the dead load can be calculated exactly and the ground beneath checked to see whether it can carry it. Soft or sandy ground may need a wider foundation or compacted hardcore to spread the static load so that the ramp does not settle and crack. The structure must carry its static loads without any part being overstressed and without sinking or tilting over time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of static load, dead load and live load, the approximate weight of the ramp slab, and why static loads are the easier part of design.

## Subtopic: Dynamic Loads: Forces That Move and Change

A dynamic load is a force that changes with time: it moves, it is applied suddenly, or it repeats. The learners running down the stairs at the bell are a dynamic load. So is a wheelchair bumping over the join at the top of the ramp, a delivery trolley dropped off a bakkie onto the landing, a learner jumping the last two steps, a crowd leaning on the handrail together to watch something in the playground, or wind pushing on a long rail. Vehicles reversing into a ramp edge, and in some places earthquakes, are dynamic loads that engineers also consider.

Dynamic loads are more dangerous than static loads of the same size for two reasons. First, impact: a mass that is moving has to be stopped, and stopping it quickly requires a force far larger than its weight. A learner of 50 kilograms standing on a step applies about 500 newtons; the same learner landing from a jump of two steps can apply three or four times that for a fraction of a second. Second, repetition: a load applied thousands of times, every school day for years, can cause cracks to grow in concrete or welds to fail in steel through fatigue, even though no single application is anywhere near the breaking load. Engineers allow for both by designing to a multiple of the expected static load and by detailing the structure to resist cracking.

For the stairs and ramp, the dynamic loads shape three decisions. Treads and the landing slab are made thicker and reinforced with mesh so that impact loads do not crack them. Rails and their posts are designed for a horizontal push, not just a vertical load, because a crowd leaning on a rail pushes sideways, and the posts must be set deep enough into the concrete to resist being levered out. And the step nosings, the front edges, are the parts that take the most impact and wear, so they are either rounded, reinforced or fitted with a nosing strip.

The questions for this section are with you now: examples of dynamic loads on the stairs and ramp, why impact and repetition make dynamic loads more severe, and the three design decisions they shape.

## Subtopic: Even and Uneven Loads on Stairs, Ramps and Landings

The distribution of a load matters as much as its size. An even load, also called a uniformly distributed load, is spread equally over a surface or along a beam: the dead weight of a slab on its fill, or a landing full of people standing shoulder to shoulder. Every part of the supporting structure carries its share, and the stress is spread. An uneven load is concentrated at one place or varies along the structure: a wheelchair's four small wheels pressing on a few square centimetres each, a trolley wheel on a kerb, a post pulling at its fixing, or a crowd bunched at one end of a landing while the other end is empty. The concentrated point carries the whole force, and the structure must be strong enough there, not just on average.

Think of the ramp slab. Sitting on compacted fill, its own weight is an even load and the fill carries it everywhere. A wheelchair user is an uneven load, perhaps 120 kilograms through four small contact points, each pressing with the pressure of a stiletto heel; the slab must be thick enough and reinforced enough that a wheel on any spot does not punch through or crack the surface. A hollow under the slab, where the fill has washed out, turns the slab into a beam spanning the gap, and a point load over the hollow becomes the most dangerous case: that is how ramps crack. The kerb along the edge is another uneven-load problem, since a wheel striking it pushes sideways at one point.

Uneven loading also produces turning effects. A crowd bunched on one side of a landing supported on posts tries to tip the landing; a rail post pushed sideways at the top tries to rotate about its base; a cantilevered step, fixed at one end only, is bent most where it meets its support. The designer counters these by spreading supports, by anchoring posts deep, and by avoiding designs where a load can act far from its support. In the working drawing this appears as a slab thickness, a mesh specification, post embedment depth and the choice of concrete on fill rather than a slab on posts. The error museum, four exhibits. One: designing for the dead load only, as if the stairs were never used. Two: treating a running crowd as the same as a standing one. Three: assuming a load is spread evenly when wheels and posts concentrate it. Four: a rail designed for weight but not for a sideways push.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between even and uneven loads, why point loads are dangerous for a slab, and the turning effects uneven loads cause.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Standing Still Versus Moving

A static load is a weight that stays still. The biggest one is the structure's own weight, the dead load: the ramp slab alone is about 3 tonnes of concrete pressing down day and night.

Other still loads come and go: a parked wheelchair, a trolley, chairs stacked by the door. These are live loads, but while they sit there they are static.

Static loads are easy to predict. Concrete weighs 2 400 kilograms per cubic metre. Multiply by the volume. Check the ground can take it. Soft ground needs a wider base.

## Subtopic: Why a Crowd on the Stairs Is Different

A dynamic load moves or changes. Three hundred learners running down the stairs. A chair bumping over a join. A learner jumping the last two steps. A crowd leaning on the rail. Wind.

Moving things hit harder. A learner standing presses with their weight. The same learner landing from a jump presses three or four times as hard, for a moment. And a load that repeats every day for years can crack concrete slowly.

So: thicker treads and landings with mesh inside. Rails built for a sideways push, posts set deep. Step edges rounded or reinforced because they take the hits.

Hold the jump and the lean in mind and try this part's questions: why moving loads are worse.

## Subtopic: Spread Out or Bunched Up

An even load is spread out: the slab's own weight on the fill, a landing full of people standing together. Every bit of the structure carries a share.

An uneven load is bunched up: a wheelchair's four small wheels, a trolley wheel on the kerb, a crowd at one end of the landing. One spot carries it all.

A slab on good fill handles both. A slab over a washed-out hollow with a wheel on top is how ramps crack. A crowd on one side tries to tip a landing. A push at the top of a post tries to lever it out.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Static loads stay still and include the structure's own dead weight; dynamic loads move, hit or repeat and can be several times more severe. Even loads spread over the structure; uneven loads concentrate at a point and can tip or lever. The slab thickness, mesh, post depth and rail strength on the working drawing come from these ideas.

Hold the four small wheels and the washed-out hollow, and take the final questions of the lesson: spread out or bunched up.
