### subtopic_1 — Tension and Compression: Pulling and Pushing Members
**Assistant:** If concrete is so weak in tension, why not build the whole ramp out of steel?

**Tutor:** Because strength is only one of the things a material is chosen for, and for a ramp the other things favour concrete, as long as the tension problem is solved, which reinforcement does. Steel is strong in tension and compression, but it is expensive per kilogram, it rusts outdoors unless galvanised or painted and maintained, it is slippery when wet and noisy underfoot, it conducts heat so a steel deck is painful in the Durban sun, and a steel ramp must be fabricated by welders rather than cast on site by a general building crew. Concrete is cheap, is made from local sand and stone, is poured into any shape on site, takes a non-slip brushed finish, stays cool, does not rust and lasts for decades with no maintenance. Its single serious weakness is that it cracks under tension, and the invention of reinforced concrete solved that by putting steel exactly where the tension is, a few bars or a sheet of mesh near the face that stretches, so that a slab uses a small amount of expensive steel where it is needed and a large amount of cheap concrete everywhere else. The result is a composite that is better than either material alone. That is why the working drawing shows a concrete slab with mesh near its bottom and steel only for the rails, where a slender member that must be gripped and must resist a sideways push suits steel tube far better than concrete. The analysis of existing products in week 4 will have shown the same pattern in almost every ramp in town.

### subtopic_2 — Bending in Beams: Both at Once
**Assistant:** How do I know which face of a beam is in tension? It seems to change.

**Tutor:** It does change, and the rule for finding it is to ask which way the beam sags and then to remember that the outside of a curve is stretched and the inside is squeezed. A plank resting on two supports with a load in the middle sags downwards; the bottom face is on the outside of that curve, so it stretches and is in tension, and the top face is on the inside, squeezed, in compression. Now take a step that sticks out from a wall with nothing under its far end, a cantilever, and stand on the end: it bends downwards too, but the curve is the other way up, hogging over the wall, so near the wall the top face is on the outside and stretches, and the bottom is squeezed. That is why a cantilevered balcony or step has its reinforcing bars near the top at the support, and a simply supported beam has them near the bottom at midspan. A continuous beam over several supports, like a long slab on posts, sags between supports and hogs over them, so the tension face swaps from bottom to top along its length and real reinforcement is placed accordingly. A quick way to check with your hands is to bend a rubber eraser or a soft foam block the way the beam would bend and watch which face opens up; the opening face is in tension. For the ramp slab on continuous fill there is very little bending at all, which is the point of the fill, but wherever a hollow or an edge leaves the slab unsupported it sags into the gap, and the bottom face is the one that needs the mesh.

### subtopic_3 — Torsion and Cross-Bracing
**Assistant:** Why does one diagonal make such a difference to a frame? It is only one more piece of wood.

**Tutor:** Because it changes the geometry of the frame from a shape that can move to a shape that cannot, and geometry matters more than the amount of material. A rectangle made of four members pinned at the corners has a hidden freedom: the corners can rotate, so the rectangle can lean into a parallelogram without any member changing length. Nothing in the frame resists that movement except the stiffness of the joints, which in a pinned or lightly glued frame is almost nothing, so a small sideways push racks it and a twist distorts it. A triangle has no such freedom. Its three sides fix its three angles completely; the only way to change its shape is to lengthen or shorten a side, and that means pulling or pushing a member along its length, which is exactly what members are strong at. Adding one diagonal to the rectangle divides it into two triangles, and now any attempt to rack the frame must stretch one diagonal or compress the other, so the push is turned into tension or compression in a member instead of rotation at a joint. The diagonal does not need to be heavy; it needs to be there. The second diagonal of a full X brace is added so that whichever way the frame is pushed, one diagonal is in tension, which matters when the brace is a cable or thin rod that would buckle in compression. Engineers call this triangulation, and it is why pylons, roof trusses, cranes, bicycle frames and the bracing on the back of a cheap bookshelf are all made of triangles. For the handrail, one knee brace between post and rail at each end does the same job for the long frame of posts and rail that a crowd is trying to twist.
