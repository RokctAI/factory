# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the analysis of existing products as part of the Investigate stage of PAT 1, applied to stairs and ramps. The anchor is a walk through a small town in the Free State with a camera and a tape measure: the ramp at the Spar, the steps at the clinic, the church entrance and the ramp bolted to the side of a school classroom block twenty years after it was built. The governing idea is that every existing product is a finished experiment, and that judging it against the design considerations, fitness for purpose, materials, safety and cost, turns that experiment into lessons for the team's own design. Analysing a product against these criteria, with evidence, is examinable and earns Investigate marks in week 4.

## Subtopic: Fitness for Purpose: Judging a Ramp by Its Users

Fitness for purpose asks one question of a product: does it do what its users need, in the place and conditions where it is used? For a ramp the users are wheelchair users first, then everyone else, and the needs are specific: a gradient they can climb, a width they fit, a surface they will not slip on, rails they can reach, landings where they can rest and a top that meets the door on the level. For stairs the users are everyone who can walk, and the needs are equal risers, treads that fit a foot, rails for the unsteady and a landing clear of the door.

Analysing an existing ramp against fitness for purpose means measuring, not guessing. Measure the rise and the length and work out the gradient: a mall ramp of 450 rise over 5 400 length is 1 in 12, which passes; a clinic ramp of 600 over 3 600 is 1 in 6, which fails, and a quick test with a loaded wheelbarrow will show why. Measure the width and ask whether a chair and a helper fit. Press a shoe on the wet surface and note whether it grips. Check whether the rails are at 900 to 1 000, on both sides, and whether they extend past the ends. Look at the top: is there a flat landing, or does the ramp arrive at the door still sloping. Each observation is written as a fact with a number, then judged against the standard.

Beyond the measurable, fitness for purpose includes ergonomics and aesthetics. Ergonomics asks whether the product suits human bodies: a rail too thick to grip, a tread too shallow for a size 10 shoe, a door handle unreachable from a chair on a slope. Aesthetics asks whether the product looks as if it belongs: a galvanised steel ramp bolted to a brick school with no attempt to match looks like an apology, and users notice. Both are legitimate design considerations and both appear in the analysis.

The questions for this section are with you now: what fitness for purpose asks of a ramp and of stairs, how to measure a gradient on site, and what ergonomics and aesthetics add.

## Subtopic: Materials, Safety and Cost in Existing Entrances

Materials are judged by suitability for the job and the place. Concrete is the common choice for ramps and stairs: it is strong in compression, durable outdoors, cheap per cubic metre and can be given a non-slip finish by brushing it before it sets. Its weaknesses are weight, which needs a foundation, and the time it takes to set. Concrete blocks with a cast slab are lighter and quicker. Steel framing with a steel or timber deck is quick to erect and can be prefabricated, but steel rusts and must be galvanised or painted and maintained, and a steel deck is slippery when wet unless it is textured. Timber is warm to the touch and easy to work but rots, splinters and is slippery when wet; it suits rails more than decks outdoors. Each existing entrance shows one of these choices and, often, what has gone wrong with it after years of use: spalling concrete, rusted bolts, rotten boards.

Safety is judged by looking for what could cause harm. Unequal risers, a missing rail, a rail with a gap a child could fall through, a ramp with no kerb, a surface that pools water, a landing too short for a chair to stand level while the door opens, a nosing that has chipped away. Each hazard is recorded, with a photograph if possible, and the analysis asks why it exists: was it designed in, or has it developed through wear and poor maintenance.

Cost is judged in two parts: what the product probably cost to build, and what it costs to keep. A plain concrete ramp costs more to build than a timber one but almost nothing to maintain; a steel ramp is cheap to install but needs painting every few years. The analysis estimates, from the materials and size, roughly what each entrance would cost today, which prepares the ground for the team's own budgeting in week 9. A good analysis notices that the cheapest entrance to build is often the most expensive to own.

The questions for this section are with you now: the strengths and weaknesses of concrete, steel and timber for ramps, the hazards to look for, and the two parts of cost.

## Subtopic: Writing an Analysis That Feeds the Design

The written analysis has a standard shape. For each product: a labelled sketch or photograph with key dimensions; a short description of what it is, where it is and who uses it; then a table with a row for each design consideration, fitness for purpose, materials, safety, cost, ergonomics, aesthetics, impact on the environment, and three columns: what was observed, judged against what standard, and the lesson for our design. The lesson column is the point of the exercise. A ramp at 1 in 6 teaches that length must be found even if the ramp has to turn; a rotten timber deck teaches that outdoor decks should be concrete or textured steel; a ramp with no top landing teaches that the door and the landing must be designed together.

Three or four products are enough if they are different from one another: at least one ramp that works well, one that works badly, one set of stairs, and if possible one entrance that combines stairs and ramp. The comparison between them is more instructive than any one alone. Where no real examples are available, photographs from the internet or textbooks may be used, with the source noted, but measured local examples earn more because they can be checked.

The analysis feeds directly into the design brief. Every lesson becomes a specification, a constraint or a design consideration: the 1 in 12 limit, the top landing, the non-slip finish, rails on both sides, a kerb along the ramp, galvanised steel for fittings. A team that skips the analysis writes its brief from memory and misses what the town's own entrances could have taught it. The error museum, four exhibits. One: describing a ramp as good or bad without a number; the gradient is a measurement. Two: judging on aesthetics alone; a beautiful ramp at 1 in 8 is a failure. Three: listing faults without the lesson; the analysis is for the design, not for complaint. Four: analysing four identical ramps; variety is what teaches.

The questions for this section are with you now: the layout of the analysis table, how many products to analyse and why, and how the analysis becomes specifications.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a camera and a tape measure outside the Spar — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Does It Do the Job for the People?

Fitness for purpose means: does it do the job for the people who use it, where they use it?

For a ramp: can a wheelchair user climb it, fit on it, grip the rails, rest on a landing, and reach the door on the flat? For stairs: are the steps equal, deep enough, with rails?

Measure, do not guess. Rise and length give the gradient. 450 over 5 400 is 1 in 12, good. 600 over 3 600 is 1 in 6, a slide. Measure the width, the rail height, the landing.

Also ask: does it suit a body, and does it look as if it belongs there?

Keep the tape measure in mind and try the questions for this part: judging by the users.

## Subtopic: What It Is Made Of and What Can Go Wrong

Materials. Concrete: strong, lasts outdoors, can be brushed for grip, but heavy and slow. Steel: quick, but rusts and gets slippery. Timber: nice to hold but rots and slips when wet. Good for rails, not decks.

Safety. Look for: odd-sized steps, missing rails, gaps in rails, no kerb, puddles, a short landing, chipped edges. Was it built wrong or has it worn out?

Cost. Two parts: what it cost to build and what it costs to keep up. Cheap to build is often dear to own.

Hold concrete, steel and timber in mind and try this part's questions: what things are made of and what goes wrong.

## Subtopic: Turning Looking into Notes

Write it up as a table. For each entrance: sketch or photo with sizes. Then rows for fitness, materials, safety, cost, ergonomics, looks. Three columns: what you saw, what the standard says, what it teaches your design.

Look at three or four different ones. One good ramp, one bad, one stair, one with both.

Every lesson becomes a line in your design brief: 1 in 12, top landing, grippy surface, rails both sides, kerb, galvanised fittings.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Analyse existing stairs and ramps against fitness for purpose, materials, safety and cost, with measurements. Concrete lasts, steel rusts, timber rots. Hazards include unequal steps, missing rails and no top landing. Write a table with observation, standard and lesson, for three or four different products, and turn every lesson into a specification.

Hold the table and the lesson column, and take the final questions of the lesson: turning looking into notes.
