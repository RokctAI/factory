# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Term 1 Practical Assessment Task through the design process and begins the Investigate stage. The anchor is the entrance to a new school hall in Mpumalanga, 600 millimetres above the paving, where a learner who uses a wheelchair waits at the bottom while the rest of the class files up a loose stack of concrete blocks. The governing idea is that the PAT is not a drawing exercise or a model-making exercise but a complete pass through IDMEC, with each stage assessed, and that the Investigate stage, done well, supplies the facts that every later stage depends on: the rise, the space, the users, the soil and the existing solutions elsewhere. The marks for Investigate are earned in week 4, and the habits of looking, measuring and asking are examinable in their own right.

## Subtopic: The Problem Situation: An Entrance That Shuts People Out

The problem situation is deliberately realistic. Many South African schools have received new buildings whose floors are raised above the surrounding ground, to keep them dry and to allow for drains and cables beneath. Where the builder's contract ended at the door, the way up has been left to improvise: a stack of blocks, a plank, a single tall step. Such an entrance is dangerous for everyone and impossible for some. A learner in a wheelchair cannot enter at all; a parent with a pushchair, a teacher carrying a box of books, a grandmother at a prize-giving, all struggle. The school needs a proper flight of stairs and a wheelchair ramp.

The scenario places each team in the role of a small building firm invited to tender. A tender is a formal offer to do a job for a stated price, backed by drawings and a plan; the client, here the school governing body, chooses between several tenders on quality, cost and fitness for purpose. Over the term each team will investigate the problem, design a solution, draw it properly, cost it, build a scale model, and present its tender to a board made up of classmates and the teacher, who will judge it against the other teams' bids.

The job has a social dimension that the design must respect. Access to education is a right, and a building that excludes a learner because of a disability denies that right. The brief will later set numbers, but the purpose behind the numbers is a door that every learner can reach with dignity, by themselves, in the rain, at the start and end of every school day.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the problem situation is, what a tender is, and why access matters beyond convenience.

## Subtopic: IDMEC as the Map for the Whole Term

IDMEC gives the term its shape. Investigate, weeks 1 to 4: understand the problem, the users, the site and existing solutions; this stage is assessed in week 4 for 15 marks. Design, weeks 5 and 6: write the design brief with specifications and constraints, sketch two possible solutions, evaluate them, choose one and produce working drawings; assessed for 20 marks. Make, weeks 7 and 8: build a neat scale model of the chosen solution, safely, to the working drawings; assessed for 35 marks. Evaluate: test the model and the design against the specifications and against the evaluation instrument the team wrote in week 5. Communicate, week 10: present the tender bid, sketches, plans, budget and model, to the tender board.

Notice that the stages are not strictly one after another. Evaluation happens inside Design, when the two sketches are judged, and again after Make, when the model is tested. Investigation continues into Design when costs are checked. The process is a spiral more than a straight line, but the order of the big stages holds: you cannot sensibly design before you have investigated, nor make before you have designed.

Each stage leaves evidence in the PAT file: notes and sketches from the site visit, the analysis of existing products, the design brief, the two ideas, the evaluation table, the working drawing, the flow chart, the budget, photographs or the model itself, and the presentation notes. The file is what is marked, so a team that investigates thoroughly but writes nothing down has not, for assessment purposes, investigated at all.

The questions for this section are with you now: which weeks belong to which stage, the mark allocation, and why the stages overlap.

## Subtopic: Investigating the Site and Possible Structures

Investigation starts at the site. Measure the rise, the vertical distance from the paving to the hall floor, at several points, because paving is rarely level; record the largest figure. Measure the space available in front of and beside the door: how far can a ramp run before it meets a path, a tree, a parking bay or a fence. Note where the door swings and where rainwater runs. Look at the ground: is it paved, concrete, soil or sand, because the foundation will depend on it. Photograph or sketch the entrance from several angles with dimensions written on.

Then watch and ask the users. Count how many people use the door at the busiest time. Ask the learner who uses a wheelchair what works and what fails at other entrances they know. Ask the caretaker what breaks and what is hard to clean. Ask the principal whether deliveries come through this door. Every answer becomes a line in the design brief later.

Then think about possible structures. Stairs could be a straight flight, or two short flights with a landing. A ramp could run straight out, or turn through 90 or 180 degrees with a landing to save space; it could be built of concrete on compacted fill, of concrete blocks with a slab, or of steel framing with a timber or steel deck, each with different cost, durability and construction time. Rails could be steel tube, galvanised or painted, or timber. The investigation does not choose yet; it lists the options with their likely strengths and weaknesses so that the Design stage has something to choose between. The error museum, four exhibits. One: measuring the rise once; paving slopes and the ramp must clear the highest point. Two: forgetting to measure the space, so the design is drawn and then found not to fit. Three: skipping the users, especially the one for whom the ramp exists. Four: deciding on a solution before investigating, so that the investigation merely decorates a choice already made.

The questions for this section are with you now: what to measure on site, who to ask, and the structural options to list.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a stack of loose blocks at a hall door — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Door Nobody Can Reach

A new hall floor stands 600 millimetres above the paving. The way up is a pile of blocks. A learner in a wheelchair cannot get in at all. Everyone else risks a fall.

Your team is a small building firm. You are going to make a tender: an offer to build proper stairs and a ramp, with drawings, a cost and a model. The school will pick the best bid.

This matters. Every learner has the right to get through that door, alone, with dignity, even when it rains.

Keep the pile of blocks in mind and try the questions for this part: the problem and the tender.

## Subtopic: Five Steps and Ten Weeks

IDMEC maps the term. Investigate: weeks 1 to 4, 15 marks in week 4. Design: weeks 5 and 6, 20 marks. Make: weeks 7 and 8, 35 marks. Evaluate: judge your ideas and test your model. Communicate: week 10, present your bid.

The stages overlap a little. You evaluate while you design. You keep investigating costs while you draw. But the big order holds: investigate, then design, then make.

Everything goes in the PAT file. Notes, sketches, brief, drawings, budget, model photos. The file is what gets marked. If it is not in the file, it did not happen.

Hold 15, 20, 35 in mind and try this part's questions: the weeks and the marks.

## Subtopic: Looking Hard Before Drawing

Go to the site. Measure the rise in a few places; use the biggest. Measure the space: how far can a ramp go before it hits something. Look at the ground. Watch the door swing. Sketch it with sizes on.

Ask people. The learner in the wheelchair. The caretaker. The principal. Count how many use the door at once.

List the options: straight stairs or two flights; straight ramp or one that turns; concrete, blocks or steel; steel or timber rails. Do not choose yet. Just list, with good and bad points.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The PAT is a tender for stairs and a ramp at a raised entrance. IDMEC maps the ten weeks: investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate, with marks for each. Investigation means measuring the site, asking the users and listing possible structures before anything is chosen, and writing it all in the file.

Hold the tape measure and the notebook, and take the final questions of the lesson: looking before drawing.
