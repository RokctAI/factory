### subtopic_1 — The Problem Situation: An Entrance That Shuts People Out
**Assistant:** Why is a school project framed as a tender? Builders tender, not learners.

**Tutor:** Because the tender is the form that real design work takes in the construction world, and framing the PAT that way teaches habits that an ordinary school project would not. When a school governing body needs a ramp built, it does not buy one off a shelf; it describes the need, invites several firms to propose a solution with a price, and chooses between them. Each firm must therefore do exactly what IDMEC asks: investigate the site and the users, design a solution that meets the rules, draw it so the client can see what they are buying, cost it honestly, and present it persuasively. A tender also introduces competition and judgement. The board does not ask whether a design is nice; it asks whether it meets the specifications, whether the price is realistic, whether the drawings are clear enough to build from and whether the firm has understood the users. Working towards that kind of judgement makes a team more careful than working towards a mark alone, and it is the judgement the Technology curriculum wants learners to be able to apply to products around them. Finally, many Grade 9 learners will go on to trades, technical colleges or engineering, where tenders, bids and quotations are daily work, and others will one day sit on a governing body or a committee that has to choose between tenders. Learning what a good bid looks like serves both.

### subtopic_2 — IDMEC as the Map for the Whole Term
**Assistant:** If evaluation happens inside design and after making, why is it listed as a separate stage?

**Tutor:** Because listing it separately makes sure it is done deliberately, with evidence, rather than left as a feeling. Evaluation is the stage in which a design is compared against the specifications that were written for it, and that comparison is useful at several moments: when choosing between two sketches, when the working drawing is finished, when the model is built, and when the real product has been in use for a year. Designers do evaluate all the way through, often without noticing, every time they look at a sketch and think that will not work. The danger is that this informal judging is biased: people favour their own idea, forget a specification that is inconvenient, or test only what they expect to pass. The Evaluate stage of IDMEC answers that by requiring an instrument, a written list of criteria drawn from the specifications with a way of scoring each, applied to every candidate in the same way and recorded in the file. In this PAT the team writes that instrument in week 5, uses it to choose between the two sketches, and uses it again on the finished model, so that the final judgement is against the same yardstick as the first. Treating Evaluate as a named stage is what forces that instrument to exist. Without it, the project would still contain evaluation, but of the unreliable kind, and the file would have nothing to show the marker.

### subtopic_3 — Investigating the Site and Possible Structures
**Assistant:** Why measure the rise in several places? The floor is level, so the height is the height.

**Tutor:** The floor may be level, but the ground almost never is, and the ramp and stairs start from the ground. Paving laid outside a building is usually given a fall of a few millimetres per metre so that rainwater runs away from the door, and old paving settles unevenly as the soil beneath it moves. Measured at the door the rise might be 600 millimetres; measured three metres out along the line where the ramp will start, it might be 650, because the ground has fallen away, or 560 because of a hump. A ramp designed to 600 and built from a point that is actually 650 below the floor is either too steep, failing the 1 in 12 rule, or too short, arriving 50 millimetres below the threshold and leaving a step exactly where a wheelchair cannot cope with one. Stairs built to four risers of 150 from a point 650 down end with a fifth odd step of 50, which is the classic trip hazard. So the investigation measures the rise at the door, at the foot of the proposed stairs and at the foot of the proposed ramp, records them all, and designs to the largest, adding a note that the first riser or the start of the ramp may need to be adjusted on site. This is a small example of a large principle in building: the drawing describes an ideal, and the site is real, and the investigation is where the two are brought together before anything is cast in concrete.
