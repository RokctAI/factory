# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the realistic costs of materials, labour and transport as part of the Investigate stage of PAT 1. The anchor is a counter at a builders' merchant in Rustenburg, where a learner with a notebook asks the price of a bag of cement, a cubic metre of river sand and a six-metre length of galvanised tube, and then the question that the merchant cannot answer, which is how much the whole ramp will cost. The governing idea is that every construction cost is made of three parts, materials, labour and transport, that prices come from suppliers and quantities come from the drawing, and that an estimate is a method, not a guess: quantity times unit price for every item, added up, with an allowance for waste and the unexpected. Building such an estimate is examinable and is the foundation of the week 9 budget and the tender price.

## Subtopic: Three Parts of Every Building Cost

When a school pays a builder for a ramp, the money goes to three places. Materials: the cement, sand, stone, reinforcing steel, blocks, tube, bolts, paint and shuttering timber that end up in or around the structure. Labour: the wages of the bricklayer, the general workers who dig, mix and carry, the welder who makes the rails, and the supervisor who makes sure it is done right, counted in days or hours at a rate. Transport: the cost of getting materials from the merchant to the site, usually a delivery charge or a hired bakkie, and of getting workers to and from the job each day.

The three behave differently. Materials cost is fixed by the design: a longer ramp needs more concrete and the price per cubic metre does not care who pours it. Labour cost depends on skill and on time: a skilled bricklayer earns more per day than a general worker, and a job that takes two weeks costs twice as much labour as one that takes one. Transport depends on distance and on how many trips: a site far from town, or a job ordered in small lots, pays more. A realistic estimate separates the three so that each can be checked and so that the tender board can see where savings might be possible.

There is also the cost that is easy to forget: waste and contingency. Some cement is spilt, some blocks break, some timber is cut short; builders allow about 10 percent extra on materials. And something always goes wrong, a buried pipe, a week of rain, a price rise; a contingency of 10 percent on the total covers it. A tender without these allowances looks cheap and loses money, which is why real builders include them and why the PAT budget should too.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three parts of building cost, how each behaves, and why waste and contingency are added.

## Subtopic: Finding Real Prices and Measuring Real Quantities

Prices come from suppliers, and the only realistic price is one somebody will actually charge today. A hardware store or builders' merchant gives prices for cement by the 50 kilogram bag, sand and stone by the cubic metre or by the bakkie load, reinforcing mesh by the sheet, steel tube by the 6 metre length, bolts by the unit, paint by the 5 litre tin. A steel fabricator quotes for rails by the metre, made and galvanised. Labour rates come from asking a local builder what a bricklayer and a general worker earn per day, or from published guides. Transport is a delivery charge quoted by the merchant or the hire cost of a bakkie per trip. All prices are written down with the source and the date, because they change.

Quantities come from the drawing, which is why the working drawing is done before the budget. Concrete volume is length times width times average thickness: a ramp 7 200 long, 1 200 wide, averaging 300 thick is 7.2 times 1.2 times 0.3, which is 2.59 cubic metres, call it 2.6. Stairs: four steps each 1 200 wide, 250 deep, averaging the riser heights, about 0.45 cubic metres, plus a foundation slab. Landing: 1 200 by 1 200 by 0.15, 0.22 cubic metres. Rails: two sides of the ramp at 7 200 plus the stairs at about 1 100 along the slope plus the landing, roughly 2 times 9.5, which is 19 metres of handrail, plus posts every 1 500, about 14 posts of 1 metre, 14 metres of post tube.

For concrete mixed on site the usual mix for paths and ramps is 1 part cement to 2.5 parts sand to 2.5 parts stone, and one cubic metre needs about 7 bags of cement, 0.5 cubic metres of sand and 0.5 cubic metres of stone. So 3.3 cubic metres of concrete needs about 23 bags, 1.7 cubic metres of sand and 1.7 of stone. Ready-mixed concrete delivered by truck is priced per cubic metre and saves labour but costs more and needs access for the truck. Both options are costed and the cheaper that suits the site is chosen.

The questions for this section are with you now: where prices and quantities come from, how to calculate a concrete volume, and the materials in a cubic metre of site-mixed concrete.

## Subtopic: A Worked Estimate for a Concrete Ramp

The estimate is a table with columns: item, quantity, unit, unit price, total. Materials: 23 bags of cement at the merchant's price; 1.7 cubic metres of sand; 1.7 cubic metres of stone; 2 sheets of reinforcing mesh; 19 metres of 40 millimetre galvanised handrail tube and 14 metres of post tube, or a fabricator's quote for the rails complete; 16 anchor bolts; shuttering timber; 5 litres of non-slip paint or a brushed finish at no extra cost. Add 10 percent for waste. Labour: one bricklayer and two general workers for 8 working days, at the daily rates found, plus a welder for 2 days if rails are made on site. Transport: two deliveries from the merchant plus the fabricator's delivery, or three bakkie loads. Subtotal all three, then add 10 percent contingency, and that is the estimated cost.

Working each line with real local prices will give a total that is typically in the range of several tens of thousands of rands for a concrete ramp and stairs with steel rails at a school; the precise figure depends on the town and the year, which is why the estimate quotes its sources. What matters for the PAT is that every line is traceable: a tender board member should be able to pick any figure, ask where it came from, and get an answer in the form of a quantity from the drawing times a price from a named supplier.

Two comparisons make the estimate useful. First, materials versus labour: for concrete work, materials usually cost more than labour, while for steelwork labour and fabrication dominate; knowing this tells the team where a cheaper design would actually save money. Second, this design versus an alternative: a straight ramp versus a turning one, site-mixed versus ready-mixed concrete, fabricated rails versus timber. The error museum, four exhibits. One: prices from memory or from a textbook printed years ago; prices must be current and sourced. Two: quantities guessed instead of calculated from the drawing. Three: forgetting labour, as if the ramp built itself. Four: no allowance for waste or contingency, so the tender is sure to lose money.

The questions for this section are with you now: the columns and lines of the estimate, why every figure must be traceable, and the two comparisons the estimate allows.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again at the counter of the builders' merchant with a notebook — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Materials, People and Getting It There

Every building job costs money in three ways. Materials: cement, sand, stone, steel, bolts, paint. Labour: the people who dig, mix, lay and weld, paid per day. Transport: getting the stuff and the people to the site.

Materials depend on the design. Labour depends on skill and days. Transport depends on distance and trips.

Add a bit extra. About 10 percent on materials for waste, broken blocks and spilt cement. About 10 percent on everything for surprises, like rain or a buried pipe.

Keep the three parts in mind and try the questions for this part: materials, people and getting it there.

## Subtopic: Prices from the Shop, Quantities from the Drawing

Prices come from shops and suppliers, today. Cement by the bag. Sand and stone by the cubic metre. Tube by the 6 metre length. Rails by the metre from a steel fabricator. Labour by the day from a local builder. Delivery from the merchant. Write the price, the shop and the date.

Quantities come from your drawing. Concrete is length times width times thickness. The ramp: 7.2 times 1.2 times 0.3 is about 2.6 cubic metres. Stairs and landing add about 0.7. Call it 3.3.

One cubic metre of site-mixed concrete takes about 7 bags of cement, half a cubic metre of sand and half of stone. So 3.3 cubic metres needs about 23 bags, 1.7 of sand, 1.7 of stone.

Hold 7 bags per cubic metre in mind and try this part's questions: prices from the shop, quantities from the drawing.

## Subtopic: Adding It Up for One Ramp

The estimate is a table: item, how many, unit, price each, total. Cement 23 bags. Sand 1.7. Stone 1.7. Mesh 2 sheets. Handrail 19 metres, posts 14 metres. Bolts 16. Shuttering. Add 10 percent for waste.

Labour: a bricklayer and two workers for 8 days, a welder for 2. Transport: a few deliveries. Add it all up, then 10 percent for surprises.

Every number must be traceable: a quantity from your drawing times a price from a named shop.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Building cost is materials plus labour plus transport, plus allowances for waste and contingency. Prices come from suppliers today, quantities from the drawing. Concrete volume is length times width times thickness, and a cubic metre needs about 7 bags of cement. The estimate is a table where every line can be checked.

Hold the table and the 10 percent, and take the final questions of the lesson: adding it up for one ramp.
