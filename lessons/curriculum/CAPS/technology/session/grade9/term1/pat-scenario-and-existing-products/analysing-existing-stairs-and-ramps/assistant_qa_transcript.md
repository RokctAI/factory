### subtopic_1 — Fitness for Purpose: Judging a Ramp by Its Users
**Assistant:** Why analyse other ramps at all? We already have the rules in the brief.

**Tutor:** Because the rules tell you what a ramp must be, and existing ramps show you what happens when those rules meet real builders, real budgets and twenty real winters. A rule says 1 in 12; a ramp at the clinic shows you what 1 in 6 feels like under a loaded wheelbarrow and why the rule exists. A rule says non-slip surface; a steel ramp outside the post office shows you what smooth painted steel does in the first rain, and the rust under the paint shows you what happens when nobody maintains it. A rule says a landing at the top; a school ramp built as an afterthought shows you a chair user holding the door with one hand and the rail with the other because the ramp arrives at the threshold still sloping. None of that is in the rule book, and all of it changes how you design. Existing products also show you solutions you would not have thought of: a ramp that doubles back on itself to fit a narrow site, a kerb cast as part of the slab, a rail with a second lower tube for children and chair users. And they give you realistic costs, since you can see what materials were used and estimate what the job would cost today. Designers in every field do this; a car company takes apart its competitors' cars and an architect walks through other people's buildings. The analysis is where the brief stops being a list and starts being understood.

### subtopic_2 — Materials, Safety and Cost in Existing Entrances
**Assistant:** Which material is best for a ramp? Just tell me so I can put it in the brief.

**Tutor:** There is no single best, and the honest answer is a comparison, which is why the analysis judges materials by suitability rather than ranking them. Concrete suits a permanent outdoor ramp at a school very well: it carries the loads, lasts for decades, needs almost no maintenance and takes a brushed non-slip finish, and its weaknesses, weight, the need for a foundation and a week or two to set, are acceptable when the ramp is being built once and left for a generation. Steel suits a ramp that must be installed quickly, perhaps during a holiday, or one that may need to be moved, and it can be fabricated off site and bolted down in a day; but every steel ramp you analyse will show rust somewhere, so steel must be galvanised and the deck textured, and the brief must allow for repainting. Timber suits rails and handrails, where its warmth and grip are an advantage and it is easy to replace, but outdoor timber decks rot, splinter and become slippery, and the analysis of any old timber ramp will show all three. A good brief therefore says something like: concrete ramp and stairs with brushed finish, galvanised steel rails with a timber handrail, and gives reasons drawn from the analysis. When the team costs the design in week 9, the comparison will matter again, because concrete costs more to build and less to keep, and the tender board will ask which the school can afford.

### subtopic_3 — Writing an Analysis That Feeds the Design
**Assistant:** How can I say what an existing ramp cost if nobody tells me? Is it just a guess?

**Tutor:** It is an estimate, which is a guess with a method behind it, and learning to make one is part of the Investigate stage. Start from what you can measure. A concrete ramp 7 200 long, 1 200 wide and averaging 300 deep contains about 2.6 cubic metres of concrete; a builder's merchant or a hardware store will tell you what ready-mixed concrete or the cement, sand and stone to make it costs per cubic metre. The rails are so many metres of steel tube; a steel supplier gives a price per metre. Add the labour, which builders quote per day for a team, and the days it would take. Add transport. The total will not be exact, but it will be in the right range, and the range is what matters: it tells you whether a ramp like this costs tens of thousands of rands or hundreds of thousands, and therefore what the school can realistically afford and what your own design should aim for. Comparing two existing entrances this way also shows which choices drive cost: a turning ramp needs more concrete and more rail than a straight one; a steel ramp is cheaper to install but needs paint every few years, which the analysis should count. Writing the estimate down with its assumptions, so many cubic metres at so much, so many metres of rail at so much, makes it checkable, and that is the habit the budgeting lesson in week 9 and the tender board in week 10 will both expect.
