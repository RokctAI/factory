### subtopic_1 — Three Parts of Every Building Cost
**Assistant:** Why separate materials, labour and transport? The school just pays one total.

**Tutor:** The school pays one total, but the tender board, and the team itself, needs to see the three parts to judge whether the total is honest and where it could change. Materials are the part that the design fixes: if the board asks why one team's price is higher, and the answer is that its ramp is longer and turns through a landing, that shows in the concrete and rail quantities, and the board can decide whether the extra access is worth the money. Labour is the part that skill and time fix: a team that has allowed two days for a job that takes two weeks has either misunderstood the work or is hoping to cut corners, and a board that sees only a total cannot tell. Transport is the part that geography fixes, and it is the one most often forgotten by beginners; a rural school thirty kilometres from the nearest merchant may pay as much to move the materials as for some of the materials themselves. Separating the three also lets the team find savings in the right place. If materials are 60 percent of the cost, shortening the ramp by using a landing saves real money, while haggling over the welder's day rate saves little. And it protects against a common trick in bad tenders, which is to price the materials fairly and hide an unrealistic labour figure in the total. Real building estimates are always itemised this way, and the PAT budget copies that form so that the team learns to read one as well as write one.

### subtopic_2 — Finding Real Prices and Measuring Real Quantities
**Assistant:** Prices change all the time. How can my estimate be right if the cement price goes up next month?

**Tutor:** It cannot be right forever, and no estimate is; what it can be is right for a stated date, with its sources shown, so that anyone can update it. That is exactly why the estimate records where and when each price was obtained: cement at a named merchant on a given date, labour rates from a named builder, delivery from the merchant's current charge. If the cement price rises by 10 percent next month, the reader multiplies the cement line by 1.1 and the total moves by a known amount; without the source and date there is nothing to update and the whole estimate becomes a rumour. Real tenders handle the same problem in two ways that the PAT can borrow. First, they state a validity period, saying that the price holds for, say, 30 days, after which it must be re-quoted. Second, they carry a contingency, the 10 percent added to the total, which absorbs modest price rises along with rain and buried pipes; a rise within that band does not break the budget. For larger or slower projects, contracts include an escalation clause that adjusts the price by an agreed index, but a ramp that takes two weeks does not need that. The lesson for a Grade 9 team is not to chase the perfect price but to be transparent about the imperfect one, because a figure with a source is useful even when it is out of date and a figure without one is useless even when it happens to be right.

### subtopic_3 — A Worked Estimate for a Concrete Ramp
**Assistant:** Is ready-mixed concrete cheating? It seems like skipping the real work.

**Tutor:** It is not cheating; it is a design decision with its own costs and benefits, and the estimate is where those are weighed. Ready-mixed concrete arrives in a truck already mixed to a specified strength, which guarantees the quality, saves the labour of mixing by hand, and lets a ramp be poured in a morning instead of over several days. Against that, it costs more per cubic metre than the cement, sand and stone to make it yourself, the truck charges for delivery and for waiting, there is usually a minimum load, and the truck needs access to within a few metres of the pour, which a school entrance tucked behind a fence may not allow. Site-mixed concrete needs no truck and uses cheaper materials, but it needs more labour, a mixer or strong backs, and careful supervision so that every batch is the same, because a weak batch in the middle of a ramp is a weak ramp. The team's job is to cost both: ready-mixed as cubic metres at the supplier's delivered price with a note about access, and site-mixed as bags, sand and stone plus the extra labour days. Whichever comes out cheaper and suits the site goes in the tender, and the comparison itself is good evidence for the Investigate marks, because it shows the team understands that labour and materials trade against each other. Real builders make this choice on every job, and there is nothing lazy about choosing the method that gives a better ramp for less money.
