### subtopic_1 — What a Tender Board Wants to Hear
**Assistant:** Why does the board want the investigation and the evaluation? Surely the final design is what they are buying.

**Tutor:** They are buying the design, but they can only judge whether it is the right design by seeing how it was reached, and the investigation and evaluation are the evidence of that. A board shown only a model and a price has no way to tell whether the ramp turns because the site demanded it or because the team liked the look; whether 1 in 12 was chosen with the wheelchair user in mind or copied from a textbook; whether the budget's hardcore quantity came from the ground conditions or from a guess. When the team shows the site measurements, the interview with the learner who uses a chair, the analysis of the clinic ramp at 1 in 6 and the scorecard that put Idea B ahead of Idea A on fit and ergonomics, the board sees a design that was reasoned into existence and can trust that the parts it cannot check, the foundation depth, the fill, the fixing of the posts, were reasoned the same way. Real tender boards work like this too; a bid that arrives as a price and a drawing with no method statement is marked down or rejected, because the client is not just buying an object but hiring judgement. There is also a plain fairness point for the PAT: the Investigate and Design stages carry 35 of the 70 marks, and the presentation is where that work is shown to have happened. A team that skips them in the talk is hiding half its work from the people who are about to score it.

### subtopic_2 — Structuring the Presentation: Sketches, Plans, Budget, Model
**Assistant:** What if the board asks something none of us can answer?

**Tutor:** Then you say so, and you say what you would do to find out, and you lose almost nothing, whereas inventing an answer can lose the whole bid. Boards ask hard questions partly to see what the team does under pressure, and the reaction they respect is a calm, honest one: we did not check the bearing capacity of the ground; we would ask the school for the builder's soil report or dig a trial hole before pricing the foundation. That answer shows the team knows what it does not know and knows how to find out, which is exactly the judgement a client is hiring. Compare the bluff: the ground is fine, it is all solid. A board member who knows the site, or simply asks how you know, exposes it, and from that moment every other figure in the bid is suspected. There are two ways to make this situation rare. The first is preparation: before the presentation the team lists the ten questions it would ask if it were on the board, finds the answers in the file and writes the key numbers on a cue card, so that most questions are met with a figure and a page reference. The second is to listen to the whole question before answering, because many hard questions are really a misreading of the drawing or the budget, and the right response is to show the board where the answer already is: the kerb is here in the section, 75 high. Boards are made of people, and people forgive not knowing far more readily than they forgive being misled.

### subtopic_3 — Speaking, Answering and Judging Other Bids Fairly
**Assistant:** How can I score a team fairly when my own team is competing against them?

**Tutor:** By scoring the bid against the sheet and writing a reason for every number, which is the same discipline the evaluation instrument imposed in week 5, and by remembering that the teacher's score runs alongside yours. The scoring sheet is built from criteria every team has lived with all term: understanding of the problem, specifications met, drawings, model, budget, presentation and answers. For each, you write a score and a short reason that points at something you saw or heard: drawings 4, three views aligned and dimensioned, section included; budget 3, totals clear but hardcore quantity not explained. A reason like that can be defended if anyone asks, and it is hard to write a dishonest one, because the evidence either exists or it does not. You also score during and immediately after each presentation, before memory blurs and before you have talked to anyone, which removes the temptation to adjust scores once you know how your own team is doing. It helps to notice that fairness is in your interest: the same board, including members of the team you are scoring, will score you, and a class where everyone marks by friendship produces random results in which good work is not rewarded, including yours. Finally, the award is given with reasons, so a score that does not match the reasons given by others and by the teacher stands out, and a learner who scores by grudge or friendship is not being clever, only visible. Treat the other team's bid as a client would, with the school's money at stake, and the scoring looks after itself.
