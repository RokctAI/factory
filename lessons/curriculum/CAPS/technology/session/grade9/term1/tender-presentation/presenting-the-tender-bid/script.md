# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the Communicate stage of PAT 1: teams present their tender bid, sketches, plans, budget and model, to the tender board in week 10. The anchor is a classroom in Welkom arranged as a boardroom, with a table for the presenting team, their model and drawings, and a row of chairs for the board made up of the teacher and members of the other teams, each holding a scoring sheet. The governing idea is that a tender presentation is a structured argument that a design meets the client's needs at a defensible price, supported by evidence from every stage of IDMEC, and that both presenting and judging are skills with rules: clear structure, shared speaking, honest answers, and scoring against criteria rather than against people. Communicating the design and evaluating others' designs are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Tender Board Wants to Hear

A tender board is a group of people who must choose one bid and spend the client's money on it, and they listen for four things. Does this team understand the problem and the users, which shows in how they describe the site, the rise, the learner who waits at the bottom. Does the design meet the specifications, which shows in the drawing and the model, and the board will check the gradient, the risers, the landings and the rails. Is the price realistic and traceable, which shows in the budget page and in how the team answers a question about any line. And can this team be trusted to build it, which shows in the quality of the drawings and model, in the safety and method of their making, and in whether they answer questions honestly.

The board is not looking for the loudest team or the longest talk. It has four or five bids to hear in a lesson, so each team has a fixed time, usually five to eight minutes plus questions, and a team that runs over is cut off and marked down. It is not impressed by claims; it wants evidence. Our ramp is very safe means nothing; our ramp is 1 in 12 with a 1 200 landing at the turn and galvanised rails on both sides at 900, as shown in the plan and the section, is what a board writes down.

Knowing the audience shapes the language. Board members are intelligent non-specialists, so technical terms are used and briefly explained: first angle orthographic projection, shown as the three views; 1 in 12, explained as one up for every twelve along. Numbers are given with their reasons. The team speaks to the board, not to the model or the floor, and uses the model and drawings as things to point at rather than to read from.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four things a board listens for, why evidence beats claims, and how the audience shapes the language.

## Subtopic: Structuring the Presentation: Sketches, Plans, Budget, Model

The presentation follows IDMEC, because that is the story the file tells and the order the board expects. Opening, half a minute: the team, the problem in one sentence, the solution in one sentence. Investigate, one minute: the site measurements, who the users are, what the analysis of existing entrances taught, with one photograph or sketch. Design, two minutes: the design brief in a sentence, the two ideas shown as the sketches, the evaluation scorecard and why the chosen idea won, then the working drawing, pointing out the plan, the elevation and the section and reading off the key dimensions. Make, one minute: the model, how it was built to the drawing, the check against specifications, with the model turned so the board sees the views. Budget, one minute: the section totals and the grand total, what is included and excluded, the date of the prices. Close, half a minute: why the board should choose this bid, in two or three plain points.

Speaking is shared. Each team member takes a stage and speaks for it, which means each must know their own part well and the others' parts well enough to help. Handing over between speakers should be quick and natural, a nod and the next person speaking, not an announcement. Visual aids are the sketches, the drawing, the budget page and the model, mounted or held so the board can see them, plus at most one summary sheet of key numbers. Reading a script word for word is avoided; cue cards with the key figures are fine.

Rehearsal is what separates a clear presentation from a muddled one. Teams time a full run-through, cut what does not fit, and practise the handovers and the pointing. They also predict the board's questions and prepare the answers: why this gradient, what the ramp costs per metre, what happens if the cement price rises, why the ramp turns, how the model was checked. The best preparation for a question is knowing the file; the answer to almost any question the board can ask is already written in it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the order and timing of the presentation, how speaking is shared, and how a team rehearses and prepares for questions.

## Subtopic: Speaking, Answering and Judging Other Bids Fairly

When a question comes, the rule is answer it, with evidence, briefly. If the gradient is questioned, the answer is the dimension on the drawing and the length on the model. If a budget line is questioned, the answer is the quantity and the source. If the team does not know, the honest answer is that they do not know but will find out, which boards respect far more than a bluff, because a bluff found out costs the whole bid its credibility. Questions are answered by the member who knows best, and no one talks over a teammate. Criticism of the design is heard calmly; if the board is right, the team says so, and if the board has misread something, the team shows where the drawing or budget answers it.

Each team also sits on the board for the other teams' bids, and judging is done with a scoring sheet built from the design considerations and the specifications, the same yardstick every team has used all term: understanding of the problem, meets specifications, quality of drawings, quality of model, realism and clarity of the budget, quality of presentation and answers. Each is scored with a short reason. The sheet is completed during and immediately after each presentation, before the next begins. Scores are for the bid, not for friends; a board member who marks a team down because of a quarrel, or up because of a friendship, has failed the task, and the teacher's own scoring is there to catch it.

Finally the board compares the bids, and the tender is awarded with reasons, which the winning and losing teams both hear. Losing a tender is normal in building and the reasons are the useful part: the team learns which of understanding, design, drawings, model, budget or presentation let it down, and that goes in the file as the final evaluation. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a presentation that describes the model and skips the investigation and the evaluation, so the board cannot see why this design. Two: one member speaking for the whole team. Three: bluffing an answer. Four: scoring other teams by friendship rather than by the sheet.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to answer questions, how the board scores, and what happens when the tender is awarded.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again in a classroom set up as a boardroom, with your model on the table — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Who You Are Talking To

The board has to choose one bid and spend the school's money. They listen for four things: do you understand the problem and the users, does your design meet the specs, is your price real, can you be trusted to build it.

They want evidence, not claims. Not very safe. Instead: 1 in 12, landings of 1 200, rails at 900 both sides, here on the plan.

They are clever but not builders. Use the proper words and explain each one in a few words. Give numbers with reasons. Talk to the board, point at the model.

Keep the four things in mind and try the questions for this part: who you are talking to.

## Subtopic: The Order of the Story

Tell the story in IDMEC order. Opening: who you are, the problem, the answer, half a minute. Investigate: the site, the users, what other ramps taught you, one minute. Design: brief, two ideas, the scorecard, the winner, the drawing, two minutes. Make: the model and its checks, one minute. Budget: totals, included, excluded, one minute. Close: why pick us, three points.

Everyone speaks. One stage each. Know your bit and know the others' bits a little. Quick handovers.

Rehearse with a timer. Cut what does not fit. Guess the questions and prepare the answers. They are all in your file.

Hold the six-part story in mind and try this part's questions: the order of the story.

## Subtopic: Say It, Show It, Answer It

When asked a question: answer it, with evidence, short. Gradient? Point to the dimension. Price? Give the quantity and the source. Do not know? Say so and say you will find out. Never bluff.

When you are on the board: score each team on the sheet, with a reason for each score, straight away. Score the bid, not the people.

The tender is awarded with reasons. Losing is normal. The reasons are what you keep.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A tender board listens for understanding, specifications met, a real price and a trustworthy team, and wants evidence not claims. Present in IDMEC order in a fixed time, every member speaking, rehearsed, with the drawings, budget and model as visual aids. Answer questions briefly with evidence and admit what you do not know. Judge other bids on the scoring sheet, not on friendship.

Hold the scoring sheet and the honest answer, and take the final questions of the lesson: say it, show it, answer it.
