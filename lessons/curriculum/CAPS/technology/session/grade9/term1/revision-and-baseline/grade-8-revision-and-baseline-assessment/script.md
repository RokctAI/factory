# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Grade 8 Technology content that Grade 9 builds on, and prepares learners for the baseline assessment written in the first week of the year. The anchor is a Grade 9 classroom in Polokwane on the first Monday, where the teacher has pinned last year's best working drawing, a gear-train model and a circuit board to the wall as reminders of the three strands: structures, mechanical systems and electrical systems. The governing idea is that CAPS Technology is a spiral: the same strands return each year at a higher level, so what was learnt in Grade 8 is not finished with but is the floor on which Grade 9 stands. The baseline assessment is diagnostic, not a formal mark, and its purpose is to show the teacher and the learner where the gaps are before the first PAT begins.

## Subtopic: What Grade 8 Technology Left in Your Toolbox

The Technology design process, IDMEC, was the spine of every Grade 8 project. Investigate: find out about the problem, the users and existing solutions. Design: write a design brief with specifications and constraints, sketch ideas and choose one. Make: build the model from a plan, safely. Evaluate: test the product against the specifications and suggest improvements. Communicate: present the work with drawings, a model and a short talk. Every PAT in Grade 9 follows the same five stages, so a learner who can name them and say what belongs in each is already halfway prepared.

Graphic communication was the second tool. Grade 8 used dark outlines, feint construction lines and dashed hidden detail, drew to scale, dimensioned in millimetres and produced single-view working drawings. It added isometric projection on a grid, with the three visible faces of an object drawn at 30 degrees to the horizontal. Grade 9 will add first angle orthographic projection, which shows three separate views instead of one picture, so the line conventions and scale skills from Grade 8 are exactly what the new drawing method needs.

Fitness for purpose was the standard against which every design was judged: does it do the job for the people who use it, safely, at a sensible cost, with acceptable looks, with attention to ergonomics and without harming society or the environment? Those design considerations will be used again this term when existing staircases and ramps are analysed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five stages of IDMEC, the Grade 8 line conventions, and the meaning of fitness for purpose.

## Subtopic: Structures, Mechanisms and Electricity in One Sweep

Structures. Grade 8 studied frame structures such as pylons and roof trusses, and the forces that act on their members: tension pulls a member longer, compression pushes it shorter, bending is a mixture of the two across a beam, torsion twists, and shear slides one part across another. Triangulation and bracing stiffen a frame, and structures fail by fracture, buckling or toppling. The strength of a structure depends on its shape as well as its material, which is why a thin steel angle iron can carry more than a thick timber beam of the same mass.

Mechanical systems. Grade 8 revised the three classes of lever and added the wedge, the wheel and axle, cams, cranks and gears. Spur gears of unequal size trade speed for force: a small driver turning a large driven gear slows the output and increases the turning force, and the gear ratio is the number of teeth on the driven gear divided by the number on the driver. An idler gear between two gears makes them turn the same way without changing the ratio. Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort, and a machine with a mechanical advantage above 1 multiplies force at the cost of distance.

Electrical systems. Grade 8 built simple circuits with cells, switches, lamps, resistors and buzzers, drew them with standard symbols, connected cells in series and in parallel, and met Ohm's law in words: for a fixed resistance, more voltage gives more current. It also looked at how electricity is generated in South Africa, from coal-fired power stations to hydroelectric schemes and wind farms, and at simple AND and OR logic using switches.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five forces on structural members, what a gear ratio tells you, and Ohm's law in words.

## Subtopic: How the Baseline Assessment Works and What It Is For

The baseline assessment is a short written task in the first week of Grade 9. It does not count towards the term mark. Its job is to find out what each learner remembers, so that the teacher can plan: if most of the class has forgotten how to dimension a drawing, that gets revised before the orthographic projection lessons; if gear ratios are shaky, that gets attention before Term 2. For the learner it is a mirror: it shows which Grade 8 ideas are solid and which need a second look before they are needed in a PAT.

A typical baseline covers four areas. Design process: name the IDMEC stages and say what happens in one of them. Drawing: identify line types on a given drawing, or sketch a simple object in isometric. Structures and mechanisms: name forces on labelled members, or calculate a gear ratio from tooth counts. Electricity: draw a circuit from a description, or state what happens to a lamp when a second cell is added in series. Questions are short and marked quickly, because the point is information, not ranking.

The best preparation is not cramming but recall. Read through last year's book, cover the explanations and try to reproduce the main idea of each page from memory, then check. Draw the line conventions once. Work one gear ratio and one mechanical advantage from numbers you make up. Draw one series circuit and one parallel circuit. An hour of that kind of active revision is worth more than an evening of rereading.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: treating the baseline as a test to pass; it is a measurement, and an honest blank answer tells the teacher more than a guess. Two: thinking Grade 8 content is over; every strand returns this year. Three: confusing the gear ratio with the mechanical advantage of a lever; both are ratios, but of different quantities. Four: drawing an isometric view when asked for a working drawing; a working drawing is flat, to scale, with dimensions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of the baseline, what it typically covers, and how to prepare for it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with last year's book open on the desk — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Five Big Ideas to Carry Forward

Grade 8 gave you five big ideas, and Grade 9 uses all of them.

One: the design process, IDMEC. Investigate, design, make, evaluate, communicate. Every project goes through those five steps in that order.

Two: drawing. Dark lines for outlines, feint lines for construction, dashed lines for hidden edges. Draw to scale, put the sizes on in millimetres.

Three: structures. Members are pulled, pushed, bent, twisted or sheared. Triangles make a frame stiff.

Four: mechanisms. Gears trade speed for force. Gear ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth. Mechanical advantage is load over effort.

Five: electricity. Cells push, current flows, resistance slows it down. Series and parallel. Symbols for every part.

Keep the five ideas in mind and try the questions for this part: naming each one and what it is about.

## Subtopic: A Quick Run Through Last Year

Here is Grade 8 in two minutes.

Term 1 was a structure for housing, with a frame, bracing and an isometric drawing. Term 2 was the impact of technology, packaging and the forces that act on materials. Term 3 was the mine headgear with gears and mechanical advantage. Term 4 was electricity: circuits, Ohm's law, logic gates and how the country makes its power.

Every term had a design brief, sketches, a drawing, a model and a presentation. That is IDMEC working.

This year the pattern repeats with new content: a staircase and wheelchair ramp, hydraulics and pulleys, an electronic device, and preserving and recycling materials.

Hold the four terms in mind and try this part's questions: which strand each term belonged to.

## Subtopic: Facing the Baseline Calmly

The baseline assessment is a short test in week one. It does not count. It tells your teacher what you remember so the lessons can start in the right place.

If you do not know an answer, leave it or write what you think. A guess that looks right hides a gap that would have been fixed.

To get ready, do not reread. Cover the page, say the idea, then check. Draw one circuit, one gear pair, one set of line types. That is enough.

The baseline assessment measures what you remember so that the year starts in the right place, and the way to prepare is active recall, not rereading.

Hold the covered page and the pencil, and take the final questions of the lesson: what the baseline is for and how to prepare.
