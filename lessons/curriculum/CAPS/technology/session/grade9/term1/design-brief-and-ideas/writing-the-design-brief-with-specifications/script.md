# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers writing the design brief with specifications and constraints for the stairs and ramp of PAT 1, the first task of the Design stage in week 5. The anchor is the PAT file of a team in Limpopo, open at a page headed Design Brief, with the site measurements, the user interviews and the analysis of existing products from the Investigate stage stacked beside it as the raw material. The governing idea is that a brief is a precise statement of the problem and the required solution, that specifications are testable statements of what the product must be, that constraints are the limits imposed from outside, and that a good brief lets the team evaluate its own ideas objectively later. Writing a brief with correct specifications and constraints is examinable and earns Design marks.

## Subtopic: What a Design Brief Is and Is Not

A design brief is a short, clear statement of what is to be designed, for whom, where and why. It is written after investigation and before any sketching, and it is addressed to the designer, who may be the team itself or someone else; the test of a brief is whether a competent stranger could read it and set to work. For the PAT the brief might read: Design a flight of stairs and a wheelchair ramp, with handrails, to give safe access for all learners, staff and visitors from the paved playground to the entrance of the new hall at a certain school, where the hall floor is 600 millimetres above the paving, in a space 12 metres long and 4 metres wide beside the door, to be built in concrete and steel within a budget to be agreed.

Notice what the brief contains: the product, stairs and ramp with handrails; the users, all learners, staff and visitors, which includes wheelchair users; the purpose, safe access; the place, a named entrance with its rise and the space available; and a hint of the constraints, materials and budget. Notice what it leaves out: it does not say how many steps, what gradient or where the ramp turns, because those are design decisions the team will make, and a brief that makes them in advance is not a brief but a finished design.

The brief is not the problem statement, which describes what is wrong now, the loose blocks and the excluded learner, though it follows from it. It is not the specification list, which comes next and gives the measurable requirements. It is not a sketch. It is the sentence or short paragraph that everything else hangs from, and it is written in plain, exact language, with the numbers that fix the situation, the rise and the space, but not the numbers that fix the solution.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a design brief contains, what it deliberately leaves out, and how it differs from the problem statement.

## Subtopic: Specifications: What the Stairs and Ramp Must Be

Specifications are statements of what the product must do or be, written so that each can be tested when the design or the model is finished. They come straight from the investigation. From the users and the rules: the ramp gradient must be 1 in 12 or gentler; the ramp must be at least 1 200 millimetres wide; all risers must be equal and between 150 and 180 millimetres; treads must be at least 250 millimetres deep; handrails must be fitted on both sides of the stairs and ramp at 900 to 1 000 millimetres above the surface, extending at least 300 millimetres beyond the top and bottom; a level landing at least 1 200 millimetres long must be provided at the top, at the bottom and at every change of direction; the ramp must have a kerb at least 75 millimetres high along any open edge; all walking surfaces must be non-slip when wet.

From the analysis of existing products: the structure must be of concrete with a brushed finish; the rails must be galvanised steel; the design must shed rainwater away from the door. From the site: the whole structure must fit within the 12 by 4 metre space; the stairs must be at least 1 200 millimetres wide for the number of users at peak times. From the purpose: a wheelchair user must be able to travel from the paving to the door without assistance.

Each specification is a test. When the two sketches are evaluated in week 5 and the model in week 8, each one is checked: does this design have a 1 in 12 ramp, yes or no; are the risers equal, yes or no. A statement that cannot be tested, such as the ramp must be good or the stairs must look nice, is not a specification; it is a wish, and it belongs among the design considerations instead. Writing specifications with numbers wherever a number exists is what makes the evaluation instrument possible.

The questions for this section are with you now: where specifications come from, examples with numbers for the stairs and ramp, and the test of whether a statement is a specification.

## Subtopic: Constraints and Design Considerations

Constraints are the limits the designer did not choose and cannot change: the things the design must work within. For the PAT: the space available on the site, 12 metres by 4 metres; the budget the school can afford, which the tender must not exceed; the materials that can be bought locally; the time available for construction, perhaps a school holiday of three weeks; the building regulations, which are law; and for the model, the materials and tools available in the classroom and the two weeks allowed for making. Constraints are listed separately from specifications because they shape the possible solutions before design begins, while specifications are tested after.

Design considerations are the values a good design respects, and they are the headings under which the final design will be judged: fitness for purpose, safety, cost-effectiveness, ergonomics, aesthetics, durability, and impact on society and the environment. They are broader than specifications and not always measurable: a design can meet every numbered specification and still be ugly, or wasteful, or hard to maintain. The brief lists them so that the team keeps them in view, and the evaluation instrument gives each a score.

The three together, specifications, constraints and considerations, are the yardstick for everything that follows. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a brief that is really a design, saying four steps and a ramp turning left, so that there is nothing left to design. Two: specifications without numbers, such as a gentle ramp, which cannot be tested. Three: constraints mixed in with specifications, so that the budget appears as something the design must do rather than a limit it must fit. Four: forgetting the model's own constraints, so that a design is chosen that cannot be built in cardboard and balsa in two weeks.

The questions for this section are with you now: examples of constraints for the PAT, the list of design considerations, and how the three kinds of statement differ.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the PAT file open at a blank page headed Design Brief — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Short Statement of the Job

A design brief says what to design, for whom, where and why. Short and exact.

Ours: design stairs and a wheelchair ramp with rails, for all learners, staff and visitors, from the paving to the hall door, which is 600 millimetres up, in a space 12 by 4 metres, in concrete and steel, within a budget.

It gives the facts of the situation: the rise, the space. It does not give the answer: not how many steps, not where the ramp turns. That is your job later.

Test: could a stranger read it and start designing? Then it is a brief.

Keep the stranger in mind and try the questions for this part: what goes in the statement and what stays out.

## Subtopic: The Must-Be List

Specifications are the must-be list. Each one can be checked, yes or no, at the end.

Ramp 1 in 12 or gentler. Ramp at least 1 200 wide. Risers equal, 150 to 180. Treads at least 250. Rails both sides at 900 to 1 000, sticking out 300 past the ends. Level landings of 1 200 at top, bottom and turns. Kerb of 75 on the ramp edge. Non-slip when wet. Concrete, brushed. Galvanised steel rails. Fits in 12 by 4. A wheelchair user gets to the door alone.

If you cannot test it, it is not a specification. The ramp must be nice is a wish, not a spec.

Hold yes or no in mind and try this part's questions: the must-be list.

## Subtopic: The Limits and the Values

Constraints are the limits you did not pick: the space, the budget, what the shops sell, the holiday time to build, the law. For the model: classroom materials and two weeks.

Design considerations are the values you judge by: fitness for purpose, safety, cost, ergonomics, looks, durability, effect on people and the planet.

Specs are tested after. Constraints shape before. Considerations are the headings for judging.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A design brief states the product, users, purpose and place with the facts of the situation but not the solution. Specifications are testable must-be statements with numbers. Constraints are outside limits such as space, budget, materials and time. Design considerations are the values the final design is judged by. Together they are the yardstick for the whole PAT.

Hold the limits and the values, and take the final questions of the lesson: the three kinds of statement.
