# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the second part of the Design stage of PAT 1: sketching two possible ideas, producing an evaluation instrument and using it as a group to choose and develop the final idea. The anchor is a team of four in a classroom in the Northern Cape with two A3 sheets on the desk, one showing a straight ramp running out from the door beside a single flight of four steps, the other a ramp that turns back on itself with a landing so that it fits in half the length. The governing idea is that a choice between designs is only meaningful if the alternatives really differ and if they are judged against the same written criteria, drawn from the brief, by the whole group. Producing two distinct ideas, an evaluation instrument and a reasoned choice is examinable and earns Design marks in weeks 5 and 6.

## Subtopic: Two Honest Ideas: Sketching Alternatives That Really Differ

The Design stage asks for two possible ideas, and the word possible is doing work: each must be a solution that could be built and that meets the brief, and the two must differ in something that matters, not in colour or in the shape of the rail ends. For the stairs and ramp the real choices are few and clear. Where does the ramp go: straight out from the door, which needs 7 200 millimetres plus landings in a line, or doubled back with a 180 degree turn, which needs half the length but twice the width and an extra landing, or along the face of the building. Where do the stairs go: beside the ramp, in front of the door, or at the end of the ramp's first leg. How is it built: concrete on fill, or a steel frame with a deck. Two ideas that take different answers to these questions are honest alternatives.

Sketches are freehand, in 3D, in oblique or isometric, with notes. They are drawn quickly and not to scale, because their purpose is to show the idea, not to build from. Each should show the ramp and stairs in relation to the door and the site boundary, the landings, the rails, and arrows or notes giving the main sizes: rise 600, ramp 7 200 at 1 in 12, width 1 200. A plan sketch beside the 3D view helps to show how the idea fits the 12 by 4 metre space. Notes should also name the materials and point out the idea's particular strength: fits the space, shortest route for walkers, cheapest.

Each idea is sketched by the team, but the usual way to get two real alternatives is for pairs within the team to develop one each and then compare. The sketches go into the PAT file labelled Idea A and Idea B, with the date, because they are evidence that a choice was made. The error museum opens later in this session; for now the standard is simple: two ideas, both buildable, both meeting the brief, clearly different in layout or construction.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes two ideas genuinely different, what a freehand idea sketch must show, and why the sketches are labelled and filed.

## Subtopic: Building an Evaluation Instrument from the Specifications

An evaluation instrument is a scorecard. Its rows are the criteria, taken from the brief; its columns are the ideas; its cells are scores with a short reason. The first rows are the specifications, each scored simply: meets or does not meet. Gradient 1 in 12 or gentler. Width 1 200. Equal risers 150 to 180. Treads 250. Rails both sides at 900 to 1 000. Landings at top, bottom and turns. Kerb. Non-slip. Fits the 12 by 4 site. Wheelchair user reaches the door unaided. An idea that fails any specification is out, however attractive it is otherwise, because specifications are the rules the brief set.

The next rows are the design considerations, each scored on a scale, say 1 to 5, with a line of reasoning: fitness for purpose, how well it serves the users beyond the minimum; safety, hazards beyond the specifications such as a dark corner or a long drop at an edge; cost-effectiveness, estimated from the quantities, with the longer ramp costing more concrete and rail; ergonomics, the ease of use for a chair user and for a crowd of walkers at the bell; aesthetics, how well it suits the building; durability and maintenance; environmental impact, the amount of material and any trees or drainage affected. Weighting may be added: if the client has said that cost matters most, cost scores are doubled.

The instrument is written before the scoring begins, by the team, from the brief, and it is applied to both ideas by the same people in the same session. This is what makes the evaluation objective: the criteria are fixed in advance and come from the client's needs, not from whichever team member argues loudest. The instrument is itself filed as evidence and is used again, unchanged, on the finished model in week 8, so that the model is judged against the same yardstick as the idea.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rows and columns of the instrument, how specifications and considerations are scored differently, and why the instrument is written before scoring.

## Subtopic: Group Evaluation, Choosing and Developing the Final Idea

Group evaluation is a meeting with the two sketches, the instrument and the brief on the table. Each specification is checked for each idea and ticked or crossed, with a measurement or a reason. Then each consideration is scored by discussion, with the reason written in the cell: Idea A scores 5 for ergonomics because walkers and chair users arrive at the same landing; Idea B scores 3 because the turning ramp arrives from the side and chair users must swing round at the top. Totals are added. If the totals are close, the group looks at the weighted criteria and at which idea has the weakness that is easiest to fix.

Choosing is then simple to defend: Idea B is chosen because it meets all specifications, scores 31 to Idea A's 27, and fits the site with room to spare, though its weaker ergonomics at the top landing must be addressed. That sentence, with the scorecard behind it, is what the tender board and the marker want to see. The choice is recorded in the file with the scorecard and the date.

Developing the chosen idea means taking its weaknesses from the scorecard and fixing them before the working drawing. If Idea B's top landing is awkward, it is widened to 1 500 so a chair can turn, and the rail is extended. If the cost score was low because of the extra landing, the fill under the ramp is reduced by using a hollow block retaining wall. Development often borrows from the losing idea: Idea A's straight stair alignment may be grafted onto Idea B's turning ramp. The developed idea is sketched again, labelled Final Idea, with the changes noted, and only then does the working drawing begin. The error museum, four exhibits. One: two sketches that are the same idea with different rail ends; no real choice. Two: scoring by feel without a written instrument; no evidence and no fairness. Three: choosing the idea of the team member who drew best; the scorecard judges the idea, not the drawing. Four: taking the winner straight to the working drawing without developing it; the scorecard showed what to fix.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the group applies the instrument, how a choice is justified, and what developing the idea involves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two sketches on the desk and a scorecard between them — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Draw Two, Not One and a Copy

You need two ideas, and they must really differ. Not the same ramp with different rails.

The big choices: ramp straight out, or doubled back with a turn; stairs beside the ramp or in front of the door; concrete on fill or a steel frame. Pick different answers for Idea A and Idea B.

Sketch each one freehand in 3D with a small plan beside it. Not to scale. Write on the sizes: 600 up, 7 200 at 1 in 12, 1 200 wide. Name the materials. Say what the idea is good at.

Label them A and B, date them, put them in the file.

Keep A and B in mind and try the questions for this part: two ideas that truly differ.

## Subtopic: A Scorecard Made from Your Own Rules

An evaluation instrument is a scorecard. Rows: the rules from your brief. Columns: Idea A, Idea B.

First the specifications: meets or does not. 1 in 12, 1 200 wide, equal risers, treads, rails, landings, kerb, non-slip, fits the site, chair user reaches the door. Fail one, and the idea is out.

Then the considerations, scored 1 to 5 with a reason: fitness, safety, cost, ergonomics, looks, durability, environment. If the client says cost matters most, double that row.

Write the scorecard before you score. That is what makes it fair.

Hold the scorecard in mind and try this part's questions: rows, columns and scores.

## Subtopic: Pick, Then Improve

Sit as a group. Tick or cross every specification for both ideas. Score every consideration and write why. Add up.

Choose, and say why in one sentence: B meets everything, scores 31 to 27, fits the site. Write it down with the scorecard.

Then improve the winner. Fix its weak rows. Borrow good bits from the loser. Sketch it again as Final Idea.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sketch two genuinely different ideas freehand with sizes and notes. Build a scorecard from the specifications, pass or fail, and the design considerations, 1 to 5 with reasons. Score both ideas as a group with the same card. Choose on the scores, record the reason, then develop the winner before the working drawing.

Hold the scores and the one-sentence reason, and take the final questions of the lesson: pick, then improve.
