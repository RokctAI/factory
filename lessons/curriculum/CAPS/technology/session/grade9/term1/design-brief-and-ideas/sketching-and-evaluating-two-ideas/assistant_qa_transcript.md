### subtopic_1 — Two Honest Ideas: Sketching Alternatives That Really Differ
**Assistant:** Why two ideas? If I already know the best layout, sketching a second one is a waste of time.

**Tutor:** Because knowing the best layout before you have compared it with anything is a feeling, not knowledge, and the second idea is how the feeling gets tested. Designers who sketch only one idea almost always sketch the first one that came to mind, and the first idea is shaped by habit and by the examples one has seen, not by the brief. A straight ramp is the obvious idea; it is only when a team is forced to draw a second, a ramp that doubles back with a landing, that it notices the straight ramp runs nine metres across the playground and blocks the path to the taps, something nobody saw while admiring the first sketch. The second idea also gives the evaluation instrument something to do. A scorecard with one column is a checklist, and a checklist tells you whether an idea is acceptable, not whether it is the best available; comparing two ideas on the same rows shows which criteria each one wins and loses, and that is the information you need to develop the winner, often by borrowing the loser's strengths. There is a further reason that matters in the tender world: a board that sees two considered options and a reasoned choice trusts the team more than a board that sees one idea defended with enthusiasm. If, after an honest second sketch and an honest scorecard, your first idea still wins, you have lost an hour and gained a defensible decision; if it loses, you have avoided building the wrong ramp.

### subtopic_2 — Building an Evaluation Instrument from the Specifications
**Assistant:** Who decides the scores? Our group never agrees on anything.

**Tutor:** The brief decides as much as possible, and the group decides the rest by argument with reasons, which is exactly what the written instrument is for. Start with the rows that need no opinion. The specifications are pass or fail by measurement: either the sketch shows a ramp at 1 in 12 or it does not, either there is a landing at the top or there is not, and if a team member insists that a 1 in 9 ramp is fine, the brief, not the group, says otherwise. Those rows settle most of the structure of the decision and leave only the design considerations to be scored by judgement. For those, the rule that keeps a group honest is that every score must have a written reason in the cell, and the reason must point at something in the sketch or the brief. A score of 5 for ergonomics because I like it is not a reason; a score of 5 because walkers and chair users arrive at the same landing is. When two members give different scores, each reads out the reason and the group picks the better-supported one, or averages. Weighting also helps: if the client said cost matters most and the brief recorded that, the cost row counts double and nobody's preference can override it. Finally, the instrument is agreed before any scoring begins, while the group is still neutral, so that the criteria cannot be bent afterwards to favour someone's idea. Groups that work this way still disagree, but they disagree about reasons rather than about people, and the scorecard records how the disagreement was resolved.

### subtopic_3 — Group Evaluation, Choosing and Developing the Final Idea
**Assistant:** What if both ideas fail a specification? Do we start again from nothing?

**Tutor:** No; you develop, which is what the Design stage expects, and the failure tells you exactly what to develop. Specifications are pass or fail, but a failure is a piece of information about a particular feature, not a verdict on the whole idea. Suppose both sketches fail the landing specification because neither shows a 1 200 landing at the top. That is a fix, not a disaster: both ideas gain a landing, which may push the ramp out by 1 200 and change which one fits the site, and that in turn may decide the choice. Suppose the straight ramp fails the fit-the-site specification because 7 200 plus two landings is 9 600 and the site is 12 metres long but the path crosses at 9 metres. Then the straight idea is either developed, perhaps by angling the ramp or by moving the stairs so the path can pass, or it is dropped in favour of the turning ramp, and the scorecard records why. What you do not do is lower the specification to let a favourite idea through: 1 in 12 does not become 1 in 10 because the ramp is too long. The specifications came from the users and the law, and the Design stage exists to find a solution that meets them, not to negotiate them away. If, rarely, every reasonable idea fails the same specification, that is a sign the brief contains a contradiction, a site too small for a 1 in 12 ramp to any possible door, and the honest response is to go back to the client, here the teacher acting as the governing body, and report that the brief must change, perhaps by allowing the ramp to approach along the building face or by raising the paving. That too is design, and it earns more respect than a ramp that quietly breaks the rules.
