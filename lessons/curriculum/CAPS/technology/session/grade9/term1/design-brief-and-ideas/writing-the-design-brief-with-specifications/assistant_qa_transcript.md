### subtopic_1 — What a Design Brief Is and Is Not
**Assistant:** Why does the brief not say how many steps? We already know it will be four.

**Tutor:** Because the brief's job is to define the problem, and the moment it specifies the answer it stops being a brief and becomes a design that nobody has evaluated. Four steps of 150 millimetres is one solution to a rise of 600. Three steps of 200 is another, legal in some settings but steep for young children; five steps of 120 is gentler and better for a primary school but needs more space. Two short flights of two steps with a landing between them might suit a site where the door is off to one side. If the brief says four steps, all of those alternatives are closed off before anyone has sketched them or weighed them against the specifications, and the Design stage, whose whole point is to generate and compare ideas, is reduced to drawing the one that was decided in advance. The same applies to the ramp: a brief that says the ramp turns left has already decided a question that the site, the door swing and the drainage should decide. What the brief must give is the fixed facts, the rise of 600, the space of 12 by 4, the users, and what the specifications must give is the rules any solution must meet, equal risers between 150 and 180, a gradient of 1 in 12 or gentler. Within those, the number of steps and the route of the ramp are the team's to choose, and the marker will look for evidence that they were chosen, not inherited.

### subtopic_2 — Specifications: What the Stairs and Ramp Must Be
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a specification and a constraint? Both seem to be rules.

**Tutor:** Both are rules, but they come from different directions and are used at different times, and the difference matters when you evaluate. A specification is a property the product must have, and it is tested on the finished design or model: the ramp must be 1 in 12 or gentler; you measure the model's ramp and tick or cross. Specifications describe the product. A constraint is a limit imposed on the designer from outside, by the site, the client, the law or the resources, and it shapes which designs are even possible before you start: the structure must fit in 12 by 4 metres; the budget is a fixed sum; only materials sold locally may be used; the model must be made in two weeks from classroom materials. Constraints describe the situation. A useful test is to ask who set the rule and when it bites. If the rule is about how the thing performs for its users and you check it at the end, it is a specification. If the rule is about what you are allowed to use or spend or where you must fit, and it rules out ideas before you sketch them, it is a constraint. There is some overlap, since the size of the site both constrains what can be drawn and could be written as a specification that the design fits the site, and that is fine; the point of separating them is that the team sees clearly which rules it must design to and which rules it must design within, and the evaluation instrument is built from the first kind.

### subtopic_3 — Constraints and Design Considerations
**Assistant:** Why list design considerations if they cannot be measured? Surely the specifications are enough.

**Tutor:** Because specifications only catch what you thought to specify, and the design considerations are a safety net for everything else that makes a product good or bad. Imagine a design that meets every specification in the brief: 1 in 12, equal risers, rails at 900, landings, kerb, brushed concrete. It could still be a poor design. It might be ugly, a raw grey wedge bolted to a brick building, so that the school is embarrassed by it; that is aesthetics. It might put the ramp where the morning sun never reaches so that the surface stays wet and slippery; that is safety beyond the specification. It might use three times the concrete of a simpler layout; that is cost-effectiveness and environmental impact. It might place the rail post exactly where a chair user's hand needs to reach the door handle; that is ergonomics. None of these were in the numbered list, because no list can anticipate everything, yet all of them matter to the people who will use the entrance every day. Design considerations are the headings under which a thoughtful judge looks at the whole product, and in the evaluation instrument each gets a score, perhaps out of five, with a reason. They are also what the tender board will actually talk about: boards rarely argue about whether the risers are 150, they argue about whether the thing is worth the money and whether it will still be sound in twenty years. Listing the considerations in the brief keeps those questions in front of the team from the first sketch.
