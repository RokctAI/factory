# Part 1 — Expert

This session consolidates the work of Term 1 for Grade 9 Technology. The anchor is a classroom in Nelspruit with the finished models lined up on the windowsill and a blank A4 sheet on each desk, headed Term 1 in one page. The governing idea is that the term's content falls into three groups, graphic communication, structures and materials, and the design process with its costing and communication, and that each group can be compressed to a handful of rules and examples that a learner can reproduce from memory. Consolidation here means active recall against the syllabus list, not rereading, and the one-page summary is the product. Every item revised is examinable in the Term 2 test and reappears later in the year.

## Subtopic: Revising Orthographic Projection and the Drawing Conventions

Graphic communication first. Five line types: dark continuous for visible outlines, feint continuous for construction and projection, dashed for hidden detail, wavy freehand for a break, chain long-short for centre lines and cutting planes. Scale is drawing size to real size in a standard ratio, 1:1, 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, 1:100, chosen as the largest that fits the sheet and written in the title block; drawing size is real size divided by the scale number, and dimensions always state real sizes in millimetres without the unit, each once, outside the outline, numbers above the line, overall sizes outermost.

First angle orthographic projection: three true views, front showing width and height, top or plan showing width and depth, side or end showing depth and height. The arrangement is the rule most often tested: top view directly below the front view, left end view to the right of the front view, each view on the far side from the face it shows, because the view is projected onto the pane beyond the object. Feint projection lines carry widths down and heights across and stay on the drawing; a 45 degree mitre line carries depths between plan and end view. A feature shows its true shape in the view that looks at it square-on and appears as a line elsewhere; a hole is a circle with chain centre lines in one view and two dashed lines in the other two; a chamfer shows its true angle only in the view along it.

The stairs and ramp drawing used all of this: a plan with the ramp legs, landing and stairs, an elevation with the sawtooth of four risers of 150 and the rail at 900, a section through the ramp showing slab, fill, kerb and post, at 1:50 on A3, every specification checked against a dimension. Recall exercise: on the blank page, draw the three views of a 60 millimetre cube with a 20 by 20 step across the top front edge, place them in first angle, add projection lines and dimension once. If that takes under five minutes and is right, the drawing section of the term is secure.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five line types, the first angle arrangement and projection lines, and how features appear across views.

## Subtopic: Revising Forces, Loads and Material Properties

Structures and forces. Static loads do not change with time: the dead load of the structure itself, concrete at 2 400 kilograms per cubic metre, and steady live loads such as a parked wheelchair; they are predictable and sized from the drawing. Dynamic loads move, strike or repeat: a running crowd, a jump, a crowd leaning on a rail, wind; impact multiplies the force several times and repetition causes fatigue, so treads are thickened and reinforced, posts set deep, rails designed for sideways push and nosings protected. Even loads are spread, every part sharing; uneven loads concentrate at wheels, posts or a bunched crowd, producing high local pressure and turning effects, so slabs sit on compacted fill with mesh and the kerb is cast with the slab.

Strength of materials. Tension pulls longer; steel, rope and timber along the grain are strong in it; concrete, brick and glass are weak. Compression pushes shorter; concrete, brick and stone are strong in it; long thin members buckle. Bending is both at once: the outside of the curve stretches and the inside is squeezed, with a neutral axis between, so reinforcing steel goes near the tension face, low in a slab on supports and high at the root of a cantilever, and deeper beams bend less. Torsion twists; tubes resist it well for their weight; a rectangle of pinned members racks until a diagonal makes it two triangles, which is cross-bracing, used as knee braces on the rail.

Properties. Density is mass per volume; hardness resists scratching and wear; stiffness resists bending and is not strength; flexibility bends and recovers; brittle snaps, tough dents. Corrosion of steel is rusting, iron plus oxygen plus water, faster with salt; paint is a barrier that fails at scratches, galvanising is zinc that protects even when scratched, stainless steel protects itself; design keeps water off by capping tubes and draining bases. Recall exercise: list the five forces with one material strong and one weak in each; then list the three ways of protecting steel with one advantage and one disadvantage of each.

The questions for this section are with you now: static versus dynamic and even versus uneven loads, where reinforcing goes in a bent beam, and the three ways of preventing rust.

## Subtopic: Revising the Design Process, Costing and the Tender

The design process as it happened. Investigate: measure the site at several points, count and ask the users, analyse three or four existing entrances against fitness for purpose, materials, safety and cost with a lesson for each, and collect real prices for materials, labour and transport. Design: write a brief that states product, users, purpose and place with the fixed facts but not the solution; list specifications that are testable with numbers, 1 in 12 or gentler, 1 200 wide, equal risers 150 to 180, treads 250, rails 900 to 1 000 both sides, landings 1 200, kerb 75, non-slip; list constraints, space, budget, materials, time, law, and design considerations; sketch two genuinely different ideas; build an evaluation instrument and score both as a group; develop the winner; produce the working drawing and check it; draw the flow chart with decision diamonds.

Make: convert to model scale in a table, build in flow chart order with checks before gluing, safely, neatly, and record with a check sheet and photographs. Evaluate: the instrument applied to the ideas and again to the model. Communicate: the tender presentation in IDMEC order within time, every member speaking, evidence not claims, honest answers, and fair scoring of other bids. Costing: materials, labour and transport, quantities from the drawing, prices from named sources with dates, rounded up to units sold, 10 percent waste and 10 percent contingency, checked for arithmetic, sense and proportion.

The one-page summary is written now, from memory, in three columns headed Drawing, Forces and materials, Process and cost, each with the rules and one example, then checked against the notes and corrected in a different colour so the gaps show. That page is the term's revision tool. The error museum, four exhibits, drawn from the whole term. One: top view above the front view. Two: steel in the middle of a slab. Three: a specification without a number. Four: a budget rounded down with no contingency.

The questions for this section are with you now: the stages of IDMEC with their products, the costing rules, and how the one-page summary is made and checked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the models on the windowsill and a blank page on the desk — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Views and Five Lines

Five lines: dark for what you see, feint for helpers, dashed for hidden, wavy for a break, chain for centres and cuts.

Scale: a standard ratio, written in the title block, real size divided by the number to draw. Dimensions: real sizes, once each, outside, number above the line.

Three views: front gives width and height, top gives width and depth, side gives depth and height. First angle: top view under the front, left end view on the right. Feint lines join them and stay.

A hole is a circle in one view and two dashed lines in the others. A slope shows its angle in one view only.

Keep the cube with a step in mind and try the questions for this part: three views and five lines.

## Subtopic: Push, Pull, Still, Moving, Rust

Static load stays still: the slab's own weight, a parked chair. Dynamic load moves, hits or repeats: a running crowd, a jump, a lean. Moving hits harder and repeating cracks slowly. Even load is spread; uneven load is bunched on wheels or posts.

Tension pulls, steel is good at it. Compression pushes, concrete is good at it. Bend a beam and the outside stretches, the inside squashes; steel goes on the stretched side. Torsion twists; tubes and triangles resist it.

Density is heavy for its size. Hardness resists scratches. Stiffness resists bending. Brittle snaps, tough dents. Rust is iron plus air plus water; paint is a barrier that scratches, zinc protects even scratched, stainless protects itself. Cap the tubes.

Hold push, pull, still, moving and rust in mind and try this part's questions: forces and materials.

## Subtopic: The Term in One Page

Investigate: measure, ask, analyse other ramps, collect prices. Design: brief without the answer, specs with numbers, constraints, considerations, two ideas, a scorecard, the winner developed, the working drawing checked, the flow chart. Make: table, build in order, check before glue, safe and neat, photos. Evaluate: the scorecard again. Communicate: the tender talk, everyone speaking, evidence, honesty.

Cost: materials, labour, transport. Quantities from the drawing, prices from named shops, round up, add 10 and 10, check three ways.

Now write the page: three columns, drawing, forces and materials, process and cost. From memory. Then check and fix in another colour.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Term 1 gave you first angle orthographic projection with five line types, scale and dimensions; static and dynamic, even and uneven loads with tension, compression, bending and torsion and the material properties including rust prevention; and the design process from investigation through brief, specifications, evaluation, drawing, flow chart, model, budget and tender. One page, in your own hand, is the revision tool.

Hold the three columns, and take the final questions of the lesson: the term in one page.
