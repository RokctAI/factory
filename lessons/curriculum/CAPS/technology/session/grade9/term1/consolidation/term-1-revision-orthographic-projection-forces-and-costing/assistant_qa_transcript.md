### subtopic_1 — Revising Orthographic Projection and the Drawing Conventions
**Assistant:** Why write the summary from memory and then correct it? Copying the notes would be neater and faster.

**Tutor:** Because neatness is not the goal; a memory that holds the content is, and copying produces a neat page and an empty memory. When you copy, your eyes move from the notes to the page and your hand follows; the ideas pass through without being pulled out of your own head, and an hour later you remember having written them but not what they were. When you write from memory, every rule you reproduce is one you have genuinely retrieved, which strengthens it, and every gap you leave is a precise map of what you do not yet know. Then the correction in a different colour does two jobs: it fills the gap with the right version, and it marks it, so that the next time you revise you can see at a glance which rules came easily and which needed help, and spend your time on the second group. Learners who revise this way, by retrieving and then checking, remember far more weeks later than learners who reread or copy, and the effect is large enough that it is worth the discomfort of staring at a blank page for a minute. The one-page format matters too: compressing a term into three columns forces you to decide what the core rules are, and that deciding is itself understanding. A learner who can write the first angle arrangement, the tension-face rule and the budgeting rounding rule from memory on a single sheet has the term; one who has a neat copy of thirty pages of notes may not.

### subtopic_2 — Revising Forces, Loads and Material Properties
**Assistant:** The forces and the drawing seem like separate subjects. Why are they in the same term?

**Tutor:** Because the drawing is how the forces get built into the structure, and the PAT put them together on purpose. Think about what the working drawing of the stairs and ramp actually shows. The section through the ramp is a drawing, in first angle projection with chain lines and dashed hidden detail, but what it shows is a slab of a certain thickness with mesh near its bottom face, fill compacted beneath it, a kerb cast on top and a post set 300 into the concrete. Every one of those is a decision about forces: the thickness and the mesh answer the bending that a wheel over a hollow would cause; the fill answers the static dead load and spreads it to the ground; the kerb answers the sideways strike of a trolley; the post depth answers the torsion of a crowd leaning on the rail. The elevation shows rails of a certain tube size at a certain spacing, which is a stiffness decision, and knee braces at the ends, which is cross-bracing. The materials column of the budget prices galvanised tube rather than bare steel, which is the corrosion lesson. So the forces and properties are not a separate subject from the drawing; they are what the drawing is about, and a drawing that ignored them would show a ramp that cracks and rails that rust. CAPS teaches them in the same term so that a learner sees that connection: the lines on the page carry the loads in the world. In Term 2 the same pairing returns, with hydraulics and gears on one side and orthographic drawings of the mechanism on the other.

### subtopic_3 — Revising the Design Process, Costing and the Tender
**Assistant:** Which parts of this term come back later in the year, so I know what to keep sharp?

**Tutor:** Almost all of it, which is the nature of a spiral curriculum, but three parts return soonest and hardest. First angle orthographic projection comes back in Term 2, when the prototype of the mechanical machine must be drawn in three views, and in Term 4, when the plastic item of your own design must be drawn the same way; the arrangement rule, the line types and the dimensioning conventions are exactly the same, so the cube-with-a-step exercise is worth repeating until it is automatic. The design process comes back in every term, since Terms 2, 3 and 4 each have a design brief with specifications and constraints, two ideas, a choice and a drawing; the habit of writing testable specifications with numbers and separating them from constraints will save time every time. The forces return in Term 2 in a different dress: hydraulics transfer force through a liquid, pulleys and gears trade force for distance, and the mechanical advantage idea you met in Grade 8 is the thread that joins them; tension and compression also reappear whenever a frame or a crane arm is discussed. The material properties, especially corrosion, return in Term 4, where preserving metals by painting, galvanising and electroplating is studied as processing in its own right, so the zinc-beats-paint reasoning from this term is a head start. Costing returns in Term 3 for the electronic device's presentation. If you keep three things sharp through the holidays, make them the first angle arrangement, the specification-writing habit and the tension-face rule; everything else can be rebuilt from your one page when it is needed.
