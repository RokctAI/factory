# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the design brief for a flight of stairs and a wheelchair ramp, the product that the Term 1 PAT will design, draw, cost and model. The anchor is a primary school in KwaZulu-Natal whose new classroom block stands on a plinth 600 millimetres above the playground, reached at present by a single rough step and a plank. The governing idea is that a design brief for stairs and a ramp is largely a set of numbers, each of which protects a user: riser height for safe stepping, ramp gradient for a wheelchair user's arms, width for two people to pass, and a handrail for anyone who needs support. Reading those numbers and explaining what each one means is examinable, and the same numbers will drive the orthographic drawing in the next sessions.

## Subtopic: Why a School Needs Both Stairs and a Ramp

A building whose floor is above the ground needs a way up. Stairs are the quickest way for most people, but they exclude anyone in a wheelchair, anyone pushing a pram or a trolley, a learner on crutches after an injury and many older people. South African law and the building regulations require that public buildings, including schools, be accessible, so a ramp is not an extra but a requirement. The two together, stairs and ramp side by side, serve everybody: the stairs for speed, the ramp for access.

The problem situation for the PAT is a real one in many schools. A new classroom block, a hall or an administration building has a raised floor to keep water out and to allow for services under the slab, and the only way in is a step that was never designed. The scenario asks each team to act as a small building firm bidding for the job: investigate the site and the users, design the stairs and ramp, draw them properly, cost them and present a tender to a board made up of the class.

Investigating the users is the first job. Who will use the entrance? Learners of what ages, teachers, parents, delivery people, a learner in a wheelchair, a grandparent at a prize-giving. What do they carry? How many arrive at once? Where does rainwater go? These answers shape the brief, and a brief written without them produces a staircase that is too steep or a ramp that nobody can push up.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a school needs both stairs and a ramp, who the users are, and what the PAT scenario asks teams to do.

## Subtopic: The Numbers in a Design Brief: Steps, Risers, Width and Gradient

A design brief for stairs and a ramp is mostly numbers, and each number has a reason. Start with the height to be climbed, called the rise, in this case 600 millimetres. Stairs: a comfortable riser, the vertical height of one step, is between 150 and 180 millimetres, so 600 millimetres divided by a riser of 150 gives four steps, and every riser must be the same height, because a single odd step is the commonest cause of falls on a staircase. The tread, the flat part you stand on, should be at least 250 millimetres from front to back so that a whole foot fits, and the stair should be at least 900 millimetres wide, wider for a school entrance that is used by many people at once, say 1 200 millimetres.

Ramp: the key number is the gradient, written as a ratio of rise to length. The accepted maximum for wheelchair users is 1 in 12, meaning one unit up for every twelve units along, and 1 in 15 is more comfortable. For a rise of 600 millimetres, a 1 in 12 ramp is 7 200 millimetres long, and at 1 in 15 it is 9 000 millimetres long. That is why ramps are often built with a turn: the length is too great to fit in a straight line. The ramp should be at least 1 000 millimetres wide, and 1 200 millimetres is better so that a helper can walk beside the chair.

The brief states these numbers as specifications and constraints. Specifications say what the product must do or be: four equal risers of 150 millimetres, treads of at least 250 millimetres, a ramp at 1 in 12 or gentler, a minimum width of 1 200 millimetres, a handrail on both sides. Constraints say what limits the design: the space available on the site, the budget, the materials the firm can get, the time allowed. A good brief separates the two, because specifications are tested at the end while constraints shape what is possible at the start.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the number of steps follows from rise and riser, what a gradient of 1 in 12 means, and the difference between specifications and constraints.

## Subtopic: Handrails, Landings and the Rules That Protect Users

A handrail is required on any stair of more than two or three risers and on every ramp, on both sides where the width allows. Its height is between 900 and 1 000 millimetres above the tread or ramp surface, which is where a standing adult's hand falls naturally, and it should extend a little beyond the top and bottom of the run so that a person can grip it before the first step. For a wheelchair user a lower second rail at about 700 millimetres helps, and a kerb or low upstand along the edge of the ramp stops a wheel from slipping off.

Landings are the flat rest areas. Building rules call for a landing at least 1 200 millimetres long every time a ramp changes direction and at least every 10 metres of straight run, so that a wheelchair user can rest their arms. Stairs need a landing at top and bottom at least as wide as the stair, and the top landing must be clear of the door swing so that nobody stands on a step while the door opens. Surfaces must be non-slip, especially where rain reaches them, which in practice means a textured concrete finish or a brushed surface rather than smooth paint.

All of these rules come from the users' bodies. The riser height matches a comfortable stride; the gradient matches the force a person can push with their arms; the rail height matches a hand; the landing length matches a chair with someone sitting in it. When a design brief lists them, it is really listing the people it is for. The error museum, four exhibits. One: making the ramp steep to save space; a 1 in 6 ramp is a slide, not a ramp. Two: unequal risers to use up the height; every riser must match. Three: treating the handrail as decoration; it is a safety element with a fixed height. Four: forgetting the landing at the top, so the door opens onto a step.

The questions for this section are with you now: handrail heights, where landings are needed, and why these rules exist.

A new classroom floor sits higher than the playground. People need to get up to the door.

## Subtopic: Riser, Tread, Width and Slope

The brief is mostly numbers. Here they are.

Rise: 600 millimetres, the height to climb. Riser: the height of one step, 150 to 180 millimetres. 600 divided by 150 gives 4 steps, all the same height. Tread: the flat part, at least 250 millimetres so your foot fits. Width: at least 900, better 1 200 millimetres for a school.

Ramp gradient: 1 in 12 at the steepest. One up, twelve along. For 600 up, that is 7 200 millimetres long. Gentler is better. Width: at least 1 000, better 1 200 millimetres.

Specifications are what the thing must be. Constraints are the limits: space, money, materials, time.

Hold 1 in 12 and 150 millimetre risers in mind and try this part's questions: steps from rise and riser, and gradient.

## Subtopic: Rails and Rests

Handrails on both sides, 900 to 1 000 millimetres high, and a bit longer than the stairs so you can grab them first. A lower rail at 700 millimetres helps a wheelchair user. A small kerb along the ramp stops wheels slipping off.

Landings are flat rests: at the top and bottom, at every turn, and every 10 metres on a long ramp. The top landing must not be where the door swings.

Surfaces must not be slippery when wet.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A raised floor needs stairs for speed and a ramp for access. The brief is numbers: equal risers of 150 to 180 millimetres, treads of 250 or more, width of 1 200, a ramp at 1 in 12 or gentler, handrails at 900 to 1 000 millimetres, and landings at turns and ends. Every number protects a user.

Hold the rail and the landing, and take the final questions of the lesson: rails, rests and the people they protect.
