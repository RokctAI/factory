# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the line types, scale and dimensioning conventions used in first angle orthographic projection. The anchor is a drawing board in a Technology room in Bloemfontein, where a learner is about to draw the stairs and ramp from the design brief and realises that the ramp alone is 7 200 millimetres long and the page is 297. The governing idea is that a drawing is a language with fixed rules: the thickness and pattern of a line tells the reader what it represents, the scale tells the reader how to convert the drawing to real sizes, and the dimensions state the real sizes directly so that nothing has to be measured off the page. These conventions are examinable and are applied in every drawing for the rest of the year.

## Subtopic: Five Line Types and What Each One Says

A technical drawing uses line types the way writing uses punctuation: each one carries a meaning that the reader is expected to know. Grade 9 Technology uses five. The outline, or object line, is dark and continuous and shows every visible edge of the object; it is the line a builder would see if standing in front of the finished stairs. The construction line is feint and continuous, drawn lightly as scaffolding while the drawing is set out, and left faint so that the outlines stand out; projection lines between views are construction lines too.

The hidden detail line is dashed, of medium thickness, and shows edges that exist but are behind a surface in that view: the back edge of a step seen from the side, or the ramp foundation under the slab. The wavy line, or break line, is a freehand continuous line used where part of a long object has been left out to save space: a ramp of 7 200 millimetres drawn at half its length with a wavy break shows the reader that the middle has been cut away and the dimension still gives the true length.

The chain line, long dash followed by short dash, has two jobs. Thin, it marks a centre line, through the middle of a hole or a symmetrical part, and it also shows the path of a moving part. Thick at the ends and thin in the middle, with arrows and letters, it marks a cutting plane: the line along which the object is imagined sliced to produce a section view. Knowing the five, dark, feint, dashed, wavy and chain, lets a learner read any drawing in the textbook and in the exam.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five line types, what each one represents, and which is used for hidden edges and for centres.

## Subtopic: Scale: Fitting a Building on a Page

Scale is the ratio between a size on the drawing and the real size. A scale of 1:1 is full size; 1:10 means every millimetre on the page stands for 10 millimetres on the object, so the drawing is a tenth of real size; 2:1 means the drawing is twice real size, used for small parts. For the stairs and ramp, with a total length of around 9 metres including landings, 1:50 or 1:100 fits an A4 page: at 1:50, 7 200 millimetres becomes 144 millimetres, and a riser of 150 becomes 3 millimetres, which is still drawable. At 1:100 the riser is 1.5 millimetres, too small to show clearly, so a detail of one step might be drawn separately at 1:10.

Choosing a scale is a design decision with three rules. Use a standard scale: 1:1, 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, 1:100, or 2:1, 5:1, 10:1, because a scale ruler has these marked and odd scales like 1:37 are unusable. Choose the largest standard scale that fits all the views on the page with room for dimensions and the title block. Write the scale in the title block, because a drawing without its scale cannot be read with confidence.

Converting is arithmetic. Drawing size equals real size divided by the scale number: 1 200 millimetres at 1:50 is 24 millimetres. Real size equals drawing size multiplied by the scale number: a line of 18 millimetres on a 1:50 drawing is 900 millimetres on site. Whichever way the conversion goes, the dimensions written on the drawing always give the real size, never the scaled size, so a builder never has to do this arithmetic at all; the scale is for the person drawing, not the person building.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a scale ratio, choosing a standard scale, and converting between drawing and real sizes.

## Subtopic: Dimensioning Rules That Make a Drawing Buildable

Dimensioning is the set of rules for writing sizes on a drawing so that they can be read without ambiguity. A dimension has four parts: two thin extension lines projecting from the edges being measured, with a small gap from the object; a thin dimension line between them, parallel to the edge; arrowheads or small oblique strokes where the dimension line meets the extension lines; and the number, written in millimetres without the unit, above the dimension line and clear of it, readable from the bottom or the right of the page.

The rules. Dimensions are placed outside the outline wherever possible, not inside the object where they clutter the view. Each size is given once, in the view that shows it most clearly, and never repeated in another view. Overall sizes, the total length, width and height, go outside the smaller dimensions, furthest from the object. Dimension lines do not cross each other or extension lines if it can be avoided. Numbers are never written on top of a line. Where many equal steps occur, the drawing may state 4 at 150 equals 600 rather than four separate dimensions.

The purpose of all this is a drawing that can be built from. A builder reading the stairs drawing must find the riser, the tread, the width, the overall rise and the overall run without a ruler and without doubt. Each missing dimension is a phone call to the designer; each ambiguous one is a wall built in the wrong place. The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing mm after every number; the title block states that all dimensions are in millimetres. Two: dimensioning the scaled size instead of the real size. Three: putting a dimension on the hidden detail view when a clearer view exists. Four: letting the extension line touch the object; a small gap keeps the outline clean.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four parts of a dimension, the placement rules, and why dimensions give real sizes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a seven-metre ramp and an A4 page — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Dark, Feint, Dashed, Wavy, Chain

Lines on a drawing talk. There are five kinds this year.

Dark and solid: the edges you can see. Feint and solid: the light helper lines you draw first. Dashed: edges that are there but hidden behind something.

Wavy: a break, where you have cut out the middle of something long so it fits. Chain, long dash then short dash: a centre line, or the line where you imagine cutting the object open.

Dark, feint, dashed, wavy, chain. Say it twice and it sticks.

Keep the five lines in mind and try the questions for this part: which line goes where.

## Subtopic: Shrinking the Ramp onto Paper

Scale is how much you shrink the real thing to fit the page. 1:50 means one millimetre on paper is 50 millimetres in real life.

The ramp is 7 200 long. At 1:50 that is 144 millimetres on paper. Fits. A riser of 150 becomes 3 millimetres. Small but fine.

Pick a normal scale: 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, 1:100. Pick the biggest one that fits. Write it in the title block.

To go from real to paper, divide by the scale number. To go from paper to real, multiply.

Hold 1:50 and 144 millimetres in mind and try this part's questions: shrinking sizes and picking a scale.

## Subtopic: Putting the Sizes On

Dimensions are the real sizes written on the drawing. Not the paper sizes. Always the real ones, in millimetres, no unit written.

A dimension has thin extension lines out from the edges, a thin line between them, arrows at the ends, and the number above the line.

Put dimensions outside the shape. Give each size once, in the clearest view. Big overall sizes go furthest out. Do not cross lines. Never write a number on a line.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Five line types: dark for outlines, feint for construction, dashed for hidden, wavy for breaks, chain for centres and cuts. Scale shrinks the object to the page using a standard ratio written in the title block. Dimensions give real sizes once each, outside the outline, so a builder never needs a ruler.

Hold the arrows and the number, and take the final questions of the lesson: putting sizes on properly.
