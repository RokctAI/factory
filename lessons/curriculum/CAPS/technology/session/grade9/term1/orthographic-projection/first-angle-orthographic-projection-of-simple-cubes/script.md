# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces first angle orthographic projection using simple cubes and rectangular blocks. The anchor is a wooden cube with a step sawn out of one corner, sitting on the drawing board in a Technology room in Kimberley, and the question of how to describe it fully on flat paper. The governing idea is that an orthographic projection replaces a single pictorial view with three true views, each showing two of the object's three dimensions without distortion, and that first angle projection arranges them according to a fixed rule: the top view below the front view, and each side view on the far side of the front view from the face it depicts. Drawing three correctly placed and aligned views of a simple object, with the correct line types, is examinable and is the method used for the stairs and ramp.

## Subtopic: Three Views Instead of One Picture

An isometric or oblique drawing shows an object as a picture, three faces at once, which is easy to understand but has a cost: the angles and some of the lengths are distorted, and a builder cannot measure true sizes from it. Orthographic projection takes the opposite approach. Each view looks straight at one face of the object, at right angles to it, so that face appears exactly as it is: a square face is a square, a 150 millimetre edge is 150 millimetres at scale. The price is that one view shows only two of the three dimensions, so at least two views are needed and usually three: the front view shows width and height, the top view, also called the plan, shows width and depth, and the side view, also called the end view, shows depth and height.

Imagine the object inside a glass box. Looking through the front pane you see the front view; through the top pane, the top view; through the left pane, the view of the left-hand end. In first angle projection, each view is drawn on the pane on the far side of the object, as if the object had cast a shadow straight through itself onto that pane; then the box is unfolded flat. The result is the arrangement that CAPS Technology uses and that South African and most European drawings follow.

Why bother with three views when a picture seems easier? Because the three views together contain every size without distortion, because each face is seen square-on so holes stay round and angles stay true, and because a complex object with details on every side cannot be shown in one picture. A set of orthographic views is what a builder, a machinist or a steel fabricator actually works from, and reading them is a skill that Engineering Graphics and Design in Grade 10 takes for granted.

The questions for this section are with you now: what each of the three views shows, why pictorial drawings are not used for working drawings, and the glass box idea.

## Subtopic: Where the Views Go in First Angle Projection

In first angle orthographic projection the views are arranged by a fixed rule. The front view is drawn first, wherever is convenient on the page. The top view is drawn directly below the front view, aligned with it so that the widths match line for line. The left side view is drawn to the right of the front view, and the right side view, if needed, to the left; in words, the side view goes on the opposite side from the face it shows, because the object is between the viewer and the pane on which the view falls. Height lines up between the front view and the side view; width lines up between the front view and the top view.

This is the opposite of third angle projection, used in North America, where the top view goes above the front view and the side view goes on the same side as the face it shows. Drawings carry a symbol, a truncated cone drawn in both views, that tells the reader which system is in use. In CAPS Technology Grade 9 the system is first angle, and placing the top view above the front view is a marked error.

Projection lines tie the views together. Feint vertical construction lines carry the widths from the front view down to the top view; feint horizontal lines carry the heights from the front view across to the side view; and depths are transferred from the top view to the side view with a 45 degree mitre line or a compass. These lines are left feint, not erased, because they show the reader how the views were derived. The three views, once drawn, are spaced evenly and labelled front view, top view and side view, or left end view, and the scale and the first angle symbol go in the title block.

The questions for this section are with you now: where the top view and side view are placed in first angle, how first angle differs from third angle, and what projection lines carry between views.

## Subtopic: Projecting a Cube with a Step Cut Out

Take a cube of 60 millimetres with a step cut from the top front edge: the cut removes a block 60 wide, 20 deep and 20 high. Front view, looking at the front face: because the step runs the full width and its vertical face is seen head-on, the front view is a plain rectangle 60 by 60 with a dark horizontal line at 40 from the bottom marking the step's edge. The side view, looking from the left, shows the L profile: 60 deep, 60 high, with the 20 by 20 notch at the top front. The top view shows a 60 by 60 square with a line across it 20 from the front edge, where the step drops.

The lesson in that example is that the shape of a view depends on which face the cut is visible on, and that a feature seen edge-on in one view appears as a single line. Working through it in order is the method: draw the front view full, project the widths down for the top view and the heights across for the side view, transfer the depth from top to side, then add the lines for the step in each view, dark where visible, dashed where hidden. For a step cut on the top there is no hidden detail; for a hole drilled from the top partway down, the front view would show the hole's sides as dashed lines.

Simple cubes and blocks with one cut are the right place to practise because every edge is horizontal or vertical and every size is easy to project. The skill transfers directly to the stairs: a staircase in side view is a set of steps, in front view a set of horizontal lines, in top view a set of rectangles, which is exactly the pattern of the cut cube repeated four times. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing the top view above the front view; that is third angle. Two: drawing the left side view on the left; in first angle it goes on the right. Three: views that do not line up, so widths differ between front and top. Four: erasing the projection lines; they stay feint.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three views of a cube with a step, how features appear edge-on, and the order of drawing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a wooden cube and a glass box — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Front, Top and Side

A picture drawing shows three sides at once but squashes the angles. You cannot measure from it.

Orthographic drawing looks straight at one face at a time. The face comes out true: a square is a square, 60 millimetres is 60 at scale.

One face only shows two sizes, so you draw three views. Front view: width and height. Top view: width and depth. Side view: depth and height.

Think of the object in a glass box. Each pane gives you one view.

Keep the glass box in mind and try the questions for this part: which view shows which two sizes.

## Subtopic: The First Angle Pattern

First angle has a fixed pattern. Front view first. Top view straight underneath it. Left side view on the right of the front view.

Yes, the side view goes on the opposite side. The object is between you and the pane, so the view lands on the far side.

Third angle, used in America, is the other way round. We use first angle. Top view above the front view is a mistake here.

Feint lines join the views: widths go down to the top view, heights go across to the side view, depth goes round a 45 degree line from top to side. Leave them feint.

Hold under and opposite in mind and try this part's questions: where each view goes.

## Subtopic: Lining the Views Up

Take a 60 cube with a 20 by 20 step cut across the top front. Front view: a 60 by 60 square with a line 40 up where the step edge is. Side view: an L shape with the notch at the top front. Top view: a square with a line 20 from the front.

A face you see edge-on becomes just a line. Hidden edges become dashed.

Order: front view, project down, project across, transfer depth, add details.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Orthographic projection shows true flat views instead of a picture. Front gives width and height, top gives width and depth, side gives depth and height. In first angle the top view sits below the front view and the left side view sits on the right. Feint projection lines line everything up and stay on the drawing.

Hold the cube and the notch, and take the final questions of the lesson: lining the views up.
