### subtopic_1 — Five Line Types and What Each One Says
**Assistant:** Why not just draw hidden edges as normal lines? The builder knows what is behind the wall.

**Tutor:** Because a drawing has to work for a reader who has never seen the object, and a solid line for a hidden edge would tell that reader the edge is visible when it is not. Consider the side view of the stairs: the edge of the concrete foundation runs under the ground and the back of each step is behind the step in front. If every edge were drawn dark, the side view would be a tangle of solid lines and the reader could not tell which surface is in front. The dashed line solves this with no extra words: it says this edge exists, it is at this position, but you are looking at it through something. That matters on site because hidden edges are often the ones that have to be built first, like a foundation or a reinforcing bar, and because the fitter of a handrail needs to know where the hidden fixing is. There is a deeper reason too. Orthographic projection shows an object as three flat views, and each view loses depth; the hidden detail line is one of the ways the drawing gives that depth back, telling the reader which of two overlapping edges is nearer. Learning to read dashed lines is a large part of learning to see a three-dimensional object in a two-dimensional drawing, which is the skill the whole term is building.

### subtopic_2 — Scale: Fitting a Building on a Page
**Assistant:** If the dimensions give the real sizes, why does the scale matter at all?

**Tutor:** The scale matters to the person making the drawing and to anyone checking it, even though the builder reads only the dimensions. When you draw, every line you set out has to be converted from the real size to the paper size, and the scale is the single number that makes all those conversions consistent, so that the ramp, the steps and the handrail are all in the same proportion and the drawing looks like the object. If one view were drawn at 1:50 and another accidentally at 1:20, the views would not line up and the projection would be nonsense. Scale also lets a reader check the drawing: if a dimension says 7 200 but the line measures 100 millimetres on a 1:50 drawing, something is wrong, because 100 times 50 is 5 000, and that mismatch catches a copying error before concrete is poured. Scale further governs what can be shown: at 1:100 a 150 millimetre riser is a line and a half thick, so the drawer knows to add a larger-scale detail. And finally the scale is written in the title block because a drawing is often photocopied, reduced or enlarged, and a reader needs to know that the stated scale applies only to the original sheet size; that is one more reason the dimensions, not the ruler, carry the truth.

### subtopic_3 — Dimensioning Rules That Make a Drawing Buildable
**Assistant:** Why is each dimension given only once? Repeating it in every view would be more helpful.

**Tutor:** It feels helpful, but repeated dimensions are a well-known source of building errors, and the rule exists because of expensive mistakes. Drawings get revised. Suppose the ramp width is changed from 1 000 to 1 200 millimetres during the design, and the width had been written in the top view, the front view and the section. The drawer changes it in two places and forgets the third. Now the drawing contradicts itself, and the builder has no way to know which figure is current; whichever one is used, there is a fair chance it is wrong, and a ramp built 200 millimetres too narrow is a ramp that fails the accessibility rules and has to be demolished. Giving each size once, in the view where it is clearest, means there is a single place to change and a single place to read. There is also a clarity reason: a drawing crowded with the same numbers in three views is harder to read than one where each view carries its own information, the top view the widths and lengths, the front view the heights. If a reader needs the riser height while looking at the top view, they glance at the front view, which is beside it and aligned with it, that alignment being exactly what orthographic projection provides. So the once-only rule is not meanness; it is how the drawing stays trustworthy through its whole life.
