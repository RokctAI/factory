### subtopic_1 — From Brief to Drawing: Choosing Views and Scale
**Assistant:** Why would someone make the side view the front view? Does the name not depend on the object?

**Tutor:** The names depend on how the drawer chooses to present the object, not on the object itself, and the choice is made for the reader's benefit. The front view is simply the view that faces the reader first, the one from which the others are projected, and the rule in every drawing standard is to pick as the front view the one that shows the most about the object's shape. For a staircase and ramp, a view from the approach direction shows the risers as flat horizontal lines and the ramp as a blank face, which tells the reader little; a view from the side shows the sawtooth of the steps, the slope of the ramp, the heights of the rails and the landing at the top, all at once. Many professional drawings of stairs therefore use the side elevation as the principal view and project the plan beneath it. What matters is not the label but the discipline that follows from it: once a view is chosen as the front, the top view goes below it and the end views go on the opposite sides from the faces they show, and the labels say which end is viewed. In the PAT you may choose either, and the marker will look for a consistent first angle arrangement, aligned views and a drawing from which every size can be read, not for a particular face called front.

### subtopic_2 — Setting Out the Front View and Projecting the Plan
**Assistant:** How do I check my drawing is right before handing it in? There are so many lines.

**Tutor:** Check it the way a builder would read it, with the brief beside you, going number by number. Start with the gradient: read the ramp length and the rise from the dimensions, divide, and confirm 7 200 over 600 is 12, so 1 in 12; if a dimension reads 144, you have written a scaled size and must correct it. Next the stairs: read the riser dimension and multiply by the number of risers; four at 150 must equal the rise of 600, and the treads at 250 must match the plan's stair depth of 1 000. Then the alignment: lay a ruler vertically and confirm that every width in the plan sits directly below the same width in the front view; lay it horizontally and confirm the heights in the side view match those in the front view; a slip here means a projection line was skipped. Then the line types, view by view: is every visible edge dark, every hidden edge dashed, every construction line feint and still present, the landing shown, the handrails at 900 in the side view. Then the title block: scale, symbol, units. Finally the hardest test, which is to hand the drawing to a classmate who has not seen the brief and ask them to tell you the riser height, the ramp width and the gradient without a ruler; if they can, the drawing works. Most errors are caught by the first two steps, the arithmetic ones, which is why the conversion table at the start is worth the two minutes it takes.

### subtopic_3 — The Side View, Hidden Detail and Dimensions
**Assistant:** Why is the ramp dashed in the side view if it is such an important part of the design?

**Tutor:** Because in that particular view it is behind the stairs, and the dashed line is the correct and complete way to show it; importance does not change how a line is drawn, position does. The side view is taken from one end of the combined structure, and from that end the stairs stand between the viewer and the ramp, so the ramp's sloping edge is a real edge that cannot be seen from there. Drawing it dark would tell the reader that the ramp is in front, which is false and would mislead a builder setting out the site. Drawing it dashed says exactly what is true: here is the ramp's slope, from ground to 600 over 7 200, and it lies beyond the stairs in this view. If you want the ramp to be seen dark, you have two honest options. You can take the side view from the other end, where the ramp is nearer and the stairs become the hidden detail; the label then says right end view, and the view goes on the left of the front view in first angle. Or you can add a section, cutting through the ramp along a chain line in the plan and drawing what the cut reveals, which is how architects usually show a ramp's gradient and construction. What you may not do is draw both stairs and ramp dark in one side view, because that is two objects in the same space and the drawing stops making sense. The dashed ramp is not a demotion; it is the drawing being truthful about depth.
