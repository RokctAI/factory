### subtopic_1 — Why a School Needs Both Stairs and a Ramp
**Assistant:** If a ramp works for everyone, why not build only a ramp and skip the stairs?

**Tutor:** Because a ramp that is gentle enough for a wheelchair is long, and for most people it is a slower and less convenient route than four steps. For a rise of 600 millimetres, a 1 in 12 ramp runs more than seven metres, so a learner late for class would take several times longer walking the ramp than taking the stairs, and at the end of the school day several hundred learners trying to leave through a single ramp would form a slow queue, which is itself a safety problem. Stairs move many people quickly in a small footprint; ramps move anyone, at any ability, but slowly and over a long distance. Providing both lets each user choose the route that suits them, and it also gives a second route out in an emergency if one is blocked. There are places where a ramp alone is the right answer, for instance a small rise of 150 millimetres at a single doorway, where a short gentle ramp serves everyone and a single step would be a trip hazard. But for a busy school entrance with a rise of 600 millimetres, the two together are what good design looks like: the stairs for throughput and the ramp for access, side by side so that a group of friends, one of them in a wheelchair, arrive at the same door together.

### subtopic_2 — The Numbers in a Design Brief: Steps, Risers, Width and Gradient
**Assistant:** Why is 1 in 12 the limit for a ramp? Who decided that and how?

**Tutor:** The figure comes from measuring what wheelchair users can actually do. A manual wheelchair user pushes themselves up a slope with their arms, and the force needed rises with the gradient: on 1 in 12 a typical adult user can climb a few metres unaided, on 1 in 8 most cannot, and on 1 in 6 the chair becomes dangerous because it tips backwards or runs away on the descent. Building regulations in South Africa and in many other countries settled on 1 in 12 as the steepest acceptable gradient, with 1 in 15 recommended where there is room, after studies of users of different ages and strengths and after consulting disability organisations. The number also protects people pushing a chair, a pram or a trolley, and people who walk with difficulty, who find a steep ramp as hard as stairs. It is important to see that 1 in 12 is a ceiling, not a target: a gentler ramp is always better for users, and the design brief should say 1 in 12 or gentler rather than exactly 1 in 12. The cost of gentleness is length, which is why ramps turn back on themselves with landings, and why the landing rule, a rest every 10 metres, exists: it is the point where arms get tired.

### subtopic_3 — Handrails, Landings and the Rules That Protect Users
**Assistant:** Why must the top landing be clear of the door swing? Surely a step is not so dangerous.

**Tutor:** A step is exactly where it becomes dangerous, because a door and a stair together produce a fall that neither would cause alone. Picture a person standing on the top step pulling a door open towards them: the door forces them to step backwards, and behind them is a drop. Or picture a door that opens outwards onto a stair: someone coming up is struck by the door at the moment they are balanced on a step, with nothing to hold. For a wheelchair user it is worse, since a chair on a slope cannot hold still while its user reaches for a handle; a flat landing at least 1 200 millimetres long lets the chair stand level while the door is opened, and a little extra width beside the handle gives room for the chair to sit alongside the door rather than in its path. The building regulations therefore require a level landing at the top of stairs and ramps at least as wide as the stair or ramp and long enough for the door to swing without reaching a step. In the PAT this is one of the things to look for when analysing existing entrances, because it is the commonest fault in buildings that were given a ramp as an afterthought: the ramp arrives at the door with no landing, and the user is left holding the door with one hand and the handrail with the other.
