# Part 1 — Expert

This session extends first angle orthographic projection to more complex objects: blocks with sloping faces, drilled holes, slots and rebates, drawn with instruments. The anchor is a steel bracket on a workbench in a Technology room in Port Elizabeth, with one chamfered corner and two bolt holes, the kind of part that holds a handrail post to a concrete step. The governing idea is that every feature of an object appears differently in each of the three views, true shape in the view that looks at it square-on and as a line or a foreshortened shape elsewhere, and that instruments, set squares, a compass and a mitre line, are what keep the three views consistent. Drawing such objects accurately, and interpreting given views, is examinable and is the level expected in PAT 1.

## Subtopic: Sloping Faces, Holes and Slots in Three Views

A sloping face is the first complication. Take a block 80 long, 40 wide and 40 high with the top front edge chamfered, that is, cut away at 45 degrees so that a sloping face 20 wide runs the length of the block. In the side view, which looks along the length, the slope appears as a true sloping line from a point 20 down the front face to a point 20 back along the top: that is the view that shows the chamfer's real angle. In the front view the chamfer is seen edge-on from below its edge, so it appears as a horizontal line 20 below the top, and the face between that line and the top edge is the slope foreshortened. In the top view the chamfer appears as a strip 20 wide along the front edge, again foreshortened. One feature, three different appearances, and only one of them shows the true angle.

A round hole drilled right through the block from the top appears in the top view as a circle, drawn with a compass, with two thin chain centre lines crossing at its centre. In the front view and the side view the hole is invisible from outside, so it appears as two dashed vertical lines the diameter apart, running from the top face to the bottom face, with a chain centre line between them. If the hole does not go through, the dashed lines stop at the hole's depth and are closed with a dashed line across the bottom.

A slot, a rectangular groove cut into the top, appears in the top view as a rectangle, in the view that looks along the slot as a U-shaped notch in the outline, and in the view that looks across the slot as two dashed lines for its sides and one for its floor. A rebate, a step cut along an edge, appears as an L in the view along it and as a line in the other two. The method for all of these is the same: find the view in which the feature is seen square-on and draw its true shape there, then project its edges into the other two views as lines, dark if visible and dashed if hidden.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a chamfer appears in each view, how a drilled hole appears, and the rule for deciding which view shows a feature's true shape.

## Subtopic: Using Instruments: Set Squares, Compass and the Mitre Line

Instruments make the drawing accurate and fast. The T-square or the parallel rule gives horizontal lines; the 90 degree set square against it gives verticals; the 45 degree set square gives chamfers and the mitre line; the 30 and 60 degree set square gives isometric axes and other slopes. Lines are drawn with a sharp pencil held at a constant angle, feint for construction with a hard pencil such as 2H and dark for outlines with a softer HB, so that the two weights are clearly different on the page.

The compass draws holes and arcs. Set it to the radius, not the diameter, place the point on the crossing of the centre lines, and swing it in one movement. For a hole of 12 millimetres diameter at 1:1 the radius is 6; at 1:2 it is 3. Centre lines are drawn first, then the circle, because the centre lines fix the position and the circle is dimensioned by its diameter, written with the diameter symbol or the word dia, with a leader line pointing to the circle.

The mitre line is the tool that transfers depths between the top view and the side view. Draw a feint line at 45 degrees from the corner where the horizontal line through the top of the side view meets the vertical line through the right edge of the top view, in the open space between them. Any depth in the top view is carried horizontally to the mitre line, then turned vertically down or up into the side view, and it arrives at the correct position because the two legs of a 45 degree triangle are equal. Alternatively, depths can be transferred with a compass centred on the corner, or by measurement; the mitre line is fastest and leaves a visible record of how the views were tied together.

The questions for this section are with you now: which set square gives which angle, how a compass is set and used, and how the mitre line transfers depths.

## Subtopic: Reading Three Views Back into a Solid

Reading three views back into a solid is the reverse skill and the harder one. Given a front view that is a rectangle with a notch at the top right, a top view that is a rectangle with a circle in the left half, and a side view that is a rectangle with dashed lines running from top to bottom, the method is to start with the simplest view and build outwards. The front view says the block has a rebate at the top right. The side view's dashed lines, which are full height, say the hole goes right through. The top view places the hole in the left half. Put together: a rectangular block with a step cut along the top right edge and a vertical through-hole near the left end. Sketching that object in isometric is the test of whether the reading is correct.

The habit to build is to pick a feature in one view and find it in the other two along the projection lines. A circle in one view must appear as a pair of parallel lines, dashed or dark, in the other two at the same width; a sloping line in one view must appear as a foreshortened face in the others; a notch in the outline of one view must appear as a line across the others. If a feature cannot be found in all three views, either the drawing is incomplete or the reading is wrong.

This is the level of drawing that Engineering Graphics and Design in the FET phase begins from, and the level at which the stairs and ramp drawing, with its handrail brackets, post holes and chamfered step nosings, is really done. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing a hole as a circle in every view; it is a circle only in the view that looks down its axis. Two: showing a chamfer at 45 degrees in the view where it appears foreshortened; the true angle appears only in the view along its length. Three: setting the compass to the diameter instead of the radius. Four: forgetting the centre lines, which fix a hole's position and are what a machinist works from.

The questions for this section are with you now: the method for reading three views into a solid, how to trace a feature across views, and the diameter and centre line conventions for holes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a steel bracket, a chamfered edge and two bolt holes — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: When the Edges Are Not Square

Not every edge is square. Some faces slope, and some blocks have holes.

A slope shows its real angle in one view only: the one that looks along the slope. In the other two views it is squashed, a flat strip or a line.

A hole drilled from the top is a circle in the top view, with two thin chain lines crossing in the middle. In the front and side views you cannot see it, so it is two dashed lines going down, a hole's width apart.

Find the view that looks straight at the feature. Draw its real shape there. In the other views it becomes lines.

Keep the squashed slope and the dashed hole in mind and try the questions for this part: where each feature shows true.

## Subtopic: Tools That Keep the Lines Honest

Tools keep the drawing honest. T-square for flat lines. 90 degree set square for upright lines. 45 degree set square for chamfers and the mitre line. 30 and 60 for other slopes.

Hard pencil, 2H, for feint lines. Softer HB for dark outlines. Keep them different.

Compass: set the radius, half the diameter. Point on the centre line crossing. Draw the centre lines first, then the circle.

The mitre line is a 45 degree feint line in the empty corner between the top view and the side view. Depths go across to it, turn the corner, and arrive in the side view at exactly the right place.

Hold the 45 degree set square in mind and try this part's questions: which tool for which line.

## Subtopic: From Flat Views to a Shape in Your Head

Now the reverse. Three views given, what is the object? Start with the easiest view. Find one feature. Hunt for it in the other two along the projection lines.

A circle in one view must be two parallel lines in the others. A notch in one view must be a line across the others. If you cannot find a feature in all three, something is wrong.

When you think you have it, sketch it in isometric. If the sketch matches all three views, you have read it right.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A feature shows its true shape in the view that looks at it square-on and appears as lines elsewhere, dashed if hidden. Slopes show their angle in one view only. Holes are a circle with centre lines in one view and dashed lines in the others. Set squares, a compass set to the radius and the 45 degree mitre line keep the views accurate and aligned. To read views, trace each feature through all three.

Hold the circle and its two dashed lines, and take the final questions of the lesson: reading views into shapes.
