### subtopic_1 — Sloping Faces, Holes and Slots in Three Views
**Assistant:** Why does a hole look like two dashed lines from the side? Surely a hole is round.

**Tutor:** It is round, but roundness is only visible when you look along the hole's axis, and the side view looks across it. Imagine a cardboard tube standing upright on the desk. From above you see a circle, the open end. From the side you see a rectangle: two vertical edges, which are the sides of the tube, and nothing round at all. A hole drilled through a block is that tube's empty space, so from the side its outline is the same pair of vertical edges, and because they are inside the block, behind its solid face, they are drawn dashed as hidden detail. The two dashed lines are exactly the hole's diameter apart and run from the top face to the bottom face if the hole goes through, or stop at the hole's depth and are closed off with a short dashed line if it does not, which is how a drawing tells a machinist whether to drill through or to a set depth. Between them runs a thin chain centre line, which marks the axis and is what the machinist uses to position the drill, and which appears in the top view as two crossing chain lines through the circle's centre. So the three views together describe the hole completely: the top view gives its position and diameter, the front and side views give its depth and show that it is vertical. That is the general lesson of complex objects: no single view describes a feature, and roundness, slope and depth each live in a different view.

### subtopic_2 — Using Instruments: Set Squares, Compass and the Mitre Line
**Assistant:** Why bother with set squares and a compass when I can draw freehand quickly?

**Tutor:** Because a working drawing is a set of instructions that will be measured, and freehand lines cannot be measured with confidence. In the design process both kinds of drawing have their place. Freehand sketches, done quickly in the Design stage, are for exploring ideas; nobody builds from them and a wobbly line costs nothing. A working drawing is different: a builder or a machinist reads a dimension and also checks it against the drawn length, aligns a plan with an elevation, and expects a 45 degree chamfer to be drawn at 45 degrees. If the verticals lean, the plan no longer sits under the front view and the projection breaks; if the circle is drawn freehand, its centre is uncertain and the hole cannot be positioned; if the chamfer is drawn by eye at 40 degrees, the dimension and the drawing disagree and the reader must guess which is right. Instruments remove that doubt. The T-square and set squares guarantee that every horizontal is parallel to every other and every vertical is at right angles, the compass guarantees a true circle about a known centre, and the mitre line guarantees that the depth in the side view equals the depth in the plan. They also make the drawing faster once the habit is learnt, because a straight line drawn against an edge needs no correction. In the PAT the marker will look for instrument-drawn lines of two clear weights, and in Grade 10 EGD it is simply assumed.

### subtopic_3 — Reading Three Views Back into a Solid
**Assistant:** When I read three views, how do I know I have found the right object and not another one that gives the same views?

**Tutor:** It is a sharp question, because for two views there can be several different objects that fit, and that is exactly why three views are drawn. A front view that is a plain square and a top view that is a plain square could be a cube, or a cube with a hole from the side, or a block with a slot along the end that you cannot see from the front or top. The third view settles it: the side view shows the hole as a circle or the slot as a notch, or shows a plain square and confirms a solid cube. With three views correctly drawn, including hidden detail, the object is fixed for any shape made of flat faces and simple holes; two different objects would have to differ in some edge, and that edge would appear as a line in at least one view. So the test of your reading is to check every line, not just the obvious features: each dark or dashed line in each view must be accounted for by an edge of the object you have imagined, and every edge of your object must appear in the views. If you find a line you cannot explain, your object is wrong or incomplete; if your object has an edge the views do not show, it is wrong too. Sketching the object in isometric and then mentally projecting it back into three views is the quickest way to run this check, and it is also the exercise that builds the spatial sense the rest of the course depends on.
