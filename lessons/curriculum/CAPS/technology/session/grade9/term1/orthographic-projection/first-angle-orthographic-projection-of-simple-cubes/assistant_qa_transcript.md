### subtopic_1 — Three Views Instead of One Picture
**Assistant:** If an isometric drawing shows three faces at once, why is it not enough for building?

**Tutor:** Because an isometric drawing is a picture and a picture always distorts something. In isometric the three visible faces are drawn with their edges at 30 degrees to the horizontal, which makes the object look solid, but the angle between two edges that is really 90 degrees appears as 60 or 120 degrees on the page, a circle on any face appears as an ellipse, and only the edges along the three isometric axes are drawn to true length; any slanting edge, such as the surface of a ramp, comes out at a false length. A builder who tried to measure the ramp's gradient from an isometric drawing would get the wrong answer. Isometric also shows only three faces, so anything on the back, the underside or the far end is invisible and would need a second picture. Orthographic views avoid all this by looking straight at each face: angles are true, circles are circles and every edge parallel to the page is at true scaled length. That is why the design process uses both. An isometric or oblique sketch is drawn early, to show a client or a tender board what the stairs and ramp will look like, and the orthographic working drawing is drawn later, to tell the builder exactly what to make. Each does the job the other cannot.

### subtopic_2 — Where the Views Go in First Angle Projection
**Assistant:** Why does the left side view go on the right in first angle? It seems backwards.

**Tutor:** It seems backwards until you picture how the view is made. In first angle projection the object is imagined sitting in the first quadrant, in front of a vertical pane and above a horizontal pane, and each view is produced by projecting the object straight through itself onto the pane behind it, like a shadow cast by light coming from the viewer's side. Stand on the left of the object and look at its left face: the pane that catches the view is on the right-hand side of the object, behind it from where you stand. When the glass box is unfolded flat, that right-hand pane opens out to the right of the front view, so the view of the left face appears on the right. The same logic puts the top view below the front view: look down from above and the shadow falls on the floor pane beneath, which unfolds downwards. Third angle projection imagines the object behind the panes instead, as if you were looking through glass at it, so each view lands on the near side and the arrangement flips. Neither system is more correct; they are two conventions, and a drawing declares which one it uses with a small symbol. South Africa follows first angle, as does Europe, and CAPS requires it, so the habit to build is: front view, top view underneath, side view on the opposite side.

### subtopic_3 — Projecting a Cube with a Step Cut Out
**Assistant:** How can I tell, from three flat views, which lines in the front view are a step and which are a hole?

**Tutor:** By reading the views together, which is the whole skill of orthographic projection: no single view tells you the shape, but the three in combination do. Take a line across the front view 40 millimetres up from the base. On its own it could be a step, a groove, a painted stripe or the far edge of a hole. Now glance at the side view, aligned with it on the right: if the side view shows an L profile with a notch at the top front, the line is the edge of a step that runs the full width. If instead the side view shows a plain square with a dashed rectangle inside it, the front view's line is the bottom of a hole or slot cut from the top, and the dashes tell you the hole does not go through. The top view settles the rest: a step across the whole width appears as a line right across the square; a slot appears as a rectangle within it; a round hole appears as a circle with a chain centre line. Reading this way, you move your eye between the views along the projection lines, which is exactly why they are left on the drawing and why the views must line up: a width in the top view must be directly below the same width in the front view, or the reading breaks down. With practice the three views assemble into a solid object in your mind, and that mental assembly is what the Grade 10 EGD course, and every engineering trade, will expect of you.
