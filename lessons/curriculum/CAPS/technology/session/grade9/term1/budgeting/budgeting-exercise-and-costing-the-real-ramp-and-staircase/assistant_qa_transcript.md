### subtopic_1 — From Estimate to Budget: Headings, Lines and Totals
**Assistant:** Why round up? If I need 22.4 bags, writing 22.4 is more accurate than 23.

**Tutor:** It is more accurate as a measurement and less accurate as a cost, because nobody sells 0.4 of a bag. The budget is a prediction of what will actually be paid, and what will be paid is the price of 23 bags, since the merchant will hand over 23 and the last one will be partly used. The same is true of every item sold in fixed units: steel tube comes in 6 metre lengths, so 19 metres means buying four lengths and ending up with 5 metres of offcut, which the budget should note so the team can think about using it for braces; planks come in standard lengths; a bricklayer is hired for a day, not for seven and a half hours. Writing the fraction makes the total look smaller than the bill will be, and a tender priced that way either loses money or has to go back to the client for more, which destroys trust. There is a second reason, which is that materials get wasted: bags split, the last of the sand is trodden into the mud, the end of a tube is cut off square and lost. That is what the 10 percent waste allowance covers, and it sits on top of the rounded quantities, not instead of them. Builders learn this the hard way; a Grade 9 team can learn it by rule. The one place where rounding is not applied is in the quantity column itself, where the calculated figure, 22.4, is written beside the rounded one, 23, so that a checker can see both the arithmetic and the purchasing decision.

### subtopic_2 — Costing the Real Ramp and Staircase Line by Line
**Assistant:** The fabricator's quote for the rails is one number. How do I show that line if I cannot break it down?

**Tutor:** You show it as a single line with the quote as its source, and that is entirely proper, because a quote is a price that someone has committed to charge, which is better evidence than any breakdown you could build yourself. Write the line as: handrails and posts, galvanised, supplied and fitted, 1 item, quoted price, source the fabricator's name and the date of the quote, with the quote itself filed behind the budget. The board can then see exactly what is included, and if a member asks why the rails cost what they do, the answer is that a named fabricator has quoted it and the quote is here. What you should still do is check that the quote matches your drawing, since a fabricator who has quoted for 15 metres of rail when your drawing needs 19 has quoted for a different job, and that the quote says whether galvanising, delivery and fitting are included, because each of those is either in the fabricator's line or in yours, and must not appear in both or in neither. It is also worth building your own rough figure for the rails from tube per metre and a welder's day rate, as the lesson did, and comparing it with the quote; if they are far apart, one of them is wrong or the quote includes something you have not noticed. That comparison is itself good evidence for the Investigate and Design marks, because it shows the team understands what it is buying. Many real budgets are mostly quotes from subcontractors with the main contractor's own work costed from first principles, so a mixed budget is the normal shape, not a compromise.

### subtopic_3 — Checking, Trimming and Presenting the Tender Price
**Assistant:** What if our total is much higher than the other teams'? Should we cut it to win the tender?

**Tutor:** Not by cutting things that make the ramp work, and not without first finding out why the totals differ, because a high total is either an error or a design choice, and those call for different responses. Start by checking. Re-multiply every line; compare your concrete volume with the others' for the same drawing; look at your rail length and your labour days. Very often a high total is a slipped unit, hardcore priced per cubic metre but entered per load, or a rail quoted twice, once in materials and again as the fabricator's line. If the arithmetic is right, look at the design. A turning ramp with an extra landing costs more concrete and rail than a straight one; a 1 in 15 ramp is longer than a 1 in 12; ready-mixed concrete costs more than site-mixed. Those are real differences, and the board may well pay more for a gentler ramp that suits the site. If your design is more expensive for reasons you can defend, defend them, and the budget page should say so plainly: this price buys a 1 in 15 gradient and a landing at the turn. What you must not do is hide the cost by removing the waste allowance or the contingency, by rounding down, by leaving out transport, or by quietly shortening the ramp to 1 in 10, because the first three mean the real job will cost more than the tender and the last breaks a specification and excludes the user the ramp is for. Tender boards see all of these tricks and mark them down; a slightly higher price with every line explained beats a low price with holes in it. If, after checking, your design is simply more expensive than it needs to be, go back to Design and choose a cheaper layout that still meets every specification, and record that decision. That is budgeting doing its job.
