# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the practical budgeting exercise and the costing of the real-life ramp and staircase including materials and labour, the week 9 task of PAT 1 that produces the tender price. The anchor is a team in Pietermaritzburg with its working drawing, its price list from the builders' merchant and the steel fabricator, and a blank budget sheet with the headings Materials, Labour, Transport, Allowances and Total. The governing idea is that a budget is an estimate made disciplined: every quantity comes from the drawing, every price from a named source, every line is traceable, waste and contingency are added openly, and the total is checked against benchmarks before it is presented. Producing such a budget is examinable and is the figure the tender board will judge in week 10.

## Subtopic: From Estimate to Budget: Headings, Lines and Totals

A budget sheet has a fixed shape. At the top: the project, the team, the date and the validity of the prices. Then sections. Materials: one line per item with quantity, unit, unit price, source and total. Labour: one line per trade with number of workers, days, daily rate and total. Transport: one line per delivery or trip with its cost. Allowances: waste at 10 percent of materials, contingency at 10 percent of the subtotal. Then the total, and a short note of what is excluded, such as removing the old blocks or painting the hall wall, so that the client knows what the price covers.

The practical exercise before the real budget is small enough to finish in twenty minutes and teaches the arithmetic. Cost a single step of 1 200 wide, 250 deep and 150 high in concrete: volume is 1.2 times 0.25 times 0.15, which is 0.045 cubic metres; at 7 bags per cubic metre that is 0.32 bags of cement, call it a third of a bag, plus 0.02 cubic metres each of sand and stone; shuttering is a metre or so of plank; labour is perhaps half an hour of a bricklayer's day. Multiplying each by its price and adding gives a cost per step, and four steps gives the stairs, before the foundation. The exercise shows how small quantities add up and how to handle fractions of a bag or a day, by rounding up to whole units that must be bought.

Rounding up is a budgeting rule. Cement is sold by the bag, so 22.4 bags becomes 23; tube is sold in 6 metre lengths, so 19 metres becomes 24, which is four lengths, with the offcut noted; a bricklayer is paid by the day, so 7.5 days becomes 8. Rounding down to make the total smaller is how tenders lose money. Another rule is that each line shows its working, quantity times price, so that a checker can repeat it. A third is that prices are copied from the source sheet exactly, with the date, so that the budget can be defended line by line.

The questions for this section are with you now: the sections of a budget sheet, how the single-step exercise is costed, and the rounding rule.

## Subtopic: Costing the Real Ramp and Staircase Line by Line

Now the real costing from the working drawing. Concrete: ramp slab 7 200 by 1 200 by 150 is 1.3 cubic metres; fill under it averaging 300 deep is 2.6 cubic metres of compacted hardcore, which is priced by the load; landing 1 500 by 1 200 by 150 is 0.27; stairs with foundation about 0.6; kerb 75 by 100 along 15 metres about 0.11; total concrete about 2.3 cubic metres, which at 7 bags, 0.5 sand and 0.5 stone per cubic metre is 16 bags, 1.2 cubic metres of sand and 1.2 of stone, rounded to 17 bags, 1.5 and 1.5 as sold. Mesh: two sheets. Shuttering: planks and pegs. Rails: handrail along both sides of ramp, stairs and landing about 19 metres plus posts 14 metres, so 33 metres of 40 millimetre galvanised tube in six lengths, or the fabricator's quote for the rails made, galvanised and delivered, which is usually the better price and is used if it is in hand. Anchor bolts 16. A rubber movement strip at the top join. Non-slip finish by brushing, no cost.

Labour: excavate and compact fill, build the block retaining edge, set shuttering, mix and place concrete, finish surfaces, remove shuttering, fix rails: one bricklayer and two general workers for 8 days, plus the fabricator's installer for 1 day if rails are supplied fitted. Daily rates from the local builder. Transport: hardcore delivered, two merchant deliveries for cement, sand, stone and mesh, the fabricator's delivery, and the crew's daily travel if they are from out of town. Each is a line.

Allowances: 10 percent on materials for waste; 10 percent on the subtotal for contingency. The result is a total that, with real local prices entered, falls in the range of several tens of thousands of rands for a job of this size, the exact figure depending on the town, the year and the rail quote. The team enters its own numbers and gets its own total; the method, not the figure, is what is taught, and a total that cannot be traced back through the lines is not a budget.

The questions for this section are with you now: the concrete quantities from the drawing, the rail length and how it is bought, and the labour and transport lines.

## Subtopic: Checking, Trimming and Presenting the Tender Price

The total is checked three ways before it goes to the board. Arithmetic: every line's quantity times price is re-multiplied by a second team member, and the columns re-added. Sense: the total is compared with what the week 4 analysis suggested similar entrances cost, and with a rough rule of thumb from a builder if one can be asked; a total far outside that range means a line is wrong, a decimal point slipped or a unit confused, and the budget is not presented until the reason is found. Proportion: materials, labour and transport are expressed as percentages of the total; for concrete work materials are usually the largest share, and a budget showing labour at 5 percent or transport at 40 percent has an error somewhere.

Trimming looks for savings that keep every specification. Replacing the turning ramp with a straight one saves a landing's worth of concrete and rail, but only if the straight ramp fits the site; shortening the ramp is not an option because the gradient is a specification. Site-mixed concrete versus ready-mixed, fabricated rails versus tube bought and welded on site, hardcore from a local quarry versus the merchant, these are legitimate comparisons and the budget may show the chosen option and note the alternative. Cutting the contingency or the waste allowance is not a saving; it is a hidden risk, and a tender board that knows its business will ask about it.

The budget page for the presentation is a clean summary: the five section totals, the allowances, the grand total in large figures, the validity date and the exclusions, with the detailed sheet available behind it for questions. The team should be ready to answer three questions the board will ask: where did this price come from, what happens if the cement price rises, and what is not included. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a total with no lines behind it. Two: rounding down to look cheap. Three: cutting the contingency to beat another team. Four: quantities that do not match the working drawing, so the board finds a 7 200 ramp costed as 6 000.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three checks on the total, which savings are legitimate and which are not, and what the presentation budget page shows.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the drawing, the price list and a blank budget sheet — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Budget Is an Estimate with Rules

A budget is an estimate with rules. Headings: Materials, Labour, Transport, Allowances, Total. Each line: what, how many, price each, where the price came from, total.

Practise on one step first. 1.2 by 0.25 by 0.15 is 0.045 cubic metres. About a third of a bag of cement, a little sand and stone, a plank, half an hour of a bricklayer. Price each, add up. Four steps make the stairs.

Round up. 22.4 bags is 23. 19 metres of tube is four 6 metre lengths. 7.5 days is 8. Rounding down is how you lose money.

Show the working on every line. Copy prices exactly, with the date.

Keep the rounding rule in mind and try the questions for this part: an estimate with rules.

## Subtopic: Concrete, Steel, People and Trips

Concrete from the drawing: ramp 1.3, landing 0.27, stairs 0.6, kerb 0.11. About 2.3 cubic metres. That is 17 bags, 1.5 of sand, 1.5 of stone, as sold. Fill: 2.6 cubic metres of hardcore by the load. Mesh: two sheets. Shuttering planks.

Rails: 19 metres of handrail plus 14 of posts, 33 metres of galvanised tube, or the fabricator's quote for rails made and fitted. Bolts: 16.

Labour: a bricklayer and two workers for 8 days. Transport: hardcore, two merchant deliveries, the fabricator's delivery, travel.

Add 10 percent to materials for waste and 10 percent to everything for surprises. Put in your own prices and get your own total.

Hold 17 bags and 33 metres in mind and try this part's questions: concrete, steel, people and trips.

## Subtopic: Does the Number Make Sense?

Check the total three ways. Redo the arithmetic with a teammate. Compare with what similar entrances cost in week 4; if it is way off, find the slipped decimal. Look at the shares: materials should be the biggest for concrete work.

Save money honestly: a straight ramp if it fits, site-mixed concrete, rails from the fabricator. Never shorten the ramp, never cut the contingency.

Presentation page: five totals, allowances, grand total big, date, what is not included. Be ready for: where did this come from, what if cement goes up, what is excluded.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A budget has sections for materials, labour, transport and allowances, with every line showing quantity times a sourced price, rounded up to units sold. Quantities come from the working drawing: about 2.3 cubic metres of concrete, 33 metres of tube, 8 days of a three-person crew. The total is checked for arithmetic, sense and proportion, trimmed only in ways that keep the specifications, and presented as a clean page with its detail behind it.

Hold the three checks, and take the final questions of the lesson: does the number make sense.
