### subtopic_1 — First Angle Orthographic Projection Recalled
**Assistant:** Why does first angle put the top view underneath the front view, when it seems upside down?

**Tutor:** Because of where the drawing plane is imagined. In first angle projection the object sits between you and the paper, and each view is what the object casts onto the paper behind it. Look at the object from the front and the front view lands on the plane behind it. Look down at it from above and the view lands on the plane beneath it, which, when the planes are unfolded flat onto the page, is below the front view. Look at it from the right and the view lands on the plane to its left, so the right side view sits on the left. Third angle projection, used in North America, imagines the paper between you and the object, like glass, so each view lands on the near side, and the top view goes above. Both are correct systems; they are simply different conventions, and the symbol in the title block tells the reader which was used. South Africa's standard, and the CAPS examination, use first angle, so a top view drawn above the front view is marked as the wrong system, however neat it is.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing the Plastic Item: Views, Lines and Dimensions
**Assistant:** How do I decide which dimensions to put on and which to leave off?

**Tutor:** Ask what someone would need to make the item with nothing but your drawing, and give each of those once. Start with the three overall sizes, length, width and height, because without them nothing else has meaning. Then the position of every feature from a datum, a reference edge or centre line that you choose and use consistently, so that a hole is placed by two distances from the same corner rather than by a chain of distances that add up errors. Then the size of every feature: a diameter for each hole, a radius for each rounded corner, an angle for each taper, a thickness for walls and ribs, usually in the section. Leave off anything that can be worked out from what is already there; if you give the overall length and the position of the clip from the left end, do not also give the distance from the right end. Leave off anything that is the same everywhere and can be covered by a note, such as all radii R2 unless stated. The test is to cover the object and hand the drawing to someone else: if they have to guess anything, a dimension is missing; if a figure appears twice, one is a repeat.

### subtopic_3 — Showing Moulding Features and Checking the Drawing
**Assistant:** Why does the drawing need a section view when I have already drawn three views?

**Tutor:** Because the outside views cannot show what matters most in a moulded part, which is what it is like inside. From the outside a cup holder looks like a solid block; only a section reveals that it is a shell with 2.5 millimetre walls, ribs standing on the inside, and a socket that tapers inward. Those are the features that decide whether the part can be moulded without sinking, warping or sticking in the mould, and whether it uses the right amount of material. Hidden-detail dashes can show some of this, but on a part with several internal features the dashes pile up and become unreadable. A section removes the near half of the object along a marked plane, shows the cut surface hatched, and leaves the inside in plain view with solid lines. It is also where wall and rib thickness are dimensioned. For a factory the section is the view the mould maker reads first, because the mould is the inverse of exactly that shape.
