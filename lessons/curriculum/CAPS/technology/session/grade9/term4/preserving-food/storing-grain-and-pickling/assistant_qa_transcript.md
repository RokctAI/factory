### subtopic_1 — Why Food Spoils and How Preservation Stops It
**Assistant:** How did people know to seal grain in pits before anyone knew about bacteria or insects needing air?

**Tutor:** By observation over generations, which is how most technology was developed before science explained it. A farmer who stored grain two ways and found that the sealed pit kept better than the open basket did not need to know why; the result was enough to repeat it, and the knowledge passed to the next generation as the right way to do it. The reasons we now give, that a sealed pit has too little oxygen for insects and moulds and stays cool because the earth insulates it, are the explanation, not the discovery. The same is true of smoking cobs over the cooking fire, which was probably noticed to keep weevils away long before anyone identified the chemicals in smoke. This is worth saying in an examination answer on indigenous technology: it was tested, refined and transmitted knowledge, as real as any engineering, and modern silos use exactly the principles it arrived at.

### subtopic_2 — Storing Grain: Indigenous and Modern Methods
**Assistant:** Why does fermentation preserve food when fermentation is a kind of spoiling?

**Tutor:** Because it is a controlled race in which a harmless organism wins. Left alone in air, a vegetable is colonised by whatever lands on it, including moulds and bacteria that rot it or make it unsafe. Put the vegetable in brine and seal out the air, and the conditions favour one particular group, the lactic acid bacteria, which are already on the vegetable, tolerate salt, do not need oxygen and produce lactic acid as they feed. As the acid builds, the brine becomes too sour for the rotting organisms and even for other bacteria, so the lactic acid bacteria have the food to themselves and then slow down in their own acid. The vegetable is changed, softer and sour, but it is safe and keeps for months. So fermentation is spoilage in the sense that organisms are eating the food, but it is preservation in the sense that the right organisms have made it uninhabitable for the wrong ones, which is why sauerkraut, atchar, yoghurt and sour porridge have fed people safely for thousands of years.

### subtopic_3 — Pickling: Acid as a Preservative
**Assistant:** If pickles keep for months, why do they say to refrigerate after opening?

**Tutor:** Because opening the jar changes two of the conditions the pickle relied on. Sealed, the jar had no fresh air and no new organisms arriving, and the acid kept the few inside from growing. Open it and air gets in, moulds from the kitchen land on the surface of the liquid, and every time a fork goes in it carries more. The acid still slows growth, which is why the pickle does not spoil in a day the way a cut tomato does, but it no longer stops it completely, especially for moulds on the surface that tolerate acid better than bacteria do. Cooling the open jar in a fridge slows the survivors down further, so the pickle lasts weeks instead of days. The two rules together, keep it sealed until you need it and keep it cool once opened, are the same denial of air and warmth used for grain, applied to a jar.
