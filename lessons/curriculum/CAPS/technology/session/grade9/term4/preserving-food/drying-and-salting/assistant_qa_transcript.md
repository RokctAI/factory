### subtopic_1 — Drying: Removing the Water That Spoilers Need
**Assistant:** If dried food still has organisms on it, why does it not spoil?

**Tutor:** Because an organism without water is alive in name only; it cannot do anything. Bacteria and moulds feed by dissolving nutrients from their surroundings and taking them in through their surfaces, and they grow and divide using water as the medium for everything happening inside them. Below a certain moisture level there is no free water to dissolve food in or to run those processes, so the organisms stop, some die and the rest sit dormant as spores. The food is preserved, not sterilised. The practical consequence is the rule that dried food must be kept dry: a bag of dried fruit left open in a humid kitchen takes up water from the air, the moisture rises back past the threshold, the dormant spores wake and the fruit moulds within days. Sealed containers, and in commercial packs a small sachet of silica gel to absorb moisture, keep dried food dry. The same principle explains why a damp corner of a grain store spoils while the rest is fine.

### subtopic_2 — Salting: Drawing Water Out and Making Food Inhospitable
**Assistant:** Why do the salted slices end up sitting in liquid if salt is supposed to dry them?

**Tutor:** Because the salt is drying them from the inside, and the water it pulls out has to go somewhere. Salt on the surface dissolves in the little moisture there to form a very strong brine. Inside the apple's cells the water is much less salty. Water moves from where it is less concentrated in dissolved substances to where it is more, through the cell walls, which is osmosis, so water flows out of the cells toward the salt. The cells shrink and the slice firms and shrivels, which is the drying; the water collects around the slice as brine, which is the liquid you see. In salting fish, that brine is drained off and the fish is then air-dried, so the two methods work together. In the investigation the brine around the salted slice is itself a result worth recording: it is water that was in the apple and is now outside it, where no organism inside the apple can use it, and it is far too salty for anything to grow in.

### subtopic_3 — The Practical Investigation: Drying and Salting Compared
**Assistant:** Why does the investigation need the control slices if we already know they will go mouldy?

**Tutor:** Because knowing and showing are different, and an investigation has to show. The control slices are the only way to prove that the treatments caused the difference. Suppose the salted and dried slices show no mould after a week; without a control, a sceptic can say that the room was too cool for mould anyway, or the apples were unusually clean, or a week is too short. The control, same apple, same thickness, same room, same week, with mould on day four, answers all of that: the conditions were right for spoilage and the only thing that stopped it was the treatment. The control also gives the baseline for the graph, so the mass loss from drying can be compared with the small natural loss from a slice just sitting there. In every investigation in this course, the untreated or unchanged case is the measuring stick; leaving it out turns an experiment into a demonstration.
