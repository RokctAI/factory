# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers preserving food by drying and salting, with a practical investigation. The anchor is a classroom windowsill in Kimberley on a hot afternoon with trays of apple slices and tomato halves: one tray plain, one dusted with salt, one in the sun under a mesh cover, and a kitchen scale and a notebook beside them, the slices weighed each day for a week. The governing idea is that drying preserves by removing the water that micro-organisms need to live and multiply, that salting preserves by drawing water out of the food and out of the organisms by osmosis and by making the food too salty for most of them, and that a controlled investigation comparing treated and untreated food by mass and appearance over time shows these effects measurably. Explaining drying and salting and carrying out and recording a comparative preservation investigation are examinable.

## Subtopic: Drying: Removing the Water That Spoilers Need

Every spoilage organism needs water to live. Bacteria and moulds are mostly water themselves and take in dissolved food through their surfaces; take the water out of the food and they cannot feed, grow or multiply. Drying reduces the moisture in food to a level where organisms stop, typically below about 15 percent for fruit and meat and 12 percent for grain. The food is not sterile, the organisms are still there, but they are inactive, which is why dried food spoils again if it gets damp.

Drying has been done in southern Africa for as long as there have been people here. Strips of meat hung in the dry wind become biltong, especially in the Karoo where the air is dry and the nights cold; the strips are first rubbed with salt and vinegar, which help preserve while the drying is under way and add flavour. Fish split and hung on racks in the sun on the West Coast become bokkoms. Maize cobs are dried on the stalk and then on racks or roofs; pumpkin and sweet potato are sliced and sun-dried; fruit, apricots, peaches and raisins, is dried on racks in the Western Cape and the Orange River valley. Modern drying uses the same principle in gas or electric dehydrators with warm moving air, and in freeze-drying for coffee and instant food.

Good drying is fast enough to beat the spoilers but not so hot that the food cooks. Thin slices dry faster than thick; moving air dries faster than still; a mesh cover keeps flies off; a dry climate helps and a humid one hinders, which is why the coast is harder than the interior. Dried food is light to carry and keeps for months in a sealed container, which is why hikers, soldiers and traders have always carried it. Its limit: it changes the food's texture and flavour, and it must be kept dry afterwards or it moulds.

The questions for this section are with you now: why removing water stops spoilage, drying in southern Africa from biltong to dried fruit, and the rules and limits of good drying.

## Subtopic: Salting: Drawing Water Out and Making Food Inhospitable

Salt preserves in two ways. First, it draws water out. When salt is rubbed on food or the food is put in strong brine, water moves out of the food's cells toward the salt, by osmosis, and the food dries from the inside; the same happens to any bacterium or mould on the food, which shrivels as its water is pulled out. Second, the salt that remains in the food makes it too salty for most organisms to live in even if some moisture is left. Heavily salted food is therefore preserved by two effects at once, low water and high salt, and keeps for months without refrigeration.

Salted fish is the classic example: snoek and other fish split, rubbed with coarse salt and stacked, then often dried as well. Salted meat preceded refrigeration on every long journey; corned beef, salt pork and bacon are salted meats. Biltong uses salt as part of its process. Salt cod from the North Atlantic fed much of Europe and Africa for centuries. In southern Africa, salt was traded over long distances from pans in the interior and from the coast because of its value in preserving food, not only in flavouring it.

Salting's limits. The food becomes very salty and usually must be soaked in water before cooking to make it edible, which is why salt fish is soaked overnight. Too much salt in the diet is unhealthy, so salted foods are eaten in moderation. Salt does not stop every organism; some salt-tolerant moulds and bacteria survive, which is why salted food is also dried, smoked or kept cool. Sugar preserves in the same way as salt, drawing water out and making food too sweet for organisms, which is how jam and candied fruit keep.

The questions for this section are with you now: salt drawing water out by osmosis and making food inhospitable, salted fish and meat in history, and the limits of salting and the parallel with sugar.

## Subtopic: The Practical Investigation: Drying and Salting Compared

The investigation compares drying, salting and no treatment on the same food over a week. Materials: apple slices or tomato halves cut to the same thickness, or thin strips of lean meat if the school allows; coarse salt; three trays or plates; a mesh cover or clean cloth; a kitchen scale reading to a gram; labels; a notebook. Hygiene: clean hands, clean knife and boards, no tasting of any sample, and everything thrown away at the end; the samples are for observation, not for eating.

Method. Cut six slices of equal thickness. Weigh each slice and record its mass. Place two on the control tray with no treatment. Rub two generously with salt and place them on the salted tray. Place two on the drying tray in the sun under the mesh, or in a warm oven on its lowest setting with the door ajar for a few hours each day. Keep all three trays in the same room apart from the drying time so that only the treatment differs. Each day at the same time, weigh each slice, record the mass, and note its appearance: colour, firmness, any mould spots, any smell, any liquid drawn out. Photograph or sketch the trays.

Expected results after a week in a warm dry room: the control slices lose some water, brown, soften and show mould spots within three to five days, with a sour smell; the salted slices lose water quickly into a brine around them, stay firm, and show little or no mould; the dried slices lose half or more of their mass in the first two days, become leathery or crisp, and show no mould. Plot mass against day for the three treatments on one graph. Conclude that drying and salting both reduce water and both prevent the mould seen on the control, with drying removing the most mass and salting preserving while drawing out a visible brine. The error museum, four exhibits. One: slices of different thickness, so the drying rate is not comparable. Two: trays kept in different places, so temperature and not treatment explains the difference. Three: tasting samples. Four: no control, so there is nothing to compare against.

The questions for this section are with you now: the materials and hygiene of the investigation, the method with daily weighing and notes, and the expected results, graph and conclusion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the three trays of apple slices on the windowsill in Kimberley and a scale beside them — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: No Water, No Spoiling

Every spoiler needs water. Dry the food below about 15 percent moisture and they stop, though they are still there, so keep it dry afterwards.

Biltong in the Karoo wind, bokkoms on the West Coast, maize on racks, dried peaches and raisins along the Orange River. Modern dehydrators and freeze-drying do the same job.

Dry fast, not hot. Thin slices, moving air, a mesh against flies, a dry climate. Light to carry, keeps for months sealed. Changes the texture and taste.

Keep no water, no spoiling in mind and try the questions for this part: drying.

## Subtopic: Salt Pulls the Water Out

Salt does two things. It pulls water out of the food and out of any germ on it, by osmosis, so they shrivel. And what salt stays in the food makes it too salty for most germs anyway.

Salted snoek, corned beef, salt pork, bacon, biltong, salt cod. Salt was traded across the interior because it kept food, not just flavoured it.

Limits: too salty to eat without soaking, unhealthy in quantity, some salt-tolerant moulds survive, so salt and dry or smoke. Sugar works the same way: jam and candied fruit.

Hold salt pulls the water out in mind and try this part's questions: salting.

## Subtopic: Weigh It, Treat It, Wait, Weigh Again

Six equal slices, a scale, three trays, salt, a mesh. Clean hands and knife. No tasting. Everything thrown away after.

Weigh each slice. Two plain. Two rubbed with salt. Two in the sun under the mesh or in a barely warm oven. Same room otherwise. Weigh and look every day: colour, firmness, mould, smell, liquid.

Expected: plain slices brown, soften and mould in three to five days. Salted slices sit in their own brine, firm, little mould. Dried slices lose half their mass in two days, go leathery, no mould. Graph mass against days.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Drying preserves by removing the water that organisms need, as in biltong, bokkoms and dried fruit, and must be fast, thin-sliced and followed by dry storage. Salting preserves by drawing water out of food and organisms by osmosis and by leaving the food too salty for most to grow, as in salted fish and meat, with sugar acting the same way. A fair investigation treats equal slices three ways in the same conditions, weighs and inspects them daily, graphs the mass loss and finds that both treatments prevent the mould seen on the control.

Hold weigh it, treat it, wait, weigh again, and take the final questions of the lesson: the investigation.
