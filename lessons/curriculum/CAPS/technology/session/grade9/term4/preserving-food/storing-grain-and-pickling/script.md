# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the theory of preserving food by storing grain and by pickling. The anchor is a homestead near Giyani with a raised woven grain basket on stilts under a thatched cap, maize cobs hanging in the smoke of the cooking hut, a sealed clay pot of sorghum in the ground, and in the kitchen a jar of atchar made from green mangoes in oil, vinegar and spices. The governing idea is that food spoils because of micro-organisms, insects and animals that need moisture, warmth, air and access, that grain storage denies them by keeping grain dry, cool, sealed and off the ground, and that pickling preserves by making food too acidic for spoilage organisms to grow, whether by adding vinegar or by fermentation that produces acid. Explaining why food spoils and how grain storage and pickling preserve food, including indigenous methods, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Food Spoils and How Preservation Stops It

Food spoils because living things consume it. Bacteria and moulds, too small to see, land on food from the air, from hands and from surfaces, and if they find moisture, warmth and usually air they multiply, breaking the food down, producing bad smells, slime and sometimes poisons. Yeasts ferment sugars. Insects such as weevils and moths lay eggs in grain and their larvae eat it from inside. Rats and mice eat and foul stored food. Each spoiler needs certain conditions: moisture above a level, a temperature range, air for most moulds, and a way in.

Preservation works by denying one or more of those conditions. Drying removes moisture. Cooling slows the organisms down; freezing stops them. Sealing keeps out air, insects and animals. Salt and sugar draw water out of the organisms. Acid stops most bacteria growing. Heat, as in canning, kills them and sealing keeps new ones out. Smoke coats food with chemicals that stop growth and dries it at the same time. Every method humans have used for ten thousand years is one or more of these, and the indigenous methods of southern Africa are good engineering by people who understood the problem without microscopes.

Preserving food is as important as growing it. A maize harvest in May must feed a family until the next harvest a year later; a catch of fish must reach a market days away; vegetables that ripen together in summer must be eaten through winter. Losses after harvest in parts of Africa still run to a third of the crop, mostly to insects, moisture and rodents in storage, so improving storage feeds as many people as growing more.

The questions for this section are with you now: the spoilers and the conditions they need, the methods of preservation as denial of those conditions, and why preservation matters as much as production.

## Subtopic: Storing Grain: Indigenous and Modern Methods

Grain is the staple: maize, sorghum, millet and wheat. Dry grain keeps for years if it stays dry, cool, sealed and out of reach. Indigenous storage in southern Africa solved all four. Grain was dried hard in the sun and sometimes in the smoke of the cooking hut, which also discouraged insects. It was stored in woven baskets raised on stilts, with a thatched cap against rain and the stilts sometimes fitted with flat stones or smooth collars that rats could not climb; the raised floor kept the grain off damp ground and let air move beneath. Where long storage or hiding from raiders was needed, grain went into pits dug in dry ground, lined with grass or dung plaster, filled and sealed with a clay cap; the sealed pit had little air, so insects suffocated and moulds could not grow, and the earth kept it cool. Large clay pots with lids, plastered inside, served the same purpose in the hut.

Modern storage uses the same four principles with new materials. The co-op silo is a tall steel or concrete cylinder, filled from the top with grain dried to about 12 percent moisture, with fans blowing air through to keep it cool and dry, sealed against rodents and birds, and sometimes fumigated to kill insects. On the farm, grain is kept in steel tanks or in large sealed plastic bags that exclude air so that insects die, a modern form of the sealed pit. In the home, maize meal is kept in a lidded plastic bucket in a cool cupboard, which is the raised basket and the clay pot in a new form.

The engineering comparison for the examination: both indigenous and modern methods dry the grain first, keep it cool, seal it against air and pests and raise it off the ground; the indigenous methods use local materials, need no power and are small-scale; the modern methods handle huge quantities, control moisture precisely and cost more to build and run. The common failure in both is moisture getting in, through a leaking cap, a cracked silo wall or a bucket left open, after which mould grows within days and can produce toxins that make the grain unsafe.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four conditions for grain storage and the indigenous methods that meet them, modern silos and sealed bags, and the comparison and the common failure.

## Subtopic: Pickling: Acid as a Preservative

Pickling preserves food in acid. Most spoilage bacteria cannot grow when the food around them is strongly acidic, so a vegetable sitting in a liquid acidic enough is safe for months. There are two routes to the acid. The first adds it: vegetables are packed in jars and covered with vinegar, which is a solution of acetic acid, often with salt, sugar and spices for flavour; the vinegar soaks into the vegetable and the whole jar becomes too acidic for spoilers. Pickled onions, gherkins, beetroot and the vinegar part of an atchar work this way.

The second route makes the acid in the jar. Vegetables are packed in salted water, brine, and sealed away from air. Salt-tolerant bacteria that are naturally present on the vegetables, the lactic acid bacteria, feed on the vegetable's sugars and produce lactic acid; over days the brine turns sour, the acid builds until nothing else can grow, and the vegetable is preserved and changed in flavour. Sauerkraut, Korean kimchi, many African fermented vegetables and the fermented maize and sorghum drinks of southern Africa, mageu and the sour porridges, are made this way. The fermentation is a controlled spoilage in which a harmless organism gets there first and makes the food uninhabitable for harmful ones.

Pickling's rules. The vegetable must be fully covered by the liquid, since anything above the surface is in air and moulds. The jar must be clean and sealed. The acid must be strong enough: vinegar of about 5 percent acetic acid, not diluted too far, or a fermentation allowed to run until properly sour. Salt helps by drawing water out of unwanted organisms. Pickles keep for months unopened and for weeks in a cool place once open. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying food spoils because it gets old rather than because organisms grow in it. Two: storing grain damp, so mould grows even in a sealed container. Three: pickling with vinegar watered down until it is not acidic enough. Four: leaving vegetables poking above the pickling liquid.

The questions for this section are with you now: how acid preserves and pickling with vinegar, pickling by fermentation and its southern African examples, and the rules of pickling.

## Subtopic: Keep the Spoilers Out

Food spoils because things eat it first: bacteria and moulds from the air, yeasts, weevils and moths, rats. Each needs some of: moisture, warmth, air, a way in.

Preserving means taking one of those away. Dry it. Cool or freeze it. Seal it. Salt or sugar it. Make it sour. Cook and seal it. Smoke it. Every method ever used is one of these.

A harvest in May must last a year. Losses in storage can be a third of the crop. Good storage feeds people as much as good farming.

## Subtopic: Dry, Cool, Sealed, Off the Ground

Grain keeps for years if it is dry, cool, sealed and off the ground. Indigenous way: dry it in sun and smoke, store it in a woven basket on stilts with a thatched cap and rat-stopping collars, or in a clay-sealed pit in dry ground where insects suffocate and it stays cool. Lidded clay pots in the hut.

Modern way, same four ideas: a steel silo with grain dried to 12 percent, fans to keep it cool, sealed and fumigated. Sealed plastic bags on the farm. A lidded bucket in a cool cupboard at home.

Compare: both dry, cool, seal, raise. Indigenous: local materials, no power, small. Modern: huge, precise, costly. Both fail the same way: moisture gets in, mould grows, grain becomes unsafe.

Hold dry, cool, sealed, off the ground in mind and try this part's questions: storing grain.

## Subtopic: Sour Enough to Stop the Rot

Pickling keeps food in acid, which most bacteria cannot grow in. Way one: pour vinegar over it. Onions, gherkins, beetroot, atchar.

Way two: let it make its own acid. Pack in brine, seal from air, and the good bacteria on the vegetable turn its sugar into lactic acid until nothing else can grow. Sauerkraut, kimchi, fermented vegetables, mageu, sour porridge.

Rules: everything under the liquid, clean sealed jar, acid strong enough, salt helps. Keeps months sealed, weeks open and cool.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Food spoils because bacteria, moulds, yeasts, insects and rodents consume it, and each needs moisture, warmth, air or access; preservation denies them these. Grain is stored dry, cool, sealed and off the ground, in indigenous raised baskets, sealed pits and clay pots or in modern silos and sealed bags, and fails when moisture gets in. Pickling preserves vegetables in acid, either added as vinegar or made by salt-tolerant bacteria fermenting the vegetable's sugars, with the food fully covered in a clean sealed jar.
