### subtopic_1 — Case Study: Why a Car Is a Fifth Plastic
**Assistant:** Why are headlamp lenses polycarbonate and not the cheaper acrylic or the old glass?

**Tutor:** Each of the three fails a different test. Glass is clear, scratch-proof and does not yellow, but it is heavy, must be moulded in simple shapes, and shatters into shards in a crash or from a stone, which is why glass headlamps were small, round and often broken. Acrylic is clear and cheap and resists sunlight, but it is brittle, so a stone chip cracks it. Polycarbonate is as clear as glass, half the weight, and so tough that a hammer blow bounces off, which is why it is also used for riot shields and spectacle lenses; it can be injection moulded into the sweeping wrap-around shapes modern car designers want, in one piece. Its weaknesses are that it scratches easily and that ultraviolet light yellows and clouds it, which is why old cars have hazy headlamps. The fix is a hard clear coating sprayed on after moulding, which resists scratches and blocks UV, so the lens stays clear for many years, though not forever; polishing a hazy lamp is removing the degraded coat.

### subtopic_2 — Where the Plastics Are: Bumper, Dash, Tank, Lights
**Assistant:** Why is the fuel tank plastic when it holds something that burns?

**Tutor:** Because a plastic tank is actually safer and better than steel for a fuel tank, once a few problems were solved. HDPE does not rust, and a rusted steel tank leaks, which is far more dangerous than anything the plastic does. Blow moulding makes the tank in one seamless piece in whatever odd shape fits the space around the axle and under the seat, so the tank holds more fuel in the same car without welded seams that can split. In a crash a plastic tank deforms and springs back where a steel one would tear. Two problems had to be fixed. Petrol vapour slowly passes through plain polyethylene, which the law does not allow, so the tank wall is made of several layers with a thin barrier layer of a different polymer in the middle that stops the vapour. And static electricity from fuel flowing can build a charge on an insulator, so the filler neck and fittings carry a path to earth. Nearly every car built today has an HDPE tank, and the fire risk is lower than with the steel tanks of the past.

### subtopic_3 — Safety, Fuel and Recycling at End of Life
**Assistant:** If the plastic is so hard to recycle, was putting it in cars a mistake?

**Tutor:** No, but the plastic had a cost that the first designers did not count, and we are now paying it. The gains were real: lighter cars that used less fuel over a fifteen-year life, which saved more oil and emissions than the plastic contained; interiors that injured fewer people; parts that never rusted and lasted the life of the car. The mistake was designing the parts only for making and using, not for taking apart. Two hundred parts in a dozen polymers, painted, glued to foam, with metal inserts, cannot be sorted economically, so they went to landfill when the steel was recovered. The lesson has been learned, and it is the lesson of this term: design for the whole life. New cars mark every plastic part with a code, join parts with clips, reduce the number of polymer types, avoid glue and paint where they can, and use recycled plastic for hidden parts. Your own design project is marked on exactly that thinking, which is why the specifications ask what happens to your item at the end.
