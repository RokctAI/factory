# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of plastics used around the home. The anchor is a three-roomed house in Khayelitsha, Cape Town, audited by its Grade 9 resident: PVC pipes and conduit in the walls, a polypropylene kettle, a polystyrene-lined fridge, HDPE buckets and bins, melamine plates, a PET cooldrink bottle, polyethylene bags, an acrylic bath, nylon brushes and a uPVC window frame fitted last year. The governing idea is that every household plastic was chosen for a property profile, that household plastics range from fifty-year services to single-use packaging, and that safety near heat and food and the end-of-life route are design questions. Naming household plastics, their properties and their lifespans is examinable.

## Subtopic: Case Study: A Room-by-Room Audit

The audit method is to go room by room, list every plastic item, and for each write the plastic if it can be identified, from the recycling code, from its feel and from its use, the property that chose it, and roughly how long it will serve. In the Khayelitsha house the count reached seventy items in half an hour, and sorting them revealed a pattern: a small number of long-life items built into the house, a larger number of durable goods used for years, and a flood of packaging that lasts days.

Built in, lasting decades: PVC water and waste pipes, which replaced galvanised steel and cast iron because they do not corrode, are light, cheap and join with solvent cement; PVC conduit and cable sheath; polypropylene or PVC-coated electrical fittings; uPVC window frames, which are rigid, weather-proof and never need painting; polystyrene insulation in the ceiling; polyethylene damp-proof sheet under the floor; and the plastic water tank on the roof, rotationally moulded polyethylene with a black UV-stabilised skin.

The house's services would have been metal, clay and wood a generation ago, and the change made them cheaper to install and longer lasting, which is why plastics are now standard in housing from RDP houses to estates. The trade-off is the fire question: PVC gives off chlorine gas when it burns, and polystyrene ceiling insulation must be behind a fire-resistant board.

The questions for this section are with you now: the audit method and what it reveals, the long-life plastics built into a house and why they replaced metal and clay, and the fire trade-off.

## Subtopic: Kitchen, Bathroom and Services: Which Plastic and Why

Kitchen. The kettle body is polypropylene, cheap, tough and able to take boiling water without softening, with a polycarbonate or glass window; the handle is polypropylene for insulation. The fridge has an ABS or HIPS inner liner that is easy to clean and does not crack in the cold, and polyurethane foam insulation in its walls. Chopping boards are HDPE, which does not absorb water and can be bleached. Food containers are polypropylene, which can take a microwave, unlike polystyrene or PET; plates for children and camping are melamine, a thermoset that resists heat and scratches but should not go in the microwave. Bread bins, bowls, jugs and colanders are polypropylene; cling film is LDPE or PVC; bags are LDPE; cooldrink bottles are PET; milk bottles are HDPE.

Bathroom. The bath is acrylic reinforced with glass fibre, warm to touch, light and easy to mould in one piece; the shower curtain is PVC or PEVA; the toilet seat is polypropylene or thermoset; the toothbrush handle is polypropylene with nylon bristles; shampoo bottles are HDPE or PET; the laundry basket and bucket are polypropylene or HDPE for toughness and water resistance; combs are nylon or ABS. Living room and bedroom. Chairs are polypropylene; the television casing is ABS or flame-retarded polystyrene; the remote control is ABS; the mattress is polyurethane foam; carpets and curtains are polyester or polypropylene fibre; light fittings are polycarbonate where heat is near, acrylic where it is not; the broom head and bristles are polypropylene.

The pattern is the same as in the car: polypropylene wherever something must be tough, cheap, water-proof and take some heat; HDPE for bottles, buckets and boards; PET where clarity matters; ABS where a hard, glossy, easily cleaned surface is wanted; nylon for bristles and gears; polyurethane foam for comfort and insulation; PVC for pipes, frames and sheet; and thermosets where heat is severe.

The questions for this section are with you now: the kitchen plastics and which can take heat, the bathroom and living room plastics, and the pattern of which plastic does which job.

## Subtopic: Lifespan, Safety and What to Do With It After

Lifespan sorts household plastics into three groups and three end-of-life routes. Built-in services last the life of the house, forty years or more, and go to rubble when the house is demolished; uPVC frames and pipes are increasingly collected and recycled. Durable goods, from the kettle to the chair to the bucket, last two to twenty years, and most are single-polymer items that can be recycled if they reach a collector; a cracked bucket is HDPE or polypropylene worth a few rand a kilogram. Packaging lasts minutes to days, is the largest number of items, and is the group where reduce and reuse matter most; only clean PET, HDPE and some polypropylene and LDPE in that group are recycled.

Safety in the home is mainly about heat and food. Only polypropylene and some PET and glass-filled nylon containers are made for a microwave; polystyrene cups and takeaway boxes soften and can release chemicals; melamine chars; PVC should not be heated with food. Sunlight on a windowsill ages polypropylene and polystyrene. Old or damaged plastic that is scratched and stained should be replaced for food use. Small parts that come off toys and bottle caps are a choking hazard for infants, which is why specifications include no loose small parts. In a fire, plastic furniture, foam mattresses and PVC give off dense toxic smoke, which is why bedding and furniture foam are made with flame retardants and why a smoke alarm matters more in a plastic-filled home.

The audit ends with a design question for each item: is it the right plastic, is it designed to last as long as its job, and is it designed for its end of life, single-polymer, marked with its code and easily separated. That is the list of questions your own item must answer. The error museum, four exhibits. One: microwaving food in polystyrene or melamine. Two: treating all household plastic as rubbish when most durable items are recyclable. Three: assuming a plastic pipe is weaker or shorter-lived than a steel one. Four: ignoring sun and heat when placing a plastic item, such as a polystyrene box on a stove.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three lifespan groups and their end-of-life routes, safety with heat, food, sun and fire, and the design questions for each household item.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Khayelitsha house audit, from the pipes in the wall to the bottle on the table — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Count the Plastic in One Room

Go room by room, list every plastic item, write the plastic, the property that chose it and how long it lasts. Seventy items in half an hour, sorted into built-in, durable, and packaging.

Built in for decades: PVC pipes and conduit, no rust, light, cheap, glue-jointed; uPVC window frames that never need paint; polystyrene ceiling insulation; polyethylene damp sheet; a rotomoulded polyethylene roof tank.

A generation ago all that was metal, clay and wood. Plastic made it cheaper and longer lasting. Trade-off: PVC gives off chlorine in a fire, and foam insulation must sit behind fire board.

Keep count the plastic in one room in mind and try the questions for this part: the audit.

## Subtopic: The Right Plastic in Each Room

Kitchen: polypropylene kettle and handle, ABS fridge liner with foam in the walls, HDPE chopping board, polypropylene microwave containers, melamine plates that must not go in the microwave, LDPE bags and cling film, PET cooldrink bottles, HDPE milk bottles.

Bathroom: acrylic bath, PVC shower curtain, polypropylene toilet seat and toothbrush with nylon bristles, HDPE shampoo bottles and buckets. Living room: polypropylene chairs, ABS television and remote, polyurethane mattress, polyester carpets, polycarbonate light fittings near heat.

The pattern: polypropylene for tough, cheap, waterproof and warm; HDPE for bottles and buckets; PET for clear; ABS for hard and glossy; nylon for bristles; foam for comfort; PVC for pipes and frames; thermosets where it is really hot.

Hold the right plastic in each room in mind and try this part's questions: room by room.

## Subtopic: Long Life, Short Life, Next Life

Three groups: built-in lasts forty years and ends as rubble or, now, recycled frames and pipes; durable goods last two to twenty years and are recyclable if collected; packaging lasts days, is the biggest group, and is where reduce and reuse count most.

Safety: only polypropylene and some PET in the microwave; polystyrene softens, melamine chars, PVC should not be heated with food. Sun ages plastic on a windowsill. Small parts choke infants. Plastic and foam burn with toxic smoke, so flame retardants and a smoke alarm.

For every item ask: right plastic, lasts as long as its job, designed for its end: one polymer, coded, easy to separate.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A room-by-room audit shows a home's plastics chosen by property: PVC and uPVC for built-in pipes, conduit and frames that last decades; polypropylene for kettles, containers, chairs and buckets; HDPE for bottles and boards; PET for clear bottles; ABS for fridge liners and casings; nylon for bristles; polyurethane foam for comfort; melamine and other thermosets where heat is severe. Items fall into built-in, durable and packaging groups with different lifespans and end-of-life routes, and safety turns on heat, food contact, sunlight, small parts and fire.

Hold long life, short life, next life, and take the final questions of the lesson: lifespan and safety.
