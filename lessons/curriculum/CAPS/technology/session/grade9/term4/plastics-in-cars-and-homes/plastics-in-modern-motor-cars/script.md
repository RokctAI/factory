# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of plastics used on modern motor cars. The anchor is a hatchback coming off the line at a plant in the Eastern Cape, followed from the painted steel body-in-white through trim and final assembly: polypropylene bumpers, a blow-moulded HDPE fuel tank, polycarbonate headlamp lenses, a dashboard of ABS and polypropylene over polyurethane foam, nylon under the bonnet, PVC on the wiring. The governing idea is that plastics now make up about a fifth of a car's volume and a tenth of its mass, chosen part by part for lightness, toughness, formability, insulation and corrosion resistance, with consequences for fuel use, safety and recycling at end of life. Naming plastics on a car and explaining their choice is examinable.

## Subtopic: Case Study: Why a Car Is a Fifth Plastic

South Africa builds cars for the world in plants in the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Gauteng, and every one of them is a case study in material choice. A modern car is about 10 per cent plastic by mass and 20 per cent or more by volume, with 100 to 150 kilograms of polymers in two hundred or more separate parts, up from almost nothing in the 1950s, when a car was steel, glass, rubber, wood and a little Bakelite. The shift happened because each kilogram of steel replaced by plastic saved weight, and weight costs fuel, and because plastic parts can be moulded in one piece in shapes that steel pressing cannot make and never rust.

The reasons are the property profile from earlier in the term. Lightness: a polypropylene bumper weighs a third of a steel one. Formability: a dashboard with air vents, recesses, clips and texture comes out of one mould in a minute. Toughness: a plastic bumper bends in a car-park knock and springs back. Insulation: wiring sheath and connector bodies. Corrosion resistance: a plastic fuel tank and wheel-arch liners never rust. Cost: once the mould is paid for, a part costs less than the steel or glass it replaces. And safety: foam and soft trim cushion an occupant where sheet steel would cut.

The limits decide what stays steel: the body shell, the chassis, the engine and the suspension carry the big loads and the crash energy, and they run hot, so they stay metal, though even there plastics creep in as composites in the most expensive cars.

The questions for this section are with you now: how much of a car is plastic and how that changed, which properties drove the change, and what stays steel and why.

## Subtopic: Where the Plastics Are: Bumper, Dash, Tank, Lights

Outside. The bumpers front and rear are injection-moulded polypropylene, often with rubber modifiers for toughness and a honeycomb energy absorber behind, painted body colour. The grille, mirror housings, door handles and wheel covers are ABS or polypropylene. The headlamp and tail-lamp lenses are polycarbonate, which is clear, tough and shatterproof but scratches and yellows, so it is hard-coated; they replaced glass, which shattered. Wheel-arch liners and the underbody shields are polypropylene. Window seals are rubber or thermoplastic elastomer.

Inside. The dashboard is a moulded skeleton of polypropylene or ABS with a soft skin of PVC or polyurethane over polyurethane foam, so it looks good, deadens sound and softens a knee impact. Door trims, the centre console, pillar covers and the glove box are polypropylene and ABS. Seats are polyurethane foam on a steel frame under fabric, leather or PVC. The steering wheel is polyurethane over a metal ring. Carpets are polyester or polypropylene fibre, often recycled PET. Switches, knobs and vent slats are ABS or polyamide.

Under the bonnet and underneath. The fuel tank is blow-moulded HDPE in a complex shape that fits around the axle, multi-layered to stop petrol vapour escaping, lighter and rust-proof. The air intake, air filter housing, engine covers and radiator end tanks are glass-filled nylon, which takes the heat. Hoses are rubber or nylon. Wiring insulation is PVC or cross-linked polyethylene; connectors are nylon. The battery case is polypropylene. Even the brake fluid reservoir and the coolant bottle are translucent polypropylene so the level can be seen.

The questions for this section are with you now: the exterior plastics and why lamp lenses are polycarbonate, the interior plastics and the layered dashboard, and the HDPE fuel tank and nylon under the bonnet.

## Subtopic: Safety, Fuel and Recycling at End of Life

Plastics changed safety in two directions. Soft interior surfaces, padded dashboards and foam in doors reduce injuries in a crash, and polycarbonate lenses do not shatter into shards. Lighter cars stop sooner and use less fuel, about 5 to 7 per cent less fuel for every 10 per cent less mass, which matters for the cost of running a car and for the carbon it emits. Against that, plastic interiors burn and give off toxic smoke, so vehicle plastics contain flame retardants and are tested for burn rate.

At end of life a car is a recycling problem. The steel and aluminium, 75 per cent of the mass, are easily recovered by shredding and magnets. The plastics are the difficult quarter: two hundred parts in a dozen polymer types, painted, glued, foamed and layered, so sorting them is slow and most of the shredder residue goes to landfill. Bumpers and battery cases, big and single-polymer, are the easiest to recycle; a laminated dashboard is nearly impossible. Car makers now mark every plastic part with its polymer code so that dismantlers can sort them, design parts to clip rather than glue so they come apart, use fewer polymer types, and use recycled plastic in parts that are hidden, such as wheel-arch liners and underbody shields.

The case study method you are practising is to look at a product, identify each material, state the property that chose it, and then ask the end-of-life question. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a car body is plastic because the bumpers are. Two: thinking the fuel tank is steel on a modern car. Three: assuming all the plastic in a scrapped car is recycled. Four: forgetting that lighter means less fuel and better braking, which is the main reason for the change.

The questions for this section are with you now: how plastics affected safety and fuel use, why end-of-life plastics are hard to recycle and what makers do about it, and the case study method.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the hatchback on the Eastern Cape line and its bumpers, dashboard, fuel tank and lamps — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Car Full of Plastic

A modern car is about a tenth plastic by weight and a fifth by volume: 100 to 150 kilograms in two hundred parts. In the 1950s it was steel, glass, rubber and wood. Plastic took over because it is lighter, which saves fuel, moulds into any shape, and never rusts.

The properties at work: light bumpers, one-piece dashboards, tough bumpers that spring back, insulating wire sheaths, rust-proof tanks, cheap parts once the mould is paid for, soft surfaces for safety.

Steel stays where the loads and the heat are: body shell, chassis, engine, suspension.

Keep a car full of plastic in mind and try the questions for this part: why cars went plastic.

## Subtopic: Part by Part

Outside: polypropylene bumpers with a honeycomb behind, ABS grille, mirrors and handles, polycarbonate lamp lenses that do not shatter but need a hard coat, polypropylene arch liners.

Inside: dashboard skeleton of polypropylene or ABS, soft skin over foam. Door trims, console, glove box the same. Polyurethane foam seats and steering wheel. Carpets from recycled PET fibre. ABS switches.

Underneath: blow-moulded HDPE fuel tank, multi-layered, rust-proof. Glass-filled nylon air intake and engine covers for the heat. PVC wire insulation, nylon connectors, polypropylene battery case and see-through fluid bottles.

Hold part by part in mind and try this part's questions: where the plastics are.

## Subtopic: Lighter, Safer, and Then What

Safety: soft padded surfaces, lenses that do not shard, lighter cars that stop sooner. Fuel: about 5 to 7 per cent less for every 10 per cent less weight. Downside: plastic burns, so it has flame retardants.

End of life: steel and aluminium recycle easily. The plastic quarter is two hundred parts in a dozen polymers, painted and glued, so most goes to landfill. Makers mark parts with codes, clip instead of glue, use fewer polymers, and put recycled plastic in hidden parts.

Method: name each material, say the property that chose it, ask what happens when it is scrapped.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A modern car is about a tenth plastic by mass and a fifth by volume, chosen part by part for lightness, formability, toughness, insulation and rust resistance: polypropylene bumpers and trim, ABS fittings, polycarbonate lamp lenses, a layered foam dashboard, an HDPE fuel tank, nylon under the bonnet and PVC on the wiring, while the body, chassis and engine stay steel. Plastics made cars lighter, more fuel-efficient and safer inside, but their many mixed, painted and glued parts make end-of-life recycling hard, which makers now address with coding, clip fittings and fewer polymers.
