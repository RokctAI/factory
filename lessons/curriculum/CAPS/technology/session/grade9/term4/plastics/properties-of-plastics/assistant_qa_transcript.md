### subtopic_1 — Thermoplastics and Thermosets: Two Families
**Assistant:** What actually happens inside a thermoset when it is heated, if it cannot melt?

**Tutor:** In a thermoplastic the long chains are separate and only attracted to one another weakly, like cooked spaghetti; heat gives them energy to slide past each other and the solid turns to a thick liquid. In a thermoset the chains were chemically bonded to each other, cross-linked, when the plastic was made, so the whole piece is really one giant molecule, a net rather than loose strands. Heat cannot make a net flow, because every chain is tied to its neighbours. So as the temperature rises the thermoset stays rigid until it reaches the point where the bonds themselves break, and then the material decomposes: it darkens, chars, gives off smoke and crumbles, exactly as a Bakelite plug does if a fault overheats it. That is a safety feature, since a plug that charred but held its shape is safer than one that melted and dripped. It is also why thermosets cannot be recycled by remelting; they can only be ground up as filler.

### subtopic_2 — Properties: Strength, Flexibility, Insulation, Resistance
**Assistant:** Why does a cheap plastic chair crack after a summer outside when a bucket of the same plastic lasts for years?

**Tutor:** Both are polypropylene, but the chair spends its life in the sun. Ultraviolet light carries enough energy to break the bonds in a polymer chain, and each break shortens the chains and leaves reactive ends that react with oxygen. Shorter chains mean a weaker, more brittle plastic, and the surface goes chalky and faded as the pigment and the plastic degrade together. The bucket lives indoors or in shade and never gets that dose. Chair makers who know this add a UV stabiliser, a chemical that absorbs ultraviolet light before it reaches the chains or that mops up the reactive ends, and perhaps carbon black pigment, which is why black plastic outdoor items last longest. The cheap chair skips the stabiliser to save a few rand per unit, and the first hot summer does the rest. For your own design, anything that will live outside needs a stabilised plastic, a dark colour or a covering.

### subtopic_3 — Matching Property to Use
**Assistant:** Plastic is lighter than steel but weaker, so how can a plastic part ever replace a steel one?

**Tutor:** By using the lightness to buy back the strength. If a plastic is a fifth as strong as steel but a sixth as dense, then a plastic part made thicker, say three times as thick, is still only half the mass of the steel part and may be strong enough for the job. Designers also shape the part so that the material is where the load is: ribs, flanges and hollow box sections make a thin plastic shell stiff, which is why a plastic crate has a grid of ribs and a car bumper is a curved shell with a honeycomb behind it. Adding glass fibre to nylon or polypropylene can double or triple the strength and stiffness. And many parts never needed steel's strength in the first place; a bucket handle, a fuel tank and a dashboard were steel because steel was what existed, and plastic does those jobs lighter, rust-free and cheaper. Where the load is truly high, a bridge or an engine block, steel still wins.
