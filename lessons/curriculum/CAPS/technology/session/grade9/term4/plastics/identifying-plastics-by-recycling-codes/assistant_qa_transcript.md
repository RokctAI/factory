### subtopic_1 — What Plastics Are and Why They Must Be Sorted
**Assistant:** Why can plastics not just all be melted together into something useful like a bench?

**Tutor:** Some are, and the result shows the problem. Mixed plastic lumber, the grey benches and bollards seen in some parks, is made from unsorted waste melted together, and it works only because the product is thick, does not need to be strong, and is never asked to look good. Melted polymers do not dissolve in each other; they form separate regions with weak boundaries, so mixed plastic is weak, brittle, blotchy and inconsistent from one batch to the next. It cannot be made into a thin bottle, a clear sheet or a food container. Sorted plastic, by contrast, melts into a uniform material almost as good as new, which is why a PET bottle can become a PET bottle again or polyester clothing, and an HDPE bottle can become a pipe that lasts fifty years underground. The value is in the purity, so sorting turns waste that is worth almost nothing per kilogram into material a factory will pay for.

### subtopic_2 — The Seven Recycling Codes and the Plastics They Name
**Assistant:** Who actually does the recycling in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Mostly tens of thousands of informal waste pickers, who collect plastic, paper, glass and metal from household bins, pavements and landfills and sell it by the kilogram to buy-back centres, which bale it and sell it on to recycling factories. Pickers are responsible for the large majority of what South Africa recycles, often working with trolleys and sacks over long distances for small sums, and they sort by code and by eye with great skill. The formal side is the buy-back centres, the recycling plants that wash, shred and pelletise, and the manufacturers who use the pellets. Municipal kerbside collection exists in some suburbs but covers a small share. This is why a household that rinses, sorts and sets out its codes 1, 2 and 5 separately helps directly: the picker gets clean, sorted material that earns more and takes less time, and far less ends up in the landfill or the river.

### subtopic_3 — Sorting a Bag of Household Plastic for Recycling
**Assistant:** Why is black plastic a problem if it has a code?

**Tutor:** Because large recycling plants sort by machine, and the machines are blind to black. After hand sorting at a buy-back centre, bales go to plants that shred everything and then separate the flakes with optical sorters, which shine infrared light on each flake and read the reflection to identify the polymer. Black pigment, usually carbon black, absorbs the light so the sorter sees nothing and rejects the flake as unknown, and it goes to landfill or to the low-value mixed stream even if it is perfectly good PP or PET. Some manufacturers have switched to dark grey or detectable black pigments, and a hand sorter can still read the moulded code and put a black tray in the PP pile, but the safest household action is to avoid buying food in black trays where there is a choice. This is one of the reasons the next part of the term asks what a designer can do about plastic waste at the design stage rather than only at the bin.
