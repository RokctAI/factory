# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers reduce, reuse and recycle for plastics. The anchor is a Saturday clean-up on the banks of the Jukskei River in Alexandra, Johannesburg: bags, bottles, polystyrene and sweet wrappers pulled from the reeds, then sorted on the bank into PET, HDPE, mixed film and unrecyclable. The governing idea is that the three Rs are a hierarchy, reduce before reuse before recycle, because each step down the list costs more energy and recovers less; that South Africa's recycling depends on informal waste pickers and on buy-back centres; and that recycling has limits of contamination, sorting cost and quality loss. Explaining the hierarchy and the local system is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Plastic Waste Problem in South Africa

Plastics do not rot. The same resistance to water and microbes that makes a pipe last fifty years makes a discarded bag last for centuries, breaking into smaller and smaller fragments called microplastics but never disappearing. South Africa generates around 2.4 million tonnes of plastic waste a year. Roughly 14 per cent is recycled into new products, which is better than many countries, but the rest goes to landfill, where space is running out, or escapes into the environment, blocking stormwater drains, choking rivers like the Jukskei, drifting into the sea and killing animals that eat it or tangle in it.

The litter has a cost that someone pays: municipalities clear drains and dumps, floods worsen when drains block, fishing and tourism suffer along the coast, and farm animals die from eating bags. The plastics themselves are made from oil and gas, so every bag thrown away also wasted the energy that made it. This is why plastic processing has a waste management side as well as a manufacturing side, and why the law now taxes plastic bags, bans some microbeads and requires producers to pay towards recycling under extended producer responsibility.

Waste is a design problem. An item that is used once for minutes and lasts for centuries is badly designed for its full life. The three Rs are the designer's and the citizen's tools for shortening that mismatch, and they are arranged in a deliberate order: reduce first, because the best waste is the waste never made; reuse second, because an item used ten times makes a tenth of the waste; recycle third, because it still costs energy, needs collecting and sorting, and recovers only part of the value.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plastic waste persists and how much South Africa makes, what litter and landfill cost, and why the three Rs are in a fixed order.

## Subtopic: Reduce and Reuse: The First Two Rs

Reduce means using less plastic in the first place. For a shopper: a cloth bag instead of a new plastic one, a refillable water bottle instead of a bought one, loose vegetables instead of wrapped. For a designer and manufacturer: thinner bottle walls, the lightweight PET bottle today using about a third less plastic than twenty years ago, no unnecessary packaging, a concentrate in a small bottle rather than a diluted product in a big one, and choosing a different material when plastic is not needed. The plastic bag levy, a few cents a bag charged at the till since 2003, is a reduce measure: it made people think before taking one.

Reuse means using the same item again for the same or a different purpose without reprocessing it. The two-litre cooldrink bottle becomes a water bottle, a drip irrigator, a bird feeder or, filled with sand, an eco-brick for a wall. The paint bucket becomes a planter. Returnable crates and glass bottles for soft drinks and beer circulate dozens of times. A school lunchbox used for years replaces thousands of sandwich bags. Reuse is cheap, needs no factory and keeps value in the object, but it depends on habit, and some items, like a thin bag, wear out quickly or cannot be cleaned safely for food.

Reduce and reuse together can cut household plastic waste by half before any recycling happens. A good design brief includes them: can the product be made with less material, can the packaging be left out, can the item be refilled or returned, can it be made durable enough to be used for years. The eco-brick and the returnable crate are both examples of design that treats the waste stage as part of the product.

The questions for this section are with you now: reduce for shoppers and for manufacturers, reuse with local examples, and why the two together are the biggest savings.

## Subtopic: Recycle: Sorting, Collecting and the Limits

Recycle means collecting used plastic, sorting it by type, cleaning it, shredding and remelting it into pellets, and moulding those into new products. Only thermoplastics can be recycled this way, and in practice only some of them pay: PET bottles become fibre for pillows, clothing and new bottles; HDPE milk bottles and drums become pipes, bins and crates; polypropylene becomes crates, buckets and car parts; LDPE film becomes refuse bags. Polystyrene, multi-layer sweet wrappers, chip packets and dirty mixed film are hard or uneconomic to recycle and mostly go to landfill.

South Africa's recycling runs on people. An estimated sixty to ninety thousand informal waste pickers collect material from bins, streets and dumps, sort it and sell it by the kilogram to buy-back centres, which bale it and sell to recyclers. They recover the majority of what is recycled in the country, for very little pay and in hard conditions, and a household that separates its clean recyclables at the kerb, in a separate bag, makes their work safer and faster. Municipal separation-at-source schemes, school collection drives and bottle deposit schemes are the organised side of the same work.

Recycling has limits. Contamination, food in a container, the wrong plastic in the bale, a PVC label on a PET bottle, spoils a batch. Sorting and washing cost money and water, so clean, bulky, single-type plastics are collected and dirty mixed ones are not. Each remelt shortens the chains a little, so recycled plastic is often lower in quality and goes to less demanding products, a bottle to a fibre to a bin, a step down each time, which is why recycling is third in the hierarchy and not a solution on its own. The error museum, four exhibits. One: believing all plastic in a bin gets recycled. Two: putting the Rs in the wrong order with recycle first. Three: recycling a dirty container, which spoils the batch. Four: forgetting the people who actually do the collecting.

The questions for this section are with you now: what recycling involves and which plastics pay, the role of waste pickers and buy-back centres, and the limits of contamination, cost and quality loss.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Saturday clean-up on the Jukskei River bank in Alexandra — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Where the Plastic Goes

Plastic does not rot. A bag lasts centuries, breaking into smaller pieces. South Africa makes about 2.4 million tonnes of plastic waste a year, recycles about 14 per cent, dumps or loses the rest. It blocks drains, chokes rivers, kills animals.

Someone pays: clearing drains, floods, lost tourism, dead livestock, wasted oil. So there is a bag levy and producers must pay towards recycling.

Waste is a design problem: used for minutes, lasts for centuries. The three Rs fix it in order: reduce first, reuse second, recycle last, because each one down costs more and recovers less.

Keep where the plastic goes in mind and try the questions for this part: the problem.

## Subtopic: Use Less, Use Again

Reduce: use less. Cloth bag, refillable bottle, loose vegetables. Makers: thinner bottles, no extra wrapping, concentrates. The bag levy at the till is a reduce rule.

Reuse: same item again. Two-litre bottle as water bottle, drip irrigator, eco-brick. Bucket as planter. Returnable crates and bottles go round dozens of times. A lunchbox beats thousands of bags.

Reduce and reuse together can halve home plastic waste before recycling. Design for it: less material, no packaging, refillable, durable.

Hold use less, use again in mind and try this part's questions: reduce and reuse.

## Subtopic: Recycle Last, and Only What Can Be

Recycle: collect, sort by type, wash, shred, melt to pellets, mould new things. Thermoplastics only, and really only PET, HDPE, polypropylene and clean LDPE pay. Bottles to fibre, milk bottles to pipes, film to refuse bags. Polystyrene and chip packets mostly go to landfill.

Waste pickers, sixty to ninety thousand of them, collect most of what gets recycled and sell by the kilogram to buy-back centres. Keep your clean recyclables in a separate bag to help them.

Limits: dirty or mixed plastic spoils a batch; sorting and washing cost money; each remelt lowers the quality a step. That is why recycle is last.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plastic waste persists for centuries and South Africa recycles only about 14 per cent of its 2.4 million tonnes a year. The three Rs are a hierarchy: reduce by using and making less, reuse by using the same item many times, and recycle, last, by collecting, sorting, cleaning and remelting thermoplastics, work done mostly by informal waste pickers and buy-back centres. Recycling is limited by contamination, sorting cost and quality loss, so reduce and reuse come first.

Hold recycle last, and only what can be, and take the final questions of the lesson: recycling.
