# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the properties of plastics. The anchor is a spaza shop in Mamelodi: a stack of polypropylene chairs outside, a kettle with a polycarbonate window, bread in low-density polyethylene bags, cooldrink in PET bottles, a melamine serving tray and a Bakelite plug on the fridge cable. The governing idea is that plastics divide into thermoplastics, which soften on heating and can be remoulded, and thermosets, which cure into a cross-linked solid that chars rather than melts, and that each plastic has a profile of properties, strength, flexibility, toughness, density, insulation, chemical and water resistance, that determines where it is used. Describing the two families and matching properties to uses is examinable.

## Subtopic: Thermoplastics and Thermosets: Two Families

Plastics are polymers: very long chain molecules made by joining thousands of small units, usually derived from oil or gas and increasingly from plant material. The way the chains are arranged decides the first and most important division. In a thermoplastic the chains lie beside one another, held by weak forces, so that heat lets them slide: the plastic softens, melts, can be shaped, and sets again on cooling, and this can be repeated. Polyethylene, polypropylene, PET, PVC, polystyrene, nylon, acrylic and polycarbonate are thermoplastics. Every plastic with a recycling code in the triangle is a thermoplastic, because recycling means remelting.

In a thermoset the chains are chemically bonded to one another in a three-dimensional network during manufacture, usually by heat and pressure or by mixing two parts. Once cured the network cannot be undone: heating a thermoset makes it char and crumble, not melt. Bakelite in old plugs and pot handles, melamine in trays and worktops, epoxy glue, polyester resin in boat hulls and the urea-formaldehyde of chipboard glue are thermosets. They are rigid, heat-resistant and good insulators, which is why they were the first plastics used around hot or electrical things, and they cannot be recycled by remelting.

The test is simple: gently warm a small sample and see whether it softens or merely scorches. Thermoplastics are the great majority of plastics by mass because they can be moulded fast, remoulded, and recycled; thermosets are used where heat and rigidity matter more than reuse. A kettle body is thermoplastic polypropylene, strong enough and cheap, but the plug on its cable may be a thermoset, because a plug must never soften if it overheats.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a polymer is, thermoplastics versus thermosets and how each responds to heat, and examples of each family.

## Subtopic: Properties: Strength, Flexibility, Insulation, Resistance

A property is a measurable way a material behaves. Plastics have a distinctive profile. Density: they are light, most floating or nearly floating in water, which is why plastic replaced metal in buckets, pipes and car parts to save weight and cost. Strength and stiffness: lower than steel for the same thickness, but good for their mass, and adjustable by thickness, by ribs and by adding glass fibre. Toughness: many plastics absorb a knock without shattering, polypropylene and polyethylene most of all, while polystyrene and acrylic are brittle. Flexibility: a bread bag, a hosepipe and a bottle cap hinge bend thousands of times without breaking, something no metal of that thickness can do.

Insulation: plastics do not conduct electricity, which makes them the standard for cable sheathing, plugs, switch covers and tool handles, and they conduct heat poorly, which is why a kettle's handle is plastic and a cool-box is foamed polystyrene. Resistance: most plastics are unaffected by water, so they do not rust or rot, and many resist acids, oils and detergents, which is why PVC pipes carry water and chemicals and HDPE drums hold them. Formability: they can be moulded into complex shapes in seconds, coloured right through, made clear or opaque, smooth or textured.

Weaknesses belong in the profile too. Plastics soften at low temperatures, mostly between 80 and 200 degrees, so a plastic bowl warps in an oven. Ultraviolet light in sunshine breaks the chains, so untreated plastics go brittle and chalky outdoors; chairs and crates left in the sun crack. Many burn, some with toxic smoke. They scratch more easily than glass. And their resistance to decay is exactly what makes discarded plastic a lasting pollutant.

The questions for this section are with you now: density, strength, toughness and flexibility; electrical and heat insulation and chemical resistance; and the weaknesses of heat, sunlight and fire.

## Subtopic: Matching Property to Use

Matching property to use is the skill. The cooldrink bottle is PET because it is clear, light, strong enough to hold a fizzy drink's pressure, does not let gas through quickly and is easy to recycle. The bread bag is LDPE because it is flexible, cheap and keeps moisture in. The chair is polypropylene because it is tough, light, cheap to mould and, with a UV stabiliser added, survives outdoors for years; the cheap chair without the stabiliser is the one that cracks. The pipe under the sink is PVC because it is rigid, resists water and chemicals and joins with solvent cement. The crash helmet shell is polycarbonate or ABS because it is tough and will not shatter.

The plug is a thermoset because it must stay rigid and insulating even if the cable overheats; the pot handle is a thermoset for the same reason. The melamine tray resists heat, scratches and stains and can be printed in colour. Nylon is used for gears, bushes and cable ties because it is strong, slippery and wears well. Foamed polystyrene insulates a cool-box and cushions a television in its carton because it is mostly trapped air, but it breaks easily and is hard to recycle, so it is a poor choice where it will be reused.

When you choose a material for the design task later this term, you will write a short property profile like this: what the item must do, which properties that demands, and which plastic has them at the lowest cost and with the least waste. The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling all plastics the same material. Two: thinking a thermoset can be melted and remoulded. Three: assuming plastic is always weaker than metal rather than weaker for the same thickness but lighter. Four: forgetting sunlight when choosing a plastic for outdoors.

The questions for this section are with you now: why PET, LDPE, polypropylene and PVC are chosen for their jobs, why plugs and handles are thermosets, and how to write a property profile for a design.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the chairs, kettle, bread bags, bottles, tray and plug of the Mamelodi spaza shop — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Softens Again or Sets for Good

Plastic is made of very long chain molecules. Thermoplastics: chains lie side by side, heat lets them slide, so they soften, mould, and can be remelted again and again. Polyethylene, polypropylene, PET, PVC, polystyrene, nylon. Anything with a recycling code.

Thermosets: chains are bonded into a net when made. Heat them and they char, never melt. Bakelite plugs, melamine trays, epoxy, boat resin. Rigid, heat-proof, good insulators, not remeltable.

Warm a scrap: softens means thermoplastic, scorches means thermoset. Kettle body polypropylene; plug thermoset so it never softens.

Keep softens again or sets for good in mind and try the questions for this part: the two families.

## Subtopic: What Plastics Are Good At

Light: most float. Strong for their weight, not as strong as steel at the same thickness. Tough: polypropylene takes a knock; polystyrene shatters. Flexible: bags, hoses, cap hinges bend thousands of times.

Do not conduct electricity: cables, plugs, handles. Poor heat conductors: kettle handle, cool-box. Do not rust or rot, resist many chemicals: pipes, drums. Mould into any shape, any colour, clear or solid.

Weak spots: soften at 80 to 200 degrees, go brittle in sunlight, many burn, scratch easily. And they do not rot, which is the pollution problem.

Hold what plastics are good at in mind and try this part's questions: properties.

## Subtopic: The Right Plastic for the Job

Bottle PET: clear, light, holds fizz. Bread bag LDPE: flexible, cheap, keeps moisture. Chair polypropylene with UV stabiliser: tough, light, cheap; without the stabiliser it cracks. Pipe PVC: rigid, resists water and chemicals. Helmet polycarbonate: will not shatter.

Plug and pot handle: thermoset, stays rigid when hot. Melamine tray: heat, scratch and stain proof. Nylon: gears, cable ties, slippery and strong. Foamed polystyrene: cool-box and packing, mostly air, breaks easily, hard to recycle.

For your design: what must it do, which properties does that need, which plastic has them cheapest with least waste.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plastics are long-chain polymers in two families: thermoplastics soften on heating and can be remoulded and recycled, thermosets cure into a network that chars rather than melts. Their property profile is light, tough, flexible, insulating against electricity and heat, resistant to water and chemicals and easy to mould, but weak in heat, sunlight and fire. Each object uses the plastic whose profile fits: PET bottles, LDPE bags, polypropylene chairs, PVC pipes, thermoset plugs.
