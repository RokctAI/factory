### subtopic_1 — The Plastic Waste Problem in South Africa
**Assistant:** If recycling works, why is it last in the hierarchy and not first?

**Tutor:** Because recycling is the most expensive and least complete of the three, even when it works well. It needs the plastic collected, which costs transport and labour; sorted by type, since a single wrong bottle can spoil a bale; washed, which costs water and energy; shredded and remelted, which costs electricity and shortens the polymer chains a little every time, so the recycled material is usually fit only for a less demanding product than the one it came from. A PET bottle becomes fibre, the fibre becomes carpet, the carpet becomes landfill: that is delaying waste, not abolishing it. Reduce avoids all of that by never making the item; reuse avoids nearly all of it by getting ten lives from one piece of plastic without reprocessing. So the hierarchy is a cost ranking: do the cheap, complete thing first and the expensive, partial thing last. Recycling is still essential for the plastic that reduce and reuse could not avoid, which is why it stays on the list.

### subtopic_2 — Reduce and Reuse: The First Two Rs
**Assistant:** Why do waste pickers take some plastics and leave others behind?

**Tutor:** Because they are paid by the kilogram for material that a recycler will buy, and recyclers only buy what they can turn into a saleable product at a profit. Clean PET bottles, HDPE milk and detergent bottles and polypropylene crates are bulky, single-type, easy to identify and have a ready market, so a buy-back centre pays for them. A chip packet is a laminate of several plastics and aluminium that cannot be separated; a sweet wrapper is light, dirty and worth almost nothing per kilogram; expanded polystyrene is mostly air so a bag of it weighs nothing; a dirty takeaway container needs washing that costs more than the plastic is worth. So the picker leaves those because collecting them would cost time and earn nothing, and they go to landfill or the river. That is a design signal: the products that get left are the ones that should be reduced or redesigned, which is what extended producer responsibility is now pushing manufacturers to do.

### subtopic_3 — Recycle: Sorting, Collecting and the Limits
**Assistant:** How does sorting plastic at home actually make a difference to a recycler in a factory?

**Tutor:** The factory's whole process depends on getting one plastic at a time, clean. A batch of PET with a few PVC bottles in it is ruined, because PVC releases acid at PET's melting temperature and turns the whole melt yellow and brittle; a batch with food residue grows mould in the bale and contaminates the pellets. So before anything is melted it must be sorted and washed, and that is the most expensive part of recycling. When a household rinses containers and puts clean recyclables in a separate clear bag, the picker or the kerbside crew gets material that is already partly sorted and not fouled with nappies and food waste, which means they can collect more in a day, the buy-back centre gets cleaner bales, and the recycler spends less on sorting and washing and rejects fewer batches. Every step up the chain gets cheaper and the material gets recycled instead of landfilled. One rinsed bottle is small; a suburb doing it changes what is economic.
