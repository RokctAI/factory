### subtopic_1 — Finding a Real Need Around You
**Assistant:** Why should the brief not say what the item is, if I already have a good idea?

**Tutor:** Because the moment the brief says cup holder, every other answer to the same need disappears, and the first idea is rarely the best one. The need at the tap is drinking without spilling; a cup holder is one answer, but so is a spout that fits over the tap and shapes the stream, a fold-flat cup that hangs on a lanyard, a small trough that catches spills, or a push-button nozzle. If the brief says cup holder, you will never sketch those, and neither will anyone marking your work be able to see that you considered them. Writing the brief in terms of the need rather than the solution is what lets the next stage, sketching several ideas and comparing them against the specifications, do its job. Keep your good idea; it will probably be one of the three you sketch, and if it still wins after honest comparison, you will know why it is good rather than just that you thought of it first.

### subtopic_2 — Writing the Design Brief and Specifications
**Assistant:** How do I turn a vague requirement like it must be strong enough into a real specification?

**Tutor:** Ask what strong enough means for this item in use, then describe a test. The cup holder on the tap will be knocked by bags and leaned on by learners, so the real requirement might be: must support a mass of 5 kilograms hanging from it without breaking or coming off the pipe, and must survive a 1 metre drop onto concrete without cracking. Now anyone can test it: hang a 5 litre bottle of water on it, drop it. Do the same for every soft word. Must last outdoors becomes must show no cracking or fading after a term in the sun, or, since you cannot wait a term, must be made of UV-stabilised polypropylene or HDPE. Must be cheap becomes material cost under twenty rand. Must be safe becomes no edge sharper than a one millimetre radius and no loose part smaller than a bottle cap. The rule is that a specification is finished when you can write the test next to it.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing Recycled Plastic and a Making Method
**Assistant:** Can I use an old bucket or bottle as my material, or does it have to be new recycled pellets?

**Tutor:** You can, and it is often the better choice. Reshaping a used container is reuse and recycling at once: the plastic gets a second life without the energy of collecting, washing, remelting and re-moulding it. A two-litre PET bottle is a ready-made clear cylinder with a threaded neck; a bucket is a tough, UV-stabilised polypropylene shell with a stacking rim; a milk bottle is thin HDPE that cuts and bends easily. You still make all the design decisions: which part of the container to use, what to cut, how to join, whether to heat and form it. In the written design, identify the plastic by its recycling code, give its properties, and explain which industrial process would make the item in volume, with the even walls and draft that process needs. The pellets in the factory and the bottle in your hand are the same material; the project is marked on how well you turned a need into a specified, drawn, made and tested item, not on where the plastic came from.
