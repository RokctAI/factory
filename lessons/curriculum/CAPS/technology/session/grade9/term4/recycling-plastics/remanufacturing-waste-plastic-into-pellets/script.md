# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of remanufacturing waste plastic into pellets and the systems diagram of a plastics recycling project. The anchor is a recycling plant in the East Rand: bales of PET bottles arriving by truck from buy-back centres, a sorting line, a hot-wash tank, a granulator, a drier, an extruder with a die and a water bath, and pellets bagged at the end. The governing idea is that recycling is a manufacturing sequence, sort, wash, shred, dry, melt, extrude, pelletise, each step removing a kind of contamination or changing the form, and that a systems diagram with input, process, output and feedback describes any such project. Describing the sequence and drawing the systems diagram is examinable.

## Subtopic: Case Study: A Plastics Recycling Plant

The plant receives its raw material not from a mine or a refinery but from the street. Trucks bring bales, each about a cubic metre and several hundred kilograms, of crushed PET bottles bought from buy-back centres across Gauteng. The bale is the input: dirty, mixed in colour, with caps, labels, some wrong bottles and sometimes a stone or a brick added to make the bale weigh more. The plant's job is to turn that into a clean, uniform pellet that a bottle or fibre factory will buy at a price that covers the collecting, the machines, the electricity, the water and the wages. If the pellet does not meet the buyer's standard, it is worth almost nothing.

A South African recycling plant of this kind processes several thousand tonnes a year and employs dozens of people, many on the sorting line. The economics are tight: the price of recycled pellet follows the price of new plastic, which follows the oil price, so when oil is cheap, recycling struggles. Producer responsibility fees and a guaranteed market from bottle makers who pledge to use recycled content keep the plants running. Some of the plants are owned by the bottle makers themselves, closing the loop from bottle to bottle.

The case study method is to walk the material through the plant and ask at each machine: what comes in, what is removed or changed, what goes out, and what is measured. That is also exactly the thinking behind the systems diagram at the end of the lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the input bale is like and what the output pellet must be, the economics of a recycling plant, and the case study method.

## Subtopic: From Bale to Pellet: Sort, Wash, Shred, Melt

Step one, breaking and sorting. The bale is cut open and the bottles tumble onto a conveyor. Workers and, in bigger plants, machines with cameras and air jets remove anything that is not PET: HDPE bottles, PVC, cans, paper, bricks. Clear PET is separated from coloured, because clear pellet is worth more. Step two, pre-washing and label removal: the bottles pass through a drum that strips labels and loose dirt. Step three, shredding: a granulator with rotating blades cuts the bottles into flakes about a centimetre across. Flake is easier to wash and feed than bottles.

Step four, washing and separating. The flake goes into a hot wash with caustic soda and detergent that removes glue, sugar, soil and oil, then into a float-sink tank: PET sinks, while polypropylene and polyethylene caps and label fragments float and are skimmed off, a separation by density that costs almost nothing. Step five, rinsing and drying: clean flake is rinsed and dried with hot air, because water in the melt would make the plastic break down. The dry flake is sampled and checked for colour, for PVC specks and for moisture before it goes further.

Step six, melting, filtering and pelletising. The flake is fed into an extruder, a heated screw inside a barrel that melts and mixes it at about 270 degrees and pushes it through a fine screen that catches any remaining specks, then through a die with many small holes. The molten strands fall into a water bath, harden, and are chopped by a rotating knife into pellets a few millimetres long, which look like rice. Pellets are tested, bagged in 25-kilogram or one-tonne bags and sold. Food-grade pellet for new bottles needs an extra decontamination step at high temperature under vacuum, and a plant that can do that earns more per tonne.

The questions for this section are with you now: sorting and shredding into flake, hot washing and float-sink separation, and extruding and chopping into pellets.

## Subtopic: The Systems Diagram: Input, Process, Output

A systems diagram shows a project as a set of boxes: input, process, output, usually with feedback and often with the resources and controls that act on the process. For the recycling plant: input is bales of waste bottles, plus water, electricity, caustic soda, labour and machines. Process is the chain of sort, wash, shred, float-sink, dry, extrude, pelletise. Output is clean pellets, plus by-products, the floated caps and labels that go to another recycler, the dirt and waste water that must be treated, and the rejects to landfill. Feedback is the quality tests: if the pellet fails on colour or moisture, the information goes back to the sorting or drying stage to be corrected.

Drawing it: boxes from left to right, input, process, output, with arrows for the flow of material. Inside the process box, or as a row of smaller boxes, list the steps in order. Draw a feedback arrow from the output test back to the process. Label the resources, energy, water, labour, feeding in from above, and the waste streams leaving below. The diagram should let someone who has never seen the plant understand what goes in, what happens and what comes out.

The same diagram works for the project you will design later this term: input is your recycled pellets or sheet, process is the moulding or forming you choose, output is your item, feedback is testing against your specifications. The error museum, four exhibits. One: melting the bottles without washing and sorting first, which makes a contaminated melt. Two: skipping the drying step so that wet flake degrades in the extruder. Three: a systems diagram with no feedback arrow. Four: mixing coloured and clear PET so that the pellet is only worth the lower price.

The questions for this section are with you now: the input, process and output of the plant, how to draw the diagram with feedback and resources, and applying it to a design project.

## Subtopic: Six Steps to a Pellet

One: cut the bale, sort out anything that is not PET, split clear from coloured. Two: drum strips labels and loose dirt. Three: granulator chops bottles into flakes.

Four: hot wash with caustic soda removes glue, sugar, oil. Float-sink tank: PET sinks, cap plastic floats and is skimmed off. Five: rinse and hot-air dry, because wet flake breaks down in the melt. Test the flake.

Six: extruder screw melts at about 270 degrees, pushes through a fine screen and a die with holes, strands cool in water, a knife chops them into rice-sized pellets. Test, bag, sell. Food-grade needs an extra hot vacuum clean.

Hold six steps to a pellet in mind and try this part's questions: the process.

## Subtopic: Draw the System

Systems diagram: input box, process box, output box, feedback arrow. Input: bales, water, electricity, chemicals, labour. Process: sort, wash, shred, float-sink, dry, extrude, pelletise. Output: pellets, plus floated caps, waste water, rejects. Feedback: pellet test results go back to fix sorting or drying.

Draw it left to right with arrows for material. Steps listed inside the process. Resources feed in from above, waste leaves below. A stranger should understand it.

Your own project uses the same diagram: pellets in, moulding as process, your item out, testing as feedback.
