# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of moulding recycled pellets into products. The anchor is a factory in Pinetown, KwaZulu-Natal, that buys recycled HDPE and polypropylene pellets and makes refuse bins, crates and plant pots, with a line of injection moulding machines and one rotational moulding oven for water tanks. The governing idea is that a thermoplastic pellet is melted and forced into or over a mould by one of four processes, injection, blow, extrusion or rotational moulding, each suited to particular shapes and volumes, and that a product must be designed for its process with uniform walls, draft and no undercuts. Describing the processes and designing for moulding is examinable.

## Subtopic: Case Study: From Pellet to Product

The Pinetown factory receives pellets in one-tonne bags: recycled HDPE from milk bottles and drums, and recycled polypropylene from crates and caps, sometimes blended with a share of new pellet and a masterbatch of colour and UV stabiliser. The pellets are dried and fed into hoppers above the machines. The factory's products are chosen to suit recycled material: a refuse bin, a crate, a plant pot and a water tank do not need the clarity or food contact of a new bottle, and their thick walls hide small variations in the material. This is where recycled plastic gets its second life, and the market for these products is what makes the collecting and the pelletising worth doing.

The case study shows a wheelie bin from start to finish. Polypropylene pellet with black masterbatch is dried, melted in an injection moulding machine, injected into a two-part steel mould the size of a small car, held under pressure while it cools for about a minute, and the mould opens and ejects a bin body. The lid is moulded on a smaller machine, the wheels on another, and the axle is a steel rod. The bin is assembled, stamped with the municipality's name and stacked for delivery. The mould cost millions of rand and will make hundreds of thousands of bins before it wears out, which is why moulding is cheap per part only when the numbers are large.

The factory employs machine setters, who change moulds and set temperatures and pressures; operators, who watch the cycle and remove parts; quality inspectors; and maintenance staff. The scrap from each cycle, the runner that fed the mould and any faulty parts, is ground up and fed back into the hopper, so the factory itself recycles.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the factory buys and why its products suit recycled material, the wheelie bin case study, and why moulds make sense only at large numbers.

## Subtopic: Injection, Blow, Extrusion and Rotational Moulding

Injection moulding: pellets are melted by a heated screw and injected under high pressure into a closed, cooled steel mould, which opens after a few seconds to a minute and ejects the part. It makes solid, detailed shapes with holes, ribs and text, from a bottle cap to a crate to a car bumper, fast and exactly repeatable. The mould is expensive, so it suits large runs. Blow moulding: a tube of melt, the parison, is extruded or injection moulded, closed in a mould and inflated with air so that it balloons against the cold mould walls, making a hollow object with a narrow neck: bottles, drums, jerry cans, toy balls. PET bottles are stretch-blow moulded from a small test-tube-shaped preform.

Extrusion: melt is pushed continuously through a die of the shape wanted and cooled as it comes out, making long things of constant cross-section: pipes, gutters, cable sheath, window frame profile, sheet and film. Film for bags is extruded as a thin tube and blown up into a bubble before being flattened and wound. Rotational moulding: a measured charge of powdered plastic is put into a hollow mould that is heated in an oven while it rotates slowly on two axes, so the powder melts and coats the inside evenly; it is cooled and the hollow part is removed. It makes large, seamless, hollow items with thick walls, water tanks, canoes, road barriers, and the moulds are cheap because there is no pressure.

Other forming methods for sheet rather than pellet: vacuum forming, where a heated sheet is sucked down over a mould to make trays, packaging and signs; and compression moulding, used for thermosets, where a measured charge is squeezed in a heated mould and cured. For the design project, vacuum forming or a simple hand method is usually what a school can do, but the thinking about moulds applies to all.

The questions for this section are with you now: injection and blow moulding and what each makes, extrusion and rotational moulding and what each makes, and the sheet methods of vacuum forming and compression moulding.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Process and Designing for Moulding

Choosing a process starts from the shape and the numbers. A solid, detailed part in large numbers: injection. A hollow container with a neck: blow. A long constant profile: extrusion. A large hollow tank in small numbers: rotational. A shallow tray or cover from sheet: vacuum forming. Then the material: HDPE and polypropylene for most of these, PET for stretch-blow bottles, PVC for extruded pipe and frame.

Designing for moulding means shaping the part so that it fills, cools and releases well. Walls should be of even thickness, because thick patches shrink more as they cool and leave sink marks and warping; stiffness comes from ribs, not thickness. Every wall that runs in the direction the mould opens needs a draft angle, a slight taper of a degree or two, so the part slides out. Undercuts, hooks or hollows that would lock the part in the mould, must be avoided or need moving mould parts that cost more. Sharp inside corners concentrate stress and trap melt, so corners are rounded. The point where the melt enters leaves a small mark, so it is placed where it will not show. A bin, a crate, a bucket and a plant pot all show these rules: tapered sides, ribbed bottoms, rounded corners, stacking rims.

When you draw your own plastic item later this term, go through this checklist: even walls, draft on the sides, no undercuts, rounded corners, ribs for stiffness. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a design with thick solid lumps that will sink and warp. Two: vertical sides with no draft that will not leave the mould. Three: choosing injection moulding for a run of ten parts, when the mould would cost more than the parts. Four: putting a thermoset in an injection moulder expecting it to melt.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching shape and numbers to a process, the design rules of even walls, draft, no undercuts and rounded corners, and the checklist for your own item.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Pinetown factory, its bags of recycled pellet and its wheelie bin mould — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pellets Become Things

Pellets arrive in tonne bags, recycled HDPE and polypropylene, blended with colour and UV masterbatch. Bins, crates, pots and tanks suit recycled plastic: thick walls, no need for clarity or food contact.

Wheelie bin: black polypropylene pellet melted, shot into a huge two-part steel mould, cooled for about a minute, ejected. Lid and wheels moulded separately, steel axle, assembled. The mould costs millions and makes hundreds of thousands of bins, so it only pays in big numbers.

Setters change moulds, operators run cycles, inspectors check, maintenance fixes. Runners and faulty parts are ground up and fed back in.

Keep pellets become things in mind and try the questions for this part: the factory.

## Subtopic: Four Ways to Shape a Melt

Injection: melt shot under pressure into a cold steel mould, out comes a solid detailed part. Caps, crates, bumpers. Dear mould, cheap parts in bulk. Blow: a tube of melt is clamped in a mould and inflated like a balloon. Bottles, drums, jerry cans.

Extrusion: melt pushed through a shaped hole, cooled as it comes, endless length. Pipes, gutters, frames, sheet, film. Rotational: powder in a hollow mould turned slowly in an oven coats the inside evenly. Tanks, canoes, barriers. Cheap moulds.

From sheet instead: vacuum forming sucks a hot sheet over a shape for trays and signs. Compression moulding squeezes and cures thermosets.

Hold four ways to shape a melt in mind and try this part's questions: the processes.

## Subtopic: Design So It Can Be Moulded

Pick by shape and numbers: solid detailed in bulk, injection; hollow with a neck, blow; long profile, extrusion; big hollow tank, rotational; shallow tray from sheet, vacuum forming.

Design rules: even walls or it sinks and warps, ribs for stiffness; a slight taper on the sides so it slides out; no hooks or hollows that lock it in; rounded corners; hide the entry mark. Every bin and bucket obeys these.

Your item: even walls, draft, no undercuts, rounded corners, ribs. Check each before you draw.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Recycled pellets become products in a moulding factory by injection moulding for solid detailed parts, blow moulding for hollow containers, extrusion for long profiles and rotational moulding for large hollow tanks, with vacuum forming and compression moulding for sheet and thermosets. The process is chosen by shape, numbers and material, and the part is designed for its process with even walls, draft angles, no undercuts and rounded corners, which is the checklist for your own plastic item.
