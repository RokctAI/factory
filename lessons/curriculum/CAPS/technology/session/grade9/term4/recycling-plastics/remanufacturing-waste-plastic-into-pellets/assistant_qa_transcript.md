### subtopic_1 — Case Study: A Plastics Recycling Plant
**Assistant:** Why does the flake have to be completely dry before it goes into the extruder?

**Tutor:** Because PET reacts with water when it is molten. PET is a polyester, made by joining its small units with the loss of a water molecule at each link, and the reaction runs backwards at high temperature: water in the melt attacks the links and cuts the chains, a process called hydrolysis. Even a fraction of a per cent of moisture in flake at 270 degrees shortens the chains enough to make the pellet weak and brittle, and the steam also makes bubbles in the strands that break in the water bath. So the plant dries the flake with hot air to well under a tenth of a per cent of moisture, measures it before extrusion, and often dries the pellets again before they are moulded. The same is true of nylon and polycarbonate. Polyethylene and polypropylene do not have that water-sensitive link, so they are more forgiving, which is one reason they are the easier plastics to recycle on a small scale.

### subtopic_2 — From Bale to Pellet: Sort, Wash, Shred, Melt
**Assistant:** Why is a float-sink tank such a good way to separate the caps from the bottles?

**Tutor:** Because it uses a property difference that costs nothing to exploit. PET has a density of about 1.38 grams per cubic centimetre, denser than water, so PET flake sinks. The caps are polypropylene or HDPE at about 0.9 to 0.96, less dense than water, so their fragments float, and so do most label scraps. Once the bottles are shredded so the pieces are small and separate, you simply pour the mixed flake into a tank of water, stir, and the two plastics sort themselves: skim the floaters off the top, draw the sinkers off the bottom. No camera, no hand sorting, no energy beyond a pump. The limit is that it cannot separate plastics of similar density, so PVC at about 1.4 sinks with PET and must be removed earlier by hand or by a sensor; a few PVC flakes in a PET melt ruin the batch. The lesson for your own work is that a cheap separation is often hiding in a property table.

### subtopic_3 — The Systems Diagram: Input, Process, Output
**Assistant:** What is the point of a systems diagram if the process is already described in words?

**Tutor:** Words describe a process in order, one step at a time, and they are good at detail. A systems diagram does a different job: it shows the whole project at one glance as a set of relationships, so you can see what feeds what, where the boundaries are, and where information flows back. Three things become visible in the diagram that are easy to miss in a paragraph. First, the resources, water, energy, labour, chemicals, which are not part of the material flow but without which nothing happens, and which are where most of the cost sits. Second, the waste streams leaving the process, which a description of making pellets tends to forget and which an environmental assessment must count. Third, the feedback arrow, the tests whose results change how the process is run; a process without feedback cannot correct itself. Engineers and project planners use the diagram to find missing pieces before building anything, which is why your design project asks for one.
