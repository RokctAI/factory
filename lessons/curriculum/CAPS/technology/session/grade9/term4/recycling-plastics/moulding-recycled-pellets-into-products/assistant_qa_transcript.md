### subtopic_1 — Case Study: From Pellet to Product
**Assistant:** Why does a thick lump in a moulded part cause a sink mark?

**Tutor:** Because plastic shrinks as it cools and solidifies, and a thick section shrinks more and later than the thin walls around it. When the melt is injected, the surface against the cold mould freezes almost at once while the inside is still molten. In a thin wall the whole thickness freezes in a second or two and the small shrinkage is taken up by the packing pressure of the machine. In a thick lump the core stays molten after the gate has frozen, so no more material can be pushed in; as that core cools and contracts it pulls the already-solid skin inward, leaving a dimple on the surface, the sink mark, and sometimes a hole inside, a void. If the lump is off-centre the uneven shrinkage bends the part, which is warping. The fix is never to make a solid thick section: keep every wall about the same thickness and get stiffness from ribs, which are thin walls standing on edge, and core out any boss or lump so it is a shell. Look at the underside of any plastic crate and you will see this rule obeyed.

### subtopic_2 — Injection, Blow, Extrusion and Rotational Moulding
**Assistant:** Why are rotational moulds cheap when injection moulds cost millions?

**Tutor:** Because of pressure. An injection mould must hold molten plastic injected at pressures of hundreds of atmospheres and must open and close every few seconds for hundreds of thousands of cycles, so it is machined from hardened tool steel with cooling channels, ejector pins and exact fits, and that is slow, skilled work. A rotational mould holds only a charge of loose powder at atmospheric pressure; the plastic melts and flows onto the inside surface under nothing more than its own weight as the mould turns in the oven. So the mould can be a fabricated sheet-steel or cast-aluminium shell, with no internal cooling and no ejectors, built in a few weeks for a small fraction of the price. The trade-off is time: a rotational cycle takes twenty minutes or more and the surface detail is softer, so it suits big hollow things made in hundreds or thousands, like water tanks, not caps made in millions. Matching the process to the numbers is the whole decision.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing a Process and Designing for Moulding
**Assistant:** For my own design project, which of these processes could a school actually use?

**Tutor:** Honestly, none of the four pellet processes at factory scale, so the project is about designing as if for one of them while making a model by a method the school has. Vacuum forming is the most realistic: many schools have a small vacuum former, and a heated sheet of HIPS or recycled PET sucked over a wooden or plaster former gives a real plastic tray, cover or casing. Small injection moulders for schools exist and make parts the size of a key ring from a hand-made aluminium mould. Otherwise you can heat and bend acrylic or PVC sheet over a former with a strip heater, cut and glue sheet, or cast a thermoset resin in a silicone mould. Whatever you make, draw the item as the factory would mould it, with even walls, draft and no undercuts, and say in your design which industrial process would make it in volume and why. The mark is for the design thinking, not for owning a factory.
