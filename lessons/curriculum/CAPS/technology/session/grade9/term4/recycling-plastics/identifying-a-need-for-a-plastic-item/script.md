# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers identifying a need that a plastic item of your own design can satisfy, and writing the brief and specifications. The anchor is a Grade 9 class in Polokwane walking the school grounds with notebooks: a leaking tap with no cup, a pile of chargers tangled in the staff room, a classroom with no way to hold a phone up for a video, a vegetable garden watered by hand with a bottle. The governing idea is that a design begins with a need observed in context, that the brief states the need and the user without prescribing the solution, that specifications turn the brief into measurable requirements, and that material and process are chosen to meet them with recycled plastic. Producing a problem statement, brief and specifications is examinable.

## Subtopic: Finding a Real Need Around You

A need is a gap between how something is and how someone wants it to be. Designers find needs by observing people rather than by sitting and imagining products: watch what people do, where they struggle, what they improvise, what they complain about. The Polokwane class found needs in an hour of walking: a tap that learners drink from with cupped hands because there is no cup holder; chargers and cables tangled on the staff room table; a teacher propping a phone against a book to show a video; a gardener watering seedlings from a bottle with no control of the flow; a wall of bare hooks where bags fall off; a bin with no lid that attracts flies.

Each of these is a candidate for a small plastic item: a cup holder that clips to the tap pipe, a cable tidy, a phone stand, a bottle-top drip irrigator, a hook with a lip, a bin lid. The test of a good need is that it is real, observed in a real place with a real user; it is specific, one problem for one person or group; it is small enough to solve with one item; and it is suited to plastic, which means it benefits from being light, moulded, water-resistant and cheap. A need that is really a wish for a new phone or a swimming pool is not a design task.

Write a problem statement in two or three sentences: who has the problem, what the problem is, where and when it occurs, and why it matters. For example: learners at the outside tap near the Grade 9 block have no clean way to drink and spill water on the ground; this happens at every break; it wastes water and wets the path. That statement names user, problem, context and consequence without saying what the solution is.

The questions for this section are with you now: how designers find needs by observing, the tests of a good need, and how to write a problem statement with user, problem, context and consequence.

## Subtopic: Writing the Design Brief and Specifications

A design brief is a short statement of what is to be designed, for whom, and to what end, written so that it does not fix the solution in advance. Design a device for the outside tap that lets learners drink without cupped hands and without spilling, that can be made from recycled plastic, that survives outdoors, and that costs very little. Notice that it does not say cup, holder or clip; a good brief leaves room for several ideas, which you will sketch and compare in the next stage.

Specifications turn the brief into a list of requirements that can be checked, and each should be measurable or at least testable. Function: must hold a cup or let water be drunk directly; must attach to a 20 millimetre pipe without tools. Size: no larger than 150 by 100 by 100 millimetres. Materials: recycled HDPE or polypropylene, UV stabilised. Durability: must survive one year outdoors and being knocked. Safety: no sharp edges; nothing a child could swallow. Cost: under twenty rand in material. Environment: made from recycled plastic and itself recyclable at end of life, one plastic only, no glued-on parts of another plastic. Aesthetics: a colour that matches the school. A specification that says it must be nice or strong is not a specification until you say how nice or how strong will be judged.

The brief and specifications are the contract against which the final item will be marked and tested, so write them before you sketch anything. The feedback arrow of the systems diagram from last week runs back to this list: each test on the finished item checks one specification.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a design brief states and leaves open, what makes a specification measurable, and the list of specification headings.

## Subtopic: Choosing Recycled Plastic and a Making Method

Choosing the plastic follows the specifications. Outdoors and knocked: polypropylene or HDPE, which are tough and, stabilised, resist sun, and are the two most common recycled pellets and sheets. Clear or see-through: recycled PET sheet. Stiff and flat: HIPS sheet or recycled PET sheet for vacuum forming. Flexible hinge: polypropylene. Avoid polystyrene foam for anything that must last, and avoid thermosets for anything that should be recyclable. Write down which plastic and the two or three properties that decided it.

Choosing the making method follows the shape and the numbers, from last session. If the school were to make hundreds, the item would be injection moulded, and you should design it that way: even walls, draft, no undercuts. For the one model you will actually make, choose from what the school has: vacuum forming over a wooden former, bending sheet with a strip heater, cutting and solvent welding sheet, or shaping a two-litre bottle or a bucket with a craft knife and a heat gun. Many good school projects take an existing recycled container as the stock material, which is reuse and recycling at the same time.

By the end of this session each learner should have one problem statement, one brief, a specification list of at least six measurable points, and a note of the chosen plastic and method. That is the input to the drawing stage. The error museum, four exhibits. One: starting from a product idea and inventing a need to fit it. Two: a brief that names the solution and so forbids better ideas. Three: specifications that cannot be tested, like must be good quality. Four: choosing a plastic for its colour alone and ignoring sun, knocks or water.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching the plastic to the specifications, choosing an industrial process to design for and a school method to make with, and the four things to have ready for the drawing stage.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Polokwane class walking the school grounds and finding the tap, the cables and the phone — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Problem Worth Solving

A need is a gap between how it is and how someone wants it. Find needs by watching people: where they struggle, what they improvise, what they complain about. The class found a tap with no cup, tangled chargers, a phone propped on a book, a gardener's bottle, bags falling off hooks, a lidless bin.

Good need: real and observed, specific to one user, small enough for one item, and suited to plastic, which is light, mouldable, waterproof and cheap. A wish for a swimming pool is not a design task.

Problem statement, two or three sentences: who, what problem, where and when, why it matters. No solution yet.

Keep a problem worth solving in mind and try the questions for this part: finding the need.

## Subtopic: Say Exactly What It Must Do

The brief: what to design, for whom, to do what, without naming the answer. A device at the tap for drinking without spilling, recycled plastic, outdoors, cheap. Not cup, not clip; leave room for ideas.

Specifications: a checklist you can test. Function, size in millimetres, material, durability, safety, cost in rand, environment, looks. Must be strong is not a spec until you say how strong and how you will test it.

Brief and specs are the contract. Write them before sketching. Every test at the end checks one spec; that is the feedback arrow.

Hold say exactly what it must do in mind and try this part's questions: brief and specifications.

## Subtopic: Which Plastic, Made How

Plastic from the specs: outdoors and knocked, polypropylene or HDPE; clear, PET sheet; stiff flat sheet to form, HIPS or PET; a living hinge, polypropylene. No foam for lasting things, no thermosets if it should be recyclable. Write down the two or three properties that decided it.

Method: design as if for injection moulding, even walls, draft, no undercuts. Make the one model with what school has: vacuum former, strip heater, cut and weld sheet, or reshape a bottle or bucket.

Have ready: problem statement, brief, six or more measurable specs, chosen plastic and method. That feeds the drawing stage.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A design starts with a need found by observing real people in a real place, written as a problem statement naming user, problem, context and consequence. The brief states what to design and for whom without fixing the solution, and specifications turn it into measurable requirements for function, size, material, durability, safety, cost, environment and looks. The recycled plastic and the making method are chosen to meet those specifications, designed for moulding but made with a school method.

Hold which plastic, made how, and take the final questions of the lesson: material and method.
