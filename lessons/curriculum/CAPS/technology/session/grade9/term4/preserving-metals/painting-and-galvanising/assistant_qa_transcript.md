### subtopic_1 — Why Metals Corrode: Iron, Oxygen and Water
**Assistant:** Why does a scratch on a galvanised sheet not rust when a scratch on a painted sheet does?

**Tutor:** Because zinc does something paint cannot: it takes part in the chemistry. Paint is an inert skin; when it is scratched, the bare iron meets water and oxygen and rusts, and nothing around the scratch helps. Zinc is a metal that is more reactive than iron, which means that when the two are touching and wet, the zinc corrodes first and the iron is left alone. The scratch becomes a tiny electric cell with the water as the electrolyte: zinc atoms on the edges of the scratch give up electrons and dissolve, those electrons flow to the bare iron and stop it dissolving, and the zinc's corrosion products even spread across the scratch and partly seal it. The iron is protected for as long as there is zinc nearby to sacrifice, which for a small scratch is years. The same idea, a more reactive metal attached to protect a less reactive one, is used on ships' hulls and buried pipelines with blocks of zinc or magnesium bolted on.

### subtopic_2 — Painting: Sealing the Surface and Its Limits
**Assistant:** If galvanising is so much better, why is anything still painted?

**Tutor:** Because better at resisting rust is not the only thing that matters, and galvanising has real limits. It is one colour, a dull grey, and a school, a shop or a car owner usually wants colour, which means paint. It is done by dipping in a factory bath, so a part must be small enough to fit and must be taken there; a bridge, a ship or a building frame cannot be dipped, so they are painted in place, often over a sprayed zinc coat. Galvanising cannot easily be repaired on site; a cut edge or a weld gets zinc-rich paint, which is a compromise. And it costs more up front, which matters when the object will be replaced anyway or kept indoors where rust is slow. So the two are often combined: a galvanised sheet painted in colour for a roof that must match a house, or a bakkie body of galvanised steel under its factory paint, which is why modern vehicles rust far less than those of thirty years ago.

### subtopic_3 — Galvanising: Zinc as a Coat and a Sacrifice
**Assistant:** Why do galvanised roofs at the coast go red so much sooner than inland?

**Tutor:** Because the zinc is being used up faster. Zinc protects by slowly corroding itself, and the rate at which it corrodes depends on what is in the air and water that touch it. Inland, rain is fairly clean and the zinc forms a stable grey carbonate skin that slows its own loss, so a coat of zinc lasts thirty to fifty years. At the coast, sea spray leaves salt on the roof, and salt water is a far better electrolyte than rain, so the zinc's protective skin is dissolved and renewed over and over, eating through the coat in ten to twenty years. Industrial areas with sulphur in the air do the same. Once the zinc is gone in a patch, there is nothing to sacrifice, the steel rusts, and the red streaks appear. Coastal builders choose thicker zinc coats, aluminium-zinc coatings, or galvanised sheet with a paint system over it to buy back the years.
