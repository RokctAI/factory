### subtopic_1 — What Electroplating Is and Where It Is Used
**Assistant:** Why does the key have to go on the negative terminal and not the positive?

**Tutor:** Because the metal that does the coating is travelling through the solution as positive ions, and positive ions go to the negative electrode. In the copper sulphate solution, copper exists as copper ions carrying a positive charge. The battery makes the key negative, so the copper ions are drawn to it, and when they touch it they pick up electrons from the key and turn back into copper metal, building the coat. At the copper strip, which the battery makes positive, the opposite happens: copper atoms lose electrons to the strip and go into the solution as new ions. Put the key on positive and it becomes the anode, so iron from the key dissolves into the solution instead and no copper lands on it, which is exactly what the reversed-leads test shows. A simple memory aid is that the object to be coated is always the cathode, and the cathode is the negative terminal in a plating cell.

### subtopic_2 — The Electroplating Cell: Anode, Cathode, Electrolyte and Current
**Assistant:** Why does a higher current give a worse coat if it moves more metal?

**Tutor:** Because a good coat is not just metal landing; it is metal landing in an orderly way. When copper ions arrive at the key slowly, each atom has time to find a place in the growing crystal structure of the coat, so the layer is dense, smooth and bright. Push the current up and ions arrive faster than they can be arranged; they pile up as loose, spongy clusters, the surface roughens, and the coat looks dark, powdery and dull, and often rubs off. Very high currents also start other reactions at the key, bubbles of hydrogen gas forming on the surface and leaving pits where no copper lands. Factories choose a current for each square centimetre of surface that gives the brightest coat and then simply wait longer for thickness. For the jar on the bench, two or three cells and a patient ten minutes beat four cells and a hurried three.

### subtopic_3 — The Practical Investigation: Copper Plating a Key
**Assistant:** Could we zinc plate the key instead, to get the sacrificial protection from the galvanising lesson?

**Tutor:** In principle yes, and industry does exactly that for small bolts and brackets, but not with the school kit. Zinc plating needs a zinc salt solution, usually zinc sulphate or a zinc chloride bath with additives, and a zinc anode, and it works the same way: zinc ions go to the negative object and build a thin grey coat that protects sacrificially at scratches, just like hot-dip galvanising but far thinner. The coat is usually a few thousandths of a millimetre instead of the hot-dip's tenth of a millimetre, so it lasts a few years outdoors rather than decades, which is why plated screws are fine indoors and hot-dip bolts are used on a fence. Copper is used for the school investigation because copper sulphate is cheap, its blue colour shows what it is, the pink coat is unmistakable on steel, and it plates easily at low voltage. The lesson learnt, metal from anode to cathode through a salt solution, transfers directly to zinc, nickel, chrome and gold.
