# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers electroplating and the practical investigation of preserving metals by electroplating. The anchor is a jam jar on a bench in Polokwane holding blue copper sulphate solution, a strip of copper clipped to the positive terminal of a 4.5 volt battery and a cleaned steel key clipped to the negative, the key turning a dull pink within minutes as copper builds on it. The governing idea is that electroplating deposits a thin layer of metal from a solution onto an object made the cathode of an electrolytic cell, that metal ions leave the anode and are deposited on the cathode as current flows, and that the coating preserves the base metal by sealing it and gives a decorative or hard surface. Explaining the electroplating cell, naming its parts, carrying out a plating investigation and stating the uses and limits of plating are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Electroplating Is and Where It Is Used

Electroplating is coating one metal with a thin layer of another by passing an electric current through a solution. The coating can be a few thousandths of a millimetre thick, far thinner than paint or hot-dip zinc, yet because it is metal bonded to metal it is hard, smooth and bright. Chromium plating gives taps, bumpers and motorcycle parts their mirror shine and hard, corrosion-resistant surface; nickel plating protects coins, cutlery and tools; gold plating gives jewellery and electrical connectors a surface that never tarnishes; zinc plating protects bolts, screws and brackets more thinly and cheaply than hot-dip galvanising; silver plating makes cutlery and trophies; tin plating lines food cans so the steel does not react with the food.

The reasons for plating are three. Preservation: the coat seals the base metal from oxygen and water, like paint, and some coats such as zinc also protect sacrificially. Appearance: a cheap steel part can be given the look of chrome, gold or silver. Properties: a hard chrome surface resists wear on a hydraulic ram or a crankshaft, a tin surface is safe with food, a gold surface conducts well and never oxidises on a connector. Often all three matter at once, as on a chromed tap that must look good, not rust and not wear.

Plating is done in factories with large tanks, careful cleaning, controlled currents and often several layers, copper then nickel then chromium for a bumper, because chromium does not plate well directly onto steel. Each layer is thin, so plating is for small and medium objects with a good surface, not for a farm gate or a roof sheet; and because the coat is thin, a deep scratch goes through it and the base metal corrodes beneath, as every peeling chrome bumper shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: what electroplating is and its common coatings, the three reasons for plating, and where it is and is not suitable.

## Subtopic: The Electroplating Cell: Anode, Cathode, Electrolyte and Current

The plating cell has four parts. The electrolyte is a solution of a salt of the coating metal, such as copper sulphate for copper plating or nickel sulphate for nickel; the salt is dissolved as ions, metal ions carrying a positive charge and sulphate ions a negative charge. The anode is a piece of the coating metal connected to the positive terminal of the supply. The cathode is the object to be plated, connected to the negative terminal. The supply is a low-voltage direct current source, a battery or a plating rectifier, since alternating current would plate and unplate in turn.

When the current flows, the positive metal ions in the solution are attracted to the negative cathode, the object, where they take electrons and become metal atoms, settling on the surface as a layer. At the positive anode, metal atoms give up electrons and dissolve into the solution as fresh ions, replacing those used up. So the anode slowly shrinks, the object slowly gains a coat, and the solution's strength stays the same. Metal leaves one side and lands on the other, carried by the current. The amount of metal moved depends on the current and the time: more current or longer time, thicker coat, which is why plating is controlled by a timer and an ammeter.

Three practical rules follow. The object must be perfectly clean, since plating grows on metal and not on grease, fingerprints or oxide, and a dirty patch stays bare or flakes. The current must be low and steady, since too high a current deposits metal faster than it can arrange itself, giving a rough, powdery, dark coat instead of a bright one. The object must be fully immersed and placed facing the anode, since plating builds thickest on the surfaces nearest the anode and thinnest in hollows and on the far side, so complex shapes are turned or given several anodes.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four parts of the plating cell, how metal moves from anode to cathode, and the rules of cleanliness, low current and placement.

## Subtopic: The Practical Investigation: Copper Plating a Key

The investigation copper plates a steel key. Equipment: a jar, copper sulphate solution, a strip or coil of copper, a steel key or a steel washer, a 4.5 volt battery or a 3 volt pair of cells, two leads with clips, fine sandpaper, detergent, a paper towel, safety glasses and gloves. Copper sulphate is harmful if swallowed and irritates skin and eyes, so gloves and glasses are worn, hands are washed after, and the solution is kept for reuse rather than poured down a drain.

Method. Clean the key: sand it bright all over, wash it in detergent, rinse, and handle it by its lead or a clip from now on. Half fill the jar with copper sulphate solution. Clip the copper strip to the positive lead and hang it in the solution on one side. Clip the key to the negative lead and hang it in the solution facing the copper, not touching it. Connect the battery and start timing. Within a minute or two a pink film appears on the key; after ten minutes it is a visible copper coat. Lift the key out, rinse, dry gently and examine. Test a second key with the leads reversed: the key, now the anode, does not plate, and copper may even be stripped from it, which confirms the direction rule.

Recording. Note the time, the voltage, the appearance of the coat, whether it is bright and even or dark and powdery, and any bare patches and what was there, a fingerprint or an unsanded spot. Try a third key at a longer time or with two cells instead of three and compare thickness and quality. A thin, bright, even coat is the result of a clean surface and a low current. Evaluate the plated key against an unplated one in a damp place over a week: the plated key resists rust where the coat is sound. The error museum, four exhibits. One: handling the cleaned key with fingers, leaving bare patches. Two: leads reversed so the key loses metal. Three: too many cells, giving a dark powdery coat. Four: letting the key and the copper touch, shorting the cell and plating nothing.

The questions for this section are with you now: equipment and safety for the investigation, the method step by step, and recording, varying and evaluating the result.

## Subtopic: A Metal Coat Put On by Electricity

Electroplating grows a very thin coat of one metal onto another using electricity. Chrome on taps and bumpers, nickel on coins and cutlery, gold on jewellery and connectors, zinc on screws, tin inside food cans.

Why: to seal the metal from rust, to make cheap metal look like chrome or gold, and to give a hard, safe or well-conducting surface.

Done in factory tanks, often in layers. Thin, so good for small shiny things, not gates or roofs, and a deep scratch goes through it.

## Subtopic: Metal Leaves One Side and Lands on the Other

Four parts: the solution of the coating metal's salt, the anode of the coating metal on plus, the cathode which is the object on minus, and a low-voltage battery.

Metal ions in the solution are pulled to the negative object and settle on it as metal. At the positive copper strip, copper dissolves to replace them. The strip shrinks, the key gains, the solution stays the same. More current or more time, thicker coat.

Rules: the object must be spotless, the current low and steady, the object fully under and facing the anode.

## Subtopic: Clean It, Wire It, Wait

Jar, copper sulphate solution, copper strip, steel key, 3 or 4.5 volts, two clipped leads, sandpaper, detergent, gloves and glasses. Copper sulphate is harmful: gloves, glasses, wash hands, keep the solution for next time.

Sand the key bright, wash it, do not touch it again. Copper on plus into the jar. Key on minus into the jar, facing the copper, not touching. Connect, time it. Pink in a minute, a coat in ten. Rinse, dry, look. Reverse the leads on a second key: no plating.

Record time, volts, look of the coat, bare patches and why. Try longer or fewer cells. Bright and even means clean and low current. Leave a plated and an unplated key somewhere damp for a week.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Electroplating deposits a thin metal coat on an object made the cathode in a solution of the coating metal's salt, with the coating metal as the anode and a low-voltage direct current. Metal ions are deposited on the object while the anode dissolves to replace them. It preserves, decorates and hardens small objects, and needs a spotless surface, a low current and good placement. Copper plating a sanded key in copper sulphate on 3 to 4.5 volts gives a pink coat in minutes, and reversing the leads gives nothing.
