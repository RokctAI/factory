# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers preserving metals by painting and galvanising. The anchor is a farm near Bethlehem in the Free State: a gate painted green ten years ago now streaked with rust where the paint chipped, beside a galvanised wire fence and a corrugated iron shed roof that are grey, dull and intact after forty years. The governing idea is that iron corrodes when oxygen and water reach its surface, that a coating prevents corrosion by excluding them, that paint is a barrier which fails where it is broken, and that zinc galvanising is both a barrier and a sacrificial coat, corroding in preference to the steel and protecting even scratched areas. Explaining why metals corrode and how painting and galvanising preserve them, with their advantages and limits, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Metals Corrode: Iron, Oxygen and Water

Corrosion is the slow conversion of a metal back into a compound like the ore it came from. For iron and steel the compound is rust, a flaky red-brown iron oxide that forms when iron reacts with oxygen in the presence of water. Both are needed: iron in dry desert air barely rusts; iron under oil does not rust; iron in damp coastal air rusts fast, and faster still where salt in the air speeds the reaction, which is why cars in Durban and Port Elizabeth rust sooner than cars in Upington. Rust occupies more volume than the iron it came from, so it lifts and flakes, exposing fresh metal, and the process continues until the metal is eaten through.

Rust is more than ugly. A rusted gate hinge seizes, a rusted roof sheet leaks, a rusted car body loses strength, a rusted reinforcing bar in concrete swells and cracks the concrete around it. The cost of corrosion to a country runs into billions of rand a year in replacement and repair, which is why preserving metal is an industry in itself. Indigenous iron workers knew this long before chemistry explained it: tools were oiled, kept dry and stored away from the ground, and the fire-blackened surface of forged iron was itself a partial protection.

The principle of all preservation is the same: keep oxygen and water away from the metal. This can be done by a barrier coat that seals the surface, by a coat of a metal that corrodes in preference to the one underneath, by alloying to make a metal that does not rust easily, as in stainless steel, or by keeping the environment dry. The term's first week looks at the two coats, paint and zinc, and next session at a third, electroplating.

The questions for this section are with you now: what rust is and the two things it needs, why corrosion matters, and the principle of keeping oxygen and water away.

## Subtopic: Painting: Sealing the Surface and Its Limits

Paint is a barrier. A paint film is a thin layer of pigment, which gives colour and body, held in a binder that dries or cures into a tough skin, carried by a solvent or water that evaporates. Applied to clean, dry steel, the film seals the surface so that oxygen and water cannot reach the iron. The preparation is most of the job: rust and loose scale are removed by wire brush, sandpaper or sandblasting, grease is cleaned off, and a primer, often containing zinc or a rust-inhibiting pigment, is applied first to grip the metal and resist corrosion, followed by one or two top coats for weather and colour.

Paint's strength is that it is cheap, can be any colour and can be applied anywhere with a brush, roller or spray, to a gate, a bridge, a car body or a ship. Its weakness is that it is only a skin. Where the skin is scratched, chipped or worn, the metal is bare, and rust starts at the break and spreads underneath the paint, lifting it. Paint also weathers, becoming chalky and cracked in the sun, and must be renewed every few years. The Bethlehem gate shows all of this: rust at every chip and along the bottom bar where the paint wore through, and the next coat will have to be preceded by hours of brushing.

Painting is still the right choice for many jobs: where colour matters, where the object is large or fixed, where the part can be repainted regularly, and where it is kept indoors or under cover. Steel roof trusses in a hall, a school's steel window frames, and a bakkie's body are painted, with the bakkie's paint applied over a factory primer and sometimes over galvanised steel for double protection.

The questions for this section are with you now: what paint is and how the surface is prepared, its strengths and its weakness at breaks, and where painting is the right choice.

## Subtopic: Galvanising: Zinc as a Coat and a Sacrifice

Galvanising coats steel with zinc. In hot-dip galvanising the cleaned steel is dipped in a bath of molten zinc at about 450 degrees, and the zinc bonds to the surface in a layer that is part alloy and part pure zinc, dull grey with a crystalline pattern called spangle. Wire, sheet, pipe, bolts, fence posts, roofing sheets, water tanks and the steel of power pylons are galvanised. The zinc layer is a barrier like paint, keeping oxygen and water from the iron, but it has a second property that paint does not have.

Zinc is more reactive than iron. When a galvanised surface is scratched through to the steel, water bridges the zinc and the iron and a tiny electric cell forms, in which the zinc corrodes and the iron does not. The zinc gives itself up to protect the steel; it is called sacrificial protection, and it means a scratch does not start rust the way it does on paint. The zinc corrodes slowly, forming a dull grey carbonate skin that itself protects, so a galvanised roof lasts thirty to fifty years inland, less at the coast where salt speeds the zinc's loss. When the zinc is finally gone the steel rusts, which is why old galvanised sheets go from grey to streaked red.

Galvanising's limits: it is one colour, dull grey, unless painted over, and paint on galvanising needs a special primer or it peels; it is done in a factory bath, not on site, so a repair to a cut edge is done with zinc-rich paint, which is a weaker version; it costs more than a coat of paint at first, though less over the life of the object; and the zinc is slowly used up. The comparison for the examination: paint is a cheap barrier that fails at breaks and needs renewal; galvanising is a longer-lasting barrier that also sacrifices itself at breaks. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying rust needs only air, or only water. Two: painting over rust without removing it. Three: thinking a scratch on galvanising rusts like a scratch on paint. Four: calling galvanising a paint.

The questions for this section are with you now: how hot-dip galvanising coats steel, sacrificial protection at scratches, and the limits and comparison with paint.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the painted gate and the galvanised fence and roof on the farm near Bethlehem — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Rust Needs Air and Water

Rust is iron turning back into an oxide. It needs oxygen and water, both. Dry desert iron barely rusts; oily iron does not; damp coastal iron rusts fast, salt faster. Rust flakes, exposes fresh metal, and keeps going.

Rust seizes hinges, leaks roofs, weakens cars, cracks concrete. It costs the country billions. Old ironworkers oiled their tools and kept them dry.

Every method does one thing: keep oxygen and water off the metal. Paint, zinc, plating, stainless, or dryness.

Keep rust needs air and water in mind and try the questions for this part: corrosion.

## Subtopic: A Skin of Paint

Paint is a skin: pigment in a binder that dries hard. Clean off the rust and grease first, prime, then top coats. Preparation is most of the work.

Cheap, any colour, brush it on anywhere. But it is only a skin: a chip exposes the steel, rust starts there and spreads under the paint. Sun chalks and cracks it. Renew every few years. That is the Bethlehem gate.

Right for colour, for big fixed things, for things you can repaint, for indoors. Roof trusses, window frames, a bakkie's body.

Hold a skin of paint in mind and try this part's questions: painting.

## Subtopic: A Skin of Zinc That Gives Itself Up

Galvanising dips clean steel in molten zinc at about 450 degrees. A grey crystalline coat bonds on. Wire, sheet, pipe, bolts, posts, roofs, tanks, pylons.

Zinc is more reactive than iron. Scratch it and the zinc corrodes instead of the steel. It sacrifices itself. A scratch does not start rust. The zinc slowly greys over and lasts thirty to fifty years inland, less at the coast. When it is gone, the steel rusts.

Limits: grey only unless specially primed and painted, done in a factory not on site, costs more up front, the zinc gets used up. Paint: cheap barrier, fails at chips, renew often. Galvanising: longer barrier plus sacrifice at chips.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Iron rusts when oxygen and water both reach it, and every preservation method keeps them away. Paint is a cheap barrier skin in any colour that needs clean preparation, fails wherever it is broken and must be renewed. Galvanising is a factory-applied zinc coat that acts as a barrier and, because zinc is more reactive than iron, corrodes sacrificially at scratches, protecting the steel for decades at the cost of a single grey colour and a higher initial price.

Hold a skin of zinc that gives itself up, and take the final questions of the lesson: galvanising.
