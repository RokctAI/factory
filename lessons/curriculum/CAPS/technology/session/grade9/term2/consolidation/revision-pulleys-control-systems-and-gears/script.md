# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises pulleys, mechanical control systems and gear systems for the mid-year examination. The anchor is a revision circuit set up in a Technology room in Polokwane, five stations with a pulley rig and spring balance, a ratchet spanner and a bicycle wheel with its brake, a cardboard gear set, a gate rack offcut and a guitar tuning peg, the learners moving round with a single sheet of rules and calculations. The governing idea is that the mechanisms of the term each have one counting rule, load-bearing ropes for pulleys and driven over driver teeth for gears, that speed and force are always traded, and that control systems add no force but govern direction, stopping and holding. Recalling the rules, calculating mechanical advantage and gear ratios, and identifying mechanisms and controls from descriptions are examinable.

## Subtopic: Revising Pulleys: Fixed, Movable and Block and Tackle

The single fixed pulley changes the direction of the pull and nothing else: mechanical advantage 1, effort equals load, rope pulled equals distance lifted, and the gain is that the lifter pulls down and uses body weight. The single movable pulley hangs the load from two rope sections, so each carries half: mechanical advantage 2, effort half the load, rope pulled twice the lift, with the awkwardness that the pull is upward. The block and tackle combines them: a fixed block to turn the pull downward and a movable block hung from several rope sections.

The rule for every pulley system is that the ideal mechanical advantage equals the number of rope sections supporting the movable block or load. Count the sections that run upward from the movable block; the section leaving the fixed block to the hand does not count. Rope pulled equals lift multiplied by the mechanical advantage. Measured values fall short of the count because the lower block has weight and each sheave has friction; efficiency is actual over ideal and falls as sheaves are added.

Worked examples. A 60 newton load on a tackle with three load-bearing sections: ideal effort 20 newtons; lifting it 0.5 metres needs 1.5 metres of rope; a measured effort of 24 newtons gives actual advantage 2.5 and efficiency 2.5 over 3, about 83 percent. A 100 newton load on a single movable pulley: 50 newton effort, 2 metres of rope per metre of lift. Draw the rig, arrow every section, count, calculate, and state ideal and actual separately.

The questions for this section are with you now: fixed, movable and tackle rules, the load-bearing rope count, and worked pulley calculations.

## Subtopic: Revising Control Systems: Ratchet, Brakes and Cleat

Control systems do not create or multiply force; they govern motion. The ratchet and pawl allows rotation in one direction only: saw-toothed wheel, spring-loaded pawl riding the slopes and jamming on the steep faces, used in winches, jacks, ratchet spanners, cable ties and handbrakes; its weakness is that it holds only at a tooth, and its design needs a strong pawl pushed into engagement by the load and a release.

Brakes stop motion by friction and convert the energy of motion into heat. The disc brake clamps pads onto a disc by hydraulic pressure, Pascal's principle again, with cooling against fade. The bicycle rim brake pinches rubber blocks onto the rim through a lever and cable, two lever stages multiplying the hand force, with wet rims the known weakness. Both are evaluated on reliable stopping in all conditions, full release and a control path that cannot fail quietly.

The cleat holds a rope under load by friction or jamming so that load tightens the grip, and releases it instantly: horn cleats with figure-of-eight turns, cam cleats with spring-loaded toothed jaws, jam cleats with a tapered slot. Examination questions ask learners to name the control from a description, state what it governs, give an application and evaluate it under the five headings. Keep the three verbs in mind: the ratchet allows, the brake stops, the cleat holds.

The questions for this section are with you now: what control systems do and do not do, the ratchet, brakes and cleat in one line each, and how to answer identification questions.

## Subtopic: Revising Gears: Spur, Idler, Bevel, Rack and Worm

Gears transmit motion between shafts by meshing teeth with no slip. Spur gears on parallel shafts turn in opposite directions; the gear ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth; a small gear driving a large one slows the output and increases turning force, the reverse speeds it up and reduces force; speed and force are always traded. An idler between driver and driven restores the direction and leaves the ratio unchanged because its teeth cancel. A compound train multiplies the ratios of its stages.

Bevel gears have teeth on cones and turn the axis through 90 degrees with the same ratio rules: hand drill, eggbeater, differential, windmill pump. The rack and pinion turns rotation into straight-line motion and back, the rack moving one tooth spacing per pinion tooth per turn: sliding gates and steering racks. The worm gear advances its wheel one tooth per turn of the worm, so the ratio equals the wheel's tooth count, giving a large reduction and force increase in one self-locking pair: tuning pegs, winches, gate motors.

Worked examples. 15-tooth driver, 45-tooth driven: ratio 3 to 1, 300 revolutions per minute in gives 100 out, triple the turning force, opposite direction. Add a 20-tooth idler: same ratio, same direction as the driver. 8-tooth pinion on a rack with 12 millimetre spacing: 96 millimetres per turn. 50-tooth worm wheel at 1500 in: 30 out, fifty times the force, self-locking. The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the hand rope as load-bearing. Two: saying a control system increases force. Three: writing gear ratio as driver over driven. Four: believing the idler changes the ratio or that the wheel can drive a worm.

The questions for this section are with you now: spur gear rules and the idler, bevel, rack and worm in one line each, and worked gear calculations.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again round the five revision stations in Polokwane — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Count the Ropes

Fixed pulley: changes direction only, advantage 1. Movable pulley: load on two ropes, advantage 2, pull twice the rope. Block and tackle: fixed block on top, movable block on the load, one rope between.

Rule: advantage equals the number of ropes holding the movable block up. The rope to your hand from the top block does not count. Rope pulled is lift times advantage. Measured is less because of block weight and friction.

60 newtons on three ropes: 20 newtons ideal, 1.5 metres of rope for half a metre up; measured 24 means efficiency about 83 percent. 100 newtons on a movable pulley: 50 newtons, double rope.

Keep count the ropes in mind and try the questions for this part: pulleys.

## Subtopic: Allow, Stop, Hold

Controls do not add force. They govern motion. Ratchet: one way only. Saw teeth, spring pawl, click one way, jam the other. Winches, spanners, cable ties, handbrakes.

Brakes: stop by friction, motion becomes heat. Disc brake: fluid pushes pads onto a disc. Bicycle brake: lever and cable pinch the rim. Judge on stopping every time, wet or dry.

Cleat: holds a rope, load tightens the grip, lets go in a second. Horn, cam, jam. Three verbs: allow, stop, hold.

Hold allow, stop, hold in mind and try this part's questions: control systems.

## Subtopic: Count the Teeth

Spur gears: opposite ways, ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth, small driving big is slow and strong, speed and force trade. Idler: flips direction back, ratio unchanged. Compound: ratios multiply.

Bevel: teeth on cones, turns the corner, same ratio rules. Rack and pinion: round to straight, teeth times spacing per turn. Worm: one tooth per turn, ratio is the wheel's teeth, self-locking.

15 drives 45: 3 to 1, 300 in, 100 out, triple force. Add an idler: same. 8-tooth pinion at 12 millimetres: 96 per turn. 50-tooth worm wheel at 1500: 30 out, fifty times the force.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Pulleys: ideal advantage equals the load-bearing ropes, rope pulled equals lift times advantage, measured falls short through friction and block weight. Controls add no force: the ratchet allows one direction, the brake stops by friction and makes heat, the cleat holds a rope and releases instantly. Gears: ratio is driven over driver, speed and force trade, an idler changes direction only, bevel gears turn the axis, a rack and pinion makes straight motion, a worm gear gives a large self-locking reduction.

Hold count the teeth, and take the final questions of the lesson: gears.
