### subtopic_1 — Revising Pneumatics and Hydraulics: Syringes, Gases and Liquids
**Assistant:** In the exam, how do I tell which cylinder is the input when the question does not say?

**Tutor:** Look for where the force is applied by a person or a motor; that is the input, and the side that does the job on the load is the output. In a jack the handle and small plunger are the input, the ram under the car is the output. In a press the pump cylinder is the input and the big ram pressing the workpiece is the output. If the question gives a force and an area on the same side, that side is where you start regardless of its name, because pressure can only be found where both are known. Write the two sides down as a little table before calculating, input and output, with force, area and distance in rows, fill in what is given, and the gaps are what the question wants. Then do the sense check: the bigger area must end up with the bigger force. If it does not, you have swapped something, and the table will show you where.

### subtopic_2 — Revising Pascal's Principle and the Distance-Force Trade-Off
**Assistant:** Why does the volume rule give the same answer as saying work in equals work out?

**Tutor:** Because they are two ways of saying one thing about the same liquid. Work is force times distance. Force on each piston is pressure times that piston's area, and the pressure is the same on both sides. So work on the input side is pressure times input area times input distance, and work on the output side is pressure times output area times output distance. Area times distance is volume, and the volume leaving the input equals the volume entering the output. Same pressure, same volume, so same work. Energy conservation and the incompressibility of the liquid are telling the same story; the volume rule is just the practical way to calculate distances, and work in equals work out is the principle that guarantees you can never get more out than you put in, friction aside.

### subtopic_3 — Revising Press Calculations, the Jack and Systems Diagrams
**Assistant:** How much of the systems diagram do I actually need to draw to get the marks?

**Tutor:** Three labelled blocks joined by arrows, with the right words in each, is the whole answer; drawing the jack earns nothing. The input block says what goes in: force from the operator on the handle. The process block, or a chain of process blocks, says what the machine does to it: the lever multiplies the force, the plunger pressurises the oil, the pressure is transmitted to the ram, the larger ram area multiplies the force again. The output block says what comes out: the load is lifted through a small distance. Arrows run left to right from input through process to output. If the question asks for it, a feedback or control note can be added, such as the one-way valve holding the load between strokes and the release valve lowering it. Keep each block to a short phrase and make sure the words in the process block are the mechanisms, not a description of the jack's appearance.
