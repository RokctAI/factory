# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises pneumatic and hydraulic systems, Pascal's principle and hydraulic press calculations for the mid-year examination. The anchor is a revision table in a Technology classroom in Bloemfontein with two syringes, a length of tubing, a bottle jack borrowed from the caretaker's bakkie and a worksheet of six calculation questions, the learners working from the simplest syringe pair up to the full press problem. The governing idea is that every fluid system in the term rests on three facts, that liquids do not compress, that pressure is transmitted equally throughout a confined liquid, and that force equals pressure times area, so that output force, input force, area and distance can all be found from one equation and one volume rule. Recalling and applying these facts in explanation, calculation and systems diagram questions is examinable.

## Subtopic: Revising Pneumatics and Hydraulics: Syringes, Gases and Liquids

Start with two equal syringes joined by tubing. Filled with air, pushing one plunger moves the other, but with a soft, springy delay, because air is a gas and gases compress: the push first squashes the air, then moves the far plunger. Filled with water, the far plunger moves at once and by the same distance, because liquids are practically incompressible: the water cannot be squashed, so the volume pushed out of one syringe must appear in the other. Pneumatic systems use gas; hydraulic systems use liquid. Pneumatics are softer, lighter and safe if they leak; hydraulics are stiff, precise and strong.

With unequal syringes the game changes. Push the small syringe and the large plunger moves out with greater force and through a shorter distance. Push the large syringe and the small plunger moves out with less force and through a longer distance. Force multiplication from small to large; force division from large to small. The volume rule explains the distances: the volume leaving one syringe equals the volume entering the other, so area times distance is the same on both sides, and the larger area must move the shorter distance.

Examination questions on this part ask for explanation. Why does the air-filled system feel spongy: gases compress. Why do brakes fail with air in the line: the pedal's push compresses the air instead of moving the pads. Why is a hydraulic jack filled with oil and not air: the liquid transmits the force fully and immediately. Why is a bicycle pump pneumatic: compressing the air is the point. Each answer names the property, compressible or incompressible, and says what follows from it.

The questions for this section are with you now: equal syringes with air and water, unequal syringes and force multiplication or division, and the compressibility explanations.

## Subtopic: Revising Pascal's Principle and the Distance-Force Trade-Off

Pascal's principle: pressure applied to a confined liquid is transmitted equally and undiminished to every part of the liquid and to the walls of its container. Pressure is force divided by area, in newtons per square centimetre or pascals. Because the pressure is the same at the small piston and the large piston, force divided by area is equal on both sides: input force over input area equals output force over output area. A small force on a small area produces the same pressure as a large force on a large area; that is the whole secret of hydraulic force multiplication.

The trade-off comes from volume. The liquid moved out of the input cylinder equals the liquid moved into the output cylinder. Input area times input distance equals output area times output distance. So if the output area is five times the input area, the output piston moves one fifth of the input distance. Multiply force by five, divide distance by five: work in equals work out, less friction. Mechanical advantage is output force over input force, which equals output area over input area. A mechanical advantage greater than 1 means force gained and distance lost; less than 1 means distance gained and force lost.

Quick examples. Input area 2 square centimetres, output area 10: mechanical advantage 5; a 40 newton push gives 200 newtons out; pushing in 10 centimetres moves the output 2 centimetres. Reverse the cylinders: mechanical advantage 0.2, 40 newtons in gives 8 out, 10 centimetres in gives 50 out. Always write which cylinder is the input, state both areas, and check that the bigger force sits with the smaller distance.

The questions for this section are with you now: the statement of Pascal's principle, the pressure equation on both sides, and the volume rule and trade-off.

## Subtopic: Revising Press Calculations, the Jack and Systems Diagrams

The press calculation is three steps, every time. One: find the pressure from the side where both force and area are known, pressure equals force over area. Two: apply that pressure to the other side, force equals pressure times area, or area equals force over pressure. Three: check for sense: the larger area should carry the larger force, the units should be consistent, and the answer should be in the right ballpark. A press with a 5 square centimetre input piston pushed with 100 newtons gives 20 newtons per square centimetre; on a 50 square centimetre output piston that is 1000 newtons. Ten times the area, ten times the force. If the output had come out smaller, something was swapped.

The hydraulic jack puts the press into a tool. A lever handle pumps a small plunger, each stroke pushing oil through a one-way valve into the large ram cylinder, so the ram rises a little with each stroke and the valve stops the oil returning; a release valve lets the oil back to the reservoir to lower the load. Evaluation of the jack under the five headings: fit for lifting a car for a wheel change, safe only with the car on firm ground and a stand under it, ergonomic with its long handle and low effort, cheap to buy, and not pretty but not required to be.

The systems diagram shows input, process and output as labelled blocks with arrows: input, force on the handle; process, lever multiplies force, plunger pressurises oil, pressure transmitted to the ram, large area multiplies force again; output, the load is lifted a small distance. It is not a drawing of the jack. The error museum, four exhibits. One: swapping input and output areas so the press appears to shrink the force. Two: mixing square centimetres with square metres in one calculation. Three: claiming the jack produces more energy than it receives; it trades distance for force. Four: drawing a systems diagram as a picture with no labelled blocks or arrows.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three-step press calculation, the jack and its evaluation, and the systems diagram.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the two syringes and the bottle jack on the revision table in Bloemfontein — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Push Here, It Pushes There

Two equal syringes and a tube. Air inside: push one and the other moves late and soft, because air squashes. Water inside: the other moves at once and the same distance, because water does not squash. Pneumatic is gas, hydraulic is liquid.

Unequal syringes. Small to big: more force, less distance. Big to small: less force, more distance. The water pushed out of one goes into the other, so area times distance matches.

Exam answers: spongy because gases compress; brakes fail with air because the pedal squashes air instead of pushing pads; jacks use oil because liquid passes the force on fully.

Keep push here, it pushes there in mind and try the questions for this part: syringes and compressibility.

## Subtopic: Same Pressure, Different Areas

Pascal: push on a trapped liquid and the pressure goes everywhere equally. Pressure is force over area. Same pressure both sides, so force over area is equal on both sides.

Volume moved is the same both sides, so the big piston moves less. Force times five, distance divided by five. Work the same. Advantage is big area over small area.

2 and 10 square centimetres: advantage 5. 40 newtons in, 200 out. 10 centimetres in, 2 out. Swap the cylinders and it is 0.2.

Hold same pressure, different areas in mind and try this part's questions: Pascal and the trade-off.

## Subtopic: Three Steps, Then Check

Press sums in three steps. Pressure from the known side: force over area. Apply it to the other side: pressure times area. Check: big area, big force, units matching. 100 newtons on 5 gives 20; 20 on 50 gives 1000.

The jack is a press with a pump handle, a one-way valve and a release valve. Judge it: good for a wheel change, safe only on firm ground with a stand, easy handle, cheap, looks do not matter.

Systems diagram: boxes and arrows. Input force on the handle; lever, plunger, pressure, ram; output load lifted a little. Not a picture.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gases compress, liquids do not, so hydraulics transmit force fully and at once. Pascal's principle says pressure in a confined liquid is the same everywhere, so force over area is equal on both sides and a larger area gives a larger force. Equal volumes mean the larger piston moves the shorter distance: force is traded for distance, work stays the same. Press calculations go pressure, apply, check. The jack is a press with a pump and valves, and a systems diagram is labelled blocks with arrows.

Hold three steps, then check, and take the final questions of the lesson: press, jack and diagram.
