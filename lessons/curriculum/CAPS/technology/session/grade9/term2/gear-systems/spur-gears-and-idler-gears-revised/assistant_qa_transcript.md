### subtopic_1 — Spur Gears Revisited: Teeth, Meshing and Direction
**Assistant:** Why does the idler's number of teeth not matter when it clearly has teeth of its own?

**Tutor:** Because the idler is driven and driving at the same time, and its teeth appear once on the top and once on the bottom of the calculation. Take a 10-tooth driver, a 15-tooth idler and a 30-tooth driven gear. Driver to idler: ratio 15 over 10. Idler to driven: ratio 30 over 15. The overall ratio is the product, 15 over 10 times 30 over 15, and the 15s cancel, leaving 30 over 10, which is 3 to 1, exactly as if the driver meshed with the driven gear directly. Physically, the idler turns at whatever speed its tooth count demands, faster than the driver here, but it passes teeth to the driven gear at the same rate the driver passes them to it. It is a messenger: it changes the direction of the message and nothing about its content.

### subtopic_2 — Gear Ratio, Speed and Force in Equal and Unequal Gears
**Assistant:** Does a bigger gear really give more force, or does it just turn slower?

**Tutor:** Both, and they are two faces of one fact. The teeth at the meshing point push with the same force on both gears, since one tooth pushes the other. But that push acts at a larger radius on the big gear than on the small one, and turning force, or torque, is force times radius. So the big gear gets more turning force out of the same tooth push, in exact proportion to its size, and because it has more teeth it also turns more slowly in exact proportion. The energy per second going in equals the energy per second coming out, less friction, so more turning force must come with less speed. If you turn a hand drill backwards by its chuck you feel this: the handle spins easily and fast but has very little force, because you are now driving the big gear from the small one.

### subtopic_3 — Idler Gears and Compound Trains
**Assistant:** If gears are so reliable, why does a bicycle use a chain instead of gears touching?

**Tutor:** Because the two shafts on a bicycle, the pedal crank and the back wheel, are about half a metre apart, and gears can only mesh when their centres are a tooth-depth away from touching. To bridge half a metre with meshing gears you would need either two gears each half a metre across, which would be heavy and absurd, or a train of several idlers, which would add weight, friction and noise. A chain is a flexible way of making two toothed wheels behave as if they meshed, carrying the teeth of one to the teeth of the other. The ratio rule is identical: rear sprocket teeth over chainring teeth, and the only difference is that the two sprockets turn the same way, because the chain does not reverse direction as a mesh does.
