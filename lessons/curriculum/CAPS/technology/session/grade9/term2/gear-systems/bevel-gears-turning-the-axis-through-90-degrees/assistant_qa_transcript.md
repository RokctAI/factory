### subtopic_1 — Bevel Gears: Cone-Shaped Teeth That Meet at a Right Angle
**Assistant:** If the ratio rules are the same, what is actually different about bevel gears compared to spur gears?

**Tutor:** Only the geometry, and that one difference is the whole point. Spur gears have teeth on cylinders, so the only way two can mesh is with their axles parallel; the teeth run straight and meet side by side. Bevel gears have teeth on cones, and two cones can roll on each other only if their points coincide, which forces their axles to meet at an angle at that point. So the one thing a bevel pair can do that a spur pair cannot is change the direction of the axis of rotation. Everything about counting teeth, the ratio, speed and torque is unchanged, because those depend only on how many teeth pass the meshing point per turn, and that is set by tooth counts, not by whether the teeth sit on a cylinder or a cone.

### subtopic_2 — Equal and Unequal Bevel Gears: Ratio, Speed and Force
**Assistant:** Why does the eggbeater have two small gears at the bottom instead of one?

**Tutor:** Because the two beaters need to turn in opposite directions so that they push the mixture toward each other rather than just stirring it round the bowl. The big bevel wheel on the handle meshes with two bevel pinions, one on each beater shaft, placed on opposite sides of the big wheel. A tooth of the big wheel passing the top of one pinion pushes it one way; the same wheel passing the other pinion on the far side pushes it the other way, because the two pinions see the wheel's teeth going in opposite directions relative to themselves. The result is two counter-rotating beaters from one handle, with the axis turned through 90 degrees at the same time, which is a lot of engineering in a cheap kitchen tool.

### subtopic_3 — Where Bevel Gears Are Used and How to Draw Them
**Assistant:** Why is the crown wheel at the back of a bakkie so much bigger than the pinion?

**Tutor:** Because the final drive has two jobs and the size difference does both at once. The first job is to turn the engine's rotation, which runs along the propeller shaft from front to back, through 90 degrees to the axle that runs across the vehicle; any bevel pair does that. The second job is to slow the rotation down and multiply the turning force, because an engine spins at a few thousand revolutions per minute while the wheels on the open road turn at a few hundred, and a loaded bakkie pulling away needs far more torque at the wheels than the engine shaft provides. A pinion of about 10 teeth driving a crown wheel of about 40 gives a ratio near 4 to 1, a quarter of the speed and four times the turning force, which is why the crown wheel is the size of a dinner plate and the pinion the size of a fist.
