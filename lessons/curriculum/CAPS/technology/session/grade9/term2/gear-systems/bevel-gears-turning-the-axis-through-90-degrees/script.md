# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers bevel gears of equal and unequal size and how they turn the axis of rotation through 90 degrees. The anchor is a hand-cranked eggbeater in a kitchen in Mitchells Plain, the big wheel on the handle turning vertically while the two beaters spin horizontally below it, the corner being turned by a pair of small bevel gears at the top of each beater shaft. The governing idea is that bevel gears have teeth cut on cones whose points meet, so that two meshing bevel gears rotate on shafts set at an angle, usually 90 degrees, while the ratio, speed and force relationships follow exactly the same rules as spur gears. Explaining how bevel gears redirect rotation, calculating with equal and unequal bevel pairs, and identifying applications are examinable.

## Subtopic: Bevel Gears: Cone-Shaped Teeth That Meet at a Right Angle

A bevel gear is a gear whose teeth are cut on the surface of a cone rather than a cylinder. Imagine two cones placed point to point so that their sloping surfaces roll on each other without slipping; cut matching teeth on those surfaces and the cones become a pair of bevel gears. Because the cones' axes pass through the shared point at an angle, the two shafts are at an angle too. In almost every school and household example that angle is 90 degrees, so a bevel pair takes rotation from a horizontal shaft and delivers it to a vertical one, or from a shaft running fore and aft to one running side to side.

The teeth of a bevel gear are wider at the outer edge and narrower toward the cone's point, which is the detail that lets them mesh along their whole length as the cones roll. When two bevel gears of equal size mesh at 90 degrees, the pair is called a mitre gear and looks like a right-angled corner of teeth. When they are unequal, the smaller is called the pinion and the larger the crown wheel, the pair found at the back axle of a rear-wheel-drive bakkie. Bevel gears, like spur gears, cannot slip, must share a tooth size, and need the two shafts to meet at the designed angle, or the teeth bind and wear.

The reason bevel gears exist is that motion is often produced in one direction and needed in another. An engine shaft runs along the vehicle; the wheels turn across it. A handle is comfortable to turn vertically; a beater needs to spin horizontally. A windmill's blades turn on a horizontal shaft; the pump rod below goes up and down from a vertical shaft. In each case a bevel pair makes the turn without a flexible link, compactly and with a fixed ratio.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes a gear a bevel gear, why the shafts are at an angle, and why turning the axis is needed.

## Subtopic: Equal and Unequal Bevel Gears: Ratio, Speed and Force

All the spur gear arithmetic carries over. The gear ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth. Equal bevel gears of 20 teeth each give a ratio of 1 to 1: same speed, same turning force, the direction turned through 90 degrees and nothing else changed. This is the eggbeater's top pair and the mitre gear in a hand drill's simplest form. The direction of rotation is still reversed in the sense that, viewed from the meshing point, teeth go one way on the driver and the other on the driven gear, but with shafts at right angles it is clearer to describe the output by which way it turns when viewed along its own shaft.

Unequal bevel gears trade speed for force exactly as unequal spur gears do. A 10-tooth pinion driving a 40-tooth crown wheel gives a ratio of 4 to 1: the crown turns at a quarter of the pinion's speed with four times the turning force. This is the bakkie's final drive, where the engine's fast rotation must be slowed and strengthened to turn heavy wheels. A 40-tooth driver turning a 10-tooth driven gear gives 1 to 4: four times the speed, a quarter of the force, which is the hand drill, where a slow handle must spin the drill bit fast.

Worked examples. Pinion 12 teeth driving crown 36 teeth at 1800 revolutions per minute: ratio 3 to 1, output 600 revolutions per minute, three times the turning force. Handle gear 32 teeth driving bevel pinion 8 teeth at 60 revolutions per minute: ratio 1 to 4, output 240 revolutions per minute, a quarter of the force. Equal mitre gears, 24 and 24, at 100 revolutions per minute: 1 to 1, 100 out, same force, axis turned 90 degrees. Write the ratio, state the speed change, state the force change, state the angle.

The questions for this section are with you now: ratios for equal and unequal bevel gears, speed and force in each case, and worked examples.

## Subtopic: Where Bevel Gears Are Used and How to Draw Them

Applications. The hand drill: a large bevel wheel on the crank turns a small bevel pinion on the chuck shaft, giving speed and turning the axis from across the body to along it. The eggbeater: the same, with two pinions so the beaters counter-rotate. The rear axle differential: a pinion on the propeller shaft turns the crown wheel on the axle, slowing and strengthening the drive and turning it through 90 degrees. The angle grinder: a bevel pair turns the motor's drive through 90 degrees to the disc. The windmill water pump on a Karoo farm: a bevel pair at the top of the tower turns the blades' horizontal rotation to the vertical pump shaft. The lid-lifting mechanism on some pots and the corner of a shaft-driven bicycle both use bevel pairs.

Drawing bevel gears. In a sketch, show each gear as a cone with teeth drawn as short lines on the sloping face, the two cones meeting point to point, and the two shafts at the designed angle with arrows for the direction of rotation. In a simple side view, a bevel gear appears as a trapezium with teeth on its slanted edge. Label the driver and the driven gear with tooth counts and write the ratio beside the drawing. For systems diagrams, the bevel pair is a single block labelled with its ratio and the angle through which it turns the axis.

Evaluating a bevel pair: fitness for purpose means the correct ratio and angle for the job; safety means guards over the teeth, which can trap fingers; cost is higher than for spur gears because cone teeth are harder to cut; ergonomics appear in whether the input handle is placed comfortably because of the turn. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing bevel gears with parallel shafts. Two: forgetting that the ratio rule is unchanged and inventing a new one for the angle. Three: drawing the teeth the same width along the cone. Four: assuming a bevel pair always gives a 1 to 1 ratio because the common examples are mitre gears.

The questions for this section are with you now: applications from the drill to the differential, how to draw a bevel pair, and evaluating one.

## Subtopic: Gears on Cones

A bevel gear has teeth on a cone instead of a cylinder. Put two cones point to point and they roll on each other. Cut teeth and you have gears whose shafts meet at an angle, almost always 90 degrees.

Teeth are wide at the outside, narrow toward the point. Equal pair: mitre gears. Unequal pair: pinion and crown wheel, like at the back of a bakkie.

Why bother: motion comes in one direction and is needed in another. Engine along the car, wheels across it. Handle vertical, beaters horizontal.

## Subtopic: Turning the Corner

Same arithmetic as spur gears. Ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth. 20 and 20: 1 to 1, same speed, same force, just turned round the corner.

10 drives 40: 4 to 1, output slower and four times stronger. That is the bakkie axle. 40 drives 10: 1 to 4, four times faster, a quarter of the force. That is the hand drill.

12 drives 36 at 1800: 600 out, three times the force. 32 drives 8 at 60: 240 out, a quarter of the force. Write ratio, speed, force, angle.

## Subtopic: Same Rules, New Angle

Hand drill, eggbeater, bakkie differential, angle grinder, Karoo windmill pump. All bevel pairs turning the axis 90 degrees.

Draw them as two cones point to point with teeth on the slopes, shafts at right angles, arrows for direction, tooth counts and the ratio written beside.
