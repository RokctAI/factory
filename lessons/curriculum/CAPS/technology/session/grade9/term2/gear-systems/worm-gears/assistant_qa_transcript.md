### subtopic_1 — The Worm and Wheel: A Screw That Drives a Gear
**Assistant:** Why can the wheel not turn the worm, when with spur gears either one can drive?

**Tutor:** It comes down to the angle of the thread. A spur gear tooth pushes on another tooth almost square-on, so a push from either side turns the other gear. A worm's thread is a very shallow slope wrapped round the shaft; when the wheel's tooth pushes on it, almost all of that push goes into pressing the tooth against the thread face, and only a tiny part acts along the thread to turn the worm. Friction between the surfaces is more than enough to cancel that tiny part, so the worm does not move. It is exactly why a screw holds a nut in place: the nut's load cannot unscrew it. If the thread were made steep, with several threads and a large lead angle, the wheel could drive the worm, and some high-efficiency worm drives are deliberately made that way, but the ordinary single-thread worm is self-locking.

### subtopic_2 — Large Speed Reduction and Force Increase in One Step
**Assistant:** If worm gears waste so much energy, why use them instead of a compound spur gear train?

**Tutor:** Because in many jobs the two things the worm gear does well matter more than efficiency. First, compactness: a 40 to 1 reduction from one small pair fits inside a gate motor housing or a guitar head, while a spur train of the same ratio needs several stages and more room. Second, self-locking: the load cannot run back, so no ratchet, brake or extra part is needed to hold a gate shut or a string in tune. If the mechanism runs occasionally and briefly, as a gate or a hoist does, wasting half the motor's energy for a few seconds costs almost nothing. For a conveyor running all day, or a vehicle drivetrain, efficiency matters and designers choose spur or helical gear trains, often with a separate brake. The choice is always the job first.

### subtopic_3 — Self-Locking, Applications and Evaluation
**Assistant:** How does an adjustable spanner use a worm when there is no wheel?

**Tutor:** The adjustable spanner uses a worm driving a rack rather than a wheel, which is the same idea with the wheel unrolled, exactly as a rack is a spur gear unrolled. The movable jaw has a row of teeth along its back, the knurled thumbwheel in the handle is a short worm, and turning the thumbwheel advances the jaw one tooth per turn. The shallow thread means that pushing on the jaw cannot turn the thumbwheel, so the jaw stays where it is set when you heave on the spanner, which is the self-locking property doing its job. The fine adjustment, many turns for a small jaw movement, is the large reduction. Two of this week's mechanisms, rack and worm, are combined in a tool found in almost every South African toolbox.
