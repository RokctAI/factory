# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises spur gears of equal and unequal size, the velocity and force relationships between them, and the idler gear. The anchor is a hand-cranked maize mill at a homestead near Giyani, the big handwheel turning a small gear on the grinding shaft so that one slow turn of the handle spins the stones several times, and a small idler between them so the stones turn the way the miller expects. The governing idea is that meshing spur gears turn in opposite directions, that the gear ratio equals driven teeth over driver teeth and sets the inverse relationship between speed and turning force, and that an idler gear between driver and driven reverses direction back again without altering the ratio. Calculating gear ratios and output speeds, stating the direction of rotation through a train, and explaining the role of an idler are examinable.

## Subtopic: Spur Gears Revisited: Teeth, Meshing and Direction

A spur gear is a wheel with straight teeth cut around its rim, parallel to its axle. Two spur gears mesh when their teeth interlock so that one turns the other; their axles are parallel and their centres are a fixed distance apart. Because the teeth of one push on the teeth of the other at the point where they meet, the two gears turn in opposite directions: clockwise drives anticlockwise. The gear that receives the input is the driver; the gear it turns is the driven gear or follower. Teeth must be the same size and shape on both gears to mesh without jamming, which is why gears in a set share a common tooth pitch.

Gears transmit motion with no slip, which belts and friction wheels cannot promise, and they do it between shafts that are close together. Gears are found in the maize mill at Giyani, in the hand drill, in the eggbeater, in the back of a wristwatch, in the gearbox of every car and truck, and in the wind-up toy. Their weakness is that they need lubrication, they are noisy if poorly cut, and they cannot span a distance; that is a job for a chain or a belt, as on a bicycle.

The relationship between two meshing gears is fixed by their teeth. As each tooth of the driver passes the meshing point, it moves one tooth of the driven gear past the same point. So in the time the driver turns through all its teeth, one full revolution, the driven gear turns through the same number of teeth, which is a full revolution only if it has the same number of teeth. Equal gears turn at equal speeds in opposite directions. Unequal gears turn at unequal speeds, and the next section puts numbers to it.

The questions for this section are with you now: how spur gears mesh and why they turn in opposite directions, where they are used, and why tooth count governs the relationship.

## Subtopic: Gear Ratio, Speed and Force in Equal and Unequal Gears

The gear ratio is the number of teeth on the driven gear divided by the number of teeth on the driver. A 10-tooth driver turning a 30-tooth driven gear gives a ratio of 30 over 10, which is 3 to 1: the driver turns three times for every one turn of the driven gear. The output speed is the input speed divided by the ratio: 60 revolutions per minute in gives 20 out. Since the energy passing through is the same, apart from friction, the turning force at the output is three times the turning force at the input. A small gear driving a large gear slows the output and increases its turning force. This is the arrangement for lifting or grinding.

Reverse the arrangement: a 30-tooth driver turning a 10-tooth driven gear gives a ratio of 10 over 30, which is 1 to 3. The output turns three times as fast as the input and its turning force is a third. This is the arrangement for speed, used in the maize mill where a slow handle must spin the stones fast, in a hand drill, and in an eggbeater. The rule of the term applies again: speed and force are traded, never both gained. Work in equals work out.

Worked examples. A 12-tooth driver and a 36-tooth driven gear: ratio 3 to 1, output a third of the input speed, three times the turning force. A 40-tooth driver and an 8-tooth driven gear: ratio 1 to 5, output five times the speed, a fifth of the force. Equal gears of 20 teeth each: ratio 1 to 1, same speed, same force, direction reversed. Always write the ratio as driven over driver, state whether speed goes up or down, and say what happens to force. Mechanical advantage of a gear pair equals the ratio when the small gear drives the large.

The questions for this section are with you now: the gear ratio formula, how speed and turning force change for small driving large and large driving small, and worked calculations.

## Subtopic: Idler Gears and Compound Trains

An idler is a gear placed between the driver and the driven gear. It takes the drive from one and passes it to the other. Its effect on direction: the driver turns the idler the opposite way, and the idler turns the driven gear the opposite way again, so the driven gear turns the same way as the driver. Its effect on ratio: none. The idler's teeth cancel out of the calculation, because the ratio from driver to idler and from idler to driven multiply to give driven teeth over driver teeth exactly as if the idler were not there. An idler changes direction and bridges a gap; it does not change speed or force.

The maize mill uses an idler so that the stones turn the way the miller expects when the handle is turned the natural way. A car's reverse gear is an idler slipped between two gears that normally mesh through another path, which is why reverse turns the wheels the other way. An idler also lets a designer place the output shaft further from the input than two gears alone would allow, and lets the same gear ratio be delivered with a different direction.

A compound gear train is different. There, two gears of different sizes are fixed on the same shaft, so one is driven by the previous stage and the other drives the next. In a compound train the ratios of each stage multiply, so a 3 to 1 stage followed by a 4 to 1 stage gives 12 to 1 in a small space, which is how a wind-up clock or a geared winch gets a large reduction. The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing the ratio as driver over driven. Two: believing the idler changes the ratio because it has its own tooth count. Three: expecting a gear train to increase both speed and force. Four: drawing two meshing gears turning the same way.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an idler does to direction and ratio, where idlers are used, and how a compound train differs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the maize mill near Giyani and a set of cardboard gears — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Toothed Wheels That Push Each Other

A spur gear is a wheel with straight teeth. Two of them mesh so that one pushes the other round. The one you turn is the driver; the one it turns is the driven gear. They turn opposite ways.

Gears never slip, which belts can. Mills, drills, eggbeaters, watches, gearboxes, toys.

Each tooth of the driver moves one tooth of the driven gear. Same teeth, same speed. Different teeth, different speed.

Keep toothed wheels that push each other in mind and try the questions for this part: how spur gears mesh.

## Subtopic: Count the Teeth

Gear ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth. 10 drives 30: ratio 3 to 1. The driver turns three times for one turn of the driven gear. Output is slower and its turning force is three times bigger.

30 drives 10: ratio 1 to 3. Output is three times faster, with a third of the turning force. The mill does this to spin the stones fast.

Speed and force trade. Never both. 12 drives 36: 3 to 1, slower and stronger. 40 drives 8: 1 to 5, faster and weaker. 20 and 20: same speed, opposite way.

Hold count the teeth in mind and try this part's questions: ratios.

## Subtopic: The Middle Gear Changes Nothing but Direction

An idler is a gear in the middle. Driver turns it one way, it turns the driven gear back the other way, so the driven gear turns the same way as the driver. The ratio does not change; the idler's teeth cancel out.

The mill uses one so the stones turn the right way. A car's reverse gear is an idler. Idlers also bridge a gap.

Compound train: two gears on one shaft. Then the ratios multiply: 3 to 1 then 4 to 1 makes 12 to 1. That is how a clock or a winch gets a big reduction.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Meshing spur gears turn in opposite directions; the gear ratio is driven teeth over driver teeth. A small gear driving a large one slows the output and increases its turning force; a large gear driving a small one speeds it up and reduces force; speed and force always trade. An idler between driver and driven reverses direction back again and leaves the ratio unchanged. A compound train multiplies the ratios of its stages.
