# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the worm gear as a means of large speed reduction and force increase. The anchor is a guitar in a music classroom in Mthatha, the learner turning the tuning peg many times while the string post creeps round slowly, the string tightening by tiny amounts and holding its pitch when the hand lets go. The governing idea is that a worm is a single-thread screw meshing with a toothed wheel on a shaft at right angles, that each turn of the worm advances the wheel by one tooth so the ratio equals the number of wheel teeth, and that the shallow thread angle means the wheel cannot drive the worm, making the system self-locking. Explaining the worm gear's working, calculating its ratio and output, and identifying applications and the self-locking property are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Worm and Wheel: A Screw That Drives a Gear

A worm is a shaft with a thread cut round it like a screw, usually a single continuous thread. A worm wheel is a gear with curved teeth shaped to fit that thread. The two mesh with their shafts at right angles, the worm lying across the top or side of the wheel. When the worm turns once, its thread advances by one pitch along its length, and because the wheel's teeth sit in the thread, the wheel is pushed round by exactly one tooth. The worm acts like a screw whose nut has been unrolled into a wheel; turn the screw and the nut, here the wheel, moves one thread-width per turn.

The direction of rotation of the wheel depends on the direction of the worm's thread, left-handed or right-handed, and on which way the worm turns. The worm always drives the wheel; because the thread angle is shallow, the wheel pressing on the thread produces mostly friction rather than rotation of the worm, so the wheel cannot normally turn the worm. This is unlike every other gear pair in the course, where either gear can be the driver.

The worm gear is compact for what it does. Where a spur gear reduction of 40 to 1 would need a 10-tooth gear driving a 400-tooth gear, or a compound train of several stages, a worm with a 40-tooth wheel does it in one pair that fits in a small housing. This compactness, with the self-locking property, is why the worm gear sits inside so many small motors and hand-cranked mechanisms.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a worm and worm wheel are, how one turn of the worm advances one tooth, and why the wheel cannot drive the worm.

## Subtopic: Large Speed Reduction and Force Increase in One Step

The ratio of a worm gear with a single-thread worm equals the number of teeth on the wheel, because the worm behaves as a gear with one tooth. A 40-tooth wheel gives 40 to 1: the worm turns 40 times for one turn of the wheel. The output speed is the input speed divided by the ratio: a motor at 1200 revolutions per minute turning the worm gives 30 revolutions per minute at the wheel. The turning force at the wheel is about 40 times that at the worm, less a considerable loss to friction, because the sliding of the thread against the teeth wastes more energy than the rolling of spur gear teeth; worm gears are often only 50 to 90 percent efficient.

Worked examples. A 30-tooth wheel driven by a single worm at 600 revolutions per minute: ratio 30 to 1, output 20 revolutions per minute, about 30 times the turning force. A 60-tooth wheel driven at 1800: ratio 60 to 1, output 30 revolutions per minute, about 60 times the force. A guitar tuning peg with a 14-tooth wheel: 14 turns of the peg for one turn of the string post, which is why tuning is fine and slow. Some worms have two threads, which advance two teeth per turn and halve the ratio; the examination level of this course uses single threads.

The trade-off is as always. A 40-fold force increase costs 40 turns of input for one turn of output; the hand on the crank travels a long way, slowly, so that the load moves a short way with great force. Work in equals work out less friction, and with worm gears the friction loss is large enough that it must be mentioned in any honest evaluation.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ratio rule for a worm gear, output speed and force calculations, and the trade-off with friction.

## Subtopic: Self-Locking, Applications and Evaluation

Self-locking is the worm gear's second gift. Because the wheel cannot drive the worm, a load on the wheel stays where it is when the input stops, with no ratchet or brake. A guitar string stays in tune; a winch drum stays wound; a sliding gate stays shut against a push, which is why most domestic gate motors contain a worm reduction; a hand-cranked workshop hoist holds its load with the handle untouched; a car's window winder stays where it is left, as does an adjustable spanner's jaw, which is driven by a worm against a rack-like row of teeth. Tuning pegs, winches, gate motors, hoists, window winders, conveyor drives and the slow-turning mechanisms of rotisseries all use worm gears.

Drawing a worm gear: show the wheel in side view as a toothed circle, and the worm as a cylinder lying across its top with diagonal lines for the thread, the two shafts at right angles. Label worm, worm wheel, tooth count and ratio, and add an arrow showing which way the wheel turns for a given worm direction. In systems diagrams, the worm gear is one block labelled with its ratio and the note that it is self-locking.

Evaluating a worm gear under the headings. Fitness for purpose: a large reduction and holding ability in a small space, so excellent for winches and gates; poor where efficiency or two-way drive is needed. Safety: self-locking is a safety feature, since a dropped handle does not spin, but the worm and wheel generate heat and must be lubricated, and exposed worms are a finger hazard. Cost: moderate; bronze wheels and steel worms are usual. Ergonomics: many turns of the input for a small output movement, fine for tuning, slow for lifting. The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking either gear can drive the other. Two: taking the ratio as wheel teeth over worm diameter instead of wheel teeth over worm threads. Three: ignoring the large friction loss. Four: expecting a worm gear to be fast as well as strong.

The questions for this section are with you now: self-locking and its uses, drawing the worm gear, and evaluating it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the guitar peg in Mthatha and a gate motor opened up — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Screw Turning a Wheel

A worm is a shaft with a screw thread. A worm wheel is a gear that fits the thread. Shafts at right angles. Turn the worm once and the thread pushes the wheel round one tooth.

The worm drives the wheel. The wheel cannot drive the worm; the shallow thread just rubs. That is different from every other gear.

It is small for what it does. A 40 to 1 reduction in one little pair instead of a huge gear or a stack of gears.

Keep a screw turning a wheel in mind and try the questions for this part: how a worm gear works.

## Subtopic: One Turn, One Tooth

Ratio equals the wheel's teeth. 40 teeth: 40 to 1. Motor at 1200 turning the worm: 30 at the wheel. Turning force about 40 times bigger, minus a lot of friction.

30 teeth at 600: 20 out, 30 times the force. 60 teeth at 1800: 30 out, 60 times the force. Guitar peg with 14 teeth: 14 turns of the peg for one of the post.

Pay with turns: 40 turns in for 1 out. Slow and strong. Friction eats a chunk; worm gears can lose half the energy.

## Subtopic: Slow, Strong and Stays Put

Because the wheel cannot turn the worm, the load stays put when you stop. Guitar stays in tune. Winch stays wound. Gate stays shut. Window stays up. No ratchet needed.

Draw it: a toothed circle, a cylinder across the top with slanted lines for the thread, shafts at right angles, label and ratio.

Judge it: huge reduction and holds itself, small, but slow, loses energy to friction, gets hot, needs grease, and the worm can catch fingers.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A worm gear is a screw-like worm driving a toothed wheel on a shaft at right angles; one turn of the worm advances the wheel one tooth, so the ratio equals the wheel's tooth count, giving a large speed reduction and force increase in one compact pair. The wheel cannot drive the worm, so the system is self-locking and holds its load. It is used in tuning pegs, winches, gate motors and hoists, and its weakness is high friction loss and slowness.
