# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the rack and pinion, with automatic gates and steering racks as the main examples. The anchor is the sliding gate at the entrance to a townhouse complex in Roodepoort, a motor in a steel box beside the driveway turning a small gear that meshes with a long toothed rack bolted to the bottom of the gate, so that the gate glides open along its track when the remote is pressed. The governing idea is that a rack is a gear unrolled into a straight bar, that a pinion meshing with it converts rotary motion into linear motion or the reverse, and that the distance the rack moves for one turn of the pinion equals the number of pinion teeth times the tooth spacing. Describing the rack and pinion, giving its applications, calculating rack travel and evaluating the mechanism are examinable.

## Subtopic: Rack and Pinion: Turning Rotation Into Straight-Line Motion

A rack is a straight bar with gear teeth cut along one edge; it is a gear whose radius has become infinite, so its rim is a straight line. A pinion is a small spur gear that meshes with it. The two share a tooth size, exactly as two spur gears must. When the pinion is turned, its teeth push the rack's teeth one after another and the rack slides along its length; one tooth of the rack passes for every tooth of the pinion that passes the meshing point. Turn the pinion the other way and the rack slides back. The rack and pinion is therefore a converter between rotary motion and linear motion, and it works in both directions.

Which part moves depends on which is fixed. If the rack is fixed to the ground or a frame and the pinion is carried on a motor, the motor and whatever it is mounted on travel along the rack, as in some rack railways and in the carriage of a large machine. If the pinion is fixed in place on a motor and the rack is free to slide, the rack travels, as in the sliding gate, where the rack is bolted to the gate and the motor sits still beside the drive. In the car steering rack, the pinion turns in a fixed housing and the rack slides sideways, pushing rods to the wheels.

Unlike the pulley systems or the hydraulic jack, the rack and pinion does not change the amount of force in any dramatic way; its ratio is set by the pinion's size and is usually modest. Its value is the change of motion type, from round and round to back and forth, with no slip and a fixed relationship, so that a given angle of pinion gives a definite distance of rack. That precision is why it steers cars and positions machine tools.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a rack and a pinion are, how rotary motion becomes linear and back, and which part moves.

## Subtopic: Automatic Gates and Steering Racks

The automatic sliding gate is the most visible rack and pinion in South African suburbs. A long steel or nylon rack runs along the bottom rail of the gate. A gearbox in the motor housing drives a pinion that meshes with the rack. When the remote signals the motor, the pinion turns, the rack and gate slide along their track until a limit switch stops the motor at the open position. Reverse the motor and the gate closes. A safety edge or infrared beam stops and reverses the gate if something is in its path. A manual release disengages the pinion so the gate can be pushed by hand during load shedding, which is a design requirement every gate installer in the country has learnt to meet.

The steering rack in almost every modern car is a rack and pinion. The steering wheel turns a column with a pinion at its lower end. The pinion meshes with a rack lying across the car behind the front wheels. Turning the wheel slides the rack left or right; tie rods from each end of the rack push or pull the steering arms on the wheels, turning them. The pinion is small, so several turns of the steering wheel move the rack only a few centimetres, which gives the driver fine control and a modest effort, helped on most cars by hydraulic or electric power assistance acting on the rack.

Other uses: the drill head on a pillar drill, lowered by a handle through a rack and pinion; the focusing mechanism on a microscope, where a knob's pinion moves the tube up and down a rack; the opening mechanism of many sluice gates on irrigation canals; the rack railway that climbs steep slopes by a pinion under the locomotive engaging a rack between the rails; lifting platforms in workshops; and the sliding doors of some buses. Each needs a controlled straight movement from a turning input.

## Subtopic: Calculating Rack Travel and Evaluating the Mechanism

Calculating rack travel is a matter of counting teeth. Each turn of the pinion moves the rack by one tooth spacing for each pinion tooth. If the pinion has 12 teeth and the teeth are spaced 10 millimetres apart along the rack, one turn moves the rack 12 times 10, which is 120 millimetres. Three turns move it 360 millimetres. Half a turn moves it 60. To find how many turns are needed for a given travel, divide: a 4 metre gate, which is 4000 millimetres, needs 4000 over 120, about 33 turns of that pinion. A larger pinion moves the rack further per turn but needs more turning force; a smaller one moves it less and needs less.

The same calculation works backwards for steering. If the rack moves 60 millimetres from straight ahead to full lock and the pinion moves it 40 millimetres per turn, then one and a half turns of the steering wheel reach full lock. Changing the pinion size changes this steering ratio: a smaller pinion means more turns, finer control and less effort; a larger pinion means fewer turns and quicker steering but more effort, which is why sports cars and trucks feel different.

Evaluating a rack and pinion. Fitness for purpose: it must give the required travel with no slip and little backlash, the small free play when direction reverses. Safety: exposed teeth must be guarded, a gate must stop for obstructions, a steering rack must never jam. Ergonomics: the steering ratio or gate speed must suit the user. Cost: nylon racks for gates are cheap; hardened steel steering racks are not. Maintenance: racks collect grit and must be cleaned and greased. The error museum, four exhibits. One: multiplying pinion teeth by the number of rack teeth instead of by tooth spacing. Two: forgetting that the rack and pinion works both ways. Three: thinking a bigger pinion gives more force; it gives more travel and needs more force. Four: designing a gate with no manual release.

The questions for this section are with you now: calculating rack travel per turn and turns for a distance, steering ratio, and evaluating a rack and pinion.

## Subtopic: A Gear and a Flat Gear

A rack is a gear flattened into a straight bar of teeth. A pinion is a small round gear that meshes with it. Turn the pinion and the rack slides. Push the rack and the pinion turns. Round and round becomes back and forth, both ways.

Fix the rack and the pinion travels, like a rack railway. Fix the pinion and the rack travels, like the gate and the steering rack.

It does not multiply force much. Its job is to change the kind of motion, precisely, with no slip.

## Subtopic: Round and Round Becomes Back and Forth

Sliding gate: rack along the bottom of the gate, motor with a pinion beside the driveway. Press the remote, the pinion turns, the gate slides until a limit switch stops it. Beam or safety edge stops it if something is in the way. Manual release for load shedding.

Steering: the steering wheel turns a pinion; the pinion slides a rack across the car; rods from the rack turn the front wheels. Small pinion, many turns, fine control, light effort.

Also: pillar drill handle, microscope focus knob, sluice gates, rack railways, bus doors.

## Subtopic: One Turn, One Tooth Length Each

One turn of the pinion moves the rack one tooth spacing per pinion tooth. 12 teeth, 10 millimetres apart: 120 millimetres per turn. A 4 metre gate: 4000 over 120, about 33 turns.

Steering the same way backwards: 60 millimetres to full lock, 40 per turn, one and a half turns. Smaller pinion, more turns, lighter. Bigger pinion, fewer turns, heavier.

Judge it: full travel, no slip, little free play, guards on the teeth, a gate that stops for obstacles, a release for power cuts, grit cleaned off.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A rack and pinion converts rotary motion to linear motion and back with no slip. A rack is a straight bar of teeth; a pinion is a small gear meshing with it. The rack moves one tooth spacing per pinion tooth per turn, so travel per turn is pinion teeth times tooth spacing. Sliding gates, steering racks, pillar drills and rack railways use it. A bigger pinion gives more travel per turn but needs more force.
