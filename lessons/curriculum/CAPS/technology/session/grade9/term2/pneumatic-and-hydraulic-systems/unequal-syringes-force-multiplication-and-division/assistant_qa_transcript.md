### subtopic_1 — Small Master, Large Slave: Multiplying Force
**Assistant:** Where does the extra force come from? It feels like getting something for nothing.

**Tutor:** It feels that way, and it is not, because what you gain in force you pay for in distance, and the thing that is conserved is the work done, which is force multiplied by distance. When you push the small master in by 20 millimetres with 2 newtons, you do a certain amount of work: 2 newtons times 0.02 metres, which is 0.04 joules. The large slave then pushes with 5 newtons but moves only 8 millimetres: 5 newtons times 0.008 metres, which is also 0.04 joules. The energy you put in equals the energy that comes out, minus a little lost to friction warming the seals. The water did not create force; it redistributed it. Your small push, acting on a small area, created a pressure, and that pressure acted on a large area to produce a large total force, but only by moving a small volume into a space where it did not go far. The same trade appears in every machine: a long crowbar lifts a slab with a small push on the handle, but the handle moves a long way while the slab lifts a little; a low gear on a bicycle lets you climb a hill with easy pedalling, but you pedal many turns for each turn of the wheel. Hydraulics is especially good at the trade because the ratio can be very large, a hand pump lifting a truck, and because the force can be sent through a flexible hose. But a jack handle pumped forty times to raise a car four centimetres is the price of the forty-fold advantage, and nothing in the system escapes that arithmetic.

### subtopic_2 — Large Master, Small Slave: Multiplying Distance
**Assistant:** Does the length of the tube between the syringes change the force?

**Tutor:** Not the force, in an ideal system, and only a little in a real one. The force at the slave depends on the pressure in the fluid and the area of the slave plunger, and the pressure, as the next session on Pascal's principle will show, is the same everywhere in a connected liquid at rest, whether the tube is 10 centimetres long or 10 metres. So a small master driving a large slave multiplies force by the area ratio however long and however bent the tube is, which is exactly why hydraulic systems are useful: the brake pedal is a long way from the rear wheels, and the pressure arrives undiminished. Two real effects do creep in with a long tube. The first is friction between the moving liquid and the tube wall, which matters when the fluid is moving fast and is why hydraulic lines are kept reasonably wide and smooth; for a syringe system pushed slowly it is negligible. The second is the tube itself stretching: a soft plastic tube bulges slightly under pressure, taking up some of the volume you pushed, so the slave moves a little less than the area ratio predicts, and a very long soft tube makes the system feel slightly spongy even with no air in it, which is why real hydraulic hoses are reinforced with braided steel or fabric. In the classroom investigation you can test this directly by repeating the measurements with a tube twice as long: the forces should be the same within the accuracy of a spring balance, and any difference in distance is the tube bulging. It is a good extension because it separates the real variable, the area ratio, from a variable that only looks as if it should matter.

### subtopic_3 — Measuring the Trade-Off Between Force and Distance
**Assistant:** Why do the 5 and 20 millilitre syringes give a ratio of about 2.5 and not 4?

**Tutor:** Because the ratio that matters is the ratio of the plunger areas, and the millilitre markings tell you the volume, which is area multiplied by length. Syringe makers do not simply make a 20 millilitre syringe four times as wide as a 5 millilitre one; they make it somewhat wider and somewhat longer, so that it is comfortable to hold and to push. A typical 5 millilitre syringe has an internal diameter of about 12 millimetres, giving a plunger area of roughly 1.1 square centimetres, and a typical 20 millilitre syringe has a diameter of about 20 millimetres, giving about 3.1 square centimetres; the area ratio is therefore about 2.8, and allowing for friction you measure something in the range 2 to 2.5. The volume ratio of 4 is made up of that area ratio and a length ratio of about 1.4. This matters because it is a common trap in the investigation: a learner who reasons from the printed volumes predicts a force ratio of 4, measures 2.5, and concludes the experiment went wrong, when in fact the experiment is right and the prediction used the wrong quantity. The lesson is one that recurs throughout hydraulics: pressure acts on area, so it is always area you must compare. The way to get the true ratio is to measure the internal diameters with a ruler or callipers, calculate the areas using pi times the radius squared, and predict from those; the agreement with measurement is then close and the small shortfall is honestly friction. It also previews the hydraulic press calculations of the next sessions, where the areas of the two pistons are given directly and the force ratio follows from them.
