# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces pneumatic and hydraulic systems through force transfer between two equal syringes filled first with compressed air and then with water. The anchor is a bench in a Technology room in Klerksdorp with two 20 millilitre syringes joined by a length of clear plastic tubing, a tray of water, a ruler and a set of masses. The governing idea is that a fluid confined in a sealed system passes a push from one end to the other, that with equal syringes the force and the distance at the output equal those at the input, and that air and water behave differently because air compresses and water does not. Describing the transfer, distinguishing pneumatic from hydraulic and carrying out the investigation fairly are examinable.

## Subtopic: Two Syringes, a Tube and a Push

A syringe is a cylinder with a plunger that slides inside it and a nozzle at the end. Push the plunger in and whatever is in the cylinder is forced out of the nozzle; pull it and the cylinder fills. Join two syringes nozzle to nozzle with a tube and the system is sealed: whatever leaves one syringe must enter the other. Push the plunger of the first, which we call the master, and the plunger of the second, the slave, moves out, because the fluid has nowhere else to go. Release and nothing moves back unless you pull the master or push the slave.

What travels along the tube is fluid, and with it a push. The push you apply to the master plunger raises the pressure of the fluid; that pressure acts on the slave plunger and pushes it out against whatever resists it, a finger or a mass. In this way a force applied in one place is transferred to another place through a tube that can bend round corners, which is the point of all fluid power: force can be sent where a rigid rod or lever could not reach, such as to the brake pads of each wheel of a car or to the jaws of a rescue cutter.

With two syringes of the same size the transfer is equal. The same volume of fluid that leaves the master enters the slave, and because the cylinders have the same cross-section, the slave plunger moves out by the same distance that the master plunger moved in. The force is also the same: a push of 10 newtons on the master produces a push of 10 newtons at the slave, minus a little lost to friction in the seals. Equal syringes transfer force and distance unchanged; they are a way of moving a push, not of multiplying it.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a sealed two-syringe system does when the master is pushed, what travels along the tube, and the force and distance relationship for equal syringes.

## Subtopic: Pneumatic Versus Hydraulic: Air or Water in the Tube

A system filled with air, or any gas, is pneumatic. A system filled with water, oil or any liquid is hydraulic. Both transfer force through a tube, but they feel completely different in the hand, and the difference comes from one property: gases compress, liquids do not. Push the master plunger of an air-filled system and at first the slave barely moves; the air in the tube and cylinders squashes into a smaller volume, the plunger feels spongy, and only when the air is compressed enough does the pressure rise far enough to move the slave. Let go and the compressed air springs back, pushing the master out again. The system behaves like a spring in series with the push.

Fill the same system with water, taking care to remove every bubble, and the feel changes. Push the master and the slave moves at once, by the same amount, with a solid, direct feel; the water does not squash, so every millimetre of travel at the master becomes a millimetre of travel at the slave immediately. Let go and nothing springs back. This is why hydraulic systems are used where precise, firm control of force is needed, car brakes, excavator arms, the hydraulic jack, and pneumatic systems where a softer, springy action or a very fast movement is acceptable, air tools, bus doors, dentist's chairs.

Both have a second difference in what happens when the system leaks. A leaking hydraulic system drips liquid and loses pressure slowly and visibly; a leaking pneumatic system hisses and loses its push quickly but leaves no mess. Compressed air also stores energy, so a burst pneumatic hose whips dangerously, while a burst hydraulic hose sprays fluid under pressure, which is its own hazard. Both systems need good seals, which in a syringe is the rubber plunger tip.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of pneumatic and hydraulic, why the air-filled system feels spongy, and where each kind of system is used.

## Subtopic: Investigating Force Transfer Fairly

The investigation asks: how does the force at the slave compare with the force at the master when the two syringes are equal, and does it matter whether the fluid is air or water? A fair test changes one thing at a time. Set up the two syringes with the tube, mark the plungers, and for the air test leave them dry. Press the master in by a measured distance, 20 millimetres, and measure how far the slave moves out; record both. Then measure force: stand the slave upright with a mass on its plunger, press the master until the slave just lifts the mass, and note the force needed, using a spring balance on the master if one is available or comparing by feel against a second mass if not. Repeat with masses of 100, 200 and 500 grams.

Then repeat exactly the same steps with the system filled with water. To fill, draw water into both syringes with the tube submerged, connect under water, and squeeze out all bubbles, because a trapped bubble makes the hydraulic system behave partly like a pneumatic one. Record distance moved and force needed for the same masses. Keep everything else the same: the same syringes, the same tube length, the same distances pressed, the same masses.

The expected results: with water, the slave moves the same distance as the master and lifts each mass with about the same force applied to the master, immediately; with air, the slave moves less than the master until the air is compressed, the push feels spongy, and more master travel is needed before the mass lifts. The conclusion in words: equal syringes transfer force and distance equally, with a hydraulic system doing so directly and a pneumatic system only after the air has been compressed. The error museum, four exhibits. One: leaving bubbles in the water system and concluding that water compresses. Two: changing the masses and the fluid at the same time, so the two effects cannot be separated. Three: pressing the master a different distance each time. Four: ignoring friction and expecting the slave force to be exactly equal; it is slightly less, and an honest table says so.

The questions for this section are with you now: the variables changed, measured and kept the same, how the hydraulic system is filled, and the expected results and conclusion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two syringes and a piece of clear tube in your hands — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Push One End, the Other End Moves

Join two syringes with a tube. Push one plunger in. The other plunger comes out. The fluid inside has to go somewhere, so it goes to the other syringe and pushes.

What travels down the tube? The fluid, and the push with it. That is how a force can be sent round a corner, to a car's brakes or a cutter's jaws.

If the syringes are the same size, the other plunger moves the same distance and pushes with the same force. Nothing gained, nothing lost, except a little to friction. Equal syringes move a push; they do not multiply it.

Keep the two plungers in mind and try the questions for this part: push one end, the other end moves.

## Subtopic: Spongy Air, Solid Water

Air in the tube: pneumatic. Water or oil in the tube: hydraulic.

Push the air one and it feels spongy. The air squashes first, then the other plunger moves. Let go and it springs back. Like pushing a spring.

Push the water one and the other plunger moves at once, the same amount, solid. Water does not squash. Let go and nothing springs back.

So brakes, jacks and excavator arms use liquid, for firm exact control. Air tools and bus doors use air, for quick springy action. A leaking liquid system drips; a leaking air system hisses.

Hold spongy air and solid water in mind and try this part's questions: air or water in the tube.

## Subtopic: Doing the Test Properly

The test: does the slave push as hard as the master, and does air or water matter? Change one thing at a time.

Dry syringes first. Push the master in 20 millimetres, measure how far the slave moves. Put a mass on the slave, push until it lifts, note the force. Try 100, 200, 500 grams.

Then fill with water under the surface so there are no bubbles. Same pushes, same masses, same tube. Record again.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Two equal syringes joined by a tube pass a push from one to the other with the same force and the same distance. Air-filled is pneumatic and feels spongy because gas compresses; water-filled is hydraulic and feels solid because liquid does not. The fair test changes one thing at a time, removes bubbles, and records that the slave force is nearly equal, a little lost to friction.

Hold the bubble-free tube and the table of results, and take the final questions of the lesson: doing the test properly.
