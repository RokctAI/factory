# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers force multiplication and force division between unequal syringes in pneumatic and hydraulic systems. The anchor is the same bench in Klerksdorp, now with a 5 millilitre syringe joined to a 20 millilitre syringe by the water-filled tube, a 500 gram mass balanced on the large plunger, and a learner surprised at how little effort lifts it. The governing idea is that a small cylinder driving a large one multiplies force in proportion to the ratio of their areas while dividing the distance moved by the same ratio, that reversing the arrangement multiplies distance and divides force, and that this trade-off is the hydraulic form of mechanical advantage. Predicting which arrangement gives a mechanical advantage above or below 1 and carrying out the investigation are examinable.

## Subtopic: Small Master, Large Slave: Multiplying Force

Take a small syringe as master and a large one as slave, joined by a water-filled tube. Push the small plunger in with a light force and the large plunger moves out slowly but pushes hard: a 500 gram mass that was difficult to lift directly with the small syringe alone rises with a finger's pressure. The reason is in the areas. The push on the small plunger creates a pressure in the water; that same pressure acts over the whole face of the large plunger, which has a much greater area, so the total force on the large plunger is greater in the same proportion as the area is greater.

If the large plunger has four times the area of the small one, the output force is four times the input force. A 5 millilitre syringe has a plunger of about 1.2 square centimetres and a 20 millilitre syringe about 3.0 square centimetres, a ratio of about 2.5, so a push of 2 newtons on the small master gives about 5 newtons at the large slave, minus friction. This is force multiplication, and the mechanical advantage, output force divided by input force, is greater than 1. It is exactly what the hydraulic jack does when a person lifts a car, and what the rescue spreader does when a modest pump produces a crushing force at the jaws.

The price is distance. The volume of water that leaves the small syringe equals the volume that enters the large one, and since the large plunger has a bigger area, the same volume moves it a shorter distance. Push the small master in 20 millimetres and the large slave moves out about 8 millimetres, in the ratio 1 to 2.5. More force, less movement; the small master must travel a long way to lift the load a short way. This is why a jack handle is pumped many times to lift a car a few centimetres.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a small master driving a large slave multiplies force, how the multiplication depends on the areas, and what happens to the distance.

## Subtopic: Large Master, Small Slave: Multiplying Distance

Now reverse the arrangement: the large syringe is the master and the small one is the slave. Push the large plunger in and the small plunger shoots out, moving much further than the master moved; push the master 8 millimetres and the slave moves about 20. The same volume of water that left the large cylinder fills a long narrow space in the small one. This is distance multiplication, useful where a short, powerful movement must be turned into a long one: the hydraulic arm of a digger uses the reverse trick in places where reach matters more than force.

The price now is force. The pressure in the water is set by the push on the large master divided by its large area, so it is low, and that low pressure acting on the small slave's small area gives a small force. A push of 5 newtons on the large master gives only about 2 newtons at the small slave. The mechanical advantage is less than 1. It feels, in the hand, like trying to lift a mass with the small syringe when you can only push the large one: the mass barely moves even though the small plunger is travelling quickly.

The two arrangements are the same system seen from opposite ends, and the rule is symmetrical: whichever plunger has the larger area carries the larger force and moves the shorter distance; whichever has the smaller area carries the smaller force and moves the longer distance. Choose which end is the master and you choose whether to multiply force or distance. It is the same choice a lever offers when the pivot is moved, and the same one a gear pair offers when the small gear drives the large or the large drives the small.

The questions for this section are with you now: what happens when a large master drives a small slave, why the force falls, and how the two arrangements are related.

## Subtopic: Measuring the Trade-Off Between Force and Distance

The investigation measures the trade-off. Set up the small master and large slave with water and no bubbles. Mark the plungers. Push the master in a measured 20 millimetres and record how far the slave moves, several times, averaging. Then place masses on the slave, 200, 500 and 1 000 grams, and for each find the force needed on the master to lift it, using a spring balance pressed against the master plunger, or a second syringe and masses as a comparison. Record input force, output force and the ratio of output to input. Then swap the syringes and repeat with the large master and small slave for the same pushes and masses.

Expected results for a 5 and 20 millilitre pair: small master in 20 millimetres gives large slave out about 8; a 500 gram mass, about 5 newtons, lifts with about 2 newtons on the master plus friction, so the ratio is around 2 to 2.5 rather than the ideal 2.5. Large master in 20 millimetres gives small slave out about 50; a 200 gram mass, 2 newtons, on the small slave needs about 5 newtons on the large master, ratio about 0.4. The product of force and distance is about the same at both ends: a small force times a long distance at the small end equals a large force times a short distance at the large end, which is the energy going in equalling the energy coming out, less friction.

The conclusion in words: unequal syringes change force and distance in opposite directions by the ratio of their areas, and a hydraulic system can multiply force only by dividing distance. The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the large syringe is simply stronger; it is the ratio that matters, and the same large syringe divides force when it is the master. Two: expecting more force and more distance together; the product is fixed. Three: comparing volumes, 5 and 20, as if they were the area ratio; the area ratio of these syringes is about 2.5, not 4, because the large syringe is also longer. Four: forgetting friction and reporting the ideal ratio as the measured one.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the trade-off is measured, the expected ratios, and why force times distance is roughly the same at both ends.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a small syringe, a big syringe and a mass that should be too heavy — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Push Small, Lift Big

Small master, big slave. Push the small plunger gently. The big plunger pushes hard and lifts the mass you could not lift directly.

Why? Your push makes a pressure in the water. That pressure presses on the whole face of the big plunger. Bigger face, bigger total force. If the big face is 2.5 times the area, the force is 2.5 times bigger.

The catch: the big plunger moves less. Push the small one in 20 millimetres and the big one comes out about 8. More force, less travel. That is why a jack handle is pumped many times.

Keep push small, lift big in mind and try the questions for this part: force multiplication.

## Subtopic: Push Big, Move Far

Swap them. Big master, small slave. Push the big plunger in 8 millimetres and the small one shoots out 20.

Now the force is smaller. Your push is spread over the big face, so the pressure is low, and the small face gets only a small force. Lots of travel, little force. Mechanical advantage less than 1.

Same system, opposite ends. The bigger face always has the bigger force and the shorter travel. The smaller face has the smaller force and the longer travel. Choose which end you push and you choose force or distance. Just like moving a lever's pivot.

Hold push big, move far in mind and try this part's questions: force division.

## Subtopic: You Cannot Have Both

Measure it. Small master, big slave, no bubbles. Push 20 millimetres, measure the slave. Put 200, 500, 1 000 grams on the slave, find the push needed each time. Then swap and do it again.

You should find about 2.5 times the force and about a 2.5 times shorter travel one way, and the reverse the other way, a bit less because of friction. Force times distance comes out about the same at both ends.

Rule: a hydraulic system multiplies force only by dividing distance. You cannot have both.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A small master driving a large slave multiplies force by the ratio of the plunger areas and divides distance by the same ratio, mechanical advantage above 1. A large master driving a small slave does the opposite, mechanical advantage below 1. Force times distance stays about the same at both ends, less friction. This trade-off is the hydraulic form of mechanical advantage.

Hold force times distance, and take the final questions of the lesson: you cannot have both.
