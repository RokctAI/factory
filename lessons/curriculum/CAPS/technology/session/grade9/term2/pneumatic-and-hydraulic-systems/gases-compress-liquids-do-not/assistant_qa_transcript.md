### subtopic_1 — Particles Far Apart, Particles Touching
**Assistant:** If liquids do not compress, how does a liquid fit into a smaller syringe when I push it through?

**Tutor:** It does not fit into a smaller space; it moves into a different space of the same size, and the distinction is the whole point. When you push the master plunger in by 20 millimetres, the volume you sweep out, say 2 millilitres, does not shrink; it flows along the tube and arrives in the slave syringe, where it occupies exactly 2 millilitres, pushing the slave plunger out by whatever distance gives that volume in that cylinder. If the slave is wider, 2 millilitres is a shorter length; if narrower, a longer one. The liquid's total volume in the system stays constant to within a hair, which is why the system works as a rigid link: every millilitre displaced at one end appears at the other end at the same moment. Compression would mean the same mass of liquid occupying less volume, and that is what does not happen; if it did, you could push the master in with the slave blocked, and you cannot. The sealed water syringe with its nozzle plugged is the proof: the plunger will not move at all, because there is nowhere for the water to go and it will not shrink to make room. The air syringe, plugged the same way, lets you push the plunger in a long way, because the gas does shrink. So in a hydraulic system the liquid flows but does not compress, and in a pneumatic system the gas does both, and it is the second behaviour, compression, that causes the sponginess, the lag and the stored energy.

### subtopic_2 — What Compressibility Does Inside a System
**Assistant:** Why is compressed air dangerous? It is only air.

**Tutor:** Because compressed air is air with energy stored in it, and stored energy is what makes anything dangerous, from a drawn bow to a charged battery. When you compress air you do work on it, and that work is held in the gas as pressure; the gas is a spring waiting to expand. A car tyre at normal pressure holds enough energy that a truck tyre bursting while being inflated has killed workshop workers, and the air receiver on a factory compressor is a pressure vessel that must be inspected by law. If a compressed-air hose splits, the escaping air expands violently, the hose whips about with enough force to break bones, and anything loose nearby is thrown. Compressed air directed at skin can inject air under it, which is a serious injury, and it blows dust and chips into eyes, which is why it must never be used to clean clothing or a workbench while people are near. A hydraulic system at the same pressure is far less explosive, because the liquid has stored almost no energy; when a hydraulic hose splits, the pressure drops almost at once and the hazard is the jet of fluid and the oil on the floor, not a whipping hose. This is one of the reasons hydraulics is chosen for very high pressure work and pneumatics is kept to lower pressures. In the Technology room the same principle applies in miniature: a blocked air syringe pushed hard and released will fire its plunger, and a syringe should never be pointed at anyone. Respect for stored energy is part of understanding fluids.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing Air or Liquid for the Job
**Assistant:** Why do mechanics bleed brakes? What is actually wrong with a bit of air in there?

**Tutor:** What is wrong is that the air turns part of the hydraulic brake system into a pneumatic one, and a pneumatic brake would be a very poor brake. The driver's foot pushes a master cylinder; brake fluid carries the pressure along pipes to slave cylinders at each wheel, which press the pads against the discs. With pure fluid in the lines, the pads move the instant the pedal does, by an amount fixed by the cylinder areas, and the pedal feels firm because the fluid will not squash. Now suppose a bubble of air has got in, through a leaking seal, a loose connection or a reservoir allowed to run low. When the driver presses the pedal, the first part of the stroke squashes the bubble instead of moving the pads; the pedal travels further before anything happens, feels spongy, and in a hard stop may reach the floor before the pads are fully applied. The car stops later and less predictably, and if the air is enough, hardly at all. Bleeding is the cure: a mechanic opens a small valve at each wheel cylinder in turn while an assistant pumps the pedal, so that fluid, carrying the bubbles with it, is pushed out into a container until no more air appears, and the reservoir is topped up so no new air is drawn in. It is exactly the procedure you used to fill the syringes under water, applied to a car, and for exactly the same reason: a hydraulic system is only as good as the absence of air in it. The spongy feel of the air-filled syringe in the first session is the feel of a brake pedal that needs bleeding.
