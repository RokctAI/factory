# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains why gases are compressible and liquids are incompressible, and what that means for pneumatic and hydraulic systems. The anchor is a Technology room in Upington with a bicycle pump, a sealed syringe of air with its nozzle blocked, a sealed syringe of water and a glass of fizzy drink, each demonstrating one aspect of the difference. The governing idea is that the compressibility of a fluid follows from how its particles are arranged, far apart in a gas and in contact in a liquid, that a compressible fluid stores energy and delays force transfer while an incompressible one transfers force immediately and rigidly, and that engineers choose air or liquid for a system according to whether springiness or precision is wanted. Explaining the particle picture and applying it to system choice are examinable.

## Subtopic: Particles Far Apart, Particles Touching

All matter is made of particles, and the three states of matter differ in how those particles are arranged and how they move. In a gas the particles are far apart, typically ten times their own diameter from each other, moving fast in straight lines and colliding; most of the volume of a gas is empty space. In a liquid the particles are touching their neighbours, sliding past one another so the liquid flows and takes the shape of its container, but with almost no empty space between them. In a solid the particles are touching and fixed in position, vibrating but not sliding.

Compressing a substance means forcing its particles closer together. In a gas there is empty space to take up, so a modest push moves the particles closer and the volume shrinks easily; double the pressure on a gas and its volume halves. In a liquid the particles are already in contact, so pushing them closer means pushing the particles themselves into each other against the forces that hold them apart, which takes an enormous pressure for a tiny change; the pressure in a car's brake system shrinks brake fluid by about a twentieth of one percent. For every purpose in this course, liquids are treated as incompressible and gases as compressible.

This is why the sealed air syringe can be pushed in a long way with the nozzle blocked and springs back when released, while the sealed water syringe cannot be pushed in at all. It is also why a bicycle pump works: the air in the barrel is compressed by the stroke until its pressure exceeds the pressure in the tyre and the valve opens, and why fizzy drinks hiss, since the gas dissolved under pressure expands when the cap releases it. The particle picture is the same one that Natural Sciences uses, and it explains every behaviour in this topic.

The questions for this section are with you now: how particles are arranged in gases and liquids, why that makes gases compressible and liquids not, and the sealed syringe demonstration.

## Subtopic: What Compressibility Does Inside a System

Inside a system, compressibility does three things. First, it stores energy. Compressed air is a spring: the work done squashing it is held in the gas and given back when it expands, which is useful in an air tool or a pneumatic door, where a reservoir of compressed air can deliver a burst of movement faster than a pump could, and dangerous when a compressed-air line bursts and whips. An incompressible liquid stores almost nothing; stop pushing and the system simply stops.

Second, compressibility delays transfer. In a pneumatic system the master must compress the air before the pressure rises enough to move the slave, so there is a lag between input and output, and the amount of output movement for a given input depends on how far the air has already been squashed, which makes exact positioning hard. In a hydraulic system the liquid cannot squash, so the slave moves the instant the master does, by an amount fixed by the area ratio, which makes exact positioning easy: a hydraulic excavator arm can be inched to the centimetre.

Third, compressibility softens force transfer. A pneumatic cylinder pushing against a load gives if the load pushes back, like a cushion; a hydraulic cylinder holds rigid. This is why a brake pedal with air in the lines feels spongy and the car stops late, and why mechanics bleed the brakes; it is also why pneumatic grippers are used to hold fragile objects, since the air cushion stops them crushing, and hydraulic jaws are used where unyielding force is needed. The sealed syringes show all three: the air one stores a push and gives it back, lags, and cushions; the water one does none of these.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three effects of compressibility inside a system, why a hydraulic system positions exactly, and why air in brake lines is dangerous.

## Subtopic: Choosing Air or Liquid for the Job

Choosing between air and liquid is a design decision made on the job's needs. Choose hydraulic where force must be large, exact and rigid: car and truck brakes, the hydraulic jack and press, excavator and crane arms, aircraft landing gear, the rescue spreader, lifts in workshops. Liquids also transmit very high pressures without the danger of stored energy, and oil lubricates the seals. Against hydraulics: leaks are messy and oil must be contained, the fluid must be kept clean and free of air, and a system needs a reservoir and return lines.

Choose pneumatic where movement must be fast, light and safe, or where a soft action is wanted: air tools such as drills and nail guns, bus and train doors, dentist's chairs, factory grippers, paint sprayers, bicycle and car tyres, which are pneumatic springs. Air is free, leaks are harmless and clean, and compressed air can be stored in a tank and piped round a factory. Against pneumatics: force is limited by the pressure a compressor can safely make, positioning is imprecise, stored energy can be dangerous, and air must be dried and filtered.

Many machines use both: a truck has hydraulic brakes on the wheels and pneumatic suspension and door openers; a factory robot uses hydraulic arms and pneumatic grippers. The investigation from the first session is the test that decides: if the job needs the solid, instant response of the water-filled syringes, it is hydraulic; if the springy, cushioned response of the air-filled pair will do or is wanted, it is pneumatic. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying liquids are stronger than gases; it is compressibility, not strength, that differs. Two: thinking pneumatics are always weaker; a large air cylinder can push hard, but not precisely. Three: ignoring stored energy in compressed air as a hazard. Four: using a pneumatic system where exact positioning is required.

The questions for this section are with you now: where hydraulic systems are chosen and why, where pneumatic systems are chosen and why, and machines that use both.

## Subtopic: Why Air Squashes and Water Does Not

Everything is made of particles. In a gas they are far apart, flying about, with mostly empty space between. In a liquid they are touching, sliding past each other.

Squashing means pushing particles closer. In a gas there is empty space to use up, so it squashes easily. In a liquid the particles already touch, so you would have to squash the particles themselves. That takes a huge force for a tiny change. So we say liquids do not compress.

Block the nozzle of an air syringe and you can push the plunger a long way; let go and it springs back. Block a water syringe and the plunger will not move at all.

## Subtopic: The Spring Hidden in Every Bubble

Squashable air does three things in a machine. It stores energy, like a spring: squash it and it pushes back. Useful in air tools, dangerous if a hose bursts.

It delays: you have to squash the air before the other end moves, so there is a lag, and you cannot put the output exactly where you want it. Water moves the output instantly and exactly.

It cushions: an air cylinder gives when pushed back, like a pillow. A water cylinder holds solid. Air in brake lines means a spongy pedal and a late stop, so mechanics bleed the brakes.

## Subtopic: Which Fluid for Which Machine

Liquid for big, exact, solid force: brakes, jacks, presses, excavator arms, rescue tools. Downside: messy leaks, must keep air out.

Air for fast, light, safe or soft action: air tools, bus doors, dentist's chairs, grippers, tyres. Downside: not exact, limited force, stored energy can hurt.

Many machines use both: a truck has liquid brakes and air suspension.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gas particles are far apart so gases compress; liquid particles touch so liquids do not. Compressible air stores energy, delays and cushions force transfer; incompressible liquid transfers force instantly and rigidly. Hydraulics for large exact rigid force, pneumatics for fast light soft action, and the sealed syringes show the difference.
