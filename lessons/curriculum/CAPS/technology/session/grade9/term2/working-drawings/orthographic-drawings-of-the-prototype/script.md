# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers drawing the model or prototype of the designed machine in first angle orthographic projection. The anchor is the Mamelodi storeroom lifter chosen in the previous session, a timber frame 1200 millimetres high and 700 wide with a winch on one upright, a pulley on the beam and a sliding platform, now to be drawn on an A3 sheet at a scale of 1 to 10 so that a classmate could build the model from the drawing alone. The governing idea is that first angle projection places the top view below the front view and the left side view to the right of it, that each view shows the true shape of one face with hidden edges dashed, and that scale, dimensions, centre lines and a parts list make the drawing complete. Producing a correctly laid out, dimensioned first angle drawing of a designed object is examinable.

## Subtopic: From Sketch to Working Drawing: Why Three Views Are Needed

A sketch shows what a machine looks like and roughly how it works. A working drawing shows exactly how big every part is, where it goes and how it is fixed, so that the thing can be made without the designer standing beside the maker. The method is orthographic projection, in which the object is viewed square-on from several directions and each view drawn flat, with no perspective, so that lengths can be measured directly from the paper. Three views are normally enough to fix any ordinary object: the front, the top and one side. A single view cannot show depth; two views can leave an ambiguity; three settle it.

The convention in South African schools and industry is first angle projection. The object is imagined inside the first quadrant, between the viewer and the planes it is projected onto, so that each view is drawn on the far side of the object from where the viewer stands. The result on the page: the front view in the middle, the top view directly below it, and the left side view to the right of the front view. The projection symbol, a truncated cone drawn in first angle, is placed in the title block so that a reader knows which convention is in use.

The lifting machine is a good subject because it has a clear front: the face the operator stands at, showing the two uprights, the beam, the winch on the left upright, the platform between the uprights and the rope path. The top view, below it, shows the two uprights as rectangles, the beam, the platform's depth and the pulley on the beam. The side view, to the right, shows the depth of the frame, the winch drum projecting from the upright and the platform's thickness. Together they contain every dimension of the frame.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a working drawing differs from a sketch, the first angle layout, and what each view of the lifter shows.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Lifting Machine in First Angle Projection

Set up the sheet. Draw a border and a title block with the title, scale, date, the designer's name, the sheet number and the first angle symbol. Choose a scale that fits the machine on the paper: a frame 1200 by 700 by 500 millimetres at 1 to 10 becomes 120 by 70 by 50 on the paper, which fits A3 with room for three views and dimensions. Draw light construction lines first: a horizontal line for the base of the front view and the base of the side view, a vertical line for the left edge of the front view and the left edge of the top view, then project heights across from front to side and widths down from front to top. A 45 degree line to the lower right transfers depths between the top view and the side view.

Draw the front view first: two uprights 50 by 50 in section at 1 to 10 becomes 5 by 5, standing 1200 apart on a base rail, with the beam across the top; the platform as a rectangle between the uprights at some chosen height; the winch drum as a circle on the left upright with its handle; the pulley on the beam as a small circle; the rope as a thin line from drum to pulley to platform. Project the uprights down to the top view and across to the side view. Add the depth of the frame, 500 millimetres, in the top and side views. Line in the outlines with a dark continuous line, construction lines left faint or erased.

Hidden detail is drawn with dashed lines: the bolt through the upright behind the winch, the guide rail on the inside of the upright hidden behind the platform in the front view, the base rail under the platform in the top view. Centre lines, long-dash short-dash, mark the axis of the winch drum and the pulley. These conventions let the drawing say what cannot be seen from outside without drawing a separate section.

The questions for this section are with you now: setting up the sheet, title block and scale, drawing and projecting the three views, and hidden detail and centre lines.

## Subtopic: Dimensions, Scale, Hidden Detail and the Parts List

Dimensioning follows the Term 1 rules. Dimension lines are thin, with arrowheads touching the extension lines, and the number sits above the line in millimetres, written as the true size of the object, not the paper size: 1200, not 120. Each size is given once, in the view where it is clearest: the overall height and width in the front view, the depth in the side or top view, the platform's size in the top view, the position of the winch on the upright in the front view. Overall dimensions sit outside detail dimensions. Diameters are marked with the diameter symbol, radii with R. A reader should be able to find every size needed to make each part without measuring the drawing with a ruler.

The parts list is a table on the sheet or on a second sheet: item number, name, quantity, material, size. For the lifter: two uprights, pine, 50 by 50 by 1200; one beam, pine, 50 by 75 by 800; one base rail; one platform, plywood, 18 by 500 by 600; one hand winch with ratchet; one pulley block; six metres of rope; bolts, screws, guides. Each item number appears as a balloon on the drawing pointing to the part. The parts list is also the shopping list, and its prices feed the cost estimate.

Checking the drawing: are the three views aligned so that widths match between front and top and heights match between front and side? Is the scale stated and used consistently? Does every part have its dimensions somewhere? Are hidden lines dashed and outlines dark? Would a stranger be able to build it? The error museum, four exhibits. One: placing the top view above the front view, which is third angle, with a first angle symbol. Two: writing paper sizes instead of true sizes on dimensions. Three: dimensioning the same length in two views. Four: drawing hidden edges as solid lines so the platform appears to float.

The questions for this section are with you now: dimensioning rules, the parts list and balloons, and checking the drawing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the Mamelodi lifter on an A3 sheet at 1 to 10 — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Flat Pictures of One Machine

A sketch shows the idea. A working drawing shows every size and position so someone else can build it. Orthographic means square-on, flat, no perspective, so you can measure off the paper.

First angle: front view in the middle, top view below it, left side view to its right. Put the first angle symbol in the title block.

The lifter's front: uprights, beam, winch, platform, rope. Top: uprights as squares, beam, platform depth, pulley. Side: frame depth, drum sticking out, platform thickness.

Keep three flat pictures of one machine in mind and try the questions for this part: what a working drawing is.

## Subtopic: Front, Top, Side, in Their Places

Border, title block: title, scale, date, name, sheet number, symbol. Scale 1 to 10: 1200 becomes 120 on paper. Faint construction lines first. A 45 degree line carries depth from the top view to the side view.

Draw the front: two uprights, beam, platform, winch circle, pulley circle, rope line. Project down for the top, across for the side. Add the depth. Darken the outlines.

Dashed lines for what is hidden: the bolt behind the winch, the guide behind the platform, the rail under it. Long-dash short-dash for the centre of the drum and pulley.

Hold front, top, side, in their places in mind and try this part's questions: drawing the views.

## Subtopic: Numbers on the Lines

Dimensions: thin lines, arrowheads, true size in millimetres above the line. 1200, not 120. Each size once, in the clearest view. Overalls outside, details inside. Diameter symbol for round things.

Parts list: number, name, how many, material, size. Uprights, beam, rail, platform, winch, pulley, rope, bolts. Balloons on the drawing point to each. It is also the shopping list.

Check: views line up, scale stated, every part sized, hidden lines dashed, outlines dark. Could a stranger build it?

Pocket summary of the lesson. A working drawing in first angle orthographic projection shows the machine in a front view, a top view below it and a left side view to its right, drawn square-on to a stated scale with a title block and the projection symbol. Hidden edges are dashed and centre lines marked. True sizes in millimetres are dimensioned once each in the clearest view, and a numbered parts list with balloons names every part, its material and size, so the model can be built from the drawing alone.

Hold numbers on the lines, and take the final questions of the lesson: dimensions and the parts list.
