### subtopic_1 — From Sketch to Working Drawing: Why Three Views Are Needed
**Assistant:** Why do I need three views when the sketch already shows the whole machine in 3D?

**Tutor:** Because a 3D sketch shows appearance and a working drawing has to deliver measurements, and the two do not mix. In a perspective or isometric sketch, lines that are the same length in the real machine are drawn at different lengths depending on their angle, so nothing can be measured from it, and surfaces facing away are hidden entirely. Orthographic views look at the machine square-on, so every length in a view is a true length at the drawing scale and every angle on a face parallel to the view is true. The price is that each view is flat, so a single view cannot say how deep anything is. The front view fixes height and width, the top view fixes width and depth, the side view fixes depth and height; between them every dimension appears, and most appear twice, which lets the maker cross-check. The sketch stays on the sheet as a pictorial view to help the reader, but the three views carry the numbers.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing the Lifting Machine in First Angle Projection
**Assistant:** How do I decide which face is the front?

**Tutor:** Choose the face that shows the most about how the machine works and the most of its shape, which is usually the face the user stands at. For the lifter, the operator faces the frame with the winch on the left upright and the platform between the uprights; that view shows the two uprights, the beam, the winch, the rope path and the platform at once, so it is the front. The top view then follows, looking down on the beam and platform, and the side view follows from the left. If a different face were chosen as the front, the drawing would still be correct as long as the other views were placed consistently, but the reader would have to work harder to understand it. Pick the front to make the drawing easiest to read, and mark it on the pictorial sketch with an arrow labelled F so there is no doubt.

### subtopic_3 — Dimensions, Scale, Hidden Detail and the Parts List
**Assistant:** If I know the true size, why not just draw the machine full size and skip the scale?

**Tutor:** Because the machine is 1200 millimetres tall and the paper is 420, so the frame does not fit, and because even when a part would fit, standard scales make drawings easier to produce and to read. At 1 to 10 every true millimetre becomes a tenth of a millimetre on paper, so 1200 becomes 120, 700 becomes 70, and the arithmetic is instant; at an odd scale like 1 to 7 every conversion is a calculation and a chance for error. Standard scales, 1 to 2, 1 to 5, 1 to 10, 1 to 20, also match the markings on a scale rule, so lengths can be read directly. Small parts, like the pulley bracket, can be drawn at 1 to 1 or even 2 to 1 as a detail on the same sheet, each with its own stated scale. The rule is that the dimension always states the true size whatever the scale, so the reader never has to multiply.
