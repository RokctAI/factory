### subtopic_1 — Writing the Design Brief With Specifications and Constraints
**Assistant:** Why must the brief not name the mechanism, when the investigation already found good ones?

**Tutor:** Because the brief describes the problem and the mechanism is a solution, and naming the solution in the problem statement quietly throws away every alternative. If the sentence says design a pulley machine, nobody will sketch the scissor platform, and the owner never finds out that a worm-driven platform would hold itself and roll out of the doorway. The investigation's shortlist is not wasted; it is carried forward into the sketches, where the mechanisms belong, and into the specifications, which say what the machine must do in a way that lets several mechanisms compete. A brief that names the mechanism also hides a risk: if that mechanism turns out to be over budget or unsafe, the whole brief fails, whereas a brief about the need stays true and a different sketch can meet it.

### subtopic_2 — Two Freehand Sketches of Possible Machines
**Assistant:** What is the real difference between a specification and a constraint? They both seem like rules.

**Tutor:** They are both rules, but they come from different places and are handled differently. A specification describes the product: what it must do and what it must have for the user to be satisfied, like lifting 500 newtons or holding the load at any height. The designer writes specifications from the investigation and can argue about them; perhaps 450 newtons is enough if the heaviest bag is really 45 kilograms. A constraint is a limit imposed on the project from outside: the money available, the materials the hardware shop sells, the ban on welding, the three weeks. The designer did not choose these and cannot negotiate them away by clever design. In practice, specifications tell you whether the machine is good; constraints tell you whether the project is possible. A design that meets every specification but costs 900 rand has failed a constraint, and it is just as much a failure.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing the Final Solution With Reasons
**Assistant:** If I prefer one sketch, why do I have to compare both against every specification?

**Tutor:** Because preference is a feeling and the comparison is evidence, and the owner is going to trust her stock and her children to the result. Going down the specification list does three things. It catches the places where the sketch you like actually fails, such as the scissor platform going over budget, which enthusiasm hides. It shows where the other sketch has an idea worth stealing, like castors or a self-locking worm, which is how combined designs happen. And it produces a written justification that someone else can check, which is what a tender, a teacher or a client expects. You may well end up choosing the one you preferred, but you will know why, and the reasons will point to the brief rather than to your taste.
