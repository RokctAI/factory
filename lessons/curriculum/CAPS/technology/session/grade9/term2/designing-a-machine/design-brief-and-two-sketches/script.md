# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers writing a design brief with specifications and constraints, producing two sketches of possible designs and choosing a final solution. The anchor is the Mamelodi spaza shop from the investigation, the owner looking at two drawings pinned to the storeroom door, one of a timber frame with a block and tackle and a ratchet winch, the other of a scissor platform driven by a hand crank through a worm gear, and asking which one she should trust with her stock and her children in the room. The governing idea is that a design brief turns investigated facts into a short statement, measurable specifications and firm constraints, that two genuinely different sketches explore the range of solutions, and that the final choice is justified against the brief point by point. Writing a brief, producing labelled sketches of alternative designs and justifying a choice are examinable.

## Subtopic: Writing the Design Brief With Specifications and Constraints

A design brief begins with a single sentence of the form: design and make a machine that lifts loads of up to 50 kilograms from the floor to a shelf 900 millimetres high in a small storeroom, for a shop owner working alone without electricity. That sentence names the product, the job, the user and the setting. It does not name a mechanism; saying a pulley machine or a jack would close off the design before it starts. The sentence is short enough to remember and precise enough that a stranger could tell whether a given machine meets it.

Specifications are what the machine must do and must have, each one measurable where possible. It must lift 500 newtons. It must lift through 900 millimetres. The operator's effort must not exceed 150 newtons. It must hold the load at any height if the operator lets go. It must stop at the top of travel. Moving parts that can pinch must be guarded. It must be operated by one person. It must fit through an 800 millimetre doorway or be built in the room. A specification that cannot be checked, such as it must be easy to use, is sharpened into one that can: a lift must take no more than 40 turns of a crank.

Constraints are the limits imposed from outside: a budget of 600 rand, materials from the local hardware store, no welding, timber and bolted steel only, and completion in three weeks. Constraints are not negotiable in the way specifications sometimes are; they bound the problem. The brief ends with a note on how the design will be evaluated: against each specification, by test where possible, and under the headings fitness for purpose, safety, ergonomics, cost and aesthetics used throughout the term.

The questions for this section are with you now: the one-sentence brief, measurable specifications, and constraints and evaluation criteria.

## Subtopic: Two Freehand Sketches of Possible Machines

Two sketches are required, and they must be genuinely different, not the same machine in two colours. Sketch A: a timber frame of two uprights and a beam, standing against the storeroom wall; a hand winch with a ratchet and pawl mounted on one upright; a rope from the drum up to a fixed pulley on the beam, down to a movable block on a timber platform that slides between the uprights on guides, giving a mechanical advantage of 2 from the tackle multiplied by the winch's own advantage. The platform is pulled up, the ratchet holds it, and the bag is slid off onto the shelf. Labels: winch, ratchet, pulleys, platform, guides, beam, rope path, load position.

Sketch B: a scissor platform, like a car scissor jack scaled up in timber and steel strip, driven by a long threaded rod turned by a crank through a small worm gear, so the platform rises steadily and holds itself at any height. The whole thing is on castors so it can roll out of the doorway and be stored against the wall. Labels: scissor arms, threaded rod, worm and wheel, crank, platform, castors, pivot pins. The two sketches use different lifting mechanisms, different controls for holding the load, and different answers to the space problem, which is what makes the comparison worth having.

Sketches are freehand, in pencil, three-dimensional where possible, with the main parts labelled and the mechanism from this term named. They show the machine in use, with the load and a figure for scale. Notes beside each sketch give the estimated advantage, cost and the parts list in outline. The sketch is a thinking tool and a communication tool; it need not be beautiful, but it must be readable and must show how the machine works.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes two sketches genuinely different, the content of each sketch, and how sketches are presented.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Final Solution With Reasons

Choosing the final solution is a comparison against the brief, specification by specification. Lifts 500 newtons: both. Operator effort under 150 newtons: A with the winch and tackle, easily; B depends on the worm ratio, probably yes. Holds the load if released: A by ratchet, B by self-locking worm; both, but B needs no extra part to do it. Stops at the top: A needs a stop on the guides, B stops when the scissor is fully open. Guards: A has an exposed rope and drum, B has an exposed threaded rod; both need guarding. Fits the room: A is fixed to the wall and always there, B rolls away. Cost: A about 450 rand, B about 650 rand and over budget unless the worm gear is salvaged.

The decision is written with reasons. Sketch B is steadier and self-locking, which matters with children present, and it clears the doorway, but it is over budget and the scissor pivots are the hardest part to make without welding. Sketch A is within budget, uses parts the hardware store sells and is simpler to build, but the platform must be guided well to stop the load swinging and the ratchet must be checked every time. A combined design is often the best answer: Sketch A's frame and winch with Sketch B's idea of castors, or a worm winch in place of the ratchet winch so the holding is automatic. Whatever is chosen, the reasons point to the brief.

The chosen design is then described in a short paragraph, ready for the working drawings in the next session, with a parts list and a first cost estimate. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a brief that names the mechanism, which is a solution pretending to be a problem. Two: two sketches that are the same idea. Three: choosing by preference without reference to the specifications. Four: a specification that cannot be tested, such as it must be strong, without a number.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing the sketches against the specifications, writing the choice with reasons, and preparing for working drawings.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two drawings pinned to the storeroom door in Mamelodi — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One Sentence, Then the Rules

Brief, one sentence: design and make a machine that lifts up to 50 kilograms from the floor to a shelf 900 millimetres high, in a small storeroom, for an owner working alone with no electricity. Do not name the mechanism.

Specifications are what it must do, with numbers: lift 500 newtons, 900 millimetres, effort under 150 newtons, hold at any height, stop at the top, guards, one person, fits through an 800 millimetre door. Easy to use becomes no more than 40 turns.

Constraints are the limits from outside: 600 rand, hardware shop parts, no welding, three weeks. Then say how you will judge it: purpose, safety, ergonomics, cost, looks.

Keep one sentence, then the rules in mind and try the questions for this part: the brief.

## Subtopic: Two Ideas on Paper

Sketch A: a timber frame against the wall, a hand winch with a ratchet, a rope over a top pulley to a movable pulley on a platform that slides between the uprights. Pull up, ratchet holds, slide the bag off.

Sketch B: a scissor platform like a big car jack, driven by a threaded rod and a crank through a worm gear, on castors so it rolls out of the way. Rises steadily and holds itself.

Both in pencil, in 3D, labelled, with the load and a person for scale, and notes on advantage, cost and parts. Readable beats pretty.

Hold two ideas on paper in mind and try this part's questions: the sketches.

## Subtopic: Pick One and Say Why

Compare them line by line against the specifications. Both lift. Both keep the effort down. A holds by ratchet, B by worm. A needs a top stop, B stops itself. Both need guards. A stays on the wall, B rolls away. A about 450 rand, B about 650 and over budget.

Write the choice with reasons. B is safer and steadier but costs too much and is harder to build. A is affordable and simple but needs good guides and a checked ratchet. Or combine: A's frame with castors, or a worm winch instead of the ratchet winch.

Describe the chosen design in a paragraph with a parts list and a cost. That feeds the working drawings next.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A design brief is one sentence naming the product, job, user and setting, followed by measurable specifications and firm constraints, with evaluation criteria. Two genuinely different sketches, labelled and in use, explore different mechanisms and controls. The final solution is chosen by comparing each sketch against each specification and writing the reasons, often combining the best of both.

Hold pick one and say why, and take the final questions of the lesson: the choice.
