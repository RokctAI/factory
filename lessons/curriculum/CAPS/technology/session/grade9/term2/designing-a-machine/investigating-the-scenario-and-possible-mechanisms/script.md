# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating a design scenario and the possible mechanisms and controls for a machine. The anchor is a spaza shop in Mamelodi where the owner needs to lift 50 kilogram bags of maize meal and 20 litre drums of cooking oil from the ground onto a storeroom shelf at waist height, several times a week, with no electricity she can rely on and a budget of a few hundred rand. The governing idea is that investigation comes before design: the need is stated precisely as what the machine must do, for whom, where and within what limits; each sub-task is matched to mechanisms studied in the term; and the hazards are matched to control systems, with everything recorded so that the design brief in the next session rests on facts. Investigating a scenario, listing suitable mechanisms and controls with reasons, and recording findings are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading the Scenario: A Machine to Solve a Real Need

A scenario is a short description of a situation in which something is needed. The first job of the investigation is to turn it into a precise statement of what the machine must do. For the Mamelodi shop: lift loads of up to 50 kilograms, which weigh about 500 newtons, through a height of about 900 millimetres, from floor to shelf, roughly twenty times a week. Then the people: the owner, who is not strong and works alone, and the children who help after school and must not be hurt. Then the place: a storeroom three metres by two, a concrete floor, a doorway 800 millimetres wide, no reliable power. Then the limits: a budget of about 600 rand, materials available at the local hardware, and no welding.

Each of these facts rules things in or out. A 500 newton load and an operator who can comfortably pull perhaps 150 newtons means the machine needs a mechanical advantage of at least 3 to 4. No reliable power means a hand-operated machine or one that works without electricity. An 800 millimetre doorway means the machine must fit through it or be built inside. Children in the room mean the load must never be able to fall and moving parts must be guarded. The budget and the hardware shop fix the materials: timber, rope, pulleys, threaded rod, perhaps a car jack.

The investigation also looks at how the problem is solved elsewhere. A supermarket uses a pallet jack and a forklift; a builder uses a block and tackle on a scaffold; a mechanic uses a hydraulic trolley jack or an engine hoist; a farmer lifts bags with a tractor's front loader; a grain store uses a hand-cranked winch and a boom. Each of these shows a mechanism doing the lifting and a control doing the holding, and each is too big, too expensive or too dependent on power for the spaza shop, which is itself useful information.

The questions for this section are with you now: turning a scenario into precise requirements, how each fact rules options in or out, and what existing solutions show.

## Subtopic: Matching Mechanisms to Jobs: Lift, Move, Turn, Hold

The next step is to break the job into parts and match each to a mechanism. Lifting the load straight up: a block and tackle with three or four load-bearing ropes gives a mechanical advantage of 3 or 4 from a beam above; a hydraulic bottle jack gives a large advantage over a short stroke; a hand winch with a worm gear or spur reduction gives a large advantage with self-locking; a lever with a long arm gives a modest advantage over a short lift. Each lifts, but each has a stroke, a speed and a cost, and those must be compared against the 900 millimetre lift and the twenty times a week.

Moving the load sideways onto the shelf once it is up: a rack and pinion could slide a platform; a simple trolley on rails could roll it; a pivoting boom could swing it over. Turning the input into something the operator can apply: a crank handle is easier than a rope for repeated lifting; a foot pedal frees the hands; a long lever uses body weight. Guiding the load so it does not swing: a frame with vertical guides, or a platform on a scissor mechanism like a car jack, keeps the load steady where a free-hanging tackle would let it swing into the children.

Each candidate mechanism is written in a table with its mechanical advantage, its stroke or travel, its speed, its approximate cost, where it would come from, and its main problem. A block and tackle: advantage 4, lifts the full height, slow, about 300 rand for two blocks and rope, needs a strong beam, load swings. A bottle jack: advantage 50, lifts only 150 millimetres, cheap second-hand, needs a frame to raise it, very slow for 900 millimetres. A worm winch: advantage 30, lifts any height, holds itself, about 500 rand, needs mounting. The table is the heart of the investigation.

The questions for this section are with you now: breaking the job into lift, move, input and guide, candidate mechanisms for each, and the comparison table.

## Subtopic: Choosing Controls and Recording the Investigation

Controls make the machine safe. The main hazard is the load falling. A block and tackle needs a cleat to hold the rope and, better, a ratchet on a winch drum so the load cannot run back if the rope is released; a worm winch holds itself and needs nothing extra. A moving platform needs a brake or a stop so it cannot slide off the shelf end. A rack-driven slide needs end stops. Guards cover gears and pinch points, especially where children help. A limit of travel at the top stops the load hitting the beam. Each hazard is written down with the control that addresses it.

Ergonomics is investigated too: the height of the handle for the owner, the force she can apply comfortably on a crank or a rope, which is found by measuring with a spring balance, and the number of turns or pulls per lift, which must not be exhausting twenty times a week. Aesthetics and the shop's space are noted: the machine must be tidy, must not block the doorway when not in use, and should fold or push against a wall.

The investigation ends with a written summary: the need in one sentence, the key numbers, the shortlist of mechanisms and controls with reasons, and the questions still open. This is what the design brief in the next session will be written from. The error museum, four exhibits. One: starting to draw a machine before the load, height and user are known. Two: choosing a mechanism because it is clever rather than because it fits the numbers. Three: forgetting the control that stops the load falling. Four: ignoring the budget and the doorway until the model is built.

The questions for this section are with you now: matching hazards to controls, investigating ergonomics and space, and the written summary of the investigation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the spaza shop in Mamelodi and the bags of maize meal — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Does the Machine Have to Do?

Start with the facts. What load: 50 kilograms, about 500 newtons. How high: 900 millimetres. How often: twenty times a week. Who: the owner, alone, with children about. Where: a small room, a narrow door, no reliable power. Budget: about 600 rand, hardware shop parts, no welding.

Each fact cuts options. 500 newtons and a person who can pull 150 means advantage of at least 3 or 4. No power means hand-operated. Children mean the load must never fall.

Look at how others do it: forklifts, tackles on scaffolds, trolley jacks, winches. All too big or too costly here, which is itself useful to know.

Keep what does the machine have to do in mind and try the questions for this part: the facts of the scenario.

## Subtopic: Pick the Right Part for Each Job

Split the job. Lift up: tackle, bottle jack, worm winch, long lever. Move sideways: rack and pinion, trolley, swinging boom. Input: crank, pedal or lever. Guide: a frame so the load does not swing.

Table every option: advantage, how far it moves, how fast, cost, where to buy, main problem. Tackle: 4, full height, slow, 300 rand, swings. Jack: 50, only 150 millimetres, cheap, very slow. Worm winch: 30, any height, holds itself, 500 rand.

The table is the investigation. It is what you will choose from.

Hold pick the right part for each job in mind and try this part's questions: mechanisms and the table.

## Subtopic: Write It Down Before You Draw

Hazards and controls. Load falling: cleat, ratchet, or a worm winch that holds itself. Platform sliding off: stop or brake. Pinch points: guards. Top of travel: a limit.

Measure the owner: how hard can she pull, how high should the handle be, how many turns per lift without getting worn out. Note the space: it must not block the door.

Write the summary: the need in a sentence, the numbers, the shortlist with reasons, the open questions.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Investigate before you design. Turn the scenario into facts: load, height, frequency, user, place and limits. Break the job into lift, move, input and guide, and table the mechanisms that could do each with advantage, travel, speed, cost and problems. Match every hazard to a control, especially one that stops the load falling. Measure the user, note the space, and write a summary the design brief can rest on.

Hold write it down before you draw, and take the final questions of the lesson: controls and the summary.
