### subtopic_1 — Reading the Scenario: A Machine to Solve a Real Need
**Assistant:** How do I know what mechanical advantage the machine needs before I have designed it?

**Tutor:** You get it from two measurements, and both are part of the investigation rather than the design. The first is the load: weigh the heaviest thing the machine must lift, or read it off the bag, and convert to newtons, so 50 kilograms is about 500 newtons. The second is the operator: find out what force the user can apply comfortably and repeatedly, not once with a heave but twenty times a week, by having them pull on a spring balance or a rope over a pulley with a known load, and most adults settle around 100 to 200 newtons for a crank or a pull. Divide the load by the comfortable effort, 500 over 150, and you get about 3.3, so the machine needs an advantage of at least 4 to leave a margin for friction. That number then sorts the mechanisms: a single pulley is out, a tackle of four ropes is in, a worm winch is in with room to spare.

### subtopic_2 — Matching Mechanisms to Jobs: Lift, Move, Turn, Hold
**Assistant:** Why does the investigation look at forklifts and engine hoists if the shop could never afford them?

**Tutor:** Because every existing solution is a worked example of the same problem, and even the unaffordable ones teach something. A forklift shows that the load is best lifted on a platform with vertical guides so it cannot swing, which the shop can copy in timber. An engine hoist shows a boom on wheels with a hydraulic jack, which suggests the shop's machine could roll out of the doorway when not in use. A builder's tackle shows that a fixed beam above is enough to lift from, if the storeroom's roof beam is strong. The grain store's hand winch shows that self-locking makes the ratchet unnecessary. Writing down why each one is too big or too costly also sharpens the limits of the brief. Investigation is partly copying ideas and partly learning what not to copy.

### subtopic_3 — Choosing Controls and Recording the Investigation
**Assistant:** What if two mechanisms both fit the numbers? How do I choose between them?

**Tutor:** That is exactly what the comparison table is for, and the answer is that you do not choose yet; you carry both into the design brief and sketch both in the next session. The table lets you see where they differ on the things the scenario cares about. The tackle is cheaper and lifts the full height but lets the load swing and needs a cleat or ratchet to hold it; the worm winch costs more and needs mounting but holds itself and gives a steady lift. Which matters more depends on the shop: with children about, the self-locking and steadiness may be worth the extra rand; on a very tight budget, the tackle with a frame to guide the load and a ratchet might win. The brief will say which of these the owner values most, and the two sketches will show what each looks like in the room. Then the choice is made with reasons, not a guess.
