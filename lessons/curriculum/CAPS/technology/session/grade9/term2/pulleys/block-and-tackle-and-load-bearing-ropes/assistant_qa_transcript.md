### subtopic_1 — Combining Fixed and Movable Pulleys Into a Block and Tackle
**Assistant:** Why does the rope going to the hand count for a single movable pulley but not for a block and tackle?

**Tutor:** It counts in both cases only when it pulls up on the movable block, and that depends on where it comes from. In the single movable pulley on its own, the free end runs straight up from the movable pulley to the hand above, so it is one of the two sections holding the load; the hand is acting as the fixed point. In a block and tackle, the free end leaves the fixed upper block and comes down to the hand. That section pulls down on the upper block, and the upper block is held by the beam, so the beam takes that pull, not the load. The rule is really about direction: a section counts if it runs upward from the movable block. Drawing an arrow on every section showing which block it pulls on makes the count mechanical and removes the guesswork.

### subtopic_2 — Counting Load-Bearing Ropes to Find the Mechanical Advantage
**Assistant:** If tackles with more ropes give more advantage, why not use twenty ropes?

**Tutor:** Three things stop it. First, friction: every sheave the rope passes over adds axle friction and rope bending resistance, so with twenty sheaves most of the effort would go into friction and the efficiency would collapse. Second, rope: a twenty-rope tackle needs twenty metres hauled for every metre lifted, which is slow and needs a huge length of rope and a long way to pull it. Third, bulk: twenty sheaves make blocks too large and heavy for the job. In practice tackles stop at about six sections. When a bigger advantage is needed, engineers switch to a different mechanism, like the geared chain block or a winch with a worm gear, which gets its advantage from gear ratio rather than rope count.

### subtopic_3 — Investigating Two-, Three- and Four-Rope Tackles
**Assistant:** Does the mechanical advantage change if I pull the rope at an angle instead of straight down?

**Tutor:** The counting rule assumes the sections are all vertical and parallel, so that every one pulls straight up on the lower block. If the free end is pulled at an angle, the section leaving the upper block is still not load-bearing, so the count does not change, but you will find the effort rises slightly because the rope rubs harder on the last sheave and some of your pull is wasted sideways. If the load-bearing sections themselves are not parallel, for example because the two blocks are far apart sideways, then part of each section's tension pulls sideways instead of up and the advantage drops below the count. Keep the blocks in line, pull along the rope, and the count is a good guide.
