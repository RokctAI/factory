# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the single fixed pulley used to change the direction of a pull, with a mechanical advantage of 1. The anchor is a Technology room in Thohoyandou with a pulley hooked to a bracket on the ceiling beam, a rope over it, a bucket of sand on one end and a spring balance on the other, beside a flagpole in the school quad whose halyard runs over a fixed pulley at the top. The governing idea is that a fixed pulley is a wheel on an axle that redirects the rope, so that the effort equals the load and the effort moves the same distance as the load, giving no force advantage, while the change of direction lets the user pull down with body weight from a safe position. Explaining and measuring the fixed pulley and recognising its uses are examinable, and it is the first element of the block and tackle that follows.

## Subtopic: The Fixed Pulley: A Wheel That Changes Direction

A pulley is a wheel with a groove around its rim, mounted on an axle in a frame called a block, with a rope running in the groove. A fixed pulley has its block attached to something that does not move: a beam, a bracket, the top of a flagpole, the jib of a crane. The load hangs from one end of the rope and the effort pulls on the other end. As the rope is pulled, the wheel turns on its axle, so the rope runs over it with little friction instead of dragging over a fixed edge.

The fixed pulley does one thing: it changes the direction of the force. Pull the rope down and the load goes up; pull sideways and the load rises; pull from round a corner and the load still rises. Without the pulley, lifting a load means standing above it or beside it and pulling upwards, using only the strength of the arms and back; with the pulley, the lifter stands on the ground, pulls down, and can use body weight, lean back, and even walk backwards to raise the load. The pulley also lets several people pull on the same rope, in a line along the ground.

A fixed pulley is the oldest and most common lifting aid. Wells use one to raise the bucket; flagpoles have one at the top so the flag is raised from the bottom; cranes have one at the end of the jib so the winch can sit on the ground or in the cab; washing lines on pulleys let clothes be hung from a balcony and run out over a yard; sash windows hide a fixed pulley in each side of the frame; gym machines use them to turn a downward pull into a lift of weights. In each case the force needed equals the weight of the load; the pulley has made the direction convenient, not the load lighter.

## Subtopic: Why the Mechanical Advantage Is 1

Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort. For a single fixed pulley, measure it: hang a 1 kilogram mass, 10 newtons, on one end and read a spring balance on the other end while lifting slowly; the balance shows about 10 newtons, slightly more because of friction at the axle. The mechanical advantage is 10 divided by 10, which is 1. Repeat with 2 kilograms and the balance reads 20 newtons; the ratio stays 1. Measure distance: pull the rope down 300 millimetres and the load rises 300 millimetres. Effort equals load and effort distance equals load distance.

Why there is no gain is seen in the rope. The rope is one continuous piece with the same tension everywhere along it, provided the pulley turns freely. The load hangs on one part of the rope, so the tension must equal the load's weight. The effort pulls on another part of the same rope, so the effort must equal that same tension. One rope, one tension, so effort equals load. The pulley wheel merely turns the rope through an angle; it does not divide the load between several rope sections, which is what a movable pulley or a block and tackle does.

The energy check agrees. Work done by the effort is effort times the distance the rope is pulled; work done on the load is load times the distance it rises; with effort equal to load and the distances equal, work in equals work out, and nothing is lost except a little to axle friction, which is why the balance reads a fraction over the load's weight. A fixed pulley therefore has a mechanical advantage of 1 by its nature, not by poor design, and a learner asked for its mechanical advantage should say 1 and explain the single tension in the rope.

## Subtopic: Where Fixed Pulleys Earn Their Place

If the fixed pulley gives no force advantage, its value lies in direction, position and safety, and those are worth a great deal. Direction: a downward pull is the strongest and most sustainable pull a human body can make, because gravity helps; hauling a bucket up a well by pulling down on a rope over a pulley is far easier on the back than leaning over and lifting, even though the force is the same. Position: the person pulling can stand where it is safe and dry and clear of the load, at the bottom of a flagpole, on the ground beside a crane, on the deck of a ship while the sail rises, rather than above a drop or under a swinging weight.

Fixed pulleys also combine. The rope from the top of a crane's jib runs over fixed pulleys down to a winch at the base, so the heavy motor sits low where the crane is stable. In a block and tackle, the fixed pulley at the top is what lets the final pull be downward while the movable pulleys below it supply the mechanical advantage; without the fixed pulley the user would have to pull upwards from above the load. A fixed pulley at a corner lets a rope turn through a right angle with little friction, which is how sash windows and some garage doors use a single weight to balance a moving part.

Designing with a fixed pulley means choosing a wheel large enough that the rope bends gently and does not wear, an axle that turns freely and is lubricated, a block strong enough for the load plus the effort, since the beam carries both, and a fixing point that can take twice the load's weight, because the rope pulls down on both sides. That last point surprises people: a 100 newton load lifted over a fixed pulley puts 200 newtons on the ceiling bracket. The error museum, four exhibits. One: claiming a fixed pulley halves the effort; its mechanical advantage is 1. Two: forgetting friction and expecting exactly equal readings. Three: fixing the pulley to a bracket rated for the load only; it carries load plus effort. Four: thinking direction change is a small benefit; for a human lifter it is often the whole point.

## Subtopic: Pull Down to Lift Up

A pulley is a grooved wheel on an axle in a frame, with a rope over it. A fixed pulley is one that is tied to something that does not move: a beam, a flagpole top, a crane arm.

It changes the direction of your pull. Pull down, load goes up. Pull round a corner, load still goes up. You stand on the ground and lean back with your weight instead of lifting with your arms from above.

## Subtopic: No Gain in Force, and Why That Is Fine

Measure it. Hang 1 kilogram, 10 newtons. The spring balance on the other end reads about 10 newtons, a bit more for friction. Load over effort is 1. Pull the rope 300 millimetres down, the load rises 300. Mechanical advantage 1.

Why? It is one rope, and one rope has one tension all the way along. The load hangs on it, so the tension equals the load. You pull on it, so your pull equals the tension. Same.

Work in equals work out: same force, same distance. The pulley is not badly made; a fixed pulley simply cannot multiply force.

Hold one rope, one tension in mind and try this part's questions: no gain in force.

## Subtopic: Flagpoles, Wells and Washing Lines

So what is it for? Direction: pulling down is the strongest pull a body can make, because gravity helps. Position: you stand somewhere safe, at the bottom of the pole, on the ground by the crane. Several people can pull in a line.

It teams up: a crane's rope runs over fixed pulleys down to a winch on the ground. In a block and tackle the top fixed pulley lets you pull downwards while the lower pulleys do the multiplying.

Design care: a big enough wheel, a free axle, and a fixing that takes twice the load, because the rope pulls down on both sides.
