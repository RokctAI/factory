# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the single movable pulley, which gives a mechanical advantage of 2. The anchor is the same ceiling bracket in Thohoyandou, now with the rope tied to it, running down round a pulley from which the sand bucket hangs, and back up to a spring balance held above, with a learner surprised to read half the bucket's weight. The governing idea is that a load hung from a movable pulley is supported by two sections of the same rope, each carrying half, so the effort on the free end is half the load while the free end must move twice as far, and that counting the rope sections that support the load is the way to find the mechanical advantage of any pulley system. Explaining, measuring and calculating the movable pulley are examinable and lead directly to the block and tackle.

## Subtopic: A Pulley That Rides on the Rope

A movable pulley is one whose block is attached to the load and moves with it. One end of the rope is tied to a fixed point above, the rope passes down round the pulley and back up, and the effort is applied to the free end. As the free end is pulled up, the pulley and the load rise with it; the pulley rides up the rope. The fixed end of the rope and the pulled end both run upwards from the pulley, so the load hangs from two sections of rope.

This arrangement is seen wherever a load must be lifted with less force and a convenient fixed point exists above it: a builder hauling a bucket of mortar up a scaffold ties the rope to the scaffold and pulls on the free end over a pulley on the bucket; a sailor hoists a dinghy with a movable block on the boat; many cranes hang their hook from a movable pulley block so that the hoist cable supports the hook twice. It is also one half of every block and tackle, in which fixed and movable pulleys are combined.

The movable pulley has an awkwardness that the fixed pulley did not: the effort must be applied upwards, from above the load, which puts the lifter in the wrong place and prevents the use of body weight. That awkwardness is removed in the next session by adding a fixed pulley at the top to turn the pull downwards, which is how real lifting tackle is always arranged. For now the movable pulley is studied alone so that the source of its mechanical advantage is clear.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a movable pulley is rigged, where it is used, and its awkward direction of pull.

## Subtopic: Two Rope Sections, Half the Effort

Count the rope sections that support the load. For the movable pulley there are two: the section from the fixed point down to the pulley, and the section from the pulley up to the effort. The rope is one continuous piece, so the tension is the same in both sections. Together the two sections hold up the load, so each carries half of it: tension equals load divided by 2. The effort pulls on one section, so the effort equals the tension, which is half the load. A 20 newton bucket needs an effort of 10 newtons, plus a little for friction and the weight of the pulley block itself.

The mechanical advantage is load divided by effort, which is 20 over 10, which is 2. In general the mechanical advantage of a pulley system equals the number of rope sections supporting the load, and for a single movable pulley that number is 2. This counting rule is the key to every pulley system that follows, so it is worth stating carefully: count the sections of rope that pull upwards on the movable block or on the load; do not count the section that runs over a fixed pulley to the effort, since that section pulls on the fixed point, not on the load.

The trade-off follows from the rope. To raise the load 300 millimetres, both supporting sections must shorten by 300, so 600 millimetres of rope must be pulled through at the free end. Effort distance is twice the load distance. Work check: half the force times twice the distance equals the load times its distance; work in equals work out, less friction. The movable pulley has bought a halving of force with a doubling of rope, exactly as the hydraulic cylinders bought force with distance.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting rope sections to find the tension in each, why the effort is half the load, and the distance trade-off.

## Subtopic: Measuring the Movable Pulley and Its Trade-Off

The investigation measures the movable pulley against the fixed one. Rig a single fixed pulley with a 20 newton load, measure the effort with a spring balance and the rope pulled for a 300 millimetre lift: expect about 20 newtons and 300 millimetres, mechanical advantage 1. Then rig the single movable pulley with the same load and pulley: expect about 10 newtons plus friction and the pulley's weight, and 600 millimetres of rope for a 300 millimetre lift, mechanical advantage about 2. Repeat with 10 and 30 newton loads to show the ratio holds. Record load, effort, load distance, effort distance and calculated mechanical advantage in a table, and note the direction of the effort in each case, downward for the fixed pulley and upward for the movable one.

The measured mechanical advantage is a little under 2, perhaps 1.8, because the effort must also lift the pulley block's own weight and overcome friction at its axle. A heavy block on a light load gives a poor result, which is why real pulley blocks are made as light as their strength allows. Expressing this honestly, the ideal mechanical advantage from counting rope sections is 2, and the actual mechanical advantage from measurement is less; the difference is the efficiency of the system.

Applications where the movable pulley's halving matters: lifting a motorbike engine with a rope tied to a roof beam, hauling a boat up a slipway with the rope anchored at the top, a child's swing seat hoist, the hook block of a crane. The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the rope that goes to the effort over a fixed pulley as a supporting section; it supports nothing. Two: forgetting the pulley block's weight, which reduces the measured advantage. Three: expecting the load to rise as far as the rope is pulled; it rises half as far. Four: thinking the movable pulley changes the direction of the pull; alone it does not, and the pull is upward.

The questions for this section are with you now: the comparison investigation and its expected results, why the measured advantage is below 2, and applications.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the rope tied to the beam and the pulley hanging from the bucket — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Wheel Goes Up With the Load

This time the pulley moves. Tie the rope to the beam. Run it down round a pulley. Hang the bucket from the pulley. Pull up on the free end. The pulley and the bucket rise together.

Builders hauling mortar up scaffolding, sailors hoisting a dinghy, a crane's hook block: all movable pulleys.

The awkward part: you have to pull upwards from above. Next lesson we add a fixed pulley on top to turn the pull downwards. For now, keep it simple.

Keep the pulley riding up the rope in mind and try the questions for this part: a pulley that moves.

## Subtopic: Two Strings Share the Weight

Count the rope sections holding the load up. Two: one from the beam down to the pulley, one from the pulley up to your hand. Same rope, same tension in both. Two sections share the load, so each carries half.

You pull on one section, so you pull half the load. 20 newton bucket, 10 newton pull. Mechanical advantage 2. Rule: mechanical advantage equals the number of rope sections holding the load up.

Price: both sections must shorten to lift the load, so you pull twice as much rope. 300 up means 600 pulled. Half the force, double the rope.

Hold two strings share the weight in mind and try this part's questions: why half.

## Subtopic: Half the Pull, Twice the Rope

Test both. Fixed pulley, 20 newton bucket: about 20 newtons, 300 pulled for 300 up, pull downwards. Movable pulley, same bucket: about 10 newtons, 600 pulled for 300 up, pull upwards. Try 10 and 30 newtons too. Write a table.

You will get a bit under 2, maybe 1.8, because you also lift the pulley itself and fight its axle friction. Ideal is 2 from counting; actual is what you measure.

Uses: lifting an engine from a roof beam, hauling a boat up a slipway, a crane's hook block.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A movable pulley hangs from the load and rides up the rope; two rope sections support the load, each carrying half, so the effort is half the load and the mechanical advantage is 2, while the rope must be pulled twice as far. Count supporting rope sections to find the mechanical advantage. Measured values fall short of 2 because of the block's weight and friction, and the pull is upwards until a fixed pulley is added.

Hold half the pull, twice the rope, and take the final questions of the lesson: the trade-off.
