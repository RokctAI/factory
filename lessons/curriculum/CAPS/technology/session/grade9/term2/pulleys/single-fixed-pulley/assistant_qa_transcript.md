### subtopic_1 — The Fixed Pulley: A Wheel That Changes Direction
**Assistant:** If the mechanical advantage is 1, why does lifting with a pulley feel so much easier than lifting by hand?

**Tutor:** Because feeling easier and needing less force are different things, and the fixed pulley improves the first without touching the second. Lift a 10 kilogram bucket straight up from a well without a pulley and you must bend over the edge, grip the rope above the water and pull upwards with your arms and back, with your body weight working against you and your balance at risk. The force is 100 newtons. Put a pulley on a beam over the well and the same 100 newtons is now a downward pull: you stand upright, grip the rope at chest height, and lean back so that gravity pulling on your body does much of the work, your legs and the strongest muscles of your back take the strain, and if you like you can walk backwards. The muscles involved are bigger and the posture is far better, so the same force costs far less effort in the everyday sense of the word, and you can keep it up longer. There is also the matter of where the effort can be applied: with a rope over a pulley, two or three people can take hold along the rope and pull together, which is impossible when one person is leaning over a hole. And there is safety, since the lifter is nowhere near the drop or the swinging load. So the pulley has not reduced the newtons, and a spring balance proves it, but it has changed who does the work, your weight and legs rather than your arms, and where you stand while doing it, and for a human being that is the difference between a job that is easy and one that hurts.

### subtopic_2 — Why the Mechanical Advantage Is 1
**Assistant:** Why does the ceiling bracket carry twice the load? The load is only 100 newtons.

**Tutor:** Because the rope pulls down on the pulley from both sides, and the bracket has to hold up everything that is pulling on the pulley. Look at the pulley when the load is hanging still and you are holding the rope. On the load side, the rope pulls down with the weight of the load, 100 newtons. On your side, the rope pulls down with your effort, which for a fixed pulley is also 100 newtons, since the tension is the same throughout the rope. The pulley wheel is being pulled down by both of those, a total of 200 newtons, plus its own small weight, and the only thing stopping it from falling is the bracket and the beam above it. So the bracket must be fixed to carry at least 200 newtons for a 100 newton load, and if you jerk the rope or several people pull at once, more. Builders and riggers know this as a rule of thumb: a fixed anchor point for a single pulley is loaded to about twice the weight being lifted. It is the reason flagpole brackets, crane jibs and well frames are built more heavily than the load alone would suggest, and the reason a learner who hooks a pulley onto a flimsy shelf bracket to lift a bucket may bring the shelf down. The movable pulley in the next session behaves differently, since there the anchor carries only half the load plus half the effort, and seeing why is a good test of whether the idea of rope tension has been understood. For now, the fixed pulley rule is simple: whatever hangs from one side, an equal pull comes down the other, and the beam holds both.

### subtopic_3 — Where Fixed Pulleys Earn Their Place
**Assistant:** Does the size of the pulley wheel change the mechanical advantage?

**Tutor:** No, and understanding why is a useful check on the whole idea. The mechanical advantage of a fixed pulley comes from the rope, not the wheel: one continuous rope has one tension, so effort equals load whatever the rope runs over. A wheel of 50 millimetres and a wheel of 500 millimetres both give a mechanical advantage of 1, and a rope run over a smooth fixed bar with no wheel at all would also give 1 in principle, though friction would spoil it in practice. What the wheel's size changes is friction, wear and rope life. A larger wheel turns more slowly for the same rope speed, bends the rope through a gentler curve so its fibres are not crushed at the inside of the bend, and spreads the load over a longer arc of groove, so a big wheel runs smoothly and the rope lasts for years; a tiny wheel on a thick rope wears the rope and feels stiff. This is why crane pulleys for steel cable are large and why the pulley in a gym machine is sized to its cable. The axle matters more than the diameter for the measured effort: a dry or rusty axle adds friction that makes the spring balance read noticeably above the load, and a freely turning, lubricated axle brings the reading close to the ideal. In a block and tackle, which the next sessions build, it is again the number of rope sections supporting the load that fixes the mechanical advantage, not the diameter of any wheel; the wheels only need to be big enough to run freely and to keep the rope sections apart. Diameter is a design choice about durability and smoothness, and a learner who hears that bigger pulleys lift more has mixed up two separate ideas.
