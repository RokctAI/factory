### subtopic_1 — A Pulley That Rides on the Rope
**Assistant:** Why does hanging the load from the pulley instead of the beam make such a difference? It is the same pulley and the same rope.

**Tutor:** Because what matters is not the pulley but how many pieces of rope are holding the load up, and moving the pulley changes that count from one to two. With the fixed pulley, the load hangs from a single section of rope; whatever that section's tension is, it must equal the whole load, and since you pull on the same rope, you pull the whole load. With the movable pulley, the load hangs from the pulley block, and the block is held up by the rope on both sides of it: the section running up to the beam and the section running up to your hand. Those two sections are parts of one rope and so have the same tension, and together they must hold the whole load, so each holds half, and your hand, pulling on one of them, feels half. The pulley wheel's only job is to let the rope slide round from one section to the other without friction so that the tensions really are equal; a smooth ring would do the same in principle. You can prove the point without a pulley at all by tying a loop of string round a heavy book and lifting it with both hands on the two sides of the loop: each hand feels half the book. Add more sections and each carries a smaller share, which is where the block and tackle is going. Understanding that the advantage lives in the rope sections, not in the wheel, is what lets you look at any tangle of pulleys and work out its mechanical advantage by counting.

### subtopic_2 — Two Rope Sections, Half the Effort
**Assistant:** If I get a mechanical advantage of 1.8 instead of 2, did I do the experiment wrong?

**Tutor:** You did it right, and the 1.8 is more informative than a 2 would have been, because it shows you the difference between an ideal machine and a real one. The ideal mechanical advantage of 2 comes from counting rope sections and assumes a weightless pulley block with a perfectly free axle. Your pulley block has weight, perhaps 1 newton for a small plastic one, and that weight hangs from the same two rope sections as the load, so your hand lifts half the block as well as half the load; with a 20 newton load the effort becomes 10.5 newtons before friction. The axle also has friction, which makes the tension on your side a little higher than on the beam side when the load is moving, perhaps another half newton. So the effort reads about 11 newtons, and 20 divided by 11 is 1.8. The ratio of actual to ideal mechanical advantage, 1.8 over 2, which is 0.9 or 90 percent, is the efficiency of your pulley system, and it is exactly the quantity engineers quote for real lifting gear. You can test the explanation: use a heavier load, say 50 newtons, and the block's weight and friction become a smaller share, so the measured advantage creeps closer to 2; use a lighter load, 5 newtons, and it falls well below. Recording the ideal figure from counting, the measured figure from the balance and the efficiency as their ratio is the honest way to write up the investigation, and it is a far better result than a suspiciously perfect 2.

### subtopic_3 — Measuring the Movable Pulley and Its Trade-Off
**Assistant:** Why do cranes put the pulley on the hook if it means pulling twice as much cable?

**Tutor:** Because for a crane, cable is cheap and force is expensive, and the trade suits it perfectly. A crane's hoist is driven by a winch motor, and the motor's maximum pull, together with the strength of the cable, is what limits the load the crane can lift. Hang the hook from a movable block with two supporting cable sections and the same motor and cable can lift twice the load; hang it from a block with four sections and the crane lifts four times as much. The cost is that the winch must wind in two or four times as much cable for every metre the load rises, so the hook moves more slowly, but a motor runs happily all day, and a slower lift of a heavier load is exactly what a construction site wants, since nobody wants a ten-tonne beam moving fast. It also lets a thinner, lighter cable do the work, because each section carries only a share of the load, and thin cable is easier to wind on a drum and to run over pulleys. Mobile cranes often let the operator change the number of sections, called parts of line, rigging the hook block with two parts for light fast lifts and six or eight for the heaviest loads. The same reasoning applies to the block and tackle on a sailing ship or a workshop chain hoist: a person's pull is limited, so the gear multiplies it and the person simply hauls more rope. Wherever the input can supply plenty of distance but only a limited force, a movable pulley is the right choice, and that describes most lifting.
