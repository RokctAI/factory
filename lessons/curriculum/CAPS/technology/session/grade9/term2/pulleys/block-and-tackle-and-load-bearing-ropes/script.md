# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the block and tackle and the relationship between the number of load-bearing ropes and the mechanical advantage. The anchor is a mechanic's workshop in Kimberley with a chain block hanging from a steel beam, lifting an engine block out of a bakkie, the mechanic hauling an arm's length of chain for every few centimetres the engine rises. The governing idea is that a tackle is a fixed block and a movable block joined by one rope, that the tension is the same throughout that rope, and that the load is shared equally among the rope sections that pull up on the movable block, so the ideal mechanical advantage equals the number of load-bearing rope sections. Counting load-bearing ropes, stating the mechanical advantage and the distance trade-off, and explaining why measured values fall short are examinable.

## Subtopic: Combining Fixed and Movable Pulleys Into a Block and Tackle

A block is a frame holding one or more pulley wheels, called sheaves, on a common axle. A tackle is the rope threaded through two blocks. The upper block is fixed to a beam or hook; the lower block is attached to the load and moves with it. One end of the rope is tied to one of the blocks, the rope runs down round a sheave in the lower block, up round a sheave in the upper block, and so on, until the free end leaves the upper block and comes down to the person pulling. The fixed block does the job of the single fixed pulley from an earlier session: it turns the pull downwards so the lifter can use body weight. The movable block does the job of the single movable pulley: it hangs the load from several rope sections at once.

The simplest tackle has one sheave in each block. The rope is tied to the upper block, runs down round the lower sheave, up round the upper sheave and down to the hand. Two sections of rope pull up on the lower block, so the mechanical advantage is 2, and the pull is conveniently downward. Add a second sheave to the lower block and the rope can be tied to the lower block instead, giving three sections pulling up on it: mechanical advantage 3. Two sheaves in each block give four sections: mechanical advantage 4. Real chain blocks in workshops use this principle with chain instead of rope and a geared wheel to reduce the effort further.

The block and tackle appears wherever a heavy load must be lifted by hand or by a small motor: the hoist on a fishing boat in Hout Bay, the main sheet tackle on a sailing dinghy at Theewaterskloof, the engine hoist in a Kimberley workshop, the boom of a mobile crane whose steel cable runs several times between the boom tip and the hook block, the lifting tackle in a stage fly tower. Each of these is two blocks and one rope, and each can be understood by the same counting rule that follows.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a block and a tackle are, how the fixed and movable blocks each contribute, and where tackles are used.

## Subtopic: Counting Load-Bearing Ropes to Find the Mechanical Advantage

The rule for any tackle is that the ideal mechanical advantage equals the number of rope sections that support the movable block. The reasoning is the same as for the single movable pulley. The rope is one continuous piece passing freely over the sheaves, so the tension is the same everywhere along it. Every section of rope that runs upwards from the movable block pulls on it with that tension. If there are three such sections and the load is 30 newtons, then three times the tension equals 30, so the tension is 10 newtons, and since the effort pulls on the same rope, the effort is 10 newtons. Mechanical advantage is 30 over 10, which is 3.

The counting must be done carefully. Only sections attached to or passing round the movable block count. The section that leaves the upper block and goes to the hand does not support the load; it pulls down on the fixed block, which is held by the beam. A quick way to check: if the rope end is tied to the movable block, the number of load-bearing sections is odd; if it is tied to the fixed block, it is even. Draw the tackle, mark each section of rope with an arrow showing which block it pulls on, and count the arrows pointing up at the movable block.

The trade-off follows at once. To raise the load by one metre, every load-bearing section must shorten by one metre, so the free end must be pulled through a number of metres equal to the number of load-bearing sections. A mechanical advantage of 4 means hauling four metres of rope for every metre of lift. Work in equals work out, less friction: effort times effort distance equals load times load distance. A tackle is a machine for exchanging distance for force, never for getting more work out than is put in.

The questions for this section are with you now: the counting rule and its reasoning, which sections count, and the distance trade-off.

## Subtopic: Investigating Two-, Three- and Four-Rope Tackles

The investigation builds three tackles with the same two pulley blocks and the same 20 newton load, measuring effort with a spring balance and rope pulled for a 200 millimetre lift. Tackle A, two load-bearing sections: expect about 10 newtons and 400 millimetres. Tackle B, three sections: about 7 newtons and 600 millimetres. Tackle C, four sections: about 5 newtons and 800 millimetres. The ideal values are 10, 6.7 and 5 newtons; the measured values will be higher by the weight of the lower block divided by the number of sections, plus friction at each sheave, which grows with the number of sheaves the rope passes over.

Tabulate load, effort, ideal mechanical advantage from counting, actual mechanical advantage from measurement, and efficiency as actual over ideal. A typical school tackle gives efficiencies between 70 and 90 percent, falling as sheaves are added, because each sheave adds a little axle friction and rope stiffness. This is why a tackle with ten sheaves would not be worth building: the friction would eat most of the advantage. Chain blocks solve the problem differently, with a worm or ratchet gear inside a small housing, a mechanism met later in the term.

Safety and good practice: never stand under a load on a tackle, keep hands clear of the sheaves, check that the hook on the fixed block is closed, and use a rope rated for the load. The error museum, four exhibits. One: counting the rope to the hand as a load-bearing section when it leaves the fixed block. Two: assuming a tackle with four sheaves always has mechanical advantage 4 without checking where the rope end is tied. Three: expecting the load to rise as far as the rope is pulled. Four: reporting the measured advantage as the ideal, or the ideal as the measured, instead of both with the efficiency between them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three-tackle investigation and its expected results, efficiency and why it falls with more sheaves, and safe practice.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two pulley blocks, one rope and a 20 newton bucket — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Blocks, One Rope

A block is a frame with a pulley wheel in it. A tackle is the rope threaded between two blocks. Top block fixed to the beam. Bottom block on the load. One rope zigzags between them and comes down to your hand.

The top block does what the fixed pulley did: lets you pull down. The bottom block does what the movable pulley did: hangs the load from several ropes.

Fishing boats, sailing dinghies, workshop engine hoists, crane booms. All two blocks and one rope.

Keep two blocks and one rope in mind and try the questions for this part: what a block and tackle is.

## Subtopic: Count the Ropes Holding the Load

Count the rope sections pulling up on the bottom block. That number is the mechanical advantage. Three sections, 30 newton load: each holds 10, you pull 10, advantage 3.

The rope going to your hand from the top block does not count. It pulls on the beam, not the load. Draw arrows, count the ones pointing up at the bottom block.

Price: you pull that many metres of rope for every metre of lift. Advantage 4 means 4 metres hauled for 1 metre up.

Hold count the ropes holding the load in mind and try this part's questions: the counting rule.

## Subtopic: More Ropes, Less Pull, Longer Haul

Build three tackles with the same blocks and bucket. Two ropes: about 10 newtons, 400 pulled for 200 up. Three ropes: about 7 newtons, 600 pulled. Four ropes: about 5 newtons, 800 pulled. Table it.

Measured efforts are a bit high because you lift the bottom block too and each wheel rubs. Efficiency is actual divided by ideal, usually 70 to 90 percent, dropping as you add wheels.

Never stand under the load. Keep fingers away from the wheels. Check the hook is closed.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A block and tackle is a fixed block and a movable block joined by one rope. The tension is the same throughout the rope, so the load is shared by the rope sections pulling up on the movable block, and the ideal mechanical advantage equals the number of those load-bearing sections. The rope must be pulled that many times further than the load rises. Measured advantage falls below the count because of the lower block's weight and friction at each sheave; efficiency is actual over ideal.

Hold more ropes, less pull, longer haul, and take the final questions of the lesson: the investigation.
