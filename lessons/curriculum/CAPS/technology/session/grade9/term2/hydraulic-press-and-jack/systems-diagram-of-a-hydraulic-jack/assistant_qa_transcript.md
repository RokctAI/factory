### subtopic_1 — Systems Thinking: Input, Process, Output
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a systems diagram and the flow chart we drew in Term 1? They both have boxes and arrows.

**Tutor:** They share the shapes and differ in what the boxes stand for, and the difference is worth keeping clear because the two are used for different jobs. A flow chart describes a procedure: the steps a person or a machine carries out, in order, with decision diamonds where the path splits, start and end ovals, and loops back when a check fails. Its boxes are actions: cut the ramp legs, check the gradient, glue. It answers the question what do I do next. A systems diagram describes a system: what flows into it, how the flows are transformed stage by stage, and what flows out. Its boxes are stages or components that act on the flow: lever, pump, valve, ram, and the arrows carry the thing being transformed, here force, oil and pressure. It answers the question what does this machine do to its inputs. There are no decision diamonds, because the diagram is not a sequence of choices, and the feedback arrow is a flow of information rather than a loop back to redo a step. You could draw a flow chart for using a jack, insert handle, pump until the wheel clears the ground, place the stand, and a systems diagram of the jack, hand force to lifted load, and both would be correct; they are simply about different things, the first about the user's procedure and the second about the machine's function. In this term the systems diagram is the tool for understanding and comparing mechanisms, and in Term 4 it describes a recycling plant, while the flow chart returns whenever you plan a making process.

### subtopic_2 — Drawing the Systems Diagram of the Jack
**Assistant:** Why is the lifted car the output and not the force on the ram? The ram force is what the jack produces.

**Tutor:** Because the output of a system is its purpose, the result its user wanted, and nobody buys a jack to produce a force on a ram; they buy it to lift a car. The ram force is a real and important quantity, but in the systems view it is an intermediate output, the result of one process stage, the ram, which becomes the input to the final transformation, raising the load against gravity. Drawing it that way keeps the diagram honest about what the machine is for and makes it comparable with other machines: a scissor jack, a trolley jack and a hydraulic jack all have the same output, a lifted car, so a diagram of each shows at a glance that they differ only in process. It also shows where waste goes. The jack's processes produce a little heat from friction in the seals and valves, and that heat is an output too, an unwanted one, drawn as a small arrow leaving the process; counting it reminds the designer that not all the input energy reaches the purpose. The same reasoning applies to any system. The output of a kettle is hot water, not a hot element; the output of a car's brakes is a stopped car, not pressure in the lines; the output of a recycling plant is pellets of clean plastic, not shredded flakes. Naming the true output first, and then working back through the stages that produce it, is the surest way to draw a systems diagram that means something, and it is the question to ask of your own machine design later in the term: what, exactly, is this for.

### subtopic_3 — Feedback, Control and Where Systems Diagrams Are Used
**Assistant:** Is the overload valve really control? It just opens when the pressure is too high.

**Tutor:** That is exactly what control is, and the overload valve is the simplest clear example of it that you can hold in your hand. Control means that a system's behaviour is adjusted in response to information about its own state or output, and the adjustment can be made by a person or by a mechanism. When you watch the car rise and stop pumping, you are the controller: the information is what you see, the adjustment is your hand stopping. The overload valve does the same job without you. The information is the oil pressure, which is a direct measure of how hard the ram is pushing; the comparison is made by a spring set to the rated pressure; and the adjustment is the valve lifting off its seat and bypassing oil to the reservoir so the pressure cannot rise further. Sense, compare, act: that is the pattern of every automatic control system, from a toilet cistern's float valve to the thermostat in a geyser to the cruise control in a car, and it is the pattern Term 3's electronic circuits will build with sensors, transistors and switches. On the systems diagram the overload valve appears as a branch from the pressurised oil stage back to the reservoir with a note saying opens above rated pressure, and that branch is a feedback loop even though no information travels along a separate wire, because the pressure itself is both the quantity being controlled and the signal that triggers the control. Recognising control in a simple valve is a good preparation for recognising it in a circuit, where the sensing, comparing and acting are done by separate components and are easier to see but harder to feel.
