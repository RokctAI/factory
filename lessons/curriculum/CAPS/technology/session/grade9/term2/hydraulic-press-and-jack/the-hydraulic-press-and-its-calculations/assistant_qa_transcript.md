### subtopic_1 — What a Hydraulic Press Is and What It Does
**Assistant:** Why does the press use oil and not water, if water works in the syringes?

**Tutor:** Water works perfectly well for the physics, which is why the classroom uses it, but a machine that must work for years in a workshop has needs beyond the physics, and oil meets them. The first is lubrication: the plunger and ram slide inside their cylinders with rubber or leather seals rubbing on polished steel, and oil keeps those seals slippery and long-lived where water would let them wear and leak. The second is corrosion: steel cylinders and valves in constant contact with water rust from the inside, pitting the polished surfaces so the seals fail, while oil coats and protects them. The third is temperature: water freezes in a Free State winter and boils in a hot press working hard, and either would stop the machine, while hydraulic oil stays liquid across the whole range. A fourth is that oil is a slightly better electrical insulator and does not encourage algae and scale. Against this, oil costs more, must be disposed of carefully, and makes a mess when it leaks, which is why the classroom syringes stay with water. Some systems still use water or a water-glycol mix where fire is a risk, since hydraulic oil burns, for example in foundries and mines, and the brakes of a car use a special fluid that resists boiling and absorbs little water. The calculation is identical for any of them, because Pascal's principle does not care what the liquid is, only that it does not compress.

### subtopic_2 — The Press Equation: Force Over Area Both Sides
**Assistant:** If the press multiplies force by 25, why does it also have a lever on the pump? Is that not double counting?

**Tutor:** It is double multiplying, not double counting, and the two stages work in series so their advantages multiply together. The hydraulic stage, the area ratio of 25, takes whatever force arrives at the pump plunger and delivers 25 times that force at the ram. The lever stage sits before it: the mechanic pushes the end of a long handle, perhaps 600 millimetres from the pivot, while the plunger is attached 60 millimetres from the pivot, so the handle is a second-class lever with a mechanical advantage of 10, and a hand push of 20 newtons becomes 200 newtons at the plunger. The hydraulic stage then turns 200 into 5 000. Overall, 20 newtons of hand force has become 5 000 at the ram, a total mechanical advantage of 250, which is the product of 10 and 25. The price, as always, is distance, and it multiplies too: the hand moves ten times as far as the plunger, and the plunger moves 25 times as far as the ram, so the hand travels 250 times the distance the ram moves, which is why a workshop press needs many strokes of the handle for each centimetre of ram travel. Engineers chain advantages like this all the time, a gearbox feeding a lever feeding a hydraulic cylinder, and the rule is simple: multiply the stage advantages for the total force gain and accept the same factor in distance. In the calculation for this section only the hydraulic stage is asked for, but a question that gives a handle length and a hand force is asking you to add the lever stage first.

### subtopic_3 — Worked Calculations and Checking the Answer
**Assistant:** How do I know which area goes on top when I rearrange? I always get them the wrong way round.

**Tutor:** Stop rearranging by rote and let the physics tell you, because the physics is simpler than the algebra. The one fact to hold is that the larger area carries the larger force, since the same pressure is pushing on more square centimetres. So before you write anything, decide which force you are finding and whether it should come out bigger or smaller than the force you are given. If you are given the effort on the small cylinder and want the load on the large ram, the answer must be bigger, so you multiply by a ratio bigger than one: large area over small area. If you are given the load on the ram and want the effort on the small cylinder, the answer must be smaller, so you multiply by a ratio smaller than one: small area over large area. Written out, that is force wanted equals force given times area at the wanted end over area at the given end, and that single sentence covers both cases. Then check the answer against the decision you made at the start: 150 newtons on 4 square centimetres becoming 3 750 on 100 is bigger, as it should be; if your working gave 6, you divided when you should have multiplied, and the sense check catches it before you move on. The same habit saves you in the distance calculations, where the ratio goes the other way: the larger cylinder moves less, so the distance wanted equals the distance given times area at the given end over area at the wanted end. Knowing which quantity should be bigger is worth more than memorising which letter goes on top.
