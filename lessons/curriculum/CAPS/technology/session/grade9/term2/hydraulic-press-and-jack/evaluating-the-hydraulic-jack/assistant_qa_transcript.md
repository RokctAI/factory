### subtopic_1 — How a Hydraulic Jack Works: Pump, Valves and Ram
**Assistant:** Why does the jack hold the car up when I stop pumping? Nothing is pushing on the oil any more.

**Tutor:** Something is pushing on the oil: the car is, through the ram, and the reason the ram does not sink is that the oil has nowhere to go. When you stop pumping, the outlet one-way valve between the pump cylinder and the ram cylinder is held shut by the pressure of the oil under the ram, and the release valve is closed, so the oil under the ram is sealed in a rigid steel chamber. Oil does not compress, the chamber does not stretch, and the ram cannot move down without displacing oil, so the load stays exactly where it is with no effort from you. This is the incompressibility of liquids doing its most useful work, and it is also why a jack whose seals have started to leak sinks slowly: a few drops a minute escaping past a worn seal or a dirty valve let the ram creep down, and a car that was safely raised can be on the ground an hour later. That is one of the reasons for the rule about axle stands. The holding also depends on the valves seating cleanly, which is why dirt in the oil is the enemy of a jack and why the reservoir has a filler plug rather than being open. When you open the release valve, you are deliberately giving the oil a path back to the reservoir, and the rate of sinking is set by how far you open it, which is why the release should be turned slowly. A pneumatic jack would not hold in the same way; compressed air would let the ram bounce and creep, which is one more reason jacks are hydraulic.

### subtopic_2 — Judging the Jack: Fitness for Purpose, Cost and Safety
**Assistant:** If the jack has a safety overload valve, why do I still need an axle stand?

**Tutor:** Because the overload valve protects the jack from being asked to lift too much; it does nothing to protect you from the car coming down, and the two dangers are different. The overload valve is a spring-loaded bypass that opens if the oil pressure rises above the rated load, so that if someone tries to lift a four-tonne truck with a two-tonne jack, the oil simply flows back to the reservoir and the ram will not rise; it stops the jack bursting or bending. The ways a raised car actually falls are not about overload at all. The jack can tip, because its base is small and the car moves as it rises, or the ground is soft or sloped. The saddle can slip off a jacking point that is rounded, rusty or wrongly chosen. A seal or valve can leak and let the car sink slowly while someone is underneath. The handle can be knocked and open the release. None of these involve the overload valve. An axle stand is a rigid steel support with a wide base and a locking pin, placed under a strong part of the car once the jack has raised it; the car is then lowered slightly onto the stand so that the stand, not the oil, carries the weight, and the jack becomes a backup. A rigid stand cannot leak, creep or be released by accident. The rule in every workshop, and in the instructions supplied with every jack, is that a jack is for lifting and a stand is for holding, and nobody goes under a car held only by a jack. Evaluating the jack's safety honestly means recording that its own design is sound and that its safe use depends on this rule.

### subtopic_3 — Judging the Jack: Aesthetics and Ergonomics
**Assistant:** How can looks matter for a tool? I would buy the one that works.

**Tutor:** You would buy the one that works, and the question is how you would know which one that is while it sits in its box on the shelf. You cannot pump it, cut it open or test its seals; what you can see is its finish, its markings and its proportions, and those are the signals a maker uses to tell you about the quality you cannot see. A jack with an even, hard coat of paint, crisp stamped figures for the rated load, a machined and polished ram and a saddle that is cast cleanly tells you that the factory took care, and factories that take care with the outside usually take care with the valves and seals too. A jack with paint runs, a rough casting with flash left on the edges and a sticker instead of a stamp tells you the opposite, and experienced buyers read those signs. That is what aesthetics means for a tool: not prettiness but the visible evidence of quality, together with the sense that the object looks like what it is, strong and stable. Aesthetics also matters for safety in a quiet way, since a rating that is stamped large and clearly is read, while a small sticker is not; and it matters commercially, because two jacks of equal rating and similar price sell on how they look, so manufacturers invest in finish. In the evaluation table, aesthetics gets its own row with its own evidence, the finish, the markings, the proportions, and it is kept separate from ergonomics, which is about how the jack feels in the hands and under the car rather than how it looks to the eye.
