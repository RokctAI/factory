# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the hydraulic press, including simple calculations using Pascal's principle. The anchor is a workshop press in a panel-beating shop in Welkom, a steel frame with a small pump cylinder worked by a lever and a large ram above the bed, pressing a bearing out of a wheel hub with a force the mechanic could never apply by hand. The governing idea is that the press is two cylinders of different areas sharing one pressure, so that force divided by area on the input side equals force divided by area on the output side, and that this equation can be rearranged to find any one of the four quantities from the other three. Setting up and solving press calculations with correct units and a sense check is examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Hydraulic Press Is and What It Does

A hydraulic press is a machine for producing a very large force over a short distance, used to squeeze, bend, punch, flatten or push things that no hand or lever could manage. It has a small cylinder, called the pump or effort cylinder, into which a plunger is driven by a lever or a motor, and a large cylinder, called the ram or load cylinder, whose piston carries a flat plate or a tool. The two are joined by a pipe and filled with oil. Oil is used rather than water because it lubricates the seals, does not rust the cylinders and does not freeze.

Press the effort plunger and oil is forced into the ram cylinder; by Pascal's principle the pressure is the same in both, and because the ram piston has a much larger area it pushes with a much larger force. A workshop press might have an effort cylinder of 2 square centimetres and a ram of 50 square centimetres, so a push of 200 newtons on the plunger produces 5 000 newtons at the ram, the weight of half a tonne, and a long lever on the plunger multiplies the hand's force before it even reaches the oil. Larger presses in factories produce forces of millions of newtons to stamp car body panels from sheet steel or to press clay into bricks.

The press is the hydraulic jack turned upside down, and the two share every principle: a one-way valve lets oil into the pump cylinder from a reservoir on the up-stroke and into the ram on the down-stroke, so repeated pumping drives the ram steadily, and a release valve lets the oil flow back so the ram returns. Uses in South Africa include pressing bearings and bushes in vehicle repair, bending steel plate in fabrication shops, compacting scrap metal and waste paper into bales, pressing bricks and roof tiles, extracting oil from sunflower seed, and forming the panels of the cars built in East London and Pretoria.

## Subtopic: The Press Equation: Force Over Area Both Sides

The equation comes from Pascal's principle. The pressure in the oil is the same at both cylinders. Pressure at the effort cylinder is effort force divided by effort area; pressure at the ram is load force divided by ram area. So effort force divided by effort area equals load force divided by ram area. In symbols, if the effort force is F1 on area A1 and the load force is F2 on area A2, then F1 over A1 equals F2 over A2.

Rearranging gives any quantity from the other three. Load force F2 equals F1 times A2 over A1: the input force multiplied by the area ratio. Effort force F1 equals F2 times A1 over A2. Ram area A2 equals A1 times F2 over F1, which is how a designer sizes the ram for a required force. Effort area A1 equals A2 times F1 over F2. In every form, the larger area goes with the larger force and the smaller area with the smaller force.

Units: forces in newtons, areas in square centimetres or square metres, the same on both sides, so that the ratio is dimensionless. Pressure, if asked for, is force in newtons over area in square metres, giving pascals, or over square centimetres, giving newtons per square centimetre, which is 10 kilopascals. If a mass is given rather than a force, convert with weight equals mass times 10 newtons per kilogram, so a 500 kilogram load is 5 000 newtons.

## Subtopic: Worked Calculations and Checking the Answer

Example one: effort area 4 square centimetres, ram area 100 square centimetres, effort force 150 newtons; find the load force. F2 equals F1 times A2 over A1, which is 150 times 100 over 4, which is 150 times 25, which is 3 750 newtons. Check: the ram is 25 times the area, so 25 times the force; 3 750 is 25 times 150. Sense: large area, large force. Correct.

Example two: a press must lift a 1 200 kilogram engine with a ram of 60 square centimetres; the effort cylinder is 3 square centimetres; find the effort force. Weight is 1 200 times 10, which is 12 000 newtons. F1 equals F2 times A1 over A2, which is 12 000 times 3 over 60, which is 12 000 over 20, which is 600 newtons. Check: area ratio 20, so the effort is a twentieth of the load; 600 is a twentieth of 12 000. Sense: small area, small force. A lever on the plunger would reduce the 600 newtons to something a hand can manage.

Example three: find the pressure in example one. Pressure equals F1 over A1, which is 150 over 4, which is 37.5 newtons per square centimetre, or 375 kilopascals; and F2 over A2 is 3 750 over 100, also 37.5. The equal pressures confirm the working. The method, every time: write the equation, substitute with units, rearrange for the unknown, calculate, and check by asking whether the larger area has the larger force and whether the ratio of forces equals the ratio of areas. The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting the large area with the small force. Two: using a mass in kilograms as if it were a force in newtons. Three: mixing square centimetres on one side with square metres on the other. Four: skipping the sense check, so that a ram force smaller than the effort force goes unnoticed.

## Subtopic: A Machine That Squashes

A hydraulic press squashes, bends or pushes things with a huge force. Small cylinder where you push, big cylinder where the work happens, oil between. Oil, not water, because it oils the seals and does not rust or freeze.

Push the small plunger, oil goes to the big one, same pressure, bigger area, bigger force. 2 square centimetres in, 50 out: push 200 newtons and get 5 000.

It is a jack upside down. Pump, one-way valve, release valve. Used for pressing bearings, bending plate, baling scrap, making bricks, squeezing oil from seeds, stamping car panels.

Keep the small and big cylinders in mind and try the questions for this part: a machine that squashes.

## Subtopic: Same Pressure, Two Areas

Same pressure both sides. Pressure is force over area. So force over area at the small end equals force over area at the big end. F1 over A1 equals F2 over A2.

Want the big force? F2 equals F1 times A2 over A1. Want the small force? F1 equals F2 times A1 over A2. Want an area? Rearrange the same way. Big area always goes with big force.

Forces in newtons. Areas the same units both sides. A mass in kilograms times 10 gives newtons.

Hold F over A equals F over A in mind and try this part's questions: same pressure, two areas.

## Subtopic: Three Steps Every Time

Example: 4 square centimetres in, 100 out, push 150 newtons. 150 times 100 over 4 is 3 750 newtons. Check: 25 times the area, 25 times the force. Yes.

Example: lift 1 200 kilograms, that is 12 000 newtons, ram 60, pump 3. 12 000 times 3 over 60 is 600 newtons. Check: a twentieth. Yes.

Three steps every time: write the equation, put in the numbers with units, rearrange and work out. Then check: does the big area have the big force?

Pocket summary of the lesson. A hydraulic press is a small effort cylinder and a large ram sharing one oil pressure. Force over area is equal on both sides, so the load force is the effort force times the area ratio. Convert masses to newtons, keep area units the same, and check that the larger area carries the larger force.
