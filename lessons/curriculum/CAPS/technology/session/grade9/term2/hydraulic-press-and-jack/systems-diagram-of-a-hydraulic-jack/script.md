# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the systems diagram of how a hydraulic jack functions. The anchor is a whiteboard in a Technology room in Vereeniging with a bottle jack on the desk beneath it and three empty boxes drawn in a row labelled Input, Process and Output, waiting to be filled. The governing idea is that any machine can be described as a system that takes inputs, transforms them through a sequence of processes and delivers outputs, that a systems diagram shows this with labelled boxes and arrows in order, and that adding feedback and control shows how the user or the machine adjusts the output. Drawing a correct, ordered systems diagram of the jack and reading one is examinable, and the skill transfers to any mechanism or process.

## Subtopic: Systems Thinking: Input, Process, Output

A system is a set of parts that work together to do a job. Systems thinking describes it in three stages. Input: what goes into the system to make it work, which may be energy, a force, a material or information. Process: what the system does to the input, the changes it makes, often in several steps. Output: what comes out, the result the system was built for, together with any waste or by-products. A kettle takes in cold water and electrical energy, heats the water, and gives out hot water and some steam; a bicycle takes in the rider's pushing force on the pedals, turns it through cranks, chain and wheels, and gives out forward motion.

Describing a machine this way is useful because it separates what the machine is for from how it does it. The output is the purpose; the input is what the user must supply; the process is the designer's business. It also lets very different machines be compared: a hydraulic jack and a scissor jack have the same input, a hand pushing, and the same output, a lifted car, but completely different processes, and the systems view puts that difference where it belongs.

Processes usually have several steps in order, and each step is a small system of its own with its own input and output: the output of one step is the input of the next. For the jack the hand's force is the input to the lever, the lever's output force is the input to the pump plunger, the plunger's output of pressurised oil is the input to the valves and the ram, and the ram's output is the lifted load. Keeping the steps in order is what makes a systems diagram informative rather than a list of parts.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Systems Diagram of the Jack

The systems diagram uses rectangles for stages and arrows for the flow from one to the next, left to right. For the jack: Input box: force from the user's hand on the handle, plus the oil in the reservoir and the load to be lifted. Process boxes in order: the lever handle multiplies the hand force about ten times and moves the pump plunger; the pump plunger forces oil through the outlet one-way valve, raising its pressure; the inlet one-way valve admits oil from the reservoir on the up-stroke; the pressurised oil enters the ram cylinder; the ram piston, with about fifty times the pump's area, converts the pressure into a large force and rises a small distance per stroke. Output box: the load raised and held at the chosen height. A second output: heat from friction in the seals, which is the small energy loss.

The release path is drawn as a separate process branch: user turns the release valve; oil flows from the ram cylinder back to the reservoir; the ram descends under the load. It is shown with its own arrows from the ram back to the reservoir, so the diagram shows both lifting and lowering.

The diagram is labelled with the quantities that change: force gets larger from the handle through the plunger to the ram, distance gets smaller, pressure appears at the plunger and is the same through the oil to the ram. A good diagram is not a picture of the jack; it is a map of what happens to the force, the oil and the load, which is why a reader who has never seen a jack can follow it. The habit of drawing it is the same one used for the flow chart in Term 1, with the difference that a flow chart shows the steps a person takes to make something and a systems diagram shows what a machine does to its inputs.

## Subtopic: Feedback, Control and Where Systems Diagrams Are Used

Real systems adjust themselves or are adjusted, and the systems diagram shows this with feedback and control. Feedback is information about the output fed back to an earlier stage so that the input or process can be changed: the user watches the car rising, the feedback, and stops pumping when it is high enough, the control action. On the diagram, feedback is drawn as an arrow from the output back to the input or to a control box, usually along the bottom of the diagram, labelled with what is observed.

The jack has two control elements. The user is one, controlling the number of strokes and the opening of the release valve by watching the load. The overload valve is the other, an automatic control: if the oil pressure rises above the rated value, the valve opens and bypasses oil to the reservoir, so the ram cannot lift more than the jack is designed for. On the diagram the overload valve is a box on a branch from the pressurised oil back to the reservoir, labelled opens above rated pressure. An automatic control like this needs no person and is the beginning of the control systems that Term 3's electronics will build.

Systems diagrams are used throughout technology and far beyond: a car's braking system, a water purification plant, a factory line, a plastics recycling plant, a school's timetable, a computer program. Engineers draw them first to agree what a machine must do before deciding how, and to find the step where a failure or an improvement would matter most. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing the parts of the jack as a picture and calling it a systems diagram; the diagram shows stages and flows, not shapes. Two: listing the process steps in the wrong order, so the ram rises before the oil is pressurised. Three: leaving out the output, so the diagram has no purpose. Four: forgetting feedback, so the diagram shows a jack that pumps forever.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of feedback and control, the two control elements of the jack, and where systems diagrams are used.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with three empty boxes on the board and a jack on the desk — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: What Goes In, What Happens, What Comes Out

A system is parts working together to do a job. Describe it in three stages. Input: what goes in. Process: what happens to it. Output: what comes out.

Kettle: cold water and electricity in, heating, hot water out. Bicycle: pedal push in, cranks and chain and wheels, moving forward out.

The output is the point of the machine. The input is what you supply. The process is how. Each process step feeds the next: the output of one is the input of the next.

Keep in, happens, out in mind and try the questions for this part: the three stages.

## Subtopic: Boxes and Arrows for the Jack

Boxes in a row, arrows left to right. Input: your hand pushing the handle, oil in the store, the car to lift.

Process, in order: handle lever makes the force ten times bigger; pump plunger pushes oil through the one-way valve, raising the pressure; the inlet valve lets more oil in on the up-stroke; pressurised oil enters the ram cylinder; the big ram turns pressure into a big force and rises a little.

Output: the car lifted and held. Small extra output: a bit of heat from friction. Lowering is a separate branch: release valve, oil back to the store, ram goes down.

Label what changes: force grows, distance shrinks, pressure the same through the oil. It is a map of what happens, not a picture of the jack.

Hold the boxes in order in mind and try this part's questions: boxes and arrows for the jack.

## Subtopic: Adding the Loop

Feedback is information about the output sent back so you can adjust. You watch the car rise and stop pumping when it is high enough. Draw it as an arrow from the output back to the start, along the bottom.

Two controls: you, choosing strokes and the release, and the overload valve, which opens by itself if the pressure gets too high and sends oil back to the store. No person needed.

Systems diagrams describe brakes, water plants, factory lines, recycling plants, even a timetable. Engineers draw them first to agree what a machine must do.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A system has input, process and output, drawn as boxes and arrows in order. The jack's diagram runs from hand force through lever, pump, valves, pressurised oil and ram to the lifted load, with a release branch back to the reservoir. Feedback is an arrow from output back to input; the user and the overload valve are the controls. The diagram is a map of flows, not a picture of parts.
