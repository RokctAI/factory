# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the hydraulic jack: how it functions and how its design is evaluated for fitness for purpose, cost, aesthetics, safety and ergonomics. The anchor is a two-tonne bottle jack on a workshop floor in Springs, beside a car with a flat tyre, a wheel chock and an axle stand, with a learner about to pump the handle. The governing idea is that the jack is Pascal's principle packaged with valves so that a small pump cylinder can drive a large ram in many strokes and hold the load between them, and that evaluating it means asking, with evidence, whether it does its job for its users, at a reasonable price, looking acceptable, without harming anyone and comfortably in the hand. Explaining the jack's operation and evaluating a product against these criteria are examinable.

## Subtopic: How a Hydraulic Jack Works: Pump, Valves and Ram

A bottle jack is a short steel cylinder with a ram that rises out of the top, a small pump cylinder beside it worked by a lever handle, a reservoir of oil around the ram cylinder, and two valves. Place it under a car's jacking point, insert the handle and pump. On the up-stroke of the pump plunger, oil is drawn from the reservoir through the inlet one-way valve into the pump cylinder. On the down-stroke, the inlet valve closes and the oil is forced through the outlet one-way valve into the ram cylinder, raising the ram a little. The outlet valve then closes and holds the oil, and the ram, in place while the plunger rises for the next stroke.

Each stroke moves a small volume from the small pump cylinder to the large ram cylinder, so each stroke raises the ram by a small distance with a large force: the area ratio of a typical bottle jack is around 50 to 1, and the handle adds a lever ratio of about 10, so a hand force of 50 newtons at the handle end raises a load of about 25 000 newtons, the weight of two and a half tonnes, a few millimetres per stroke. Twenty strokes raise the car enough to change a wheel.

To lower, a release valve is opened by turning the handle or a screw; this lets oil flow from the ram cylinder back to the reservoir, and the load pushes the ram down at a rate set by how far the valve is opened. The jack therefore has three states: pumping, when oil moves in one direction only through the valves; holding, when both valves are closed and the ram stays up with no effort from the user; and releasing, when the bypass is open. A safety overload valve on good jacks opens if the pressure exceeds the rated load, so the jack cannot be used to lift more than it was built for.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a bottle jack, what happens on the up-stroke and down-stroke, and the three states of pumping, holding and releasing.

## Subtopic: Judging the Jack: Fitness for Purpose, Cost and Safety

Evaluating the jack starts with fitness for purpose: does it do the job its users need? The users are motorists changing a wheel at the roadside and mechanics lifting vehicles in a workshop. The job is to raise one corner of a vehicle of up to a stated mass by about 300 millimetres and hold it there. A two-tonne bottle jack does this for most cars; its evidence is the rated load stamped on it, the lift height from its lowest to its highest position, and the test of pumping it under a car. Weaknesses that the evaluation records: the minimum height may be too tall to fit under a low sports car, the lift may be too short for a bakkie with high clearance, and it must stand on hard level ground, because on soft ground it sinks and on a slope it tips.

Cost is judged against alternatives and against what the user gets. A two-tonne bottle jack costs a few hundred rand, more than a scissor jack but less than a trolley jack, and it lasts for decades with no maintenance beyond keeping the oil topped up. Its cost-effectiveness is high for a workshop that uses it daily; for a motorist who uses it once a year the cheaper scissor jack supplied with the car may be enough, and the evaluation says so rather than declaring the jack simply good or bad value.

Safety is the criterion where the jack has the most to answer for, because a car that falls off a jack kills. The jack itself holds the load reliably through its closed valves, and a good one has an overload valve; but the jack's small base and rigid ram mean it must never be the only thing holding a car while anyone is underneath. The instructions, and every mechanic, insist on an axle stand under the car once it is raised, wheel chocks, firm level ground and the handbrake on. Hazards the evaluation lists: tipping on uneven ground, slipping off a rounded jacking point, slow sinking if a seal leaks, and the handle springing back. Safety is scored on how well the design and its instructions control these, and a jack with a wide base, a saddle that grips the jacking point and clear warnings scores higher.

The questions for this section are with you now: how fitness for purpose is judged with evidence, how cost is compared with alternatives, and the safety hazards and how they are controlled.

## Subtopic: Judging the Jack: Aesthetics and Ergonomics

Aesthetics asks whether the jack looks the part, and for a tool that means looking strong, well made and trustworthy rather than pretty. A bottle jack is usually painted a single bold colour with the rated load stamped in large figures; the finish signals quality, since a rough casting with paint runs suggests careless manufacture inside too. Aesthetics matter commercially: two jacks of equal rating on a shop shelf sell on their finish. The evaluation notes the finish, the clarity of the markings and whether the design looks like what it is.

Ergonomics asks how the jack suits the human body in use. The handle must be long enough to give the lever advantage, which a two-part handle that stores short and assembles long provides; it must be at a height that lets a kneeling person pump without bending awkwardly; the pumping force at the handle end must be modest, under about 100 newtons, so that an older driver or a small adult can lift a car; the release must be controllable so the car comes down slowly; and the jack must be light enough to lift from the boot, which at 3 to 4 kilograms it is. Poor ergonomics in cheap jacks include a short handle that needs a hard push, a release screw that is stiff and sudden, and a saddle too small to position by feel under the car.

The evaluation is written as a table, criterion by criterion, with an observation, a judgement and evidence, exactly as the ramp analysis was in Term 1, and it ends with an overall verdict and two improvements the evaluator would make, such as a wider base plate for soft ground and a two-speed release valve. The error museum, four exhibits. One: evaluating by liking, with no evidence; the rated load and lift height are measurements. Two: scoring safety on the jack alone and forgetting that it must be used with stands. Three: dismissing aesthetics as irrelevant to a tool; buyers choose by it. Four: confusing ergonomics with aesthetics; one is about the body, the other about the eye.

The questions for this section are with you now: what aesthetics means for a tool, the ergonomic features of a good jack, and the form of the written evaluation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again on a workshop floor with a bottle jack and a car with a flat tyre — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pump, Hold, Release

A bottle jack: a fat cylinder with a ram that rises, a thin pump cylinder with a handle, oil stored round the outside, and two one-way valves.

Pump up: oil is sucked from the store into the pump. Pump down: oil is pushed into the ram cylinder and the ram rises a little. The valve shuts and holds it. Pump again. Fifty to one area ratio plus a ten to one handle: 50 newtons from your hand lifts two and a half tonnes, a few millimetres each stroke.

To let it down, open the release valve. Oil goes back to the store and the car sinks as slowly as you like. Three states: pumping, holding, releasing. Good jacks have an overload valve too.

Keep pump, hold, release in mind and try the questions for this part: how it works.

## Subtopic: Does It Do the Job, Safely, for the Money?

Fitness for purpose: does it lift one corner of a car about 300 millimetres and hold it? Check the rated load stamped on it, the lift height, and try it. Weak points: too tall for a low car, too short for a high bakkie, needs hard flat ground.

Cost: a few hundred rand. Dearer than a scissor jack, cheaper than a trolley jack, lasts for decades. Great value for a workshop, maybe more than a once-a-year motorist needs.

Safety: it holds, but never trust it alone with someone underneath. Axle stand, chocks, handbrake, flat ground. Hazards: tipping, slipping off, slow sinking, handle flicking back. Wide base and gripping saddle score higher.

Hold job, money, safety in mind and try this part's questions: three of the five criteria.

## Subtopic: Looks and Handling

Aesthetics for a tool means looking strong and well made: bold colour, clear stamped rating, clean finish. Rough casting and paint runs put buyers off and hint at rough insides.

Ergonomics: a handle long enough and at kneeling height, a pumping force under about 100 newtons, a release you can control, and a weight you can lift out of the boot. Cheap jacks fail on short handles and sudden releases.

Write it as a table: criterion, what you saw, judgement, evidence. Finish with a verdict and two improvements.
