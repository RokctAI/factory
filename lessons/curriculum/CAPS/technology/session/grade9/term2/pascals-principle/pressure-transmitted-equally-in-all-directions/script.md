# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers Pascal's principle, that pressure is transmitted equally in all directions in a hydraulic system, together with the definition of pressure that it rests on. The anchor is a plastic bottle in a Technology room in George, filled with water, with four small holes drilled around it at the same height; when the cap is screwed on and the bottle squeezed, four equal jets leave at once. The governing idea is that pressure is force per unit area, that in a confined liquid at rest a change in pressure anywhere appears everywhere at the same value, and that this is why a hydraulic system can send one push to several places and multiply it by changing the area it acts on. Defining pressure, stating Pascal's principle and applying it to the brake system are examinable, and the calculations follow in the next sessions.

## Subtopic: Pressure: Force Spread Over an Area

Pressure is force divided by the area over which it acts. Press your thumb on a desk with a force of 10 newtons and the pressure under the thumb is modest, because the force is spread over a few square centimetres; press a drawing pin into the same desk with the same force and the pressure under the point is thousands of times greater, because the area is a fraction of a square millimetre, so the pin enters the wood and the thumb does not. Pressure is measured in pascals, where one pascal is one newton spread over one square metre, a very small pressure; the air around us presses at about 100 000 pascals, written 100 kilopascals, and a car tyre holds about 200 kilopascals above that.

The formula is pressure equals force divided by area, with force in newtons and area in square metres giving pascals. In hydraulics the areas are small and it is convenient to work in square centimetres and newtons per square centimetre, or in kilopascals, as long as the same units are used throughout. Rearranged, force equals pressure times area, which is the form that explains force multiplication: the same pressure acting on a larger area produces a larger force.

Pressure in a fluid acts in every direction, not only downwards. A fluid pushes on every surface it touches, at right angles to that surface, with a force equal to the pressure times the area of the surface. That is why a submerged object is pushed from all sides, why a dam wall must resist sideways push, and why the plunger of a syringe feels the pressure whichever way the syringe is pointed. It is also why pressure, unlike force, has no direction of its own: it is a number at each point in the fluid.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition and unit of pressure, why a pin pierces and a thumb does not, and the two forms of the pressure formula.

## Subtopic: Pascal's Principle in a Sealed Liquid

Blaise Pascal, a French scientist of the 1600s, stated the principle that carries his name: when pressure is applied to a confined, incompressible fluid at rest, the pressure change is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and to the walls of the container, acting equally in all directions. Confined means sealed, so the fluid cannot escape; incompressible means a liquid; at rest means not flowing, which the principle describes exactly, though it is near enough true for slow movement such as a jack lifting.

The principle says two things. First, the pressure is the same everywhere in the connected liquid at the same moment: push on a master cylinder and the pressure rises by the same amount at the slave cylinder, at the end of a long pipe, at the top and the bottom of the system, ignoring the tiny differences due to the liquid's own weight. Second, the pressure acts in every direction, so a slave cylinder pointing up, down or sideways receives the same push.

Combine Pascal's principle with force equals pressure times area and the behaviour of the syringes is explained completely. A push on the small master produces a pressure, force over small area; that pressure is transmitted undiminished to the large slave; there it produces a force, pressure times large area, which is larger than the input in the ratio of the areas. The principle also explains why one master can drive several slaves at once with equal pressure at each, which no lever or gear can do, and why the tube between them can be as long and as bent as the designer needs, since pressure is transmitted along it without loss in a liquid at rest.

The questions for this section are with you now: the statement of Pascal's principle with its conditions, what it says about pressure throughout a system, and how it explains force multiplication.

## Subtopic: Seeing the Principle: Holes, Balloons and Brakes

The principle can be seen in three demonstrations. The holed bottle: fill a plastic bottle with water, drill four small holes at the same height around it, cap it and squeeze; four jets of equal strength emerge at once, whichever side is squeezed, because the pressure applied by the hand is transmitted equally to every hole. Squeeze harder and all four jets strengthen together. The balloon: fill a balloon with water, squeeze it anywhere and it bulges everywhere else equally; the pressure from the fingers reaches the whole skin. The syringe wheel: join one master syringe to four slaves by a cross-connector and push; all four slaves move out together.

The car brake system is the practical case. The driver's foot pushes a lever that pushes the master cylinder; brake fluid fills a sealed network of pipes to a slave cylinder at each of the four wheels; by Pascal's principle the pressure at every slave is the same at the same instant, so all four brakes apply together and the car stops in a straight line. Larger slave cylinders at the front wheels, which carry more of the car's weight when braking, give a larger force there by the area rule, from the same pressure. A split in any pipe lets the pressure escape, which is why modern cars have two separate circuits.

Pascal's principle also underlies the hydraulic jack, the press, the excavator arm and the dentist's chair, each of which will be met as the term continues. It is worth saying what the principle does not say: it does not say the force is the same everywhere, only the pressure; the force at each surface is the pressure times that surface's area. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying pressure is a force; it is force per area. Two: thinking pressure in a liquid acts only downwards. Three: saying Pascal's principle makes the force equal everywhere; it is the pressure that is equal. Four: applying the principle to a gas as if it were incompressible; the pressure does equalise, but the gas squashes first.

The questions for this section are with you now: three demonstrations of the principle, how it works in car brakes, and what the principle does not say.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a water bottle full of holes and a brake pedal — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Same Push, Different Spread

Pressure is a push spread over an area. Same push, small area, big pressure: a pin goes into the desk. Same push, big area, small pressure: your thumb does not.

Pressure equals force divided by area. The unit is the pascal, which is tiny; the air around you presses at about 100 000 pascals. Turn it round: force equals pressure times area. Bigger area, bigger force from the same pressure.

In a liquid, pressure pushes in every direction, on every wall, straight out from the wall. It is a number at each point, not an arrow.

Keep the pin and the thumb in mind and try the questions for this part: same push, different spread.

## Subtopic: Squeeze Here, Feel It Everywhere

Pascal's principle: squeeze a sealed liquid anywhere and the extra pressure appears everywhere in the liquid, the same amount, pushing in every direction.

Sealed, liquid, not flowing. Those are the conditions.

So push the small master, make a pressure, and that pressure arrives at the big slave undiminished. There it presses on a big area, so the force is big. One push can also reach four slaves at once, equally, and the pipe can be long and bendy.

Hold squeeze here, feel it everywhere in mind and try this part's questions: the principle.

## Subtopic: One Pedal, Four Wheels

The bottle with four holes: squeeze anywhere and four equal jets come out. The water balloon: squeeze one place, it bulges everywhere. One master pushing four slaves: all four move together.

Brakes: the pedal pushes a master cylinder, fluid runs to a slave at each wheel, the pressure is the same at all four at the same instant, so the car stops straight. Bigger front cylinders give more force at the front from the same pressure.

The principle is about pressure, not force. Force at each place is the pressure times that area.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Pressure is force per area, in pascals, acting in every direction in a fluid. Pascal's principle: a pressure change in a sealed liquid at rest is transmitted undiminished everywhere and in all directions. Force at any surface is pressure times area, which explains force multiplication, several slaves from one master, and four brakes from one pedal.

Hold one pedal and four wheels, and take the final questions of the lesson: the principle at work.
