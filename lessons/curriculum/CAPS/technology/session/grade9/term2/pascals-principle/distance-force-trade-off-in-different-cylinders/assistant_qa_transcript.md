### subtopic_1 — Equal Volumes Moved: The Rule That Fixes Distance
**Assistant:** If the slave only moves 2 centimetres when I push 10, is the hydraulic system wasting most of my effort?

**Tutor:** It is wasting almost none of it; it is converting it, and the 8 centimetres you seem to lose reappear as extra force. Effort, in the physical sense, is work, which is force multiplied by distance. You pushed the master with a certain force through 10 centimetres; the slave pushed with five times that force through 2 centimetres; multiply each pair and the two products are the same. The system took a long weak push and gave back a short strong one, exactly as a crowbar does when you press its long handle down a long way to lift a heavy slab a little, or as a low gear does when you pedal many turns to climb a hill slowly. The only real waste is friction in the seals, which turns a small part of your work into heat and makes the slave force slightly less than five times, and that loss would be there whether or not the cylinders differed in size. If what you want is distance rather than force, the system can give you that instead by swapping the cylinders, so that a 10 centimetre push moves a narrow slave 50 centimetres with one fifth of the force, which is just as efficient and just as honest. What the system cannot do is give you both, because that would mean more work out than in. So the question to ask of any hydraulic arrangement is not whether it wastes effort but what it is converting your effort into, and whether that is the force or the distance the job needs.

### subtopic_2 — Working Out Extension from Areas
**Assistant:** Why does doubling the diameter make four times the area? It is only twice as wide.

**Tutor:** Because area grows with both the width and the depth of a shape, and doubling the diameter doubles both at once. Picture a square 1 centimetre on a side: its area is 1 square centimetre. Make it 2 centimetres on a side and it is 2 wide and 2 tall, so it contains four of the original squares, not two. A circle behaves the same way; its area is pi times the radius squared, and squaring is exactly the operation of multiplying by the size twice. Double the radius and the area is pi times 2 times 2 times the original radius squared, which is four times as much; triple it and the area is nine times. For hydraulics this means cylinder diameters are a very sensitive design variable. A slave with 1.5 times the master's diameter already has 2.25 times the area, so 2.25 times the force and 0.44 of the distance; a slave with three times the diameter has nine times the force. It is why a hydraulic jack can have a modest-looking lifting cylinder and a small pump plunger and still achieve a mechanical advantage of 50 or more, and it is why the 5 and 20 millilitre syringes in the earlier investigation, whose diameters are about 12 and 20 millimetres, gave an area ratio near 2.8 rather than the volume ratio of 4. When a problem gives diameters, the first step is always to turn them into areas with pi times the radius squared, and the quick check is that the area ratio equals the diameter ratio squared. Forgetting the square is the single commonest error in hydraulic calculations.

### subtopic_3 — Mechanical Advantage Above and Below 1
**Assistant:** A mechanical advantage below 1 sounds useless. Why would anyone build that?

**Tutor:** Because distance and speed are sometimes exactly what a job needs, and a mechanical advantage below 1 buys them with force you can spare. Think about the hydraulic system that moves the bucket on a small excavator or the boom of a cherry picker. The engine drives a pump that can produce a very high pressure easily, so force is cheap; what the operator needs is for the bucket to travel a long way quickly in response to a small movement of the control lever. A master cylinder of generous area driven by the pump, feeding a slave of smaller area, gives exactly that: a short stroke at the master becomes a long fast stroke at the slave, at a lower force, and because the pump supplies plenty of pressure the lower force is still enough. The same trick appears wherever a machine must reach far. In the human body, by contrast, the biceps pulls on the forearm close to the elbow with a mechanical advantage well below 1, so a short contraction of the muscle moves the hand a long way fast, which is what throwing and catching need, and the muscle simply supplies the extra force. In the Grade 7 lever work the third-class lever, tweezers and the fishing rod, was the same choice. So a mechanical advantage below 1 is not a failure; it is the right answer when the input can afford to be strong and the output needs to be far and fast, and a designer who understands that the product of force and distance is fixed can choose either side of 1 deliberately.
