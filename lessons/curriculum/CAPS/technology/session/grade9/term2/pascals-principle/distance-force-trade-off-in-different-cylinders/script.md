# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how equal volumes moved give different extensions in cylinders of different sizes, and the resulting trade-off between distance and force expressed as mechanical advantage above or below 1. The anchor is a pair of cylinders drawn on the board in a Technology room in Polokwane, one of 2 square centimetres and one of 10 square centimetres, with a shaded block of liquid being pushed from one to the other. The governing idea is that an incompressible liquid conserves volume, so the product of area and distance is the same at both cylinders, which makes the distance ratio the inverse of the area ratio while Pascal's principle makes the force ratio equal to the area ratio; together they mean force times distance is constant and mechanical advantage is bought with movement. Calculating extensions and classifying mechanical advantage are examinable and lead into the hydraulic press calculations.

## Subtopic: Equal Volumes Moved: The Rule That Fixes Distance

Because a liquid does not compress, the volume that leaves the master cylinder equals the volume that enters the slave cylinder. Volume swept by a plunger is its area multiplied by the distance it moves, so area of master times distance of master equals area of slave times distance of slave. This single equation fixes how far the slave moves for any movement of the master once the two areas are known.

Take a master of 2 square centimetres pushed in 10 centimetres: it displaces 20 cubic centimetres. If the slave has an area of 10 square centimetres, those 20 cubic centimetres fill a length of 20 divided by 10, which is 2 centimetres, so the slave extends 2 centimetres. The slave moved a fifth of the master's distance because its area is five times larger. If instead the slave had an area of 1 square centimetre, 20 cubic centimetres would fill 20 centimetres, twice the master's travel, because its area is half.

The distance ratio is therefore the inverse of the area ratio: slave distance equals master distance times master area divided by slave area. A wide slave moves little, a narrow slave moves far, and the liquid in between is simply being poured from one shape into another. Nothing about pressure is needed to work this out; it is pure geometry of volume, and it is the reason a jack handle must be pumped many times, since each pump moves a small master a long way to move a large slave a short way.

The questions for this section are with you now: the equal volume rule as an equation, a worked extension, and why the distance ratio is the inverse of the area ratio.

## Subtopic: Working Out Extension from Areas

The calculation has a fixed method. Write down the two areas and the master distance. Find the volume moved: master area times master distance. Divide by the slave area to get the slave distance. Check the sense: a larger slave area must give a smaller distance. Example: master 4 square centimetres moved 15 centimetres gives 60 cubic centimetres; slave 12 square centimetres extends 60 divided by 12, which is 5 centimetres. Example in the other direction: master 12 square centimetres moved 5 centimetres gives 60 cubic centimetres; slave 4 square centimetres extends 15 centimetres.

If diameters are given instead of areas, the areas are found first from pi times the radius squared, and the area ratio is the square of the diameter ratio: a slave of twice the diameter has four times the area and moves a quarter of the distance. This is why small changes in cylinder diameter make large changes in the trade-off, and why the 5 and 20 millilitre syringes, whose diameters differ by less than two to one, gave an area ratio of about 2.5 rather than 4.

Units must match: areas in square centimetres with distances in centimetres give volumes in cubic centimetres; areas in square metres with distances in metres give cubic metres. A mixed calculation, square centimetres with metres, gives nonsense, and a sense check catches it: if a slave five times the master's area appears to move further than the master, a unit has slipped.

The questions for this section are with you now: the method for finding extension, the effect of giving diameters rather than areas, and the unit rule.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Advantage Above and Below 1

Now join the two rules. Pascal's principle gives the force ratio: slave force equals master force times slave area divided by master area, so a slave five times the area pushes with five times the force. The equal volume rule gives the distance ratio: slave distance equals master distance times master area divided by slave area, so the same slave moves one fifth of the distance. Multiply force by distance at each end: master force times master distance equals slave force times slave distance. Work in equals work out, which is the conservation of energy, and it means no arrangement of cylinders can gain force and distance together.

Mechanical advantage is output force divided by input force, which for hydraulics equals slave area divided by master area. When the slave is larger, the mechanical advantage is greater than 1: the system is a force multiplier, strong and slow, the arrangement of the jack, the press, the brake and the excavator's lifting cylinder. When the slave is smaller, the mechanical advantage is less than 1: the system is a distance multiplier, fast and weak, used where a short stroke at the master must become a long quick movement at the slave, as in some control linkages. When the areas are equal, the mechanical advantage is exactly 1, pure transfer.

A designer chooses the mechanical advantage by choosing the areas, and the cost is always paid in the other quantity. A jack with a mechanical advantage of 50 lifts a car with a light push but needs fifty times the handle travel: fifty pumps of 10 centimetres for a lift of 10 centimetres. The error museum, four exhibits. One: expecting the slave to move as far as the master when it is larger; the volume rule forbids it. Two: using the diameter ratio as the area ratio; areas go with the square. Three: mixing units in the volume calculation. Four: claiming a mechanical advantage above 1 with no loss of distance; the product is fixed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the combined force and distance rules, the definition of mechanical advantage for hydraulics, and the three cases above, equal to and below 1.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with two cylinders drawn on the board and a block of water moving between them — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Same Water Has to Fit Somewhere

Water does not squash, so the water that leaves one cylinder has to fit into the other. Area times distance in equals area times distance out.

Master 2 square centimetres pushed 10 centimetres: 20 cubic centimetres of water. Slave 10 square centimetres: 20 divided by 10 is 2 centimetres out. Five times the area, a fifth of the distance.

Narrow slave, the same water makes a long column, so it moves far. Wide slave, a short slab, so it moves little.

That is why you pump a jack handle again and again.

Keep the block of water in mind and try the questions for this part: the same water has to fit somewhere.

## Subtopic: Divide by the Area

Method: areas, master distance. Volume is master area times master distance. Divide by slave area. Check: bigger slave, smaller move.

Master 4 by 15 is 60. Slave 12: 60 over 12 is 5 centimetres. Swap: master 12 by 5 is 60. Slave 4: 15 centimetres.

Given diameters, work out areas first. Double the diameter means four times the area and a quarter of the distance.

Keep units the same: centimetres with square centimetres. If the big cylinder seems to move further, a unit slipped.

Hold divide by the area in mind and try this part's questions: the calculation.

## Subtopic: Strong and Slow, or Fast and Weak

Force goes up by the area ratio. Distance goes down by the same ratio. Multiply them: force times distance is the same at both ends. No free gain.

Mechanical advantage is output force over input force, which is slave area over master area. Bigger slave: above 1, strong and slow, the jack. Smaller slave: below 1, fast and weak. Same size: exactly 1.

A jack with mechanical advantage 50 needs fifty pumps for one lift. You choose the ratio, you pay in distance.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Equal volume moved means area times distance is the same at both cylinders, so distance ratio is the inverse of area ratio. Pascal gives force ratio equal to area ratio. Force times distance is constant. Mechanical advantage equals slave area over master area: above 1 strong and slow, below 1 fast and weak, 1 for equal cylinders.
