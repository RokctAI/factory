# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the cleat as a mechanical control system. The anchor is the yacht basin at Durban harbour, a dinghy sailor stepping onto the pontoon and dropping a figure-of-eight of rope over a two-horned cleat so the boat stops drifting, then at the flagpole by the clubhouse, the halyard locked under a cleat while the flag flies. The governing idea is that a cleat holds a rope by friction and by jamming it in a tapered gap, so that a load on the rope increases the grip rather than loosening it, while the free end can be released instantly without untying anything. Describing how horn, cam and jam cleats hold a rope, identifying where cleats are used, and evaluating them for fitness for purpose, safety and ergonomics are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Cleat Is and How It Holds a Rope Without a Knot

A cleat is a fitting fixed to a deck, a post or a wall to which a rope can be secured quickly. Its purpose is to hold a rope under tension, so that the load on the rope is carried by the cleat and the fixing, and to release the rope quickly when needed. Unlike a knot, which must be tied and untied and can jam when wet and loaded, the cleat relies on friction and geometry. A rope wrapped round a cleat several times is held by friction along each wrap; a rope pulled into a tapered gap is held by jamming. Both effects grow as the load on the rope grows, which is the quality that makes the cleat a reliable control.

The cleat is a control system because it governs whether the rope may move. It supplies no force and multiplies nothing; the load in the rope comes from the boat, the flag in the wind or the tension in the line. The cleat decides that the rope stays where it was put. In the systems view of the course, the input is the rope under tension, the control is the cleat, and the output is a rope held at a fixed length until the operator chooses to release it. This parallels the ratchet, which decides which way a wheel may turn, and the brake, which decides whether a wheel may turn at all.

The forces on a cleat are worth noticing. The rope pulls along the deck or post, so the cleat is in shear at its fixing bolts and the horns are in bending. A cleat for a mooring line must be bolted through the deck to a backing plate, because a screwed-on cleat tears out under the load of a boat in a swell. The rope itself is in tension; the friction between rope and cleat is what transfers that tension into the fitting. Choosing a cleat means matching its size and fixing to the load and to the rope diameter, which must fit the gap.

## Subtopic: Types of Cleat: Horn, Cam and Jam Cleats

The horn cleat is the oldest and most common: a bar with two horns projecting outward, fixed at its middle. To secure a rope, take a full turn round the base, then lay figure-of-eight turns over the horns, two or three, and finish with a half hitch under the last horn if the rope must stay for a long time. Each turn adds friction, so the load is taken up gradually along the wraps and the free end can be held with little force. To release, lift the turns off; the rope runs free at once. Horn cleats hold mooring lines on boats and pontoons and halyards on flagpoles.

The cam cleat uses two spring-loaded toothed cams facing each other with a gap between them. The rope is pulled down into the gap; the cams swing to let it pass in one direction, but when the rope tries to run back the teeth grip it and the cams rotate to pinch it harder, exactly like a pawl engaging a ratchet, which is why the cam cleat is sometimes described as a ratchet for rope. Lift the rope upward and out of the cams and it is released. Cam cleats are used on sailing dinghies to hold the sheets that control the sails, because a sailor must be able to adjust and release them in a fraction of a second.

The jam cleat is the simplest: a fitting with a tapered slot, wide at one end and narrow at the other. The rope is laid in the slot and pulled toward the narrow end, where it wedges. The harder the rope pulls, the tighter it jams, and a flick back toward the wide end frees it. Jam cleats hold the halyard on a small dinghy mast, the cord of a window blind, a washing line, and the drawstring on many bags and tents. Each type trades holding power against ease of release: horn cleats hold hardest but take longest to secure; cam cleats release fastest but can slip with the wrong rope size; jam cleats are cheap but chew the rope.

## Subtopic: Where Cleats Are Used and How to Evaluate Them

Cleats appear wherever a rope must be held and released repeatedly. At Durban harbour, horn cleats on the pontoon hold mooring lines; on the yachts, horn cleats and cam cleats hold halyards and sheets. The flagpole at every school has a horn cleat for its halyard. A washing line between two posts is tensioned and held by a jam cleat or a horn cleat. A tent guy line uses a small sliding jam. Rock climbers use a cam device whose spring-loaded cams grip a rope or a crack, a cousin of the cam cleat. In each case the alternative would be a knot, slower to tie and harder to free under load.

Evaluating a cleat uses the course headings. Fitness for purpose: does it hold the rope at the intended load without slipping, with the rope diameter it is meant for, wet or dry? Safety: does it release without trapping fingers, and will it not release by accident, which on a mooring line could set a boat adrift and on a flag halyard could drop a flag? Ergonomics: can it be worked with one cold hand, and is it placed where the rope naturally leads? Cost: a cast aluminium horn cleat is cheap; a cam cleat with hardened teeth costs more. Aesthetics: on a yacht, a polished stainless cleat is part of the look.

A simple investigation: fix a horn cleat, a cam cleat and a jam cleat to a plank, load a rope with a bucket of sand through a pulley, and test how many wraps or what rope size each needs to hold, how quickly each releases, and whether any slips when the rope is wet. Record results in a table and write a recommendation for a given use. The error museum, four exhibits. One: tying a knot on a horn cleat so tight it cannot be released under load. Two: using a rope too thin for a cam cleat so the teeth cannot grip. Three: screwing a mooring cleat to thin plywood without a backing plate. Four: calling the cleat a device that increases force; it only holds.

## Subtopic: A Rope Catcher

A cleat is a fitting that holds a rope without a knot. Wrap the rope round it or jam the rope into it, and friction holds. Pull harder on the rope and it holds harder. Flick it off and the rope runs free.

The cleat does not pull anything. The boat or the wind pulls the rope. The cleat just says: stay there. That makes it a control, like the ratchet and the brake.

The rope pulls sideways on the cleat, so the cleat must be bolted through with a backing plate, not screwed into thin wood.

## Subtopic: Wrap It, Pinch It, Jam It

Horn cleat: a bar with two horns. One turn round the base, two or three figure-of-eights over the horns. Lift them off to release. Mooring lines and flagpoles.

Cam cleat: two toothed jaws with springs. Pull the rope down between them and they grip; pull back and they bite harder, like a ratchet for rope. Lift the rope up and out to release. Dinghy sail sheets.

Jam cleat: a tapered slot. Pull the rope to the narrow end and it wedges. Flick it back to free it. Blind cords, washing lines, tent lines.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A cleat holds a rope under load by friction and jamming, so that pulling harder tightens the grip, and releases it instantly without untying. Horn cleats hold by figure-of-eight wraps, cam cleats by spring-loaded toothed jaws, jam cleats by a tapered slot. Cleats are controls: they govern whether the rope moves and add no force.
