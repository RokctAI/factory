### subtopic_1 — What a Ratchet and Pawl Is and How It Allows Motion One Way
**Assistant:** If the pawl takes the whole load when the wheel tries to turn back, why does it not just break?

**Tutor:** Because it is designed for exactly that moment, and two choices make it strong. First, the pawl and the wheel teeth are made of hardened steel and the pawl is short and thick, so the force on it is compression along its length, which steel resists very well, rather than bending. Second, the pawl's pivot is placed so that the line of force from the tooth passes almost straight through the pivot; that means the load pushes the pawl deeper into the tooth instead of trying to lift it out, and the pivot pin carries most of the force. A badly placed pivot makes the load tend to lift the pawl, and that is the ratchet that slips. On a boat winch the pawl is also usually wide enough to engage the full width of the tooth, spreading the load across more metal.

### subtopic_2 — Where Ratchets Control Machines: Winches, Jacks, Spanners and Ties
**Assistant:** How is a ratchet spanner different from a winch ratchet if they are both ratchets?

**Tutor:** The principle is identical, a toothed wheel and a spring-loaded pawl, but the job is arranged the other way round. In the winch, the handle drives the drum and the ratchet stops the drum turning back when the handle is released; the pawl acts on the output. In the spanner, the socket is on the ratchet wheel and the pawl is in the handle; pushing the handle drives the wheel through the pawl, and pulling the handle back lets the pawl ride over the teeth so the handle returns without turning the socket. So in the winch the ratchet holds the load, and in the spanner it lets the handle reset. The spanner also has a small lever that flips the pawl to the other side of the teeth, reversing the free direction for loosening, which most winches do not need because they release by lifting the pawl entirely.

### subtopic_3 — Designing, Drawing and Evaluating a Ratchet
**Assistant:** Why does a cable tie count as a ratchet when there is no wheel?

**Tutor:** Because a ratchet does not have to be round; it has to have asymmetrical teeth and a pawl. A cable tie's strap is a long straight row of saw-tooth ridges, which is a ratchet unrolled into a line, and the head contains a tiny flexible tongue that is the pawl. Pull the strap through and the sloping sides of the ridges flex the tongue aside with a soft clicking; try to pull it back and the tongue catches the steep side of a ridge and holds. The same straight ratchet appears in the ratchet strap for tying a load on a trailer, in the adjustable strap on a helmet, and in some zip-tie style clamps in plumbing. The engineering idea is the tooth shape plus the catch, whether it runs in a circle or in a line.
