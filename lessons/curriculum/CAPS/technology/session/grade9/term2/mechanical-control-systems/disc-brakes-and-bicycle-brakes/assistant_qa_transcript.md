### subtopic_1 — How a Disc Brake Uses Friction and Hydraulics to Stop a Wheel
**Assistant:** Why do cars use hydraulics for brakes when a bicycle manages with a cable?

**Tutor:** Because a car needs far more clamping force, delivered equally to four wheels, and fluid does both jobs better than cable. A car weighs twenty times a loaded bicycle and travels much faster, so stopping it needs clamping forces of thousands of newtons at each caliper. A cable could carry that force to one wheel but would stretch, fray and need constant adjustment, and splitting one cable to four brakes equally is nearly impossible. Fluid under pressure reaches all four calipers at the same pressure automatically, so the brakes balance themselves, and Pascal's principle multiplies the pedal force by the area ratio without any lever long enough to fit under a dashboard. The cost is a sealed system that must never let air in, which is why brake servicing is a skilled job.

### subtopic_2 — Bicycle Brakes: Rim Brakes, Cables and Levers
**Assistant:** If brakes turn motion into heat, why does a car not simply catch fire on a long hill?

**Tutor:** It can get close, and the design works hard to prevent it. The discs are thick steel with a lot of mass, so they can soak up heat quickly without their temperature rising too fast, and they are usually ventilated with internal vanes that pump air through as they spin. They sit in the airstream behind the wheel, so cooling is continuous. Even so, a heavy vehicle on a very long descent can overheat the pads until they lose friction, which is brake fade, and the fluid can boil, which puts gas in the line and makes the pedal useless. That is why trucks and minibuses on passes like Van Reenen's or the Hilton descent are told to select a low gear so the engine does much of the slowing, and why runaway lanes exist. The brake is designed for repeated stops with cooling in between, not for one endless stop.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Disc and Rim Brakes and Evaluating Them
**Assistant:** Why is a wet bicycle rim so much worse at braking than a wet car disc?

**Tutor:** Two reasons, one about the surface and one about the material. The rim is at the outside of the wheel, where water, mud and road spray land first and are flung around by the spinning, and the rubber block has to squeeze a film of water out from under itself before it grips, which takes a revolution or two and is the frightening delay a rider feels in the rain. The car disc sits close to the hub, shielded by the wheel, and spins fast enough that water is thrown off almost at once. Second, rubber on wet aluminium has a much lower friction than brake pad material on steel, which is designed to grip even when wet. This is exactly why mountain bikes and many new bicycles have moved to small disc brakes: the same wet ride, far less delay.
