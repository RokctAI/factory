# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the ratchet and pawl as a mechanical control system. The anchor is a boat trailer at the slipway in Port Alfred, the owner winding a winch handle to haul the boat up the trailer, each turn giving a run of clicks, and the handle staying exactly where it is left when the owner lets go to adjust the strap. The governing idea is that a ratchet is a toothed wheel with asymmetrical teeth and a pawl is a spring-loaded arm that rides over the sloped face of each tooth in one direction and locks against the steep face in the other, so that the wheel can turn freely one way and is held against turning back. Describing how the ratchet and pawl works, identifying applications, and evaluating a ratchet mechanism for fitness for purpose and safety are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Ratchet and Pawl Is and How It Allows Motion One Way

A ratchet wheel has teeth shaped like saw teeth: each tooth has a long sloping face and a short steep face. A pawl is an arm hinged near the wheel, pressed against the teeth by a spring or by gravity. When the wheel turns in the allowed direction, the sloping face of each tooth slides under the pawl and lifts it, the pawl drops into the next gap with a click, and the wheel turns on. When the wheel tries to turn the other way, the pawl tip meets the steep face of a tooth and cannot ride over it; the pawl is pushed harder into the tooth and the wheel is locked. The ratchet converts a wheel that could turn either way into one that turns only one way.

Two things matter in the design. First, the pawl must be strong enough to take the full load when the wheel tries to turn back, because at that moment the whole load is held on one tooth and one pawl; the pawl is usually made of hardened steel and pivoted so that the load pushes it further into engagement rather than out. Second, the pawl must be able to lift clear of the teeth when the operator wants to release the load, usually by a small lever or by flipping the pawl over; otherwise the winch could never be unwound.

The ratchet is a control system, not a drive. It does not supply force or change speed. It governs what the mechanism is permitted to do, in the same way a one-way valve governs a pipe or a turnstile governs a gate. In the systems language of this course, the ratchet sits between the input and the output of a mechanism and controls the direction of motion allowed. This distinction between parts that provide motion and parts that control it runs through the whole week.

The questions for this section are with you now: the shape of ratchet teeth and the pawl, why the wheel turns one way only, and why the ratchet is a control rather than a drive.

## Subtopic: Where Ratchets Control Machines: Winches, Jacks, Spanners and Ties

The boat winch is the clearest example. The handle turns a small gear that drives a drum, the strap winds on the drum and pulls the boat up the trailer. Without a ratchet, the moment the owner stopped winding, the weight of the boat would spin the handle backwards, dangerous to fingers and useless for the job. The ratchet wheel on the drum shaft and its pawl let the drum turn in the winding direction only. To launch the boat, the pawl is lifted and the drum turns freely. A hand brake winch on a bakkie recovery kit works the same way.

Car jacks of the scissor type use a ratchet on the screw handle so the operator can crank back and forth without turning the handle through full circles in a cramped space. The ratchet socket spanner uses the same idea: push the handle one way and the socket turns the nut; pull it back and the ratchet freewheels, so the socket never leaves the nut, which is why mechanics can tighten a bolt deep in an engine bay with short strokes. A small lever reverses the pawl for loosening.

Smaller ratchets are everywhere: the cable tie, whose strap is a straight ratchet and whose head holds a pawl, so it pulls tight and never loosens; the ratchet strap for tying a load on a trailer; the winding key of an old clock; the turnstile at a stadium gate; the handbrake lever in a car, which clicks up and holds until the button lifts the pawl; the fishing reel's anti-reverse. In each, the user supplies the motion and the ratchet makes sure it is not undone.

The questions for this section are with you now: the boat winch, the ratchet spanner and the jack, and small ratchets like cable ties and handbrakes.

## Subtopic: Designing, Drawing and Evaluating a Ratchet

Drawing a ratchet and pawl is a good exercise in showing how a mechanism works rather than what it looks like. Draw the wheel with eight or twelve saw teeth, mark the direction of free rotation with an arrow, draw the pawl as a short arm pivoted at one end with its tip in a tooth gap, and draw the spring that presses it down. Add a second arrow, crossed out, for the blocked direction. Label the sloping face and the steep face of one tooth. A simple cardboard model can be made with a disc cut into teeth, a strip of card pinned near it, and a rubber band for the spring; it clicks convincingly.

Evaluating a ratchet uses the same headings as the hydraulic jack from an earlier session. Fitness for purpose: does it hold the load reliably and release when asked? Safety: can the pawl slip under load, and are fingers kept clear of the handle if it does? Ergonomics: can the pawl be released with one hand, and is the handle a comfortable size? Cost: hardened steel for the pawl and wheel costs more but a soft pawl that rounds off is dangerous. Aesthetics matter less here, but a well-made ratchet with a crisp click signals quality to a buyer.

The ratchet's weakness is that it holds only at a tooth. Between teeth it allows a little backward movement before locking, which is why a winch strap sags slightly when released and why fine teeth are used where precise holding matters. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing symmetrical teeth, which would let the pawl slip both ways. Two: pivoting the pawl so that load pushes it out of the teeth instead of into them. Three: calling the ratchet a mechanism that increases force; it only controls direction. Four: forgetting the release, so the design can never be unwound.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing and modelling a ratchet, evaluating it under the five headings, and its weakness between teeth.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the boat winch at Port Alfred and a cardboard wheel — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Toothed Wheel and a Finger

A ratchet is a wheel with saw teeth. Each tooth has a long slope and a short steep side. A pawl is a little arm that presses on the teeth.

Turn the wheel one way and the slopes lift the pawl: click, click, click. Turn it back and the pawl hits the steep side and jams. Wheel locked.

The ratchet does not push or pull anything. It only decides which way the wheel may turn. That makes it a control, not a drive.

Keep a toothed wheel and a finger in mind and try the questions for this part: how a ratchet works.

## Subtopic: One Way Only

Boat winch: wind the boat up, let go, the handle stays. Without the ratchet it would spin back and break your fingers. Lift the pawl to let the boat down.

Ratchet spanner: push to turn the nut, pull back and it freewheels, socket stays on. Scissor jack handle: same idea.

Cable ties, ratchet straps, handbrake levers, turnstiles, fishing reels. All one-way-only.

Hold one way only in mind and try this part's questions: where ratchets are used.

## Subtopic: Click, Hold, Release

Draw it: a wheel with saw teeth, an arrow for the free way, a crossed arrow for the blocked way, a pawl on a pivot, a spring pressing it. Label slope and steep face. Make one from card and a rubber band.

Evaluate it: does it hold and release? Can the pawl slip? Can you release it one-handed? Is it hard steel or soft metal that wears round?

Weak point: it only holds at a tooth, so it gives back a little before it locks. Fine teeth for fine holding.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A ratchet and pawl is a saw-toothed wheel with a spring-loaded arm that rides over the sloping faces in one direction and locks against the steep faces in the other, so the wheel turns one way only. It controls direction; it does not add force. Winches, jacks, ratchet spanners, cable ties and handbrakes all depend on it, and every design needs a strong pawl pushed into engagement by the load and a way to release it.
