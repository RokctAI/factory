# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers disc brakes and bicycle brakes as mechanical control systems. The anchor is a taxi rank in Pietermaritzburg on a hot afternoon, a minibus pulling in with its front discs glowing faintly after the long descent from Hilton, and beside it a learner's bicycle with worn rubber blocks squealing against the rim. The governing idea is that a brake is a friction device that converts the kinetic energy of a moving vehicle into heat at a controlled rate, that the disc brake applies that friction through hydraulic pressure on pads clamping a disc while the bicycle rim brake applies it through a lever and cable pinching the rim, and that both are evaluated by how reliably and safely they stop the wheel. Describing the working of disc and bicycle brakes, tracing the control path from hand or foot to wheel, and evaluating brakes for safety and fitness for purpose are examinable.

## Subtopic: How a Disc Brake Uses Friction and Hydraulics to Stop a Wheel

A disc brake has a flat steel disc, the rotor, bolted to the wheel hub so it spins with the wheel. A caliper straddles the edge of the disc, holding two brake pads, one each side, made of a hard friction material. Inside the caliper are one or more pistons in cylinders. When the driver presses the brake pedal, the pedal pushes a piston in the master cylinder, raising the pressure of the brake fluid; the fluid carries that pressure through steel pipes and flexible hoses to the caliper cylinders, and the pistons push the pads against both faces of the disc. Friction between pads and disc resists the disc's rotation and slows the wheel.

This is Pascal's principle at work. The master cylinder piston is small and the caliper pistons are larger, so the force at the pads is many times the force at the pedal, in the ratio of the areas, while the pedal moves much further than the pads. The pressure is the same throughout the fluid, which is also why one pedal can apply all four brakes at once and why a leak anywhere, letting air into the line, makes the pedal feel spongy: air compresses, liquid does not. Release the pedal and the pressure falls, the pads back off by a fraction of a millimetre, and the wheel turns freely.

The friction does work on the disc and the work becomes heat. A minibus coming down from Hilton to Pietermaritzburg converts a great deal of energy, and the discs can reach several hundred degrees. Discs are made thick, often with internal vanes so air flows through them, and are exposed to the airstream to cool. Overheated pads lose their grip, called brake fade, which is why long descents are taken in a low gear so the engine does some of the slowing. The brake is a control system: it does not drive the vehicle but governs its speed on demand.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a disc brake, how pedal pressure reaches the pads through fluid, and why brakes get hot.

## Subtopic: Bicycle Brakes: Rim Brakes, Cables and Levers

The common bicycle brake is a rim brake. A lever on the handlebar pulls a steel cable inside an outer casing; the cable runs to a pair of arms pivoted near the wheel, and pulling the cable swings the arms inward so that two rubber brake blocks pinch the rim from both sides. Friction between rubber and rim slows the wheel. Release the lever and a spring in the brake arms pulls the blocks clear. Caliper brakes have both arms on one pivot above the wheel; V-brakes have an arm each side of the wheel on the fork, which gives more force and is common on mountain bikes.

Levers are everywhere in this system. The handlebar lever is a second-class lever: the pivot at one end, the cable attached near it, the hand far away, so a modest squeeze gives a strong pull on the cable, at the cost of the hand moving further than the cable. The brake arms are levers again, multiplying the cable pull before it reaches the blocks. Together the two stages let a child's hand hold a bicycle on a steep hill. The cable is in tension the whole time it works; the outer casing is in compression; the brake arms bend; all the forces from Term 1 reappear.

Adjustment matters. The blocks must sit square on the rim, not touching the tyre, which they would cut, and not below the rim, where they would do nothing. The cable stretches with use and the blocks wear, so a barrel adjuster at the lever takes up the slack. Wet rims halve the friction, which every learner who rides in the rain knows, and worn blocks squeal and score the rim. Some bicycles now use small disc brakes with cable or hydraulic operation, which work exactly like the car's disc brake in miniature and are not affected by a wet rim.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a rim brake works from lever to block, the lever stages that multiply the hand's force, and adjustment and wet-weather problems.

## Subtopic: Comparing Disc and Rim Brakes and Evaluating Them

Disc and rim brakes both stop a wheel by friction, but they differ in where the friction acts and how the force is delivered. The disc brake clamps a dedicated steel disc near the hub; the rim brake clamps the wheel rim itself. The disc is small in radius, so it needs a large clamping force, which hydraulics provide; the rim is large in radius, so a modest force from a cable gives a good braking effect, which is why bicycles managed for a century with cables. The disc stays clean and dry behind the wheel; the rim collects water and mud. The disc wears a replaceable rotor; the rim brake slowly wears the wheel itself.

Evaluation under the course headings. Fitness for purpose: a brake must stop the wheel reliably, repeatably, in wet and dry conditions, and must release fully. Safety: the control path from hand or foot to friction surface must not fail without warning; hydraulic systems need sealed fluid and cables need no fraying. Ergonomics: lever reach for a small hand, pedal effort for an average driver. Cost: cables and rubber blocks are cheap; hydraulic discs cost more and need skilled servicing. Aesthetics: a clean disc and caliper are a selling point on a bicycle; on a car they are hidden.

A good exercise is to draw the control path for each brake as a systems chain: hand on lever, cable in tension, arms pivot, blocks on rim, friction, heat; foot on pedal, master cylinder, fluid pressure, caliper pistons, pads on disc, friction, heat. The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying the brake absorbs energy and it vanishes; it becomes heat. Two: thinking the pads touch the tyre; they touch the rim or a disc. Three: forgetting that air in a hydraulic line makes the pedal spongy because gases compress. Four: adjusting rim blocks so they drag on the tyre, which will cut it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the differences between disc and rim brakes, evaluating a brake under the five headings, and drawing the control path.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the hot minibus discs at the Pietermaritzburg rank and the squealing bicycle — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Squeeze the Spinning Plate

A disc brake is a steel plate spinning with the wheel, and two pads that squeeze it. Press the pedal, push fluid, the fluid pushes the pads. Friction slows the plate and the wheel.

Small pedal piston, big pad pistons: the force grows. Pascal again. One pedal, four brakes, same pressure. Air in the line makes the pedal feel spongy because air squashes.

All that stopping becomes heat. Discs glow after a long downhill. Pads that overheat stop gripping, so use a low gear on long hills.

Keep squeeze the spinning plate in mind and try the questions for this part: how a disc brake works.

## Subtopic: Pull the Lever, Pinch the Rim

Bicycle: squeeze the lever, it pulls a cable, the cable swings two arms, two rubber blocks pinch the rim. Let go and a spring pulls them off.

The lever is a lever and the arms are levers, so a small squeeze becomes a strong pinch. Cable in tension, casing in compression, arms bending.

Set the blocks on the rim, not the tyre. Tighten the cable when it stretches. Wet rims brake badly. Worn blocks squeal.

## Subtopic: Friction Turns Motion Into Heat

Disc: small plate, big force, hydraulics, stays dry, replace the disc. Rim: big wheel, small force, cable, gets wet, wears the wheel.

Judge a brake: does it stop every time, wet or dry? Can it fail without warning? Can a small hand reach the lever? What does it cost to fix?

Draw the chain: hand, cable, arms, blocks, rim, friction, heat. Foot, master cylinder, fluid, pistons, pads, disc, friction, heat.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A brake is a friction control that turns a vehicle's motion into heat. The disc brake uses pedal pressure carried by fluid to press pads on a spinning disc, multiplying force by Pascal's principle and needing cooling against fade. The bicycle rim brake uses a lever and cable to swing arms that pinch rubber blocks on the rim, multiplying force through two lever stages. Both are judged on reliable, safe stopping in all conditions, easy operation and sensible cost.
