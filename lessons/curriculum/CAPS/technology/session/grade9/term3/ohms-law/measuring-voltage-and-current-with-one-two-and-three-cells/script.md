# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers measuring voltage and current with one, two and three cells across a resistor. The anchor is a Technology room in Pietermaritzburg where each pair has a 100 ohm resistor, three fresh 1.5 volt cells in a holder with movable links, a digital multimeter set to volts and a second set to milliamperes, and a results table with columns for cells, voltage, current and voltage over current. The governing idea is that an investigation of Ohm's law changes one variable, the supply voltage, by adding cells in series, measures the two quantities that respond, and records them faithfully in a table so that a later graph can test the predicted proportionality; and that correct meter connection, voltmeter across and ammeter in series, is the condition for the readings meaning anything. Conducting and recording the investigation and connecting the meters correctly are examinable.

## Subtopic: Setting Up the Investigation: Resistor, Cells and Two Meters

The circuit is the simple series loop with the lamp replaced by a resistor: cells in a holder, a switch, the resistor, and back to the cells. A 100 ohm resistor is a good choice because it gives currents of tens of milliamperes that a school meter reads clearly without warming the resistor; a 470 ohm resistor gives smaller currents and also works. The holder for three cells has links that can be moved so that one, two or three cells are in the loop, or separate cells can be clipped in series one at a time, positive to negative.

The voltmeter is connected across the resistor, one probe at each end, so that it reads the potential difference between the resistor's ends. It is connected in parallel and draws almost no current itself, so it does not disturb the circuit. The ammeter is connected in series, in the loop, so that all the current flows through it: the loop is opened between the switch and the resistor and the ammeter inserted in the gap. It has almost no resistance and so does not reduce the current it measures. Both meters have a polarity: the red probe toward the positive side of the supply, the black toward the negative, or a digital meter shows a minus sign.

Before the first reading, the meters are set: the voltmeter to a direct current voltage range above 5 volts, the ammeter to a direct current milliampere range above 50 milliamperes. A digital meter on the wrong range shows an overload sign or reads zero. The whole circuit is drawn with symbols on the results page before being built, with the two meters shown, a circle with V across the resistor and a circle with A in the loop, so that a reader can see the method from the diagram.

The questions for this section are with you now: the circuit with a resistor and cell holder, connecting the voltmeter across and the ammeter in series, and setting ranges and drawing the method.

## Subtopic: Taking Readings With One, Two and Three Cells

With one cell in the loop, close the switch and read both meters. A fresh cell gives about 1.5 volts across the resistor, perhaps 1.45 because a little voltage is lost inside the cell; the ammeter reads about 14 or 15 milliamperes for 100 ohms. Write both readings in the table against one cell. Open the switch; leaving it closed runs the cells down and warms nothing here but is the habit for later circuits where it would.

Add the second cell in series, positive to negative, and repeat: about 2.9 to 3 volts and 29 to 30 milliamperes. Add the third: about 4.4 to 4.5 volts and 44 to 45 milliamperes. At each step the voltage rises by roughly the same amount and the current by roughly the same amount. If the current does not rise when a cell is added, the cell is dead or reversed; if the voltage rises but the current does not, the ammeter is probably on the wrong range or not in the loop. Fault-finding runs alongside measuring.

Read the meters to the precision they offer, a digital display to its last stable digit, and do not round early. Record what the meter showed, 2.93 volts, not what was expected, 3.0 volts. The difference between expected and measured is information about the cells, not an error to be hidden. The readings are repeated, each cell count measured twice or three times with the switch opened in between, and the repeats recorded in their own columns so that a mean can be taken.

The questions for this section are with you now: the readings for one, two and three cells, what a non-rising current means, and reading to precision and repeating.

## Subtopic: Recording, Repeating and Handling the Readings Honestly

The results table has a row for each number of cells and columns for number of cells, voltage reading one, two and three, mean voltage, current readings one, two and three, mean current, and a final column of mean voltage divided by mean current. That last column should be close to 100 for every row, the resistor's marked value, and that constancy is the first test of Ohm's law before any graph is drawn. A row that gives 115 while the others give 100 has something wrong in it, a misread meter or a loose clip, and is remeasured rather than deleted.

Honest recording is a scientific habit and an examination requirement. Readings are written in ink as they are taken, not copied from a neighbour, not adjusted to match the formula. Anything unusual is noted beside the table: cell two was warm, the ammeter flickered between 29 and 30, the clip on the resistor was loose at first and the reading was retaken. A table with every value exactly on the formula looks invented; a table with small honest scatter looks measured.

Safety and care: fresh cells only, so that the voltage steps are even; no short circuits across the cells, which heat them; the ammeter never across the cells; probes held by their insulated bodies; switch open between readings. The error museum, four exhibits. One: the ammeter connected across the resistor instead of in the loop. Two: readings rounded to what Ohm's law predicts. Three: a single reading per cell count with no repeat. Four: a bad row deleted instead of remeasured and noted.

The questions for this section are with you now: the layout of the results table and the voltage over current column, honest recording and notes, and safety and care.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the 100 ohm resistor, three cells and two meters on the bench in Pietermaritzburg — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Build the Loop, Add the Meters

The loop: cells in a holder, switch, resistor, back to the cells. 100 ohms gives readable milliamps without warming. Move the links so one, two or three cells are in the loop.

Voltmeter across the resistor, one probe each end. Ammeter in the loop: open a gap and put it in. Red toward positive. Set volts above 5 and milliamps above 50 before you connect.

Draw the circuit with both meters on the results page first: a V across the resistor, an A in the loop.

Keep build the loop, add the meters in mind and try the questions for this part: the setup.

## Subtopic: Add a Cell, Read Both Meters

One cell: close the switch, read both. About 1.45 volts, about 14 or 15 milliamps. Write both. Open the switch.

Two cells: about 2.9 volts, about 29 milliamps. Three: about 4.4 volts, about 44 milliamps. Each cell adds about the same to both. No rise in current? Dead cell, reversed cell, or ammeter not in the loop.

Read the display to its last steady digit. Write what it says, not what you hoped. Repeat each count twice or three times, switch open in between.

Hold add a cell, read both meters in mind and try this part's questions: the readings.

## Subtopic: Write It Down the Way It Was

Table: cells, three voltage readings, mean, three current readings, mean, volts over amps. The last column should sit near 100 every row. A row near 115 gets remeasured, not rubbed out.

Ink, as you go, your own readings. Note anything odd: warm cell, flickering meter, loose clip. Perfect numbers look invented; small scatter looks real.

Fresh cells, no shorts, ammeter never across the cells, probes by the insulation, switch open between readings.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Ohm's law investigation puts a 100 ohm resistor in a series loop with one, two and then three cells, with a voltmeter across the resistor and an ammeter in the loop, ranges set first. Each added cell raises the voltage by about 1.5 volts and the current by about 15 milliamperes. Readings are taken to the meter's precision, repeated, recorded honestly in a table with a volts over amps column that should stay near 100, and anything unusual is noted rather than hidden.

Hold write it down the way it was, and take the final questions of the lesson: the table and honesty.
