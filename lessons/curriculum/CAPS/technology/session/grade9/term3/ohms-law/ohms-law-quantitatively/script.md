# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers Ohm's law quantitatively: current increases with voltage when resistance is constant. The anchor is a bench in Kimberley with a 100 ohm resistor, a variable supply, a voltmeter across the resistor and an ammeter in series, the readings going 1 volt and 10 milliamperes, 2 volts and 20, 3 volts and 30, the learners seeing the pattern before the formula is written. The governing idea is that for a conductor at constant temperature the current through it is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, that the constant of proportionality is the resistance, and that the relationship V equals I times R can be rearranged to find any of the three quantities. Stating Ohm's law, defining the units and performing simple calculations in all three forms are examinable.

## Subtopic: Voltage, Current and Resistance as Measured Quantities

Three quantities describe what happens in a circuit. Potential difference, usually called voltage, is the electrical push between two points, measured in volts with a voltmeter connected across the two points. Current is the rate of flow of charge, measured in amperes with an ammeter connected in the path so the whole flow passes through it; small currents are given in milliamperes, thousandths of an ampere, and the LED's 10 milliamperes is 0.01 amperes. Resistance is the opposition a component offers to current, measured in ohms, and for a resistor it is the marked value read from the bands.

The water analogy from Grade 8 still helps as long as its limits are remembered. Voltage is like the pressure difference that drives water through a pipe; current is like the flow rate in litres per second; resistance is like the narrowness and length of the pipe. More pressure, more flow; a narrower pipe, less flow for the same pressure. The analogy fails on details, but it gives the right instinct for which way each quantity moves when another changes.

Measuring is the foundation of the law, so the meter connections are repeated until they are automatic. Voltmeter across the component, in parallel with it, reading the push between its ends. Ammeter in the loop, in series, so the flow goes through it. A multimeter is set to the correct range before connecting, volts for across and amperes or milliamperes for in-line, and is never set to amperes and connected across a battery. These are the readings that produce the data in the next session.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three quantities and their units and meters, the water analogy and its limits, and correct meter connection.

## Subtopic: Ohm's Law: Current Rises in Step With Voltage at Fixed Resistance

Ohm's law states that the current through a conductor at constant temperature is directly proportional to the potential difference across it. Directly proportional means that doubling the voltage doubles the current, tripling it triples the current, and the graph of current against voltage is a straight line through the origin. The constant that connects them is the resistance: voltage equals current times resistance, written V equals I times R, with V in volts, I in amperes and R in ohms. One volt across one ohm drives one ampere.

For the 100 ohm resistor on the bench: 1 volt gives 10 milliamperes, since 1 over 100 is 0.01 amperes; 2 volts gives 20 milliamperes; 3 volts, 30; 4.5 volts, 45. Each pair of readings divided, volts over amperes, gives 100, the resistance, which is why the resistance is the gradient of the voltage-against-current graph. The law holds for resistors, wires and most metals as long as they do not heat up much; it does not hold for a filament lamp, whose resistance rises as it gets hot, nor for a diode or LED, which is why those components are treated as fixed voltage drops instead.

The condition constant temperature is not a detail. A resistor carrying too much current warms, its resistance drifts, and the straight line bends slightly. For the small currents of the PAT circuits the drift is negligible and the law can be used with confidence. An examination statement of the law should include the condition: current is proportional to voltage provided the temperature stays constant.

The questions for this section are with you now: the statement of Ohm's law and what proportional means, V equals I R with units, and the constant temperature condition and where the law fails.

## Subtopic: The Three Forms of the Law and Simple Worked Examples

The one equation gives three forms. To find voltage: V equals I times R. To find current: I equals V over R. To find resistance: R equals V over I. A triangle with V on top and I and R side by side underneath is the usual memory aid: cover the quantity you want and the other two show whether to multiply or divide. Units must be consistent: amperes not milliamperes in the formula, so 10 milliamperes becomes 0.01 amperes before multiplying, and the answer converted back if needed.

Worked examples. A 470 ohm resistor carries 5 milliamperes: V equals 0.005 times 470, which is 2.35 volts across the resistor. A 4.5 volt battery across a 1500 ohm resistor: I equals 4.5 over 1500, which is 0.003 amperes, 3 milliamperes. A lamp takes 0.3 amperes from 4.5 volts: R equals 4.5 over 0.3, which is 15 ohms when hot. The PAT LED resistor: the battery is 4.5 volts, the LED drops 2, so 2.5 volts is across the resistor; for 10 milliamperes R equals 2.5 over 0.01, which is 250 ohms; the kit's 470 ohms gives I equals 2.5 over 470, about 0.0053 amperes, 5.3 milliamperes, safe and bright enough.

Setting out a calculation earns marks and avoids slips: write the formula, substitute with units, calculate, state the answer with its unit, and check it for sense, a few milliamperes for an LED, not a few amperes. The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting milliamperes straight into the formula without converting. Two: using the battery voltage for the resistor when an LED is taking 2 volts of it. Three: applying Ohm's law to the LED itself as if it were a resistor. Four: stating the law without the constant temperature condition.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three forms and the triangle, the worked examples including the LED resistor, and setting out and checking a calculation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the 100 ohm resistor and the two meters on the bench in Kimberley — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Push, Flow and Opposition

Voltage is the push, in volts, measured across a part. Current is the flow, in amperes, measured in the loop; 10 milliamperes is 0.01 amperes. Resistance is the opposition, in ohms, read off the bands.

Water: pressure is voltage, flow is current, a narrow pipe is resistance. More push, more flow; narrower pipe, less flow.

Voltmeter across. Ammeter in line. Set the range first. Never amperes across the battery.

Keep push, flow and opposition in mind and try the questions for this part: the three quantities.

## Subtopic: Double the Push, Double the Flow

Ohm's law: current is proportional to voltage if the temperature stays the same. Double the volts, double the amps. Straight line through zero. V equals I times R.

100 ohms: 1 volt, 10 milliamperes; 2 volts, 20; 3 volts, 30; 4.5 volts, 45. Volts over amps always gives 100. The resistance is the slope.

Works for resistors and wires. Not for a hot lamp, whose resistance climbs. Not for an LED, which is a fixed 2 volt drop.

Hold double the push, double the flow in mind and try this part's questions: the law.

## Subtopic: One Triangle, Three Sums

Three forms: V equals I R; I equals V over R; R equals V over I. Triangle with V on top. Cover what you want. Convert milliamperes to amperes first.

470 ohms at 5 milliamperes: 2.35 volts. 4.5 volts across 1500 ohms: 3 milliamperes. 4.5 volts and 0.3 amperes: 15 ohms. LED resistor: 2.5 volts spare, 10 milliamperes, 250 ohms; the kit's 470 gives about 5 milliamperes.

Formula, substitute, calculate, unit, sense check.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Voltage in volts is measured across a component, current in amperes in the loop, resistance in ohms. Ohm's law: at constant temperature, current is directly proportional to voltage, and V equals I times R. The law gives three forms for finding any quantity from the other two, with milliamperes converted to amperes. It applies to resistors and wires, not to hot lamps or LEDs, and the PAT LED resistor is found from the voltage left over after the LED's 2 volt drop.

Hold one triangle, three sums, and take the final questions of the lesson: calculations.
