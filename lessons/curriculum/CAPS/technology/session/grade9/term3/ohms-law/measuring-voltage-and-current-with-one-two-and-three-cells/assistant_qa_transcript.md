### subtopic_1 — Setting Up the Investigation: Resistor, Cells and Two Meters
**Assistant:** Why is the voltage across the resistor less than 1.5 volts per cell when the cells are fresh?

**Tutor:** Because a cell is not a perfect source; it has a small resistance of its own inside, and when current flows some of the cell's voltage is used up pushing the current through that internal resistance. With no current flowing, a fresh cell measures about 1.55 volts across its terminals. Connect the 100 ohm resistor so that about 15 milliamperes flows and the internal resistance, perhaps half an ohm to an ohm, takes a few hundredths of a volt, so the resistor sees about 1.45 to 1.5 volts. Three cells in series have three internal resistances, so the loss is a little larger, and an older cell has a higher internal resistance and shows a bigger drop. This is why recording what the meter shows matters: the shortfall is real information about the cells. It is also why the investigation measures the voltage across the resistor itself rather than assuming 1.5 volts per cell.

### subtopic_2 — Taking Readings With One, Two and Three Cells
**Assistant:** What is actually wrong with putting the ammeter across the resistor like the voltmeter?

**Tutor:** Two things, one about the measurement and one about the meter. An ammeter is built to have almost no resistance so that it does not reduce the current it is placed in. Connected across the resistor, it forms a parallel path of almost zero resistance beside the resistor, so nearly all the current goes through the meter and almost none through the resistor; the reading is then not the resistor's current but a short-circuit current limited only by the cells, which tells you nothing about the resistor. Worse, that short-circuit current can be large enough to blow the meter's internal fuse or, if the meter is connected across the cells themselves, to heat the cells. The voltmeter is the opposite: it has a very high resistance, so across a component it draws almost nothing and reads the voltage without disturbing it, and in series it would stop the current almost entirely and read the supply voltage. Across for volts, in line for amps, every time.

### subtopic_3 — Recording, Repeating and Handling the Readings Honestly
**Assistant:** If one of our rows gives volts over amps of 115 and the rest give 100, should we just leave it out?

**Tutor:** No: measure it again, and keep a note of what happened. A single row that disagrees usually has a cause you can find, a crocodile clip that was gripping insulation and adding resistance, a meter on the wrong range, a digit misread. Repeating the measurement with the clip checked usually brings it back to 100, and then the table shows the first reading, the retake and a note saying the clip was loose, which is good practice. If it stays at 115 on repeat, that is also information: perhaps that cell combination has a tired cell with high internal resistance, and the voltage reading is right but the current is lower than the simple sum predicts. Deleting the row hides whatever happened and leaves a table that cannot be trusted; a scientist or an examiner would rather see the odd value with its explanation than a suspiciously clean set.
