# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers plotting the readings from the Ohm's law investigation and determining the relationship between potential difference and current. The anchor is a sheet of squared paper on a desk in Pietermaritzburg with three plotted points and a ruler laid across them and through the origin, the learner reading the gradient as 4.4 volts over 0.044 amperes and getting 100 ohms from the resistor's own bands. The governing idea is that a graph of voltage against current for a resistor is a straight line through the origin, that this is what direct proportionality looks like, that the gradient of the line is the resistance, and that a conclusion must state the relationship found and compare the graphical resistance with the marked value. Plotting a graph correctly, drawing a line of best fit, determining the gradient and writing a supported conclusion are examinable.

## Subtopic: Setting Up the Graph: Axes, Scales and Plotting the Points

A graph makes a relationship visible in a way a table does not. The convention in this investigation is to plot potential difference in volts on the vertical axis and current in amperes on the horizontal axis, so that the gradient of the line comes out directly as resistance, volts over amperes. The independent variable, the one changed on purpose, was the number of cells and therefore the voltage, so some teachers put voltage horizontal; either is acceptable if labelled, but the vertical voltage convention gives resistance as the gradient and is used here.

Each axis is labelled with the quantity and its unit: potential difference in volts, current in amperes or milliamperes. Scales are chosen so that the points fill most of the paper: with readings up to 4.5 volts and 45 milliamperes, a vertical scale of 1 volt per two large squares and a horizontal scale of 10 milliamperes per two large squares works. Scales start at zero on both axes, because the origin is a meaningful point: no voltage, no current, and the line should pass through it. Scales run in even steps; a scale that goes 0, 1, 2, 4 is a common error.

The three mean readings are plotted as small neat crosses, not blobs, at the exact positions: about 1.45 volts and 14.5 milliamperes, 2.9 and 29, 4.4 and 44. The crosses are checked against the table before the line is drawn, because a misplotted point drags the line. If repeats were taken the means are plotted; the individual readings may be shown as smaller marks to display the scatter.

The questions for this section are with you now: which quantity goes on which axis and why, labelling and choosing even scales from zero, and plotting the points as crosses.

## Subtopic: The Line of Best Fit and What a Straight Line Through the Origin Means

A line of best fit is a single straight line drawn with a ruler so that it passes as close as possible to all the points, with roughly as many points above it as below. It is not drawn by joining the crosses dot to dot, which would produce a zigzag that gives the measurement scatter more importance than it deserves. For this investigation the line is also expected to pass through the origin, because zero volts drives zero current, and if the points allow it the ruler is placed through the origin and tilted until it best fits the crosses.

A straight line through the origin is the picture of direct proportionality. It says that voltage divided by current is the same for every point, which is what the table's last column already suggested, and that doubling one doubles the other. If the points lay on a curve, or on a straight line that missed the origin, the relationship would not be simple proportionality: a filament lamp gives a curve that flattens as it heats, because its resistance rises, and a diode gives a line that stays at zero current until about 0.7 volts and then shoots up. The resistor's straight line is the special case the law describes.

Judging the fit honestly matters. Three points can always have a line drawn near them; the question is how close. Points within a small square of the line indicate a good fit; a point well off the line is either a measurement error, to be noted and possibly remeasured, or a sign that the relationship is not linear. The conclusion must match the graph actually drawn, not the graph expected.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a line of best fit is drawn, what a straight line through the origin shows, and judging the fit against curves and offsets.

## Subtopic: Finding Resistance From the Gradient and Writing the Conclusion

The gradient of the line is its rise over its run: choose two points far apart on the line, not necessarily data points, read the voltage difference between them and the current difference between them, and divide. From the origin to the point where the line passes 4.4 volts, the current is about 0.044 amperes, so the gradient is 4.4 over 0.044, which is 100 volts per ampere, and a volt per ampere is an ohm. The gradient of a voltage against current graph is the resistance of the component. Current must be converted to amperes for the division, or the gradient will come out in kilohms if milliamperes are used: 4.4 over 44 is 0.1 kilohms, which is the same 100 ohms.

The graphical resistance is compared with the marked value from the colour bands: brown, black, brown, gold is 100 ohms at 5 percent, so anything from 95 to 105 agrees. A graphical value of 98 or 103 is a good result; a value of 120 points to a systematic problem such as a loose connection adding resistance throughout. The comparison, with its reasoning, is part of the conclusion.

The conclusion is written in three sentences. The relationship: the graph of potential difference against current is a straight line through the origin, so the current through the resistor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, confirming Ohm's law at the temperatures of the experiment. The resistance: the gradient gives a resistance of about 100 ohms, agreeing with the marked value within tolerance. The limitations: the cells' internal resistance made the voltages a little less than 1.5 per cell, which does not affect the gradient, and more cell counts would give more points. The error museum, four exhibits. One: joining the dots with a zigzag. Two: a scale with uneven steps or not starting at zero. Three: a gradient taken from two close data points instead of a long run of the line. Four: a conclusion that claims a perfect straight line when the plotted points visibly scatter.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding the gradient as resistance with correct units, comparing with the marked value, and writing the three-sentence conclusion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the squared paper, the ruler and the three crosses in Pietermaritzburg — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Dots on Squared Paper

Volts up the side, amps along the bottom, so the slope comes out as ohms. Label each axis with the quantity and its unit.

Pick scales so the points fill the page: 1 volt per two big squares, 10 milliamps per two big squares. Start both at zero. Even steps only.

Plot the three means as small neat crosses: about 1.45 and 14.5, 2.9 and 29, 4.4 and 44. Check each against the table.

Keep dots on squared paper in mind and try the questions for this part: axes, scales and points.

## Subtopic: A Straight Line Through Zero

Lay a ruler through the origin and tilt it until it sits as close as possible to all three crosses, some above, some below. One straight line. Do not join the dots.

A straight line through zero means proportional: double one, double the other. A lamp would curve as it heats. A diode would sit at zero then shoot up. The resistor gives the straight line.

Be honest about the fit. Points within a small square: good. One far off: note it, maybe remeasure. Write about the graph you drew, not the one you wanted.

Hold a straight line through zero in mind and try this part's questions: the line of best fit.

## Subtopic: Rise Over Run Is the Resistance

Gradient is rise over run. Take two points far apart on the line. Volts difference over amps difference. 4.4 over 0.044 is 100. Volts per amp is ohms. Convert milliamps to amps, or you get kilohms.

Compare with the bands: brown black brown gold is 100 at 5 percent, so 95 to 105 agrees. 98 is a good result. 120 means something added resistance everywhere.

Three-sentence conclusion: straight line through the origin, so current is proportional to voltage, Ohm's law confirmed. Gradient gives about 100 ohms, matching the bands. Cells lost a little voltage inside, which does not change the slope.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plot potential difference in volts on the vertical axis against current in amperes on the horizontal, with labelled axes, even scales from zero, and the mean readings as small crosses. Draw one straight line of best fit through the origin, not a zigzag. A straight line through the origin shows direct proportionality and confirms Ohm's law. The gradient, rise over run in volts per ampere, is the resistance, compared with the marked value within tolerance, and the conclusion states the relationship, the resistance and the limitations.
