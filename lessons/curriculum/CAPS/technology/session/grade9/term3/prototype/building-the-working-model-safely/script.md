# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers completing a neat, to-scale working model using safe working practices. The anchor is the Rustenburg team's workbench on build day: parts laid out on a sheet marked with their balloon numbers, the soldering iron in its stand with a damp sponge, safety glasses on, the strip board taking its first components while a second member drills the lid for the LED and a third labels the box, the exploded view pinned above the bench as the assembly order. The governing idea is that a model is built to its drawings in a planned order, that soldering and assembly are done neatly because neat work is reliable work, that every tool has a safety rule that is followed without exception, and that the finished model is tested against the brief's numbered specifications before it is declared complete. Building a neat working model safely and testing it against specifications are examinable and form the Make component of PAT 2.

## Subtopic: Planning the Build: Parts, Tools, Order and Safety Rules

Building starts before any tool is picked up. The parts list from the exploded view is laid out on the bench on a sheet of paper, each part placed beside its balloon number, and ticked off against the list; a missing part found now costs a trip to the shop, found later it costs a day. Tools are gathered: soldering iron and stand, solder, wire cutters, wire strippers, small pliers, screwdriver, hand drill with bits for the LED and switch holes, a craft knife, a ruler and a marker. The drawings are pinned where they can be seen: circuit diagram for the board, exploded view for the assembly order, orthographic drawing for sizes.

The order of work follows the exploded view in reverse: prepare the box first, drilling and cutting all holes while it is empty, because drilling a box with a circuit inside fills the board with swarf; build and test the circuit on its board as a separate unit; fit the battery holder; fit the board; fit the lid components and connect them; close the box; label it. Team members take parts of the job in parallel, one drilling, one soldering, one preparing wires and labels, which is why the exploded view and the parts list must be shared and agreed first.

Safety rules are stated before work starts and enforced throughout. Safety glasses for drilling, cutting and soldering, because swarf and solder spit. The soldering iron always in its stand, never laid on the bench; the iron is held by its handle only; hot parts are not touched for a minute after soldering. Wire cutters cut away from the face and from others, since the offcut flies. The craft knife cuts on a board, away from the hand holding the work, with a fresh blade. The drill is clamped or the work is clamped, not hand-held against the bit. Long hair tied back, no loose sleeves, no food on the bench, hands washed after handling solder. One person speaks up when something is unsafe, and work stops until it is fixed.

The questions for this section are with you now: laying out parts against the list, the build order from the exploded view, and the safety rules for each tool.

## Subtopic: Soldering and Assembling the Circuit Neatly

Soldering joins a component lead to a copper track or wire with molten solder. The iron is heated to working temperature and its tip wiped on the damp sponge and tinned with a touch of solder so it is bright. The component is pushed into the board and its lead bent slightly to hold it. The tip is placed so that it touches both the lead and the copper pad at once and is held there for about two seconds to heat both. Solder is fed to the joint, not to the iron; it melts on contact with the hot metal and flows round the lead and over the pad. The solder is withdrawn, then the iron, and the joint is left still for a few seconds while it sets. A good joint is bright, smooth and shaped like a small volcano around the lead; a bad joint is dull, lumpy or a ball sitting on top, called a dry joint, which looks connected but is not.

Components go in with their polarity checked against the circuit diagram before soldering: the LED's long leg, the capacitor's stripe, the transistor's flat face, the buzzer's plus. Resistors and ceramic capacitors go either way. Leads are trimmed close to the joint after soldering with cutters pointed down and away. On strip board the copper strips run in one direction and are cut with a drill bit or a track cutter where the circuit needs a break, exactly as the diagram requires; a track not cut is the commonest fault on a strip board, shorting two parts of the circuit together.

Neatness is checked as the work goes: components flat to the board, leads trimmed, joints bright, wires routed tidily and the right length, red for positive and black for negative where possible, no stray strands of wire that could short. The board is tested before it goes into the box, with the battery connected and the sensor worked, exactly as on the breadboard; a problem found now is fixed with the board in the hand rather than in the box. The test result is written down.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of a good solder joint and what a dry joint looks like, polarity checks and track cuts, and testing the board before fitting.

## Subtopic: Fitting the Enclosure, Testing to Scale and Finishing

The enclosure is prepared to the orthographic drawing. Hole positions for the LED, switch and cable are marked with the ruler and marker, centre-punched or started with a small bit so the drill does not wander, and drilled to the diameter of the part: 5 millimetres for a 5 millimetre LED, the switch's stated size, a cable gland or a grommet for the wire so it cannot chafe or pull the board. Burrs are trimmed with the knife. The battery holder is fixed with double-sided tape or a screw so it cannot rattle loose. The board is fixed on small spacers or a foam pad so no copper touches the box if the box is metal, or simply glued if plastic. The lid parts are fitted and their wires soldered to the board with enough slack to open the lid.

To scale means the model's sizes match the drawing. For a small device the model is full size and every dimension is checked against the orthographic drawing with the ruler; for a device that stands in for something large, such as the tank sensor on a model tank, the scale is stated and kept. A check against the 3D sketch confirms the model looks like what was designed, with the LED on the face the user sees and the button within reach.

Finishing and testing. The box is closed, the label applied with the device name and a one-line instruction, and the specifications are tested one by one with results recorded: does the LED light when the sensor is triggered, is it visible from ten metres, does the test button work, is the current in standby near zero on the meter, does the sound switch off, does the sensor housing keep water out when dunked. Each pass or fail is written against the specification number, and failures are fixed or honestly reported. Good practice on the bench: clean up, iron off and cold, offcuts in the bin, parts returned. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drilling the box after the circuit is inside. Two: a dry joint that passes the eye and fails on the day. Three: a strip board track not cut, shorting the circuit. Four: closing the box without testing the board, and without recording the results.

The questions for this section are with you now: preparing the enclosure and fitting parts, building to scale, and testing against the specifications and finishing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again at the team's bench in Rustenburg on build day — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Lay It All Out First

Lay every part on a sheet beside its number and tick the list. Gather the tools. Pin up the circuit diagram, the exploded view and the orthographic drawing.

Order: prepare the empty box first, drill and cut. Build and test the board on its own. Battery holder in. Board in. Lid parts in and wired. Close. Label. Split the jobs between the team.

Safety: glasses on. Iron in its stand always. Cut away from faces. Knife on a board, away from your hand. Clamp the work for drilling. Hair back, no food, wash hands after solder. Anyone can stop the work.

Keep lay it all out first in mind and try the questions for this part: planning and safety.

## Subtopic: Hot Iron, Steady Hands, Clean Joints

Soldering: hot iron, wipe on the sponge, tin the tip. Part in, lead bent. Tip touches lead and pad together for two seconds. Feed solder to the joint, not the iron. Solder away, iron away, hold still. Good: bright, smooth, a little volcano. Bad: dull, lumpy, a ball on top.

Check polarity before you solder: LED long leg, capacitor stripe, transistor flat face, buzzer plus. Trim leads pointing down. On strip board, cut the tracks where the diagram needs breaks. An uncut track is the top fault.

Neat as you go: parts flat, leads trimmed, red and black wires, no stray strands. Test the board before it goes in the box. Write the result.

Hold hot iron, steady hands, clean joints in mind and try this part's questions: soldering.

## Subtopic: Does It Match the Drawing and Do the Job?

Box: mark the holes from the drawing, start them small so the drill does not wander, drill to size, trim the burrs. Grommet for the cable. Battery holder fixed so it cannot rattle. Board on spacers or glue. Lid parts wired with slack.

To scale: measure the model against the drawing. Full size for a small device. Compare with the sketch: LED on the front, button in reach.

Close, label, test each specification and write pass or fail against its number. Fix or report honestly. Clean up, iron off.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The model is built to its drawings in a planned order: parts laid out and ticked, box prepared empty, circuit built and tested on its board, then fitted, closed and labelled. Safety rules for iron, cutters, knife and drill are followed without exception. Solder joints are bright and smooth with polarity checked first and strip board tracks cut as the diagram requires. The finished model is checked to scale against the drawings and tested against each numbered specification with results recorded.

Hold does it match the drawing and do the job, and take the final questions of the lesson: enclosure, scale and testing.
