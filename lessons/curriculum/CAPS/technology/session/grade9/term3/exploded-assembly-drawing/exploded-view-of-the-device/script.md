# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the 3D exploded-view assembly drawing of the device. The anchor is the Rustenburg team's indicator unit drawn on isometric paper: the lid floating above with its LED and push button, the circuit board below it, the battery holder below that, the box base at the bottom with four screws hovering above their holes, and thin dashed lines running vertically through all of them. The governing idea is that an exploded view separates the parts of an assembly along one or more axes while keeping each part's orientation and alignment, so that the drawing shows every component, the order of assembly and how parts fit, and that it is drawn in isometric with alignment lines, part numbers and a parts list. Producing a clear, numbered exploded assembly drawing of the designed device is examinable and forms part of the PAT Design mark.

## Subtopic: What an Exploded View Is and Why Assembly Needs One

An assembled device hides most of its parts. Look at the finished indicator box and only the lid, the LED, the button and the screw heads are visible; the circuit board, battery holder, wires and sensor connector are inside. An exploded view solves this by drawing the device as if it had been gently pulled apart: each part is moved away from its neighbours along a straight line, far enough to be seen clearly but not so far that the relationship is lost. The parts keep their orientation, so the lid is still the right way up above the base, and thin lines, usually dashed or chain, run along the direction of separation to show that the parts slide back together along them.

Exploded views are the standard way to show assembly. Furniture instructions use them so that a buyer can see which screw goes where; repair manuals for cars, bicycles and appliances use them to identify every part by number for ordering; toy construction kits use them in their booklets. For the PAT the exploded view shows the examiner that every part has been thought about, in what order the device will be assembled, and where the fixings go, which the orthographic working drawing from earlier terms does not show well because it draws the assembled object.

The exploded view complements the other drawings rather than replacing them. The circuit diagram shows the electrical connections; the 3D sketch shows the appearance; the orthographic drawing fixes sizes; the exploded view shows assembly. Together they let a stranger understand, build and repair the device, which is the test of a complete set of design drawings.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an exploded view shows that an assembled drawing hides, where exploded views are used, and how it complements the other drawings.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Device Exploded: Axis, Order and Alignment Lines

Drawing begins with the axis. Most small boxes come apart along one line, usually vertical: the lid lifts off upward, the circuit board lifts out, the battery holder lifts out, and the screws come up out of their holes. The axis is drawn first as a light vertical construction line, and every part is positioned on it. If a part comes off sideways, such as a sensor plug in the side of the box, it is exploded along a second horizontal axis from its hole, with its own alignment line. Keep the number of axes small; two is usual, three at most.

The order along the axis is the order of assembly, reversed. The base goes at the bottom, drawn in isometric as a hollow box with its screw bosses. Above it, the battery holder, drawn as a small block with its two wires. Above that the circuit board with the transistor, resistors and capacitor as small shapes standing on it. Above that the lid, drawn from below if the LED and button are mounted in it, or from above with the LED and button exploded upward out of their holes. The screws float above the lid in line with their holes, drawn as short cylinders with slotted heads. Parts are spaced evenly, each gap about half the height of the part below it, so the drawing stays compact.

Each part is drawn in isometric at the same scale and the same angle as the rest, using the 30 degree lines of isometric paper or an isometric grid. Alignment lines, thin dashed lines along the axis, run from each part to the part below, passing through screw holes and locating features, so the reader can see where every part lands. Hidden detail is not usually shown in an exploded view, since the separation reveals what would be hidden; outlines are medium weight, alignment lines thin.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing the axis, ordering the parts from base to lid with screws above, and isometric drawing with alignment lines.

## Subtopic: Labelling, Parts List and Checking the Exploded Drawing

Every part is numbered. A small circle, a balloon, with a number inside is drawn beside each part, joined to it by a thin leader line, and the numbers run in assembly order from the base upward: 1 box base, 2 battery holder, 3 circuit board, 4 lid, 5 LED, 6 push button, 7 screws times four, 8 sensor cable gland. Identical parts share a number with the quantity noted. The parts list is a table on the same sheet or the next, with columns for number, name, quantity, material and size or type, and it matches the balloons exactly; it is the same list used for the working drawing and the costing, kept consistent across all three.

The sheet has a border and a title block with the device name, the drawing title Exploded Assembly, the scale, which is often not to scale and marked NTS for an exploded sketch, the date and the team. A short note may be added for assembly steps that the drawing cannot show, such as solder wires before fitting the board, or fit the LED in the lid before closing. The drawing is checked by asking: is every part in the parts list drawn, is every drawn part numbered, does each alignment line lead to a real hole or feature, could someone assemble the device from this alone.

A good exploded view makes the Make stage faster, because the team follows it as the assembly plan and ticks parts off as they go, and it makes the presentation clearer, because the audience sees the inside of the device without opening it. The error museum, four exhibits. One: parts rotated or tilted so the orientation is lost and the reader cannot tell how they fit. Two: parts scattered off the axis with no alignment lines, which is a pile of parts, not an exploded view. Three: balloon numbers that do not match the parts list. Four: the circuit board drawn but its mounted components, the LED or the switch, forgotten, so the list is incomplete.

The questions for this section are with you now: balloons, numbering and the matching parts list, the title block and assembly notes, and the four checks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Pull It Apart on Paper

A finished box hides its insides. An exploded view pulls the parts apart along a line on paper, each one still the right way up, with thin lines showing how they slide back together.

Flat-pack furniture, repair manuals, toy kits. For the PAT it shows every part, the order they go together and where the screws go.

It works with the other drawings: circuit diagram for wiring, sketch for looks, orthographic for sizes, exploded for assembly.

Keep pull it apart on paper in mind and try the questions for this part: what an exploded view is.

## Subtopic: Everything in a Line

Pick the axis, usually vertical for a box. Draw it faint. Everything sits on it. A side part gets its own sideways axis. Two axes at most, usually.

Bottom to top: base, battery holder, circuit board, lid, then the LED and button out of the lid, screws floating above their holes. Even gaps, about half a part high.

Draw each part in isometric, same scale, same angle, on the 30 degree grid. Thin dashed lines from part to part through the holes. No hidden lines needed.

Hold everything in a line in mind and try this part's questions: drawing it.

## Subtopic: Number Every Piece

Balloon with a number beside each part, leader line to it, numbered from the base up. Same parts share a number with a quantity. Parts list: number, name, quantity, material, size. Must match the balloons and the costing.

Border, title block: device, Exploded Assembly, NTS, date, team. Notes for things the drawing cannot show, like solder before fitting.

Check: every listed part drawn, every drawn part numbered, every alignment line leads to a real hole, could a stranger assemble it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An exploded view shows the parts of the device separated along an axis in assembly order, each in its true orientation and drawn in isometric at one scale, with thin alignment lines showing how they fit together. Parts are numbered with balloons in assembly order and listed in a matching parts list with quantity, material and size, and the sheet carries a title block and any assembly notes. It complements the circuit diagram, sketch and orthographic drawing, and serves as the assembly plan for the Make stage.

Hold number every piece, and take the final questions of the lesson: labelling and checking.
