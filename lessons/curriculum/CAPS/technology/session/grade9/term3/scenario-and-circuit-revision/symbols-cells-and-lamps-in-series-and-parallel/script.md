# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises component symbols and cells and lamps in series and parallel. The anchor is a Technology room in Upington with a box of 1.5 volt cells, three identical torch bulbs in holders, crocodile leads and a worksheet of circuit diagrams, the learners predicting brightness before closing each circuit and then testing. The governing idea is that circuit diagrams use agreed symbols so that any reader can build the same circuit, that series connection forms one path in which the current is the same everywhere and the cells' voltages add while the lamps share that voltage, and that parallel connection forms separate paths in which each branch receives the full supply voltage and the branches are independent. Drawing circuits with standard symbols and predicting brightness and behaviour in series and parallel arrangements are examinable.

## Subtopic: Circuit Symbols and Why Diagrams Use Them

A circuit diagram is a drawing of how components are connected, not of what they look like. Each component has an agreed symbol: a cell is a long thin line for the positive terminal beside a short thick line for the negative; a battery is two or more cells drawn together, or a pair of cells with a dashed line between them for more; a lamp is a circle with a cross inside; a switch is a gap in the line with a hinged bar, drawn open; a resistor is a rectangle; a variable resistor is a rectangle with a diagonal arrow; an LED is a diode triangle and bar with two small arrows pointing away; a buzzer is a half circle on a line; a motor is a circle with M; wires are straight lines drawn horizontally and vertically with right-angle corners, and a dot marks a junction where wires join.

The reason for symbols is the same as the reason for orthographic drawing: a stranger must be able to build exactly what was designed. A picture of a torch bulb drawn by one learner looks different from another's, and a photograph hides the connections, but the circle with a cross is understood by every electrician and electronics technician in the country and beyond. Diagrams are drawn tidily, components spaced out, the positive terminal of the supply conventionally at the top or left, and nothing crossing unnecessarily.

Reading a diagram means tracing the path that current can take from the positive terminal of the supply, through components, back to the negative terminal. If there is a complete path with no gap, current flows; a gap, such as an open switch or a broken wire, stops it. Every circuit in the term, up to the transistor circuits later, is read this way, so the habit of tracing the loop with a finger is worth forming now.

The questions for this section are with you now: the standard symbols, why symbols rather than pictures are used, and tracing the current path.

## Subtopic: Cells and Lamps in Series: One Path, Shared Voltage

In a series circuit the components are joined end to end so that there is only one path. Current leaves the positive terminal, passes through every component in turn, and returns to the negative terminal; the same current flows through each component because there is nowhere else for it to go. Cells in series add their voltages: two 1.5 volt cells give 3 volts, three give 4.5 volts, provided they are connected positive to negative. This is how a torch or a remote control gets its working voltage from small cells, and the 4.5 volt battery in the PAT circuits is three cells in series.

Lamps in series share the supply voltage. One lamp across 3 volts receives 3 volts and glows at full brightness. Two identical lamps in series across the same 3 volts receive 1.5 volts each and glow dimly; three receive 1 volt each and glow dimmer still, because the same current must push through more resistance and the supply is divided among them. If one lamp in a series string burns out, the single path is broken and every lamp goes out, which is the familiar fault of cheap festive lights.

Investigation: one cell and one lamp, note the brightness; two cells in series and one lamp, brighter; two cells and two lamps in series, back to the single-cell brightness since each lamp again gets 1.5 volts; remove one lamp from its holder and the other goes out. Draw each circuit with symbols before building it and predict, then test. The records become the revision notes for the PAT circuit diagram.

The questions for this section are with you now: the single path and equal current, cells adding voltage, and lamps sharing voltage and failing together.

## Subtopic: Cells and Lamps in Parallel: Many Paths, Same Voltage

In a parallel circuit components are connected side by side across the same two points, so there are several paths. Each branch connects directly to the positive and negative terminals, so each branch receives the full supply voltage. Two identical lamps in parallel across 3 volts each receive 3 volts and both glow at full brightness, as bright as a single lamp would; the supply must provide twice the current, so the cells run down sooner. If one lamp in a parallel pair burns out, its branch is broken but the other branch is complete and its lamp stays lit, which is why house lights and car lights are wired in parallel.

Cells in parallel do not add voltage. Two 1.5 volt cells in parallel, positive to positive and negative to negative, still give 1.5 volts, but they share the current and last twice as long. This is used where a long life at a low voltage matters more than a high voltage. Connecting cells in parallel the wrong way round, positive to negative, shorts them and is dangerous; cells are connected in parallel only when they are identical and the same way round.

The comparison, which examinations ask for directly: series has one path, the same current everywhere, voltage shared among components, cells adding voltage, and one failure stopping everything; parallel has several paths, the full voltage on each branch, current divided among branches, cells sharing current without adding voltage, and branches independent. The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing a lamp as a picture of a bulb. Two: expecting two series lamps to be as bright as one. Three: expecting cells in parallel to double the voltage. Four: drawing a parallel circuit without junction dots, so the branches appear to cross without connecting.

The questions for this section are with you now: full voltage on each parallel branch, cells in parallel sharing current, and the series versus parallel comparison.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the box of cells and three torch bulbs in Upington — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pictures That Electricians Agree On

A circuit diagram shows connections, not looks. Cell: long thin line and short thick line. Lamp: circle with a cross. Switch: a gap with a bar. Resistor: a rectangle. LED: triangle, bar, two little arrows. Buzzer: half circle. Wires: straight lines, right-angle corners, a dot where they join.

Symbols mean anyone can build your circuit. Draw tidily, positive at the top or left.

Read a diagram by tracing from positive, through everything, back to negative. Complete loop: current flows. Gap: nothing.

Keep pictures that electricians agree on in mind and try the questions for this part: symbols.

## Subtopic: One Loop or Several Loops

Series: one loop, everything in a line. Same current through each part. Cells in series add up: two 1.5 volt cells make 3, three make 4.5. That is the PAT battery.

Lamps in series share the volts. One lamp on 3 volts: bright. Two: 1.5 each, dim. Three: dimmer. One burns out, all go out.

Try it: one cell one lamp, two cells one lamp brighter, two cells two lamps back to dim, pull a lamp and the other dies. Draw first, predict, then test.

Hold one loop or several loops in mind and try this part's questions: series.

## Subtopic: Series Shares, Parallel Copies

Parallel: side by side, each branch straight across the supply. Each lamp gets the full 3 volts, both bright. The cells run down faster. One burns out, the other stays on. That is house wiring.

Cells in parallel do not add volts. Two 1.5 volt cells in parallel: still 1.5, but they last twice as long. Same way round only.

Compare: series, one path, same current, volts shared, cells add, one fault kills all. Parallel, many paths, full volts each, current shared, cells last longer, branches independent.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Circuit diagrams use standard symbols so that anyone can build the circuit, and are read by tracing the loop from positive to negative. In series there is one path: the same current flows through every component, cells add their voltages and lamps share the supply, so each is dimmer and one failure stops all. In parallel there are several paths: each branch gets the full voltage, lamps are at full brightness and independent, and cells in parallel share current without adding voltage.

Hold series shares, parallel copies, and take the final questions of the lesson: parallel and the comparison.
