### subtopic_1 — Circuit Symbols and Why Diagrams Use Them
**Assistant:** If two lamps in parallel are both as bright as one lamp alone, where does the extra energy come from?

**Tutor:** From the cells, which are now working twice as hard. Brightness depends on the voltage across the lamp and the current through it. In parallel each lamp sits directly across the supply, so each gets the full voltage and draws its full current, exactly as if it were alone. The supply therefore has to deliver the sum of the two currents, twice as much as for one lamp. The cells are converting chemical energy to electrical energy at twice the rate, so they run flat in about half the time. Nothing is free: two bright lamps cost twice the energy of one, whether they are in parallel on a battery or in parallel on the mains, where the meter simply turns faster.

### subtopic_2 — Cells and Lamps in Series: One Path, Shared Voltage
**Assistant:** Why do torches put cells in series but houses put lights in parallel?

**Tutor:** Because they are solving different problems. A torch has one bulb that needs, say, 3 volts, and the available cells are 1.5 volts each, so two must be stacked in series to add their voltages and reach the bulb's rating; the torch does not need a second path because there is only one lamp. A house has dozens of lamps and appliances, each designed for the full mains voltage, and the owner wants to switch each one independently and not have the whole house go dark when one bulb fails. Parallel wiring gives every lamp the full voltage on its own branch with its own switch. The two ideas are often combined: the PAT battery is three cells in series to make 4.5 volts, and the device may then have an LED and a buzzer in parallel across it so that each gets the full 4.5 volts and the buzzer can be switched off without affecting the LED.

### subtopic_3 — Cells and Lamps in Parallel: Many Paths, Same Voltage
**Assistant:** How do I tell series from parallel in a messy diagram?

**Tutor:** Ignore the shapes and count the paths. Put a finger on the positive terminal of the supply and ask, at every junction dot, whether the current has a choice. If there is never a choice, only one way to go until you reach the negative terminal, the circuit is series, however the wires bend. If at some dot the current can go two ways and the two routes rejoin later, those two routes are in parallel with each other. Many real circuits are mixed: a switch in series with the whole thing, then two branches in parallel. Describe them component by component: the switch is in series with the supply, the LED and the buzzer are in parallel with each other. Redrawing the messy diagram neatly, with the supply on the left and the branches as vertical lines between two horizontal rails, usually makes the structure obvious.
