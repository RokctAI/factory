# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers investigating the need so that an appropriate electronic circuit can be incorporated into a device. The anchor is a household in Mahikeng where the municipal water is unreliable and a plastic tank on a stand keeps the family going, the grandmother asking for something that tells her when the tank is nearly empty before the taps run dry. The governing idea is that an electronic device begins as a human need, that the investigation converts the need into facts about user, setting, inputs and outputs, and that every electronic circuit can be understood as something that senses a condition, decides, and shows or does something in response. Investigating a scenario, identifying the user's needs and the device's required inputs and outputs, and recording findings are examinable and form the Investigate component of PAT 2.

## Subtopic: The PAT Scenario: A Need That an Electronic Device Can Meet

A scenario for an electronic device describes people with a problem that information could solve: knowing when the tank is low, knowing someone is at the door, knowing the soil is dry, finding the way to the bathroom at night. In each case the device must detect something and tell someone. The investigation begins by reading the scenario twice and writing down, in plain sentences, what the problem actually is. For the Mahikeng tank: the family cannot see the water level inside an opaque tank on a two metre stand, so they discover it is empty only when a tap runs dry, usually in the morning when everyone needs water.

Then the people are described. The main user is the grandmother, who runs the house and would check the device several times a day; she has good hearing but her eyesight is weak, so an indicator must be large or bright. Other users are the children, who fill buckets and could learn to read the device too. The setting is an outdoor tank in strong sunlight, which washes out small lamps, and a kitchen ten metres away where the family actually is. The constraints are a small budget, components available from an electronics shop in town, no mains electricity at the tank and a device that must survive rain and sun.

A good investigation also records what the device must not do: it must not need the tank to be opened, must not use mains electricity near water, must not drain its battery in a week, must not be so complicated that nobody fixes it when it fails. These negatives are as useful as the positives when the design brief is written.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading a scenario as a problem of detecting and telling, describing users and setting, and recording what the device must not do.

## Subtopic: Investigating Users, Settings and Existing Devices

Next, how is the problem solved now, and how do others solve it? The family at present bangs on the tank and listens to the sound, or climbs a ladder and lifts the lid. Shops sell float switches with a wire to an indicator, electronic level sensors with several LEDs in a row, and expensive ultrasonic gauges with a display. Each is examined for what it senses, how it shows the result, what it costs and what it needs to run. The float switch is simple and cheap and needs only a battery and a lamp; the LED ladder shows more information but costs more; the ultrasonic gauge is too costly and needs mains power.

Looking at existing devices teaches what the circuit might contain. A float switch is just a switch closed by a floating ball, so the circuit is a cell, a switch and a lamp, which is Grade 8 work. An LED ladder uses several sensors and several LEDs. A moisture alarm uses two probes whose resistance drops when the soil is wet, feeding a transistor that switches a buzzer. A night light uses a light-dependent resistor and a transistor. These are the components of this term, and seeing them in real products connects the lessons to the PAT.

The investigation also asks the users. A short set of questions for the grandmother: when do you most need to know the level, where would you like to see the warning, would a light or a sound suit you better, how often would you change a battery. Her answers, that she wants a bright light she can see from the kitchen window and that she would rather not have a buzzer at night, shape the brief directly. Record the questions and answers; they are evidence.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the problem is solved now and by existing products, what those products reveal about circuits, and asking the users.

## Subtopic: From Need to Circuit: What the Electronics Must Do

Every electronic device can be described as three parts: an input that senses a condition, a process that decides what to do, and an output that shows or does something. For the tank: the input senses the water level, by a float switch or by probes that lose contact when the water drops below them; the process decides that the level is low, which for a simple device is just the switch closing or a transistor turning on; the output shows it, with a bright LED in the kitchen window, perhaps a flashing one, and possibly a buzzer that can be switched off at night. Writing the device in this input, process, output form is the bridge from the need to the circuit.

The investigation closes with a list of what the circuit must do, in the user's terms and then in electronic terms. User terms: show clearly when the tank is nearly empty, be visible from the kitchen, work for months on a battery, survive outdoors. Electronic terms: a sensor that changes state at a chosen level, a switch or transistor that responds to it, an LED bright enough to see in daylight through a window, a battery of about 4.5 or 6 volts with a resistor to protect the LED, a waterproof box, a long wire from tank to kitchen or a sensor at the kitchen end of a pipe.

All of this is recorded on the investigation pages of the PAT: the scenario in your own words, the users and setting, the existing solutions with a short comparison, the user interview, and the input, process, output statement with the electronic requirements. The error museum, four exhibits. One: jumping to a favourite circuit before the user has been asked. Two: ignoring the setting, so the LED is invisible in sunlight. Three: describing only what the device does and not who it is for. Four: forgetting the power source and how often it must be replaced.

The questions for this section are with you now: input, process and output for the device, the circuit requirements in user and electronic terms, and the investigation record.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the water tank on its stand in Mahikeng and the grandmother at the kitchen window — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Who Needs What, and Why

The scenario is a problem: the family cannot see inside the tank and finds out it is empty when the tap runs dry. The device must detect something and tell someone.

Who: the grandmother, weak eyesight, checks often. The children too. Where: outside, in bright sun, with the family ten metres away in the kitchen. Limits: small budget, town shop parts, no mains at the tank, rain and sun.

What it must not do: open the tank, use mains near water, eat batteries, be too complicated to fix.

Keep who needs what, and why in mind and try the questions for this part: reading the scenario.

## Subtopic: Look at What Already Exists

How is it done now? Banging on the tank, climbing a ladder. What do shops sell? A float switch with a lamp, a row of LEDs, an expensive ultrasonic gauge. Compare: what it senses, how it shows, what it costs, what it runs on.

Those products show you the circuits: a float switch is a switch, a cell and a lamp. A moisture alarm is probes and a transistor and a buzzer. A night light is an LDR and a transistor. This term's components.

Ask the user. She wants a bright light visible from the kitchen window, no buzzer at night. Write down the questions and the answers.

Hold look at what already exists in mind and try this part's questions: existing solutions and the interview.

## Subtopic: Sense, Decide, Show

Every device is input, process, output. Input: sense the water level. Process: decide it is low, by a switch closing or a transistor turning on. Output: a bright LED in the kitchen window, maybe flashing, maybe a buzzer with an off switch.

Write what the circuit must do, in her words and then in electronics: a sensor at the right level, a switch or transistor, a bright LED with its resistor, a 4.5 or 6 volt battery, a waterproof box, a wire to the kitchen.

Record it all on the PAT investigation pages: scenario, users, setting, existing products, interview, input-process-output, requirements.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An electronic device starts as a need: something to detect and someone to tell. Investigate the users, the setting and the limits, including what the device must not do. Study how the problem is solved now and by existing products, and ask the user. Write the device as input, process and output, then list what the circuit must do in the user's terms and in electronic terms, and record everything for the PAT.

Hold sense, decide, show, and take the final questions of the lesson: input, process, output.
