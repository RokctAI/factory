# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the five-minute team presentation of sketches, artistic impressions, plans, costing and model, and each learner's record of their individual contribution. The anchor is the Rustenburg team standing at the front of the class with their portfolio pages pinned on a board in order, the model on the desk with a jug of water and the sensor housing ready for the demonstration, each member holding a card with their part of the talk, and the teacher with a stopwatch. The governing idea is that a presentation is a planned story told in a fixed time with every member speaking and every claim shown by a drawing or a demonstration, and that the individual contribution record states specifically what each learner investigated, designed, drew, built and tested so that individual marks are fair. Planning and delivering a timed team presentation and writing an honest individual contribution record are examinable and complete PAT 2.

## Subtopic: Planning a Five-Minute Presentation: Story, Roles and Timing

Five minutes is about 600 spoken words, so the presentation cannot say everything in the portfolio; it tells the story and points to the pages for the detail. The story runs in the order the work was done: the need, one sentence about the Mahikeng household and the empty tank; the brief, the sentence and three key specifications; the investigation, what existing products were found and what the grandmother said; the ideas, the three sketches and the comparison table; the final design, the circuit diagram and the 3D sketch; the drawings, orthographic and exploded; the cost, the parts list total against the budget; the model, demonstrated; and the evaluation, which specifications passed, which failed, and what the team would change.

Roles are assigned so that every member speaks for about a minute and presents the part they led, which also makes the individual contribution visible. One opens with the need and the brief; one presents the investigation and the sketches; one explains the circuit and the drawings; one demonstrates the model and gives the costing and evaluation. The order is written on cards, one per speaker, with the first line of each part and the page to point to, not a script to read aloud.

Timing is rehearsed with a stopwatch at least twice. A first run usually takes eight minutes; the team cuts repetition, moves detail to the pages and tightens the demonstration until it fits five with half a minute to spare for questions. The handover between speakers is practised so that it is a short sentence, not a shuffle. The model is set up before the presentation starts so that no time is spent connecting a battery in front of the audience.

The questions for this section are with you now: the nine-part story in working order, assigning roles so each member speaks, and rehearsing to time.

## Subtopic: Showing the Sketches, Impressions, Plans, Costing and Model

Showing beats telling. Every claim in the story is backed by something the audience can see. The need: a photograph or sketch of the tank on its stand. The brief: the brief page pinned up with the specifications numbered. The investigation: the comparison table of existing products and the interview answers. The ideas: the three sketches side by side with the tick table. The final design: the circuit diagram, large and clear, with the presenter tracing the current path with a finger; and the 3D sketch or artistic impression showing the box on the windowsill with the tank through the window. The drawings: the orthographic sheet and the exploded view. The costing: the parts list with prices and the total compared to the budget, honestly including anything that went over.

The model is the centrepiece and is demonstrated, not described. The reed switch sensor is shown with its magnet float; the float is lowered in a jug of water as the tank level drops and the LED lights in the box ten metres away, or as far as the room allows; the test button is pressed; the silence switch is flipped with the buzzer sounding and stopping. If something does not work on the day, the presenter says so plainly, shows the test record where it did work, and explains the fault; a calm account of a failure earns respect, and marks, where a cover-up earns neither.

The artistic impression deserves a word of its own. It is a pictorial drawing, in colour if possible, of the device in its setting as the user will see it, and its job in the presentation is to make the audience feel the need being met: the grandmother at the window glancing at a small red light. It is not a technical drawing and it is not marked for accuracy; it is marked for communicating the design's purpose. One good impression at the start or end of the presentation does more than a paragraph of description.

The questions for this section are with you now: backing every claim with a visible page, demonstrating the model including handling a failure, and the artistic impression's purpose.

## Subtopic: Delivering It Well and Recording Each Learner's Contribution

Delivery is a skill with simple rules. Stand so that the audience can see the pages and the model, not with a back to them. Speak to the audience, not to the page, using the card for the first line of each part. Speak slowly and clearly; nervousness speeds people up, so the rehearsal pace is deliberately a little slow. Point to the page or part being discussed. Use the technical words correctly, specification, transistor, series, orthographic, because the audience includes an examiner listening for them. Finish with a sentence that says what the team learnt and what it would do next, then invite questions and answer them briefly, saying I do not know rather than guessing.

Each learner's individual contribution is recorded on a page in their own portfolio, written in the first person, specific and honest. Not I helped with the design, but I drew Sketch B, the scissor platform idea, I built and tested the LDR divider on the breadboard, I drilled and fitted the lid, I compiled the comparison table and wrote the justification, I measured the standby current for specification 3. Dates or weeks are given. Where the team worked together, that is said: we soldered the board together with each person doing two joints. The record also notes what the learner found difficult and what they learnt.

The contribution record is checked against the team's work: the sketches carry their author's name, the test pages carry the tester's name, the parts list has the compiler's name. A record that claims work the pages do not support is a problem for the learner, and a record that modestly leaves out real work loses marks, so the team reads one another's records before they are handed in. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a presentation that reads the portfolio aloud for eight minutes. Two: a model described instead of demonstrated. Three: a vague contribution record that says I helped with everything. Four: a failure on the day hidden instead of explained with the test record.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rules of delivery and answering questions, writing a specific individual contribution record, and checking records against the work.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the team at the front of the class in Rustenburg, cards in hand and the model on the desk — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Five Minutes, One Story

Five minutes is about 600 words. Tell the story in the order you did the work: need, brief, investigation, ideas, final design, drawings, cost, model, evaluation. Point to the pages for the detail.

Everyone speaks for about a minute on the part they led. One card each with the first line and the page to point at. Not a script.

Rehearse with a stopwatch, twice. Cut until it fits with half a minute spare. Practise the handovers. Set the model up before you start.

Keep five minutes, one story in mind and try the questions for this part: planning.

## Subtopic: Show, Do Not Just Tell

Show, do not just tell. Need: a picture of the tank. Brief: the page with numbered specifications. Investigation: the comparison table and the interview. Ideas: three sketches and the tick table. Final design: the circuit diagram, traced with a finger, and the impression of the box on the sill. Drawings: orthographic and exploded. Cost: the list and the total against the budget.

Demonstrate the model: lower the float in a jug, the LED lights across the room, press the test button, flip the silence switch. If it fails, say so, show the test record, explain the fault.

The artistic impression is a coloured picture of the device in use. It makes people feel the need being met. One good one is worth a paragraph.

Hold show, do not just tell in mind and try this part's questions: showing and demonstrating.

## Subtopic: Say What You Did

Delivery: face the audience, speak slowly, point at what you are talking about, use the right words, finish with what you learnt and would change, take questions, say I do not know if you do not.

Your own contribution: first person, specific, dated. I drew Sketch B. I tested the LDR divider. I drilled the lid. I compiled the table. I measured the standby current. Say what you found hard and what you learnt.

Check the records against the pages: sketches have names, tests have names. Read each other's before handing in. Claim what you did, all of it, and nothing more.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A five-minute presentation tells the PAT story in working order with every member speaking on the part they led, every claim backed by a page or a demonstration, the model demonstrated rather than described and any failure explained with the test record, all rehearsed to time. Each learner records their individual contribution specifically and honestly in the first person, checked against the named pages of the portfolio.

Hold say what you did, and take the final questions of the lesson: delivery and contribution records.
