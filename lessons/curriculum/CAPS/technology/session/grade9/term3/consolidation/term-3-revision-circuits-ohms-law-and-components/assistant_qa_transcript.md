### subtopic_1 — Revising Circuits: Symbols, Series, Parallel and Logic
**Assistant:** The exam may show a circuit I have never seen. How do I approach it?

**Tutor:** The same way as every circuit this term: identify the parts, trace the loop, and ask what each part does to the current. First name every component from its symbol and note the polarised ones. Second, find the supply and trace from positive, following every path, counting where the current can choose and marking series and parallel sections. Third, for each component apply its one rule: a switch opens or closes its path, a resistor obeys Ohm's law, an LED drops 2 volts and lights if forward, a diode passes one way, a transistor turns on when its base is above 0.6 volts and then passes the collector current, a sensor changes its resistance with the condition, a capacitor charges slowly through its resistor. Fourth, answer the actual question, usually what happens when a switch closes or a sensor changes, by following the chain: the LDR's resistance falls, the base voltage rises past 0.6, the transistor turns on, the buzzer sounds. An unfamiliar circuit is only familiar parts in a new arrangement, and the tracing method works on all of them.

### subtopic_2 — Revising Ohm's Law: Measuring, Graphing and Calculating
**Assistant:** Which calculations are most likely, and what loses marks on them?

**Tutor:** Three appear almost every year. The LED series resistor: supply minus the LED's 2 volts, divided by the current, with the current in amperes; the mark-losing error is using the full supply voltage as if the LED were not there, or forgetting to convert 10 milliamperes to 0.01. A resistor's value from readings, R equals V over I, often from a graph's gradient; the error is taking two close points or leaving the current in milliamperes so the answer is in kilohms without saying so. The voltage divider or series pair, where two resistors share the supply in proportion; the error is forgetting that the larger resistor takes the larger share. In every case, the five-line layout saves marks: knowns with units, formula, substitution with units, calculation, answer with unit and a sense check. A method that is right with a slip in arithmetic still earns most of the marks; a bare number earns none.

### subtopic_3 — Revising Components: Diodes, LEDs, Resistors, Switches, Transistors, Sensors and Capacitors
**Assistant:** How much do I need to write when asked to explain a circuit?

**Tutor:** Enough to name every part's job and to follow the chain of events once, which is usually five or six sentences for a full circuit. Start with the supply and the switch. Then the sensing part: the divider of sensor and variable resistor sets the base voltage, and say which way it moves when the condition changes. Then the deciding part: when the base passes 0.6 volts the transistor turns on and passes the collector current; the base resistor limits the base current. Then the showing part: the LED lights, with its resistor limiting its current; or the buzzer sounds. Then any extra: the capacitor smooths and delays, or the test button bypasses the sensor. Finish with one sentence of purpose: so the LED comes on when the tank is low. Examiners award marks per component understood and per correct step in the chain, so a paragraph that says it is a temperature alarm that uses a transistor earns one or two marks where the six sentences earn six.
