# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers writing a design brief with specifications and constraints for the PAT electronic device. The anchor is a team of four in a classroom in Rustenburg with their investigation pages spread on the desk, the Mahikeng tank scenario at the top, arguing over whether the brief should say light or buzzer and settling it by going back to the grandmother's interview. The governing idea is that a brief for an electronic device states the need without naming the circuit, turns each investigated fact into a measurable specification covering input, output, supply, enclosure and use, lists the constraints of kit, cost, time and safety, and names the criteria the finished device will be evaluated against. Writing a clear design brief with measurable specifications and constraints for the PAT device is examinable and carries Design marks.

## Subtopic: From Investigation to Brief: One Sentence That Names the Need

The design brief opens with one sentence: design and make a device that warns a household when the water in their outdoor tank is nearly empty, showing the warning clearly inside the house, for a family in Mahikeng without reliable mains electricity. The sentence names the product, a warning device; the job, telling the household the tank is low; the user, the family and especially the grandmother; and the setting, an outdoor tank with the warning seen indoors. It does not say LED, float switch, transistor or buzzer, because those are solutions, and the brief describes the problem.

The sentence is built from the investigation. Each phrase traces back to a page: nearly empty from the problem of taps running dry; clearly inside the house from the grandmother's eyesight and her kitchen window; without reliable mains from the setting. A reader can check the brief against the investigation and see that nothing was invented. If a phrase cannot be traced back, it comes out or the investigation is extended to justify it.

Writing the brief is a team job. Each member reads the sentence and asks whether a device meeting it would actually help the user they investigated, and whether it rules anything in or out that it should not. If the sentence says light, a team member who thinks a buzzer is better has a case to argue, and the interview, where the grandmother asked for a light she could see from the window and no noise at night, settles it: the brief says show the warning clearly, which allows a light and permits an optional sound.

The questions for this section are with you now: the one-sentence brief and its four parts, tracing each phrase to the investigation, and agreeing the sentence as a team.

## Subtopic: Specifications for an Electronic Device

Specifications say what the device must do and must have, and each one is written so that it can be checked by a test or a measurement at the end. For an electronic device they fall into groups. Input: the device must detect when the water level falls below a chosen point, about one fifth of the tank, and must not need the tank to be opened. Output: it must show a warning visible from the kitchen window in daylight at ten metres, and any sound must be able to be switched off. Supply: it must run from a 4.5 or 6 volt battery and draw almost no current while the tank is full, so that the battery lasts at least six months.

Enclosure and setting: the sensor part must survive rain and sun outdoors; the indicator part must mount indoors by the window; the wire between them must be at least twelve metres and protected where it crosses the yard. Use and safety: it must be operated and understood by the grandmother without instructions beyond a single label, must have a test button so the battery can be checked, and must have no exposed mains or sharp edges. Size and appearance: the indoor unit must be no larger than a cellphone, and must look tidy enough to sit on a windowsill.

A specification that cannot be tested is rewritten until it can. Must be bright becomes visible from ten metres in daylight. Must be reliable becomes operates correctly on twenty consecutive tests. Must be easy to use becomes operated by the user on first attempt without help. Each specification is numbered so that the evaluation at the end can refer to it: specification 3, battery life, passed; specification 5, visible at ten metres, passed on a sunny day.

The questions for this section are with you now: specification groups for an electronic device, examples from the tank warning, and making every specification testable.

## Subtopic: Constraints, Evaluation Criteria and Team Agreement

Constraints are the limits the team did not choose and cannot move. The components must come from the class kit and the local electronics shop: the kit holds 4.5 volt battery holders, LEDs, 470 ohm and other resistors, transistors, light-dependent resistors, thermistors, buzzers, switches and wire. The budget is a stated figure, perhaps 80 rand per team beyond the kit. The time is the term's weeks 3 to 9 for design and making. Safety rules forbid mains electricity in the device. The model must be built to a stated scale if the real device would be large, and for a small indicator it is built full size.

The brief closes with the evaluation criteria: each numbered specification will be tested; and the device will be judged under fitness for purpose, safety, ergonomics, cost and aesthetics, the five headings used all year. Writing the criteria now, before the device exists, stops the team from choosing tests later that their device happens to pass. It also tells each member what good looks like, which is what a brief is for.

The finished brief is a single page: the sentence, the numbered specifications in their groups, the constraints, the evaluation criteria, and the team members' names. It goes into the PAT portfolio as the first Design page. The error museum, four exhibits. One: a sentence that names the circuit, such as design an LED float switch indicator. Two: specifications that cannot be tested, such as must work well. Three: constraints and specifications mixed together, so the budget appears as something the device must do. Four: a brief written by one team member and never read by the others, so the sketches that follow ignore it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the constraints of kit, cost, time and safety, writing the evaluation criteria in advance, and the one-page brief.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the team of four and their investigation pages in Rustenburg — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Say What, for Whom, Where

One sentence: design and make a device that warns a household when the tank is nearly empty, showing the warning clearly inside the house, for a family without reliable electricity. Product, job, user, setting. No circuit named.

Every phrase comes from a page of the investigation. Nearly empty: the dry taps. Inside the house: her eyesight and the window. No mains: the setting. If you cannot trace it, cut it.

The whole team reads it. Light or buzzer? Go back to the interview: she wants a light, no noise at night. So the brief says show clearly, and sound is optional.

Keep say what, for whom, where in mind and try the questions for this part: the sentence.

## Subtopic: Rules You Can Measure

Specifications in groups, each one testable. Input: detect the level below one fifth, without opening the tank. Output: visible from the window at ten metres in daylight, sound can be switched off. Supply: 4.5 or 6 volt battery, almost no current when full, six months of battery.

Enclosure: sensor survives rain and sun, indicator sits by the window, twelve metres of protected wire. Use: understood by the grandmother with one label, a test button, no mains, no sharp edges. Size: no bigger than a phone, tidy on a sill.

Bright becomes visible at ten metres. Reliable becomes twenty tests in a row. Easy becomes used first time without help. Number them.

Hold rules you can measure in mind and try this part's questions: specifications.

## Subtopic: Limits You Cannot Move

Constraints you cannot move: kit parts and the local shop, about 80 rand extra, weeks 3 to 9, no mains, full size for a small indicator.

Evaluation criteria now, not later: each numbered specification gets a test, and the five headings, purpose, safety, ergonomics, cost, looks.

One page: sentence, numbered specifications, constraints, criteria, names. First Design page of the PAT.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A design brief for the PAT device is one sentence naming the product, job, user and setting with no circuit named, each phrase traced to the investigation and agreed by the team. Specifications are numbered, grouped by input, output, supply, enclosure, use and size, and written so each can be tested. Constraints are the fixed limits of kit, cost, time and safety. Evaluation criteria are written before the device exists.

Hold limits you cannot move, and take the final questions of the lesson: constraints and criteria.
