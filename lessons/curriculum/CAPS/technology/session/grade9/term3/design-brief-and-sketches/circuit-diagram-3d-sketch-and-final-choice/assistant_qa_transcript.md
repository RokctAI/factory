### subtopic_1 — Drawing the Circuit Diagram for Each Idea
**Assistant:** Why do we need both a circuit diagram and a 3D sketch? Does one not cover it?

**Tutor:** They answer two different questions, and the device fails if either is unanswered. The circuit diagram answers: what is connected to what, and will it work? It is the only drawing from which the electronics can be checked by tracing the loop, the only one that tells the builder which resistor and which way round the LED goes, and the one examiners read to see if the team understands the circuit. The 3D sketch answers: what will the user hold, see and press, and where does each part physically go? It is the only drawing that shows whether the battery fits, whether the LED is on the face the grandmother looks at, whether the test button is reachable and whether the sensor housing is sealed. A beautiful circuit that does not fit its box, or a lovely box with a circuit that lights the LED when the tank is full, are both failures, and each drawing catches one of them.

### subtopic_2 — The 3D Sketch: What the Device Looks Like and Where the Circuit Lives
**Assistant:** Idea B has a transistor, which we have not covered yet. Should we leave it out of the comparison?

**Tutor:** Keep it in, but be honest in the table about what is known and unknown. The transistor is taught in a few weeks, so a team member who has read ahead or seen it in a kit can sketch the idea now and mark the circuit as to be confirmed. The comparison can still say a great deal about Idea B without the transistor details: its flashing LED is the best output, its probes will corrode, and its standby current needs measuring. If the final choice depends on a component the team cannot yet check, note that as a risk and plan to test it when it is taught. In this case the table showed that the reed switch could drive the LED directly with no transistor, so the final solution avoided the risk altogether, which is a good outcome; but if the chosen sensor had been a light-dependent resistor or thermistor, a transistor would be unavoidable and the team would simply build and test that stage in week 6 when the lesson arrives.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Ideas Against the Brief and Choosing as a Team
**Assistant:** What if our combined design is nobody's original idea? Whose work is it?

**Tutor:** It is the team's, and that is exactly what the PAT and real design work expect. The three individual sketches were the raw material; the comparison table was the method; the combined design is the result of applying the brief to the material, and every member's contribution is visible in the table and the written reasons. Record it that way: Idea A by one member contributed the simple series circuit, Idea B by another the flashing LED and silenceable buzzer, Idea C by a third the sealed reed switch, and the fourth member compiled the table and wrote the justification. That record goes into the individual contribution pages each learner keeps for the presentation in week 10. A team whose final device is one person's untouched idea has usually skipped the comparison, and that shows in the marks; a team whose device borrows from everyone has done the design job.
