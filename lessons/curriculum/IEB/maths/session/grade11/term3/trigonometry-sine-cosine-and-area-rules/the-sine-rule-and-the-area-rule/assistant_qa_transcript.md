**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the labelling convention — small letters opposite capitals — keeps being called crucial. What actually goes wrong when it slips?

**Tutor:** Everything downstream of the slip, quietly. The formulae in this topic are not sentences about letters — they are sentences about GEOMETRY, written in a code where each small letter must mean the side facing its capital. Take the area rule, half a b sine C: its true content is two sides times the sine of the angle wedged between them. If your small a has accidentally been written on the side next to angle A instead of opposite it, the code and the geometry disagree, and the formula computes the area of some other triangle — while every individual line of your working looks impeccable. That is what makes labelling errors so expensive: they are invisible in the algebra and only visible on the sketch. The same trap bites the sine rule, whose whole engine is the pairing of a side with its FACING angle; pair a side with an adjacent angle and the exchange rate is simply wrong. So build the physical habit. Draw the triangle yourself, even when one is printed. Write the three capitals at the vertices first. Then, for each capital, run your pen across the triangle to the far side and write the small letter there — a deliberate crossing, three times. Ten seconds of ritual, and every formula for the rest of the question becomes safe to use on autopilot.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why does the area rule insist on the INCLUDED angle? Some learners feel any angle should do if the calculator is happy.

**Tutor:** The calculator is always happy — it will cheerfully multiply whatever you feed it, which is exactly the danger. The insistence comes from the derivation, so revisit it. We built the rule by manufacturing a height: dropping a perpendicular from a vertex and noticing that the little right-angled triangle gives height equals side times sine of the angle at ITS base. Walk through that construction and you will see the angle that appears is forced to be the one BETWEEN the two sides being multiplied — the hinge where they meet. Half a b sine C works because sides a and b meet at vertex C; use angle A there instead and you are multiplying by the squash factor of a different hinge, one that does not control the opening between a and b. The gate picture says it fastest: the area of the cell depends on the angle at the hinge holding the two arms, not on any angle elsewhere in the gate. Practically, run the two-finger test before every area line — one finger on each given side, and the angle where the fingers collide is the only legal choice. If the fingers do not meet at the given angle, the area rule is not ready: you first need the sine rule, or the angle sum, to reach a configuration where they do. That readiness check is precisely what the multi-step questions are examining.

***

**Bianca:** Third: the ambiguous case. When must we actually worry about the second suspect, and when is checking it a formality?

**Tutor:** Worry precisely when you FIND AN ANGLE FROM ITS SINE — that is the only doorway the second suspect can walk through. The mechanism: inverse sine on a calculator always answers with the acute angle, but sine is positive in the second quadrant too, so 180 minus that answer wears the identical sine. Whether the obtuse twin survives depends on the angle sum. In our example the twin was 150,3 degrees, and 150,3 plus the given 48 already tops 180 — dead on arrival, and writing that one-line execution is worth a mark. But do not let the check become a mindless stamp, because sometimes the twin LIVES: if the given angle is small and the side opposite your target is longer than the side opposite the given angle, both candidates can fit under 180, and then the data genuinely describes two different triangles — a full answer computes both and says so. Two useful shortcuts for triage. If the side opposite the hunted angle is SHORTER than the side opposite the known angle, the hunted angle must be smaller than the known one, so the obtuse twin usually dies immediately. And the moment any angle of the triangle is already 90 or more, no second obtuse angle can exist. Finally, remember which tools are immune: angles found by inverse COSINE carry no twin, because obtuse angles have negative cosines — a fact that sometimes makes the other rule the safer route on purpose.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: in the multi-step problems, learners freeze at the start — they cannot see which rule to begin with. What is the unfreezing procedure?

**Tutor:** Stop hunting for the first step and instead interrogate the LAST one — plan backwards, like the driver choosing the route before starting the engine. Ask: what does the question finally want, and what is the fixed price of the formula that delivers it? Area wanted? The area rule charges two sides plus the included angle. A specific side wanted? The sine rule charges a complete pair plus one more piece; the cosine rule charges two sides with the included angle. Now compare the price against your stocktake and the missing ingredient IS the middle of the problem: in our worked example the area rule lacked a second side, the sine rule could build that side but lacked a complete pair, and the pair was completed by the free angle from 180 minus 44 minus 58. Read that chain backwards and it is deduction; run it forwards and it is three easy calculations — free angle, then side, then area. So the unfreezing procedure is three written lines before any arithmetic: GOAL and its price; HAVE, the labelled stocktake from the sketch; BRIDGE, the missing ingredients in reverse order. Then drive the route forwards, carrying unrounded values between legs, and at every leg state which rule and why it is entitled to fire — complete pair for the sine rule, two embracing sides for the area rule. The freeze comes from searching forwards through fog; planning backwards burns the fog away.
