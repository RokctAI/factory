Hey, it's Bianca — if the cosine rule felt like a wall of letters today, here is the secret that shrinks it: you have known this rule since grade eight. It is Pythagoras wearing a jacket.

Look at it again: a squared equals b squared plus c squared — that IS Pythagoras — minus two b c cos A, the jacket. The jacket measures the corner's attitude. Corner at exactly 90? Cos of 90 is zero, the jacket vanishes, plain Pythagoras. Corner tighter than 90? The correction subtracts — the far side shrinks. Corner wider than 90? Cosine goes negative, minus times minus makes plus, and the far side stretches. Wide corner, longer side: say that sentence before every calculation and the sign trap can never catch you.

When do you use it? Only two situations, and both are stocktakes: two sides with the angle squeezed between them, or all three sides and no angle. No complete side-and-opposite-angle pair means the sine rule is asleep — cosine rule time. And when hunting angles, the cosine rule is actually the kind one: a negative answer for the cosine simply means the angle is obtuse. No second suspect, no ambiguity, no drama.

Calculator discipline is half the topic: type the whole line in one go, cosine in brackets, equals once, square root last. And in the big story questions, find the side the two triangles share — the bridge — solve for it first, and carry the unrounded value across.

Pythagoras with a jacket, stocktake before tool, bridge before crossing. That is the whole lesson. The practice questions are waiting — you've got this!
