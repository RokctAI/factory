# Part 1 — Expert

Up to now, trigonometry has been locked inside right-angled triangles. This session picks the lock. The area rule and the sine rule operate in ANY triangle — no right angle needed — and between them they can price a wedge-shaped plot of land, span a river no one can cross, and settle a large share of every trigonometry section. The whole topic balances on one labelling convention and two formulae, and you will prove both, because the proofs themselves are asked.

## Subtopic: Labelling Triangles and the Area Rule

First, the convention everything depends on. In triangle ABC, the capital letters A, B and C name the angles at the vertices, and the small letters a, b and c name the sides — with small a always facing capital A, small b facing capital B, small c facing capital C. Every formula in this topic presumes that pairing. One mislabelled side and every mark downstream of it evaporates, so treat the labelling as a deliberate opening move, never an afterthought.

Now the area rule. Grade ten said: area equals half base times perpendicular height. The catch is that questions almost never hand over the perpendicular height. The area rule builds its own. Take triangle ABC and drop a perpendicular from B onto side b. The small right-angled triangle at vertex C has hypotenuse a, so the height h equals a sine C. Substitute into the old formula: area equals half times b times a sine C — customarily written half a b sine C.

Study that formula's shape: two sides, times the sine of the angle BETWEEN them — the included angle. Half a b sine C is legal because C sits wedged between sides a and b. The identical pattern yields half b c sine A and half a c sine B. Three costumes, one rule: two sides and the included angle.

Worked example. In triangle PQR, PQ is 6 centimetres, QR is 11 centimetres, and the angle at Q is 40 degrees. The two given sides meet at Q, so Q is the included angle, and the rule fires directly: area equals half times 6 times 11 times sine 40 degrees. Half of 66 is 33, sine of 40 degrees is 0,6428, and the product is 21,21. The area is 21,21 square centimetres, to two decimals — and the unit is squared, always, because area lives in square units.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the opposite-side convention and the included angle: confirm which angle sits between the two given sides before the calculator comes out.

## Subtopic: Proving and Using the Sine Rule

The sine rule drops straight out of the area rule, in a proof short enough to carry in your head. The three costumes of the area rule all measure the same triangle, so they are equal: half b c sine A equals half a c sine B equals half a b sine C. Divide every part by half a b c. The first fraction sheds b and c, leaving sine A over a. The second sheds a and c, leaving sine B over b. The third leaves sine C over c. Hence sine A over a equals sine B over b equals sine C over c — the sine rule. It flips happily: a over sine A equals b over sine B equals c over sine C, and the flipped version is the one for hunting a SIDE, because it seats the unknown on top.

When does it apply? The sine rule links a side to the angle facing it. You need one complete opposite pair — a side and its facing angle both known — plus one further piece. Given two angles and any side, the sine rule recovers the remaining sides.

Worked example. In triangle ABC, angle A is 35 degrees, angle B is 70 degrees, and side b is 14 centimetres. Find side a. The complete pair is b with B. Write the rule with the unknown upstairs: a over sine A equals b over sine B, so a equals 14 times sine 35 degrees, divided by sine 70 degrees. Sine 35 is 0,5736 and sine 70 is 0,9397. Fourteen times 0,5736 is 8,0301, and division by 0,9397 gives 8,55. Side a is 8,55 centimetres.

One calculator discipline: hold the full value on screen and round only at the final line. Trimming sine 35 to 0,57 before dividing bends the answer, and the accuracy marks bend away with it.

Stop for this section's questions now — write the complete opposite pair down first, seat the unknown on top, and round last.

## Subtopic: Finding Angles with the Sine Rule

For an angle, flip the rule so the sines ride on top. In triangle ABC, side a is 12 centimetres, side b is 8 centimetres, and angle A is 48 degrees. Find angle B. Sine B over b equals sine A over a, so sine B equals 8 times sine 48 degrees, divided by 12. Sine 48 is 0,7431, so sine B equals 0,4954. Now invert: B equals inverse sine of 0,4954, which is 29,70 degrees — 29,7 to one decimal. The third angle is free: C equals 180 minus 48 minus 29,70, which is 102,3 degrees.

But a subtlety hides here, worth marks whenever it surfaces. The calculator's inverse sine only ever volunteers an acute angle, yet sine is also positive in the second quadrant. Sine of 150,30 degrees is ALSO 0,4954, because 150,30 is 180 minus 29,70. In principle, then, angle B has two candidates: 29,70 and 150,30. Interrogate the second against the angle sum: 150,30 plus the given 48 already blows past 180 degrees, so no triangle can contain it — rejected, with that reason written in the answer. Occasionally, though, the obtuse candidate DOES survive the test, and then two distinct triangles satisfy the data; a complete answer presents both.

The habit to install: every time inverse sine yields an angle, compute 180 minus it, test that candidate against the angle sum, and record the verdict either way.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — find the acute answer, test its 180-minus partner, and write the reason for keeping or rejecting it.

## Subtopic: Multi-Step Problems — Chaining the Two Rules

Longer questions rarely want a single application; they chain them. The strategy never varies: list what is known, identify the complete pairs, and map the route from given to goal before any button is pressed.

Worked example. In triangle ABC, angle A is 44 degrees, angle B is 58 degrees, and side c, the side AB, is 20 centimetres. Find the area of the triangle. Consult the area rule's price list: two sides and the included angle. We hold one side and no second — the area rule cannot fire yet, so the sine rule must manufacture the missing side.

Step one: the third angle. C equals 180 minus 44 minus 58, which is 78 degrees. Now c and C form a complete pair. Step two: build side a with the sine rule. a equals 20 times sine 44 degrees over sine 78 degrees. Sine 44 is 0,6947, making the numerator 13,8932, and dividing by 0,9781 gives 14,20 centimetres. Step three: the area. Sides a and c are now in hand, and the angle between them is B, which is 58 degrees. Area equals half times 14,20 times 20 times sine 58 degrees. Half of 284,07 is 142,04, sine 58 is 0,8480, and the product is 120,45. The area is 120,45 square centimetres.

Mark the two disciplines that drove that solution. The angle sum of a triangle is a working tool, not trivia — it completes pairs at no cost. And the included angle for the closing step was read off the sketch, never guessed: sides a and c meet at vertex B, so B is the angle between them. Draw the triangle, label it fully, and the route tends to announce itself.

The final questions of this part are with you now — claim the free angle first, build the missing side, and confirm on your sketch which angle is genuinely included before the area line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same two rules again — through a squashing gate, a market with one fixed rate, and a route planned before the wheels turn.

## Subtopic: The Squashed Gate

Think of a trellis security gate — the concertina kind that folds open and shut. Isolate one diamond cell of it: two rigid arms hinged at a corner. With the arms at 90 degrees, the cell holds the most space it ever will. Squash the gate and the arms keep their exact lengths while the space between them collapses, until, fully folded, there is no space left at all.

That is the area rule speaking. Half a b sine C instructs: multiply the two arms, halve, then multiply by sine of the angle between them — and sine is the squash factor. At 90 degrees sine equals 1: no squash, maximum area, and the formula quietly reduces to the old half base times height. Away from 90 in either direction, sine slides below 1 and the area shrinks in sympathy. At 0 or 180 the arms lie flat, sine reads 0, and the triangle has vanished.

So when a question supplies two sides of a plot and the angle where they meet, no perpendicular height is needed. The two sides and their squash factor ARE the area: half, times side, times side, times sine of the wedged-in angle. The only fatal move is grabbing an angle that is not between the two sides — that is measuring the squash of some other cell of the gate. Put a finger on each given side of your sketch and take the angle where your fingers meet.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are with you right now. Ask of each one: is this angle honestly wedged between the two sides I was given?

## Subtopic: Fair Trade at the Triangle Market

Every triangle honours one trade agreement: the bigger the angle, the longer the side that faces it — biggest angle, longest side; smallest angle, shortest side. The sine rule turns that instinct into an exact exchange rate: side divided by sine of its facing angle is the SAME number all the way around the triangle. Like a market with one fixed price per kilogram — different bags, different totals, one rate for every customer.

Seen as an exchange rate, the rule becomes shop arithmetic. One complete pair — a side with its facing angle — fixes the rate. In our example, side b was 14 with angle B at 70 degrees, so the rate was 14 over sine 70. Every other side then trades at that same rate: side a equals sine 35 — its own facing angle — times the rate. Answer: 8,55.

And the writing trick that prevents rearrangement errors: seat whatever you are hunting on TOP of the fraction. Hunting a side, sides go on top. Hunting an angle, flip so the sines ride on top. The final line is then a plain multiplication instead of an algebra wrestle.

One caution carries over with its own picture. When an angle is recovered from its sine, the calculator produces the acute suspect — but a second suspect waits at 180 minus that answer, wearing the same sine. March both suspects past the angle-sum judge: the three angles must total 180. Usually the second suspect's alibi collapses and the case closes; occasionally the second suspect fits too, and the question honestly owns two triangles.

Your questions for this part are up now — fix the rate with the complete pair, buy the missing piece at that rate, and always interrogate the second suspect.

## Subtopic: Planning the Route Before You Drive

A delivery driver covering two towns does not start the engine and improvise; the route is settled before the wheels move. Multi-step trigonometry is the same trade. Before computing, take stock: which sides are known, which angles, which opposite pairs are complete, and what does the destination formula demand?

The destination is usually the area rule, and its fare is fixed: two sides plus the angle wedged between them. Handed two angles and one side, you cannot pay yet. So plan the route backwards: the area rule wants a second side; the sine rule builds sides; and the sine rule may first need the third angle — which costs nothing, since the three angles always total 180.

That was the entire story of our worked example: 180 minus 44 minus 58 delivered the 78, the sine rule stretched 20 into a second side of 14,20, and the area rule ended the trip at 120,45 square centimetres. Three legs, planned in reverse, driven forwards.

Two habits keep the plan honest. Sketch and label every triangle yourself, even when the question prints a diagram — YOUR labels, small letters facing capitals, are what make the formulae safe. And at each step, state which rule is running and why it is entitled to run: a complete pair for the sine rule, two sides embracing an angle for the area rule. The written reason earns its own credit, and writing it forces the check.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now — take stock, claim the free angle, plan backwards, then drive the route one rule at a time.
