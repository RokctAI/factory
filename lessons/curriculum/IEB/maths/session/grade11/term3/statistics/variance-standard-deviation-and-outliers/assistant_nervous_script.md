Hey, it's Bianca — if variance, skewness and fences are swirling together, let me shrink this lesson to its true size: one road of houses, one recipe, one trial.

The road first. Sort the data, plant the five landmarks — start, quarter mark, halfway, three-quarter mark, end — and the box-and-whisker draws itself: the box is the neighbourhood where the middle half lives, the whiskers are the roads out to the ends. Neighbourhood width is the interquartile range, and it never panics when something weird happens at the edges.

The recipe next, and it never changes its five steps: mean, deviations, squares, average, root. The deviations always sum to zero — that is why we square them — and the square root at the end just brings the answer home to real units. Variance is the step-four number; standard deviation is step five, the typical distance from home. Say the steps out loud while you work and the method carries you.

Then the trial. One and a half neighbourhood-widths past each end of the box stand the fences: 31,5 and 67,5 for our marks. Any value beyond a fence is an outlier, and your verdict is one inequality: 86 is greater than 67,5, so 86 is an outlier. No feelings, just fences.

And the wisdom that ties it together: outliers bully the mean and the standard deviation, but the median and interquartile range stand firm. Skew? The mean chases the tail — compare it with the median and quote both numbers.

Road, recipe, trial. That is the entire lesson. Go take it to the practice questions — you've got this!
