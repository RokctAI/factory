**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: histograms and bar charts keep getting confused — when exactly do the bars touch, and when do they stand apart?

**Tutor:** The bars answer to the data type, not to taste. A histogram belongs to CONTINUOUS measurements — time, height, mass, marks treated as a scale — where the classes are intervals that share boundaries: our 15-to-30 class ends at the precise instant 30-to-45 begins, so their bars share a wall. A gap would falsely claim that some study times between the classes are impossible. A bar chart belongs to CATEGORIES — favourite subject, taxi route, eye colour — where the labels have no scale between them, and the gaps honestly say these are separate boxes, not neighbouring intervals. Two habits follow. When you draw a histogram, mark the horizontal axis as a genuine number line with the class boundaries — 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 75 — not with the class labels floating under the bars; the boundaries are what let the ogive and polygon live on the same axis later. And when you read someone else's diagram, check the axis before trusting the picture: touching bars over a number line invite midpoints, cumulative totals and estimated means, while gapped bars over categories invite none of that machinery. One glance at the axis tells you which toolkit the picture will accept.

***

**Bianca:** Next: for the estimated mean, why the midpoint? Someone in class asked why not the lower boundary, or the most common value inside the class.

**Tutor:** Because the midpoint is the only choice that is fair without extra information. Grouping has destroyed the individual values: all we know about the seventeen learners in 30-to-45 is that each sits somewhere in that interval. If we assume they all sit at the lower boundary, 30, we systematically understate everyone and drag the mean down; the upper boundary overstates and drags it up. The midpoint, 37,5, is the balance point of the interval — learners below it and above it cancel on average — so the errors from individuals have the best chance of washing out across the class. That is also why the answer must be reported as an ESTIMATE: if the seventeen actually cluster near 44, the true mean is higher than our 40,8, and we cannot know. As for using the most common value inside the class — we would love to, but the grouped table does not reveal it; that information died when the data was grouped. There is a lovely check hiding here too: the estimated mean must land inside the range of midpoints, between 7,5 and 67,5, and usually near the crowded classes. Our 40,8 sits just above the modal class's midpoint of 37,5, pulled up by the substantial 45-to-60 and 60-to-75 classes — exactly the sanity story you want to be able to tell.

**Bianca:** Third: the ogive plotting rule — upper boundaries — keeps costing marks. Is there a way to make it stick?

**Tutor:** Give every cumulative number its full sentence, and the plotting rule enforces itself. The number 13 in our column is not just thirteen — its sentence is thirteen learners study for LESS THAN thirty minutes. Ask where that sentence becomes true on the number line: only at 30, the end of the second class. At 22,5 it might already be false — perhaps only nine or ten learners sit below 22,5 — and at 15 it certainly is. So the point earns its place at the upper boundary: 30 across, 13 up. Run every point through its sentence and the temptation to plot at midpoints — a habit dragged over from the frequency polygon — dies immediately, because the polygon's points carry a different sentence entirely: nine learners study for BETWEEN fifteen and thirty minutes, a fact that belongs to the whole class and is drawn at its centre. Different sentence, different address. The starting point has a sentence too: zero learners study for less than zero minutes, giving the point 0 with 0 — and forgetting it leaves your curve starting mid-air at 15 with 4, which loses the anchoring mark. Say the sentence, place the point. And the final sentence — fifty learners study for less than seventy-five minutes — lands the curve at exactly n, your built-in proof that no learner leaked out of the column on the way up.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: in the across-and-down readings, how accurate do our answers need to be? The class worries their ruler answer differs from the memo.

**Tutor:** The memo expects a corridor, not a bullseye — graph readings are judged within a tolerance, precisely because the ogive only ever promised estimates. What is actually being marked is threefold. First, the position: the median of fifty values is read at 25 on the cumulative axis — not at 25 minutes, not at position 26 — and the quartiles at 12,5 and 37,5; choosing the right position is where most of the credit sits. Second, the visible method: the dotted line across from 25, the elbow at the curve, the drop to the horizontal axis. Those construction lines ARE marks; an answer floating alone, however close, cannot prove it came from the graph. Third, the honest sentence: approximately 41 minutes, units included. Whether your careful ruler says 40, 41 or 42 rarely matters — all live inside the corridor — but 35 or 47 signals a position error or a sloppy curve, and that is what actually gets penalised. So the discipline is: plot the ogive points exactly, since a point half a block adrift bends every later reading; draw the smoothest curve through them; then read with dotted lines you leave on the page. And keep the reversal straight for threshold questions: how many above 55 goes UP from the value, reads the cumulative count, and subtracts from n — about 38 below, so about 12 above. Position, journey, sentence: deliver those three and the corridor takes care of the rest.
