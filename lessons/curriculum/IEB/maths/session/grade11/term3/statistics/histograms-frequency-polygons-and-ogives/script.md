# Part 1 — Expert

Real data seldom arrives one value at a time — it arrives in bulk, pre-sorted into class intervals: fifty study times, three hundred test marks, a suburb's water usage. This session sharpens the grade ten toolkit for grouped data — intervals, histograms, the estimated mean — and then constructs the two pictures grade eleven adds: the frequency polygon, and the ogive, the cumulative frequency curve from which medians, quartiles and percentiles can be read with a ruler. One dataset carries the whole session: the daily study times of fifty grade elevens.

## Subtopic: Grouped Data Revision — Intervals, Histograms and the Estimated Mean

Meet the dataset. Fifty learners recorded their daily study time in minutes, grouped into five classes: from 0 up to but not including 15 minutes, 4 learners; from 15 up to 30, 9 learners; from 30 up to 45, 17 learners; from 45 up to 60, 12 learners; and from 60 up to 75, 8 learners. Audit the total before anything else: 4 plus 9 plus 17 plus 12 plus 8 is 50. If the frequencies do not add to the number of learners, a digit is wrong somewhere, and every later answer inherits the mistake.

The histogram draws the classes as touching bars — touching, because the classes share boundaries with no daylight between them; gaps belong to bar charts of categories, not to continuous measurements like time. Bar height carries frequency, so the tallest bar, 30 to 45, is the modal class: study times crowd there more than anywhere else.

Grouping erases the individual values, so the mean can only be estimated. The working assumption: every learner in a class sits at its midpoint. The midpoints are 7,5; then 22,5; then 37,5; then 52,5; then 67,5. Multiply each by its frequency: 4 times 7,5 is 30; 9 times 22,5 is 202,5; 17 times 37,5 is 637,5; 12 times 52,5 is 630; 8 times 67,5 is 540. The products total 2 040, and 2 040 divided by 50 gives an estimated mean of 40,8 minutes. And locate the median's home: with 50 values, the middle lies between the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth. Running the count — 4, then 13, then 30 — both the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth values land inside 30 to 45: the median class.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test boundaries, midpoints and the estimated mean: totals first, midpoints times frequencies, divide by n.

## Subtopic: Frequency Polygons

One histogram is a clear picture; two histograms on the same axes are a collision of bars. The frequency polygon exists for exactly that problem. The recipe: place a point at the MIDPOINT of each class at the height of its frequency — midpoint along the horizontal axis, frequency up the vertical — then join consecutive points with straight lines.

For our data, the points are 7,5 at height 4; then 22,5 at height 9; then 37,5 at height 17; then 52,5 at height 12; then 67,5 at height 8. To close the shape honestly, anchor both ends to the horizontal axis: plot a zero at the midpoint of the empty class before the first — minus 7,5 — and a zero at the midpoint of the empty class after the last — 82,5. The figure now starts and finishes on the axis, a true polygon whose enclosed area agrees with the histogram's.

Why draw a second picture of the same numbers? Comparison. Two polygons share one grid gracefully — this term's study times against last term's, one class against another — where two histograms turn to visual mud. The polygon also declares shape instantly: ours climbs to a single peak at 37,5 and descends more gradually on the right, a hump in the thirties with a tail of marathon studiers.

The drawing errors to dodge: plotting at class boundaries instead of midpoints — the first point belongs at 7,5, not at 15; joining the dots with curves — a polygon has straight edges; and skipping the two anchoring zeros, which leaves the shape floating in mid-air.

Stop for this section's questions now — midpoints, straight lines, and anchor both ends to the axis.

## Subtopic: Ogives — Building the Cumulative Frequency Curve

The ogive asks a different question. Where the histogram counts how many in EACH class, the ogive counts how many SO FAR — a running total. Build the cumulative frequency column first: 4 learners study under 15 minutes; 4 plus 9 is 13 under 30; 13 plus 17 is 30 under 45; 30 plus 12 is 42 under 60; 42 plus 8 is 50 under 75. The last cumulative value must equal n — a built-in audit.

Then the plotting rule that carries the marks: each cumulative frequency is plotted at the UPPER boundary of its class, because 13 is the count of learners below 30 minutes — not below 22,5 and not below 15. The points are therefore 15 with 4; 30 with 13; 45 with 30; 60 with 42; 75 with 50. One extra point launches the curve: the lower boundary of the first class paired with zero — the point 0 with 0 — since nobody studies for less than no time. Join everything with a smooth curve and the classic lazy-S appears: rising gently through the sparse early classes, steeply through the crowded middle, then levelling off as the data runs dry.

Read that S like a geologist. The ogive is steepest exactly where the histogram is tallest — dense data makes the running total sprint. Flat stretches are empty classes. And an ogive can never slope downhill, not for a single step, because a running total cannot shrink; if your curve dips, a subtraction has crept into a column that only ever adds.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — cumulative column first, plot at upper boundaries, start from zero, and end at n.

## Subtopic: Reading the Ogive — Median, Quartiles and Percentiles

Now the payoff. The ogive converts every position question into two ruler moves: go ACROSS from the position on the vertical axis until you meet the curve, then turn DOWN to read the value.

The median lives at position n over 2 — here, 25. Across from 25 to the curve, then down: the reading falls inside the 30-to-45 class, at approximately 41 minutes. Verify against the cumulative column: 13 learners sit below 30 minutes and 30 sit below 45, so the twenty-fifth learner does indeed live in that class, well past its lower boundary. The lower quartile sits at position n over 4, which is 12,5: across and down, approximately 29 minutes. The upper quartile sits at three n over 4, which is 37,5: across and down, approximately 54 minutes. The interquartile range is then about 54 minus 29, which is 25 minutes: the middle half of the learners spans roughly twenty-five minutes of study time.

Percentiles are the same move generalised: the ninetieth percentile sits at position 90 percent of 50, which is 45 — across from 45, down to approximately 66 minutes. And the ogive answers threshold questions run backwards: how many learners study MORE than 55 minutes? Go UP from 55 on the horizontal axis to the curve — the reading is about 38 learners below 55 — and subtract from 50: about 12 learners study longer than 55 minutes.

Language seals the marks: every ogive reading is an estimate, because the true values are locked inside their classes. Write approximately 41 minutes, and leave your construction lines visible on the graph — the across-and-down journey earns credit, not just the destination.

The final questions of this part are with you now — position first, across, down, and say the word estimate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three pictures again — as a city skyline, a tuckshop's running total, and a ruler that walks across and down.

## Subtopic: The Skyline and the Mountain Profile

Stand back and look at a city skyline: buildings of different heights, shoulder to shoulder, no alleys between them. That is a histogram. Each building is a class interval, and its height counts the residents — how many of our fifty learners fall in that band of study times. The buildings must share walls because time has no gaps: 29 minutes and 31 minutes live in neighbouring buildings with a common wall at 30, not across a road. The tallest building is the modal class — the address where study times pile up.

But a skyline hides its residents. Once a learner enters the 30-to-45 building, their exact floor is lost — 31 or 44, the building swallows it. So estimating the mean makes the fairest guess available: stand every resident in the middle of their building, at the midpoint. Four at 7,5; nine at 22,5; seventeen at 37,5; twelve at 52,5; eight at 67,5 — add the guesses, divide by fifty, and 40,8 minutes is the best mean grouped data can offer. The word estimate is doing honest work: it owns up to the guess.

Now place a dot at the centre of each rooftop and join the dots with straight lines, running the line down to the ground before the first building and after the last. The skyline melts into a mountain profile — the frequency polygon. Identical information, but two cities can now be compared by resting their profiles on one grid, which two skylines will never politely allow.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are with you right now. One dataset: buildings. A comparison: mountain profiles.

## Subtopic: The Running Total Tells a Different Story

A tuckshop owner keeps two columns in her notebook: what came in TODAY, and the total SO FAR this term. Both describe the same money, but they answer different questions. Tuesday's column answers how good Tuesday was; the running total answers when the term crossed a thousand rand. The histogram is the today column. The ogive is the so-far column, drawn as a curve.

So build the ogive like the owner balances her book. After each class, write the total so far: 4, then 13, then 30, then 42, then 50. And borrow her logic about where each number belongs: the total 13 is only complete at the END of the second class — at 30 minutes, not earlier. That is why ogive points sit at upper class boundaries, and why the curve launches from zero at zero minutes: before the first class, nothing has accumulated yet.

The finished curve reads like the term's bank statement. Steep stretch? Busy trading — the crowded classes. Flat stretch? Quiet days — empty classes. And the curve can never come downhill, because a running total never shrinks; money already banked stays banked. If your ogive dips anywhere, a subtraction sneaked into a column that only ever adds.

Your questions for this part are up now — today's numbers make bars, so-far numbers make the climbing curve, and the curve only ever climbs.

## Subtopic: Across and Down — Reading Answers off the Curve

Here is the move that makes the ogive worth its ink, and it never changes: across, then down. Imagine all fifty learners standing in a line along the curve, ordered by study time, from the two-minute page-flipper to the marathon reviser.

Who is the exact middle of the line? Position 25 of 50. Ruler at 25 on the vertical axis, slide ACROSS to the curve, turn DOWN to the horizontal axis: approximately 41 minutes. That is the median — half the grade studies less, half studies more. The quarter mark, position 12,5, reads about 29 minutes: the lower quartile. The three-quarter mark, position 37,5, reads about 54 minutes: the upper quartile. The distance between those two readings, about 25 minutes, is the interquartile range — the span of the middle half of the line, the ordinary crowd with both extremes trimmed away.

The same ruler serves every percentage question. Ninety percent of the line is position 45: across and down, approximately 66 minutes — the ninetieth percentile. And the reverse question — how many study more than 55 minutes — retraces the path backwards: UP from 55 to the curve, across to read about 38, then subtract from 50. About twelve learners.

Two honesties close the topic. Every reading is an estimate, so write the word — approximately 41 minutes — because the exact values are sealed inside their classes. And leave the dotted ruler lines on your graph: the marker pays for the visible journey, not only the destination.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now — find the position in the line, go across, go down, and say approximately like you mean it.
