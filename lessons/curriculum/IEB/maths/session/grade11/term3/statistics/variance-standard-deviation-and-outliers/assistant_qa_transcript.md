**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: when finding quartiles by hand, does the median itself get included in the two halves? Different textbooks seem to do different things.

**Tutor:** The convention we work with is clean: when n is even, split the sorted list into two equal halves and take the median of each half — nothing is shared, nothing left over. Our ten marks split into a bottom five and a top five; the lower quartile is the middle of the bottom five, 45, and the upper quartile the middle of the top five, 54. When n is odd, the overall median is an actual data value, and the convention EXCLUDES it from both halves: with eleven values, the sixth is the median, values one to five give the lower quartile and values seven to eleven the upper. You are right that other conventions exist in the wider statistical world — software packages famously disagree with each other about quartiles — but at school level the medians-of-halves method is the expected working, and consistency is what the marks reward. Two practical guards. First, ALWAYS sort before slicing; the single most common quartile error is quartiling unsorted data. Second, count your halves with your pen: five below, five above, and if a question hands you an ogive instead of a list, the quartiles come from positions n over 4 and three n over 4 with the across-and-down reading — the ogive and the hand method estimate the same landmarks by different routes.

**Bianca:** Next: in the variance recipe, why square the deviations? A learner asked why we don't just drop the minus signs and average the positive distances — it seems more natural.

**Tutor:** It IS more natural, and the measure your learner invented exists — averaging the absolute distances gives the mean absolute deviation, a perfectly sensible statistic. For our striker it would give 5 plus 3 plus 0 plus 2 plus 6 over 5, which is 3,2 — in the same neighbourhood as our standard deviation of 3,85. So why does mathematics prefer the square? Three working reasons at your level. First, algebra: absolute values fight back when you differentiate or expand them, while squares flow through algebra smoothly — which is why every formula built on spread, from regression lines onwards, grows out of squared deviations. Second, the square deliberately AMPLIFIES big strays: the 6-goal wander contributes 36, nearly half the whole sum of 74, while dropping signs would let it contribute just 6 of 16. A measure that shouts about big deviations is exactly what you want when big deviations are what matters — consistency, risk, quality control. Third, convention with consequences: the standard deviation is the measure the entire syllabus, every calculator's statistics mode, and the scientific world agree on, so it is the one your answers must produce. The recipe order is non-negotiable — mean, deviations, squares, average, root — and the halfway quantity, the variance, keeps its own name because the algebra often wants to stop there.

**Bianca:** Third: for the fence rule — where does the one and a half come from? Is there something magical about 1,5 times the interquartile range?

**Tutor:** No deep magic — it is a well-chosen convention, and understanding its design is worth more than memorising it. The rule wants a boundary that adapts to the data's own spread. Using a fixed distance — say, twenty marks from the median — would flag nothing in widely spread data and everything in tight data. So the rule measures in units of the data's OWN middle spread: the interquartile range. The box already covers the middle half; stepping one and a half box-widths further out is generous enough that ordinary values almost never cross, yet tight enough that genuinely strange values do. Make the multiplier much smaller, say 0,5, and you would convict respectable marks; much larger, say 3, and even our 86 would need to be more extreme to be caught — in fact some statisticians do use 3 times the IQR to flag only far outliers. The 1,5 convention is the standard school and industry default, and it is the one to apply unless a question explicitly gives another rule. Notice also WHY the fences hang off the quartiles and not the mean: the mean is dragged by the very suspect we are testing, so fences built on it would shift towards the outlier and might acquit it. Quartiles hold still. A rule that cannot be manipulated by the accused — that is the design principle, and writing the final verdict as an inequality, 86 greater than 67,5, is what turns the rule into marks.

**Bianca:** Last one: interpretation questions that ask which average best represents the data — what is the full-marks structure for answering those?

**Tutor:** Three sentences, each doing a distinct job. Sentence one: identify the feature of the data that forces a choice — here, the value 86 is an outlier, proven by the fence rule, or the data is positively skewed, proven by the mean of 52 sitting above the median of 50. No feature, no argument; this sentence is your evidence. Sentence two: state the effect of that feature on the fragile tools — the outlier drags the mean upward from about 48 to 52, so the mean overstates a typical learner's mark, and it more than doubles the standard deviation from about 5,5 to about 12,5, so the spread looks wilder than the class really is. This sentence shows you know WHY the fragile tools fail, not just that they do. Sentence three: name the recommendation with its virtue — the median of 50 and the interquartile range of 9 best represent this class, because they are robust: the median counts positions, not totals, and the quartiles cannot be reached by the extremes. Evidence, effect, recommendation. And a final subtlety that distinguishes top answers: robust does not mean better in all weather. In clean, symmetric data with no outliers, the mean and standard deviation are the superior pair precisely because they use every value's exact position. The skill being tested is not loyalty to one set of tools — it is matching the tool to the state of the data, and saying so out loud.
