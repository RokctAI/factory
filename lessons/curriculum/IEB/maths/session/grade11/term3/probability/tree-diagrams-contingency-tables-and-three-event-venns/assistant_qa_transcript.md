**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the perennial: in three-circle questions, how can I tell whether a given number already includes the centre?

**Tutor:** Only the little words can tell you — the numbers never announce their own meaning. Two families of phrasing exist. The first family fences off one exact region: choir and drama ONLY, or choir and drama BUT NOT chess. Those phrases name a single room of the diagram, so the number walks straight in, untouched. The second family speaks loosely: 12 do choir and drama, full stop. That sentence covers everyone who belongs to both circles — and the four all-three members in the centre certainly do choir and drama, so they are hiding inside that 12. The exactly-two region then receives 12 minus 4, which is 8. My practical drill: before placing any number, ask it one question — could an all-three member be counted inside you? If the phrasing cannot exclude them, subtract the centre. And after every placement round, run the audit: the eight regions must sum to the population, 120 in our survey. An audit that lands on 124 is almost always a centre that was counted twice — the diagram catches the misreading before the marker ever sees it.

**Bianca:** Next: on tree diagrams, why do the second-draw fractions change but the FIRST draw's fractions never do? Some of us keep wanting to adjust stage one as well.

**Tutor:** Because the first draw faces the untouched packet, and nothing that happens later can reach back in time to change that. When you stand at the root of the tree, all six sweets are present: orange is 4 over 6, mint is 2 over 6, and those numbers are facts about the full packet, permanently. The second draw is different because it happens AFTER history: by then one sweet has left, and which sweet left decides the world you are in. That is why the tree splits — the branch after an orange lives in a three-orange, two-mint world with fractions over 5; the branch after a mint lives in a four-orange, one-mint world, also over 5. So the rule is directional: probabilities never flow backwards, only forwards along the arrows. If it helps, write the little inventory next to each node — six sweets at the root, five at every second-stage node, with the colours listed — before writing any fraction. The inventory makes the changed world visible, and the fractions then write themselves. Adjusting stage one is trying to give the first draw knowledge it cannot have; keeping stage two unchanged is denying the packet its memory. Both errors die the moment the inventories are on the page.

**Bianca:** Third: for the independence test on tables, do we have to use the product test, or is comparing the row percentages enough for full credit?

**Tutor:** Treat them as intuition and proof, and give the marker the proof. Comparing rows — 40 percent of boys cycle, 40 percent of girls cycle — is the fastest way to SEE independence, and I want you doing it first in every table question because it also protects you from arithmetic slips: rates that agree strongly suggest the product test will pass. But the definition of independence is the product law: probability of A times probability of B must equal the probability of A and B together. So the safe structure of a full answer has three lines. Line one: the two marginal probabilities, 0,375 and 0,4, each shown as a fraction from the margins. Line two: their product, 0,15, set against the actual cell probability, 24 over 160, which is 0,15. Line three: the verdict sentence — since these are equal, gender and transport are independent. If the numbers had disagreed, the same three lines end in the opposite sentence, and quoting BOTH numbers in it is not decoration: the comparison is the answer. Row percentages alone, without the product line, leave the definition untested; the product line alone, without the sentence, leaves the conclusion unstated. Do all three and there is nothing left to deduct.

**Bianca:** Last one: when a story question gives no diagram at all, what should the very first pen stroke be?

**Tutor:** The first stroke is never a number — it is the tool, chosen from the story's grammar. Read the question once for its shape, not its values. Everyone counted at a single moment, with overlap words like or, only, neither, at least one? Draw the circles — two or three as the cast demands — before re-reading any number. Events one after another — first, then, without replacement? Draw the trunk and branches of a tree, leaving the fractions blank. Every person described by two facts at once — gender and transport, grade and sport? Rule the grid with its margin row and column. This matters because the tool is what turns the words into arithmetic: once the container exists, each sentence of the question deposits its number into an obvious place, and the question practically solves itself. Students who lose marks here almost never chose the wrong tool — they chose no tool, and tried to hold eight regions or four conditional branches in their heads. And one warning for the mixed items: a story can wear a tree's clothing without sweets — weather then a match result, a test then a retest. If stage two's chances depend on stage one's outcome, it is a tree, whatever the props. Multiply along, add across, and the machinery is identical.
