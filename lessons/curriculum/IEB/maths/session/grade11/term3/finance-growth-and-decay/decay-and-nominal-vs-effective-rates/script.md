# Part 1 — Expert

In grade ten, finance meant money climbing: simple and compound interest lifting a deposit year by year. Grade eleven adds the downhill half of the story — simple and compound decay, the mathematics behind every ageing minibus and worn-out laptop — and then interrogates the uphill half more closely: when a bank pays interest quarterly or monthly instead of once a year, what is the advertised rate actually worth? By the end of this lesson you will price depreciation two different ways, convert any advertised nominal rate into its honest effective equivalent, and chain rates and currencies along a timeline without dropping a period.

## Subtopic: From Growth to Decay — the Two Depreciation Formulae

Start from what you own. Simple growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i n. Compound growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i, raised to the power n. Decay keeps both skeletons and swaps a single sign. Simple decay, also called straight-line depreciation: A equals P times the quantity one minus i n. Compound decay, also called reducing-balance depreciation: A equals P times the quantity one minus i, all to the power n. The letters keep their jobs: P is the value at the start, A the value at the end, i the rate per period written as a decimal, n the number of periods.

Although both models can quote the same rate, they describe different machines. Straight line removes a fixed slice of the ORIGINAL value every single year — twelve percent of the purchase price, again and again. Reducing balance removes twelve percent of whatever the item is worth RIGHT NOW, so the rand amount lost gets smaller every year as the value falls.

Run the numbers on a school minibus bought for 360 000 rand, depreciating at 12 percent per annum for 5 years. Straight line: i n is 0,12 times 5, which is 0,6, so A equals 360 000 times 0,4, which is 144 000 rand. Reducing balance: A equals 360 000 times 0,88 to the power 5. Now 0,88 to the fifth is 0,52773 to five decimals, so A equals 189 983,49 rand. Reducing balance leaves about forty-six thousand rand MORE on the books, because each year's twelve percent bites a smaller number than the year before.

And note the long-run split, a favourite reasoning question: the straight line actually reaches zero — at 12 percent, after one divided by 0,12, which is eight and a third years, the minibus is worth exactly nothing on paper. Reducing balance never gets there: multiplying by 0,88 forever makes the value tiny, but can never make it zero.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. One decision drives everything: a fixed slice of the original, or a shrinking slice of the current value.

## Subtopic: Different Compounding Periods

Interest seldom arrives in one annual lump. Banks credit it half-yearly, quarterly, monthly — and the compound formula absorbs all of it through one discipline: make i and n speak the language of the COMPOUNDING PERIOD. If an annual rate is compounded m times a year, the rate per period is the annual rate divided by m, and the number of periods is the number of years multiplied by m.

Worked example. Invest 15 000 rand at 8 percent per annum compounded quarterly, for 3 years. The quarterly rate is 0,08 divided by 4, which is 0,02. Time is measured in quarters: n is 3 times 4, which is 12. So A equals 15 000 times 1,02 to the power 12. That power is 1,26824, so A equals 19 023,63 rand.

Now hold it against the same money at 8 percent compounded annually: A equals 15 000 times 1,08 cubed, which is 15 000 times 1,259712, which is 18 895,68 rand. Quarterly compounding delivers about 128 rand more over the three years. The mechanism: each quarter's interest joins the principal immediately and starts earning its own interest, instead of standing in the queue until December. At the same quoted rate, more frequent compounding always pays more — monthly beats quarterly beats yearly — though each additional step of frequency adds a little less than the one before.

The habit that protects your marks: never let two clocks into one formula. A quarterly rate with n counted in years, or an annual rate with n counted in quarters, is the classic finance slip. Before you touch the formula, write one conversion line — rate per period, number of periods — and for the longer stories, sketch the timeline with every deposit and rate change marked on it.

Stop for this section's questions now — divide the rate, multiply the periods, and keep everything on one clock.

## Subtopic: Nominal versus Effective Rates

That advertised 8 percent has a technical name: the NOMINAL rate. It is a headline, and a headline means nothing until you attach the compounding frequency underneath it. The EFFECTIVE rate answers the question the headline dodges: what single once-a-year rate would grow your money by exactly the same amount?

Extract it from the example. One rand at 8 percent compounded quarterly becomes 1,02 to the power 4 after a full year, which is 1,08243. The money actually grew by 8,243 percent — not 8. So 8 percent nominal, compounded quarterly, is EFFECTIVELY about 8,24 percent per annum. The general law: one plus i effective equals one plus the nominal rate over m, all raised to the power m.

Effective rates exist so that offers can be compared honestly. Bank X advertises 8,3 percent compounded annually; Bank Y advertises 8,1 percent compounded monthly. On headlines, X looks stronger. Convert Y: one plus 0,081 over 12 is 1,00675, and 1,00675 to the power 12 is 1,08408 — an effective rate of about 8,41 percent. Bank Y actually pays more: 8,41 beats 8,3. The smaller headline wins because twelve compounding paydays a year more than close the gap. Marketing counts on customers comparing headlines; the effective rate is your mathematical defence.

Two safeguards for full credit. Whenever compounding happens more than once a year, effective must come out LARGER than nominal — if yours is smaller, a power and a root have swapped places somewhere. And expect the relationship to be run in both directions: from nominal and frequency to effective, or from a target effective rate backwards to the nominal rate that achieves it, which is the same equation solved with roots instead of powers.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — grow one rand for one year, read off the true percentage, and let effective rates referee every bank contest.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates and Mixed Timeline Problems

Two final skills complete the toolkit. First, exchange rates. An exchange rate is a price: 17,60 rand per dollar means each dollar costs 17,60 rand. A camera listed at 650 dollars therefore costs 650 times 17,60, which is 11 440 rand. Let the rand WEAKEN to 18,40 per dollar — more rand needed for each dollar — and the identical camera now costs 11 960 rand: five hundred and twenty rand more, while the dollar sticker never moved. That is how a weakening rand imports inflation: fuel, electronics and machinery arrive priced in foreign currency, so every weakening feeds through to the shelf price. The compensation runs the other way: a weaker rand makes South African goods and holidays cheaper for foreign buyers, which helps exporters and tourism. A stronger rand reverses both sentences. Be ready to do the arithmetic and to state the consequence in words.

Second, the mixed timeline, where the earlier skills join hands. Deposit 9 000 rand at 7 percent per annum compounded quarterly, for 4 years. Conversion line first: the quarterly rate is 0,0175 and the clock ticks 16 quarters. A equals 9 000 times 1,0175 to the power 16, and that power is 1,31993, so A equals 11 879,36 rand. Longer questions splice segments together: when the rate changes partway, the timeline breaks into pieces, each piece carries its own i and its own n, and the closing value of one piece becomes the opening P of the next. Draw the line, mark every event, convert every rate to its own period, and multiply the growth factors through in order.

The final questions of this part are with you now — price the currency move in rand, then walk the timeline one clean segment at a time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same formulae, second pass — this time with a ruler, a photocopier and a till slip doing the heavy lifting.

## Subtopic: The Photocopy and the Ruler

Two ways the minibus loses value, and you have watched both happen. The first is the ruler. Lay a ruler on the graph and draw one straight stroke from 360 000 downwards: the same fixed amount vanishes every year. Twelve percent of the ORIGINAL price is 43 200 rand, so five years erase five equal steps — 216 000 rand gone, 144 000 rand left. And a straight stroke drawn downhill must eventually cross the floor: at this rate, eight and a third years and the value is exactly zero, at least in the books.

The second is the photocopy of a photocopy. Set the copier to 88 percent and copy the picture; then copy the COPY at 88 percent; then copy that. Every generation keeps 88 percent of the one before it — never of the original. That is reducing balance: 360 000 becomes 316 800, becomes 278 784, becomes 245 329,92, becomes 215 890,33, becomes 189 983,49 after five copies. The first copy costs 43 200 rand of value; the fifth costs only about 25 900, because twelve percent of a smaller number is a smaller bite. And no matter how many times you copy, a photocopy never comes out blank — fainter every generation, but never nothing. Reducing balance cannot reach zero.

One advertised rate, two entirely different machines, and the question always names its machine: straight-line or simple decay is the ruler; reducing balance or compound decay is the photocopier. Circle the keyword before you write a single formula.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are with you right now. Equal steps off the original, or fading copies of the current?

## Subtopic: Interest Paydays

Two savings accounts both advertise 8 percent a year. Account one pays everything in a single December payday. Account two pays four times a year — a small payday at the end of every quarter, at two percent each. Same headline on both posters; which account is richer at the end?

Account two — because of what happens BETWEEN paydays. The March payday drops straight into the balance, and from April that little extra is itself earning. Every early payday is put to work at once instead of idling until December. Interest earning interest is the engine of compounding, and more paydays start the engine earlier. On 15 000 rand over three years, the quarterly account finishes on 19 023,63 rand against the annual account's 18 895,68 — about 128 rand earned by timing alone.

The bookkeeping is a clock change. If paydays come quarterly, the whole calculation must think in quarters: the rate per payday is the yearly rate divided by four, and time is counted in paydays — twelve of them in three years. Divide the rate, count the paydays, and never let a yearly clock and a quarterly clock share one formula. Monthly paydays: divide by twelve, count months. Half-yearly: divide by two, count half-years.

One honest footnote: each extra slicing of the year helps less than the last. Quarterly beats yearly clearly; daily beats monthly by barely a cent. Early paydays are powerful, but the power flattens out.

Your questions for this part are up now — slice the rate, count the paydays, and remember why the early ones matter most.

## Subtopic: The Headline and the Till Slip

The poster in the bank window says one number; the till slip says another. The nominal rate is the headline — 8,1 percent in bold print, with compounded monthly tucked into the fine print below. The effective rate is the till slip: what the year ACTUALLY did to your money once the fine print has been rung up.

Printing the till slip takes one calculation: push a single rand through a full year. At 8,1 percent monthly, that is 1,00675 multiplied by itself twelve times, giving 1,08408. The slip reads 8,41 percent — that is what the account truly pays, whatever the headline claimed. The fine print always pushes one way: compounding more than once a year lifts the true rate ABOVE the headline, never below it. If your effective lands under nominal, the calculation flipped somewhere — recheck the power.

Why does this matter? Because headlines from different banks cannot be compared, and till slips can. Bank X's poster says 8,3 percent, settled once a year; Bank Y's says 8,1 percent, compounded monthly. Headlines vote X. Till slips say: X truly pays 8,3; Y truly pays 8,41. Y wins — the quieter poster with the busier machinery pays more. Every compare-the-accounts question is this exact move: convert every offer to its till slip, then compare slips with slips. Write the closing sentence with both effective rates in it; that sentence carries the conclusion.

And the camera from Part 1 is the same honesty applied to shopping: the headline says 650 dollars all year, but the till slip in rand depends on the day's exchange rate — 11 440 rand at 17,60, and 11 960 rand once the rand slips to 18,40. When the currency moves, every imported price tag in the country quietly reprints.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now — never trust the headline, always print the till slip, and convert every offer to effective before you choose.
