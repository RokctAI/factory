# Part 1 — Expert

With grade eleven, geometry steps inside the circle — and the rules of the game change with it. From here on, theorems are not merely quoted; they are proved, and every line of a solution must carry the reason that makes it true. This session builds the first half of the circle toolkit: the family of chord theorems, the doubling relationship between the angle at the centre and the angle at the circumference, the right angle that lives in every semicircle, and the equal angles that share a segment. We close with the craft that converts theory into marks — writing a rider solution as a chain a marker can audit line by line.

## Subtopic: The Vocabulary of the Circle and the Chord Theorems

The reasons you will quote are built from definitions, so the definitions come first. A radius joins the centre to the circle, and all radii of one circle are equal — a modest sentence that unlocks more riders than any single theorem. A chord joins two points on the circle; the longest chord passes through the centre and is called the diameter, twice the radius. An arc is a portion of the circle's curve, and any chord splits the circle's interior into a major and a minor segment.

Theorem one of the family: the perpendicular drawn from the centre to a chord bisects the chord. The proof is a congruency template worth owning. Let O be the centre, AB the chord, and N the point where the perpendicular from O meets AB. Set triangles ONA and ONB side by side. OA equals OB — radii. ON is shared. The angles at N are right angles by construction. Right angle, hypotenuse and a side match: the triangles are congruent, so AN equals NB, and the chord is cut exactly in half.

The family has two more members. The converse: the line joining the centre to the midpoint of a chord meets that chord at ninety degrees. And the closing relative: the perpendicular bisector of any chord must pass through the centre — the reason an engineer can recover the centre of a worn circular gear by bisecting two chords and marking where the bisectors cross.

The standard calculation this family produces is a Pythagoras triangle. A chord of length twenty-four sits in a circle of radius thirteen: how far is the chord from the centre? The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord into two lengths of twelve, and the radius to an endpoint serves as hypotenuse: distance squared equals thirteen squared minus twelve squared — one hundred and sixty-nine minus one hundred and forty-four — twenty-five. The chord lies five units from the centre. Half the chord, the distance, one radius: that triangle answers every chord-distance question ever set.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They exercise all three members of the chord family, and one checks that you use half the chord, never the whole.

## Subtopic: The Angle at the Centre Is Double the Angle at the Circumference

Here is the theorem the whole topic leans on. Choose an arc AB, let O be the centre, and let C be any point on the remaining arc. The claim: the angle AOB at the centre is exactly twice the angle ACB at the circumference, provided both stand on the same arc AB.

The proof is examinable, so walk it slowly. Draw the line from C through the centre O and extend it to a point D on the far side. Triangle AOC is isosceles — OA and OC are radii — so its base angles at A and C are equal; call each one x. By the exterior angle theorem, angle AOD, exterior to triangle AOC, equals x plus x — two x. Repeat on triangle BOC with base angles y: angle BOD equals two y. Now assemble: angle AOB is two x plus two y, while angle ACB is x plus y. The centre holds exactly double. If C sits so that the line CD falls outside the angle, the same construction runs on subtraction instead of addition, and the factor of two survives untouched.

Applying it is one division: a centre angle of one hundred and twenty degrees over an arc forces every circumference angle on that arc to be sixty degrees. The danger is not the halving but the arc. When the centre angle is reflex — say two hundred degrees measured the long way round — the circumference angle standing on that same long arc is half of two hundred: one hundred degrees. The theorem never misfires; what misfires is halving the wrong centre angle. Before dividing anything, shade the arc that both angles genuinely stand on.

One corollary falls out immediately and outworks almost everything else. Let the chord be a diameter: the centre angle is then a straight one hundred and eighty degrees, so every circumference angle on a diameter is ninety degrees. The angle in a semicircle is a right angle — quoted exactly as: angle in semicircle. It reverses too: if a chord subtends ninety degrees at the circumference, that chord is a diameter and runs through the centre.

Stop for this section's questions now — halve the centre angle that stands on the correct arc, and treat every diameter as a ninety-degree gift.

## Subtopic: Angles in the Same Segment

Fix a chord AB and confine yourself to one side of it — one segment. Place two points C and D anywhere on that arc. The theorem: angle ACB equals angle ADB. Any two angles subtended by the same chord from the same side are equal, regardless of where on the arc their vertices sit.

Given the centre theorem, the proof takes one sentence: each of those circumference angles is half of the same central angle AOB, so they must equal each other. Notice the architecture of this topic — one theorem carries the weight, and its corollaries arrive nearly free.

In riders, same-segment is the transporter: it moves an angle from one triangle into another. Suppose chords AD and BC intersect at a point K inside the circle. Angle BAD and angle BCD both stand on chord BD from the same side, so they are equal; angle ABC and angle ADC both stand on chord AC, equal again. Two overlapping triangles now share two pairs of equal angles — frequently the entire skeleton of a proof, before a single number appears.

The phrase same side is load-bearing. Vertices on opposite sides of the chord occupy different segments, and those angles are not equal — they turn out to be supplementary, which is next session's business. For now, adopt the discipline: run your pen along the chord and confirm both vertices lie on the same arc before quoting the theorem.

A short chain to set the habit. Chord AB subtends angle ACB equal to forty-seven degrees at C, and D lies on the same arc as C. Then angle ADB is forty-seven degrees — reason: angles in same segment. Let AD cut BC at K: the angles at K are vertically opposite and therefore equal, and with one pair of same-segment angles already matched, the third angles of triangles AKC and... trace it on the diagram: two triangles with two matching angle pairs, meaning the third pair matches automatically — similar triangles, assembled from one chord and one crossing.

The questions for this section arrive now — same chord, same side, equal angles, and always state which chord is doing the subtending.

## Subtopic: Riders — Reading the Diagram and Writing Reasons

A rider is a chain-puzzle built from the theorems, and the marking is unsentimental: each line is a statement plus a reason, and a true statement without its reason earns nothing. The accepted reasons are short, standardised phrases — radii equal; line from centre perpendicular to chord; angle at centre twice angle at circumference; angle in semicircle; angles in same segment; base angles of isosceles triangle; exterior angle of triangle; angles of triangle sum to one eighty.

Before writing, read the diagram with a plan. One: darken every radius the moment the centre appears — any two radii manufacture an isosceles triangle, and isosceles triangles hand out equal base angles on request. Two: hunt for a diameter, because it plants a free right angle on the circumference. Three: shade the arc beneath any centre angle before halving or doubling anything. Four: chase angles in pencil on the diagram until the route is visible, and only then write.

Now a complete worked rider. O is the centre; A, B and C lie on the circle; angle AOB is eighty degrees, standing on the minor arc AB, with C on the major arc. Find angle ACB and the base angles of triangle AOB. Line one: angle ACB equals forty degrees — reason: angle at centre is twice angle at circumference. Line two: OA equals OB — reason: radii. Line three: triangle AOB is isosceles, so the angles at A and B are equal — reason: base angles of isosceles triangle. Line four: those two angles share one hundred degrees after the eighty is removed from one eighty, so each is fifty degrees — reason: angle sum of triangle. Four claims, four reasons, full credit.

Extend the rider one link, the way real questions do. Let BC be a diameter. Then angle BAC is ninety degrees — angle in semicircle — and angle ABC comes from the angle sum of triangle ABC: one eighty minus ninety minus forty, fifty degrees. Watch the structure: every fresh line spends a result from an earlier line. A rider is a chain, and the marks live in the links, not the destination.

The final questions of this part are with you now — a reason on every line, radii darkened first, and let each answer finance the next.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the identical theorems rebuilt from things you can hold — a folded paper circle, a stadium full of spectators, and a detective's case file — same reasons, same marks.

## Subtopic: Folding the Circle

Cut a clean circle from a sheet of paper. Draw any chord across it, then fold the paper so the chord's two endpoints land on each other. Study the crease: it crosses the chord at its exact middle, and it crosses at a perfect right angle — the fold forced both at once. Draw a second chord anywhere else and fold again: same double result. And now the quiet magic: both creases pass through the same point, the exact centre of the circle. Two chords, two folds, and the centre reveals itself — no ruler, no measurement, no guessing.

That single fold carries all three chord theorems on its back. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord cuts it in half. The line from the centre to a chord's midpoint stands at ninety degrees to it. The perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre. Three examination sentences, one crease.

Every calculation in this corner of the topic is one right-angled triangle. Chord twenty-four, radius thirteen: the fold splits the chord into two twelves; a radius reaches out to the chord's endpoint; fold-line, half-chord and radius close into a right triangle. Pythagoras: thirteen squared minus twelve squared is twenty-five, so the fold-line — the distance from centre to chord — is five. The classic lost mark is using the full twenty-four instead of the twelve. The fold cuts the chord in half; your triangle must too.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Fold the circle in your mind, and build the triangle from half the chord, always.

## Subtopic: The Whole Stadium Sees the Same Game

Picture a circular stadium wrapped around a pitch, and let the goal mouth be a chord — two posts standing on the circle. Walk the curved stand on one side of that chord, pausing seat by seat, always watching the goal. The theorem you can feel in your neck: every seat on that same arc opens the goal at exactly the same viewing angle. Near the middle of the arc, far along the end — the goal mouth subtends one unchanging angle. That is angles in the same segment: one chord, one side, one angle, wherever the vertex.

Now let one spectator stand at the exact centre of the stadium. The centre is greedy: whatever angle the seats on the far arc enjoy, the centre claims precisely double. If the centre sees the goal at one hundred and twenty degrees, every seat on the far curve sees it at sixty. Halve the centre, and the whole stand is measured in one division.

One seat's view is famous. Stretch the goal-chord until it runs through the centre — a full diameter. The centre's angle is then a dead-straight one hundred and eighty, so every seat on the curve sees the diameter at half of that: a perfect ninety degrees. Angle in a semicircle. It is the most quoted line in the topic — the instant a diameter appears in a diagram, a right angle is waiting somewhere on the circle to be claimed.

The only genuine trap is halving on the wrong side. Seat and centre must be watching the same arc between the posts. Shade that arc before dividing, and the stadium never lies to you.

Your questions for this part are up now. Same curve, same angle; the centre takes double; a diameter hands every seat a ninety.

## Subtopic: Solving a Rider Like a Case File

A rider gives you a diagram, one or two facts, and asks for an angle that sits three deductions away. Work it like a detective works a case: never guess the ending — collect small certainties in order, each one earning the next.

Open every case the same way. Darken the radii first: two radii always close an isosceles triangle, and an isosceles triangle volunteers a pair of equal base angles without being asked. Scan for a diameter, because a diameter donates a free ninety on the circumference. Then shade the arc under any centre angle so that the doubling and halving happen on the correct side of the circle.

Run the case from upstairs in plain language. Fact: the centre angle on the minor arc is eighty. Certainty one: the seat on the far arc sees forty — the centre claims double, the seat keeps half. Certainty two: the two radii to the chord's endpoints make an isosceles triangle whose apex holds the eighty; the leftover hundred splits evenly, fifty and fifty. Certainty three: if a diameter enters the picture, a ninety comes free, and the triangle's angle sum finishes whatever remains. Each certainty leans on the one before — that lean is the entire method.

And the habit that actually carries the marks: give the reason, every single line. In this topic the reason is always one of a short menu — radii equal, centre is double, same segment, angle in semicircle, angle sum of a triangle. Claim, reason, next link. An answer without reasons is a case without evidence: however right it feels, the court of the marker must dismiss it.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: darken the radii, shade the arc, collect the free ninety at every diameter, and file a reason with every claim.
