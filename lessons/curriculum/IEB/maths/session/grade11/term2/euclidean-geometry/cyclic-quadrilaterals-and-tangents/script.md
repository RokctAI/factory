# Part 1 — Expert

The last session worked angles inside the circle; this one finishes the toolkit with the two families still missing: the cyclic quadrilateral, a four-sided figure whose every vertex touches the circle, and the tangent, the line that grazes the circle at a single point. Together they carry the theorems that decide the toughest riders in your final exams — opposite angles that always total one eighty, the two tangents from a point that must be equal, and the tangent-chord angle that reappears, unchanged, in the alternate segment.

## Subtopic: Cyclic Quadrilaterals — Opposite Angles Add to One Eighty

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four of its vertices on one circle. The theorem that defines its behaviour: each pair of opposite angles is supplementary — together they make one hundred and eighty degrees.

The proof recycles the doubling theorem. Name the quadrilateral ABCD with centre O. The interior angle at B stands on arc ADC — the arc that stays away from B — so its central partner on that arc is twice B. The angle at D stands on the complementary arc ABC, with central partner twice D. But those two central angles between them sweep out the entire revolution about O: three hundred and sixty degrees. Twice B plus twice D equals three sixty, therefore B plus D equals one eighty. Run the identical argument on the other diagonal pair and A plus C follows.

In use it is a single subtraction: if angle A measures one hundred and five degrees, angle C measures seventy-five. The reason is quoted as: opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral — and it may only be quoted when all four vertices genuinely sit on the circle.

A second theorem comes almost free of charge. Extend side AB beyond B to a point F. The exterior angle CBF makes a straight line with the interior angle ABC, so CBF equals one eighty minus ABC — but one eighty minus ABC is precisely the opposite interior angle D. Conclusion: the exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle. In a rider, this teleports an angle diagonally across the figure in one written line.

One reading discipline before quoting anything: confirm the four vertices lie on the circle in cyclic order. A vertex parked inside or outside the circle, or a crossed quadrilateral, voids the theorem — and diagrams are drawn to tempt exactly that mistake.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They run the supplementary pairs in both directions, and one dangles a quadrilateral with a vertex off the circle.

## Subtopic: Proving a Quadrilateral Is Cyclic

The reverse task appears just as often: no circle is drawn, and you must prove that four points deserve one — that they are concyclic. Every forward theorem from the last section has a converse, and any single converse settles the proof.

Converse one: if one pair of opposite angles of a quadrilateral is supplementary, the quadrilateral is cyclic. Only one pair is needed — the angles of any quadrilateral total three sixty, so once one pair reaches one eighty the other pair has no choice.

Converse two: if an exterior angle of a quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle, the quadrilateral is cyclic.

Converse three, the one learners overlook: if a line segment subtends equal angles at two points on the same side of it, then those two points and the segment's endpoints lie on one circle. This reverses angles in the same segment — equal angles seen from the same side certify a shared circle.

Choosing the test is a matter of matching it to the given information. Handed two angles that sum towards one eighty across the figure: converse one. Handed a produced side: converse two. Handed two triangles on a common base whose apex angles are equal and on the same side: converse three. Write the conclusion with the converse named — for instance: PQRS is cyclic, converse, exterior angle equals interior opposite.

Understand why these questions anchor long riders. The instant a quadrilateral is proved cyclic, the full circle arsenal switches on for those four points — supplementary pairs, same-segment transfers, everything. Multi-part questions are built on that hinge: the first part earns the circle, the later parts spend it. Miss the proof and every downstream part starves.

Stop for this section's questions now — one supplementary pair suffices, and each test is simply a forward theorem read backwards.

## Subtopic: Tangents — the Perpendicular Radius and the Two Equal Tangents

A tangent meets the circle at exactly one point — the point of contact. The foundation fact is accepted without proof in your course: the tangent stands perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of contact. Free to use, but never free to leave unquoted: the reason is written as tangent perpendicular to radius.

That guaranteed right angle turns tangent questions into Pythagoras. Let a point P lie seventeen units from the centre O of a circle of radius eight, with PT the tangent touching at T. Since OT meets PT at ninety degrees, triangle OTP is right-angled at T with OP as hypotenuse: PT squared equals seventeen squared minus eight squared — two eighty-nine minus sixty-four — two hundred and twenty-five. The tangent measures fifteen. Distance to centre plus radius plus tangent: always this triangle.

Next, the two-tangent theorem. From any point outside the circle exactly two tangents can be drawn, touching on either side, and the theorem declares them equal in length. The proof is the same congruency template as the chord theorem. Tangents from P touch at A and B. In triangles OAP and OBP: OA equals OB, radii; OP is common, and it is the hypotenuse of both, since the angles at A and B are ninety degrees by the tangent-radius fact. Right angle, hypotenuse, side — congruent, hence PA equals PB. Quoted as: tangents from same point.

Equal tangents deposit an isosceles triangle into every such diagram, and it pays interest immediately. If the angle between the tangents at P is fifty-two degrees, then triangle PAB has PA equal to PB, so its base angles share one eighty minus fifty-two — one twenty-eight — sixty-four degrees each. Hold that sixty-four carefully; the final theorem is about to put it to work.

The questions for this section arrive now — a right angle at every point of contact, Pythagoras for lengths, and twin tangents building isosceles triangles.

## Subtopic: The Tangent-Chord Theorem and a Full Rider

The closing theorem is the topic's showpiece: the angle between a tangent and a chord drawn at the point of contact equals the angle that chord subtends in the alternate segment — the segment on the far side of the chord.

Set the stage precisely: tangent touching at A, chord AB running into the circle, splitting it into two segments. The angle between tangent and chord on one side of AB equals every angle standing on AB from within the opposite segment. The proof skeleton is worth carrying into the exam. From A draw the diameter AC and join CB. The tangent meets the diameter at ninety — tangent perpendicular to radius — so the angle between chord and diameter is ninety minus x, where x names the tangent-chord angle. Angle ABC is ninety — angle in semicircle. The angle sum of triangle ABC forces angle ACB to equal x. And angle ACB stands on chord AB in the alternate segment, where same-segment makes every other angle on that arc equal to it too. The claim is delivered.

One clean rehearsal: tangent at A, chord AB, tangent-chord angle fifty-eight degrees — then every angle on AB from the alternate segment is fifty-eight. The reason is quoted as: tangent-chord.

Now the full rider that binds the session. PA and PB are tangents from P touching at A and B, with angle APB equal to fifty-two degrees, and C sits on the major arc AB. Find angle ACB. Line one: PA equals PB — tangents from same point. Line two: the base angles PAB and PBA of isosceles triangle PAB are each sixty-four degrees — base angles plus angle sum with fifty-two. Line three: angle PAB is a tangent-chord angle for chord AB, so the angle in the alternate segment matches it: angle ACB equals sixty-four degrees — tangent-chord. Three lines, three reasons, and the answer lands on the far side of the circle.

Good geometry lets you audit yourself along an independent road. In quadrilateral OAPB, the angles at A and B are each ninety, so the central angle AOB is three sixty minus ninety minus ninety minus fifty-two — one hundred and twenty-eight. Half of that stands at the circumference on the major arc: sixty-four. Two unrelated theorem chains, one identical answer — that agreement is what certainty feels like in this topic.

The final questions of this part are with you now — cross the chord into the alternate segment, and let a second route confirm your first.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four theorems, carried by pictures from an ordinary afternoon — a kraal wall, a wheel against a kerb, ropes from a peg, and a shout that comes back — same reasons, same marks.

## Subtopic: Two Arcs That Share the Whole Circle

Why must opposite corners of a cyclic quadrilateral total one eighty? Put four gate posts on a circular kraal wall and name them A, B, C, D in order. Stand at post B and look across at the far stretch of wall — your viewing angle stands on the arc that runs from A through D to C. Now cross to the opposite post D and look back — your angle stands on the leftover arc, from A through B to C. Notice what the two of you have done between you: your two arcs, joined end to end, are the entire wall. Nothing is missed, nothing is counted twice.

Bring in the greedy centre from last session — the centre always claims double what any wall-post sees. Its claim for B is double B; its claim for D is double D; and since the two arcs exhaust the whole circle, the two claims together must sweep the full three hundred and sixty degrees. Double B plus double D is three sixty, so B plus D is one eighty. The theorem is bookkeeping: one whole wall, split between two opposite viewpoints, then halved.

The exterior angle version is the same fact plus a straight line. Walk out through gate C and keep walking: the angle behind you, outside the kraal, completes the inside angle at C to one eighty. But the angle at A also completes C to one eighty — the theorem just proved it. Two quantities completing the same angle to one eighty are equal: the outside angle at C is the angle at A, delivered across the diagonal unchanged.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Two posts, two arcs, one whole wall — the entire theorem in one sentence.

## Subtopic: The Kerb and the Two Ropes

Roll a wheel up against a straight kerb until it touches at one point. The spoke from the hub to that contact point stands perfectly square to the kerb — roll the wheel along, and at every new contact point the same square angle appears. That is the tangent fact: radius to the point of contact, ninety degrees, always. It costs nothing to use — but the reason still gets written: tangent perpendicular to radius.

A guaranteed right angle is an invitation to Pythagoras. Stand seventeen paces from the hub of a circular stock tank of radius eight and stretch a rope from where you stand so it just touches the tank's side: rope, spoke and your line to the hub close a right-angled triangle, and the rope works out to fifteen — two eighty-nine minus sixty-four, square-rooted.

Now drive a peg into the ground outside a circular kraal and run two ropes from the peg to touch the wall on opposite sides. The theorem promises the ropes are exactly equal — every time, wherever the peg stands. See it through symmetry: the line from peg to centre is a mirror down the middle of the picture, and each rope is the reflection of the other. Equal ropes make the peg and the two touch-points an isosceles triangle, and isosceles triangles hand out equal base angles for free. If the ropes open at fifty-two degrees at the peg, the remaining one twenty-eight splits into sixty-four and sixty-four at the wall. Every two-tangent diagram conceals this triangle, and finding it is normally the first mark of the question.

Your questions for this part are up now. Square at the touch-point, Pythagoras along the rope, and two ropes from one peg are always twins.

## Subtopic: The Echo Across the Chord

The tangent-chord theorem carries the most intimidating name in the topic, so trade the name for a picture: an echo. The tangent is a long wall grazing the circle at one point. From that point a chord strikes into the circle, opening some angle against the wall — say fifty-eight degrees. The theorem says: shout that fifty-eight across the chord, and it returns as a perfect echo from the far arc. Every listener standing in the opposite segment, watching the same chord, hears — sees — exactly fifty-eight degrees.

Two conditions keep the echo true. First, the echo must cross the chord. The angle you start with lives on one side of the chord; its equal partners live on the arc on the other side — the alternate segment. If your answer landed on the same side as the shout, nothing was crossed and the answer is wrong. The wall's other angle — one eighty minus fifty-eight — echoes to the other arc, so each side of the chord serves its own audience. Second, the shout must start at the point of contact: a chord drawn from anywhere else on the circle makes no tangent-chord angle at all.

Now listen to the full rider as a story told twice. Two ropes from one peg, fifty-two degrees between them. Twin ropes force an isosceles triangle: sixty-four and sixty-four at the wall. Each sixty-four is a shout against the tangent wall, so it echoes across its chord to the far arc: the listener on the major arc catches sixty-four degrees. Twins, isosceles, echo — three sentences, full marks. Then walk the second road to be sure: around the peg-and-centre quadrilateral, two nineties and a fifty-two leave one twenty-eight at the centre, and the greedy centre grants the far arc half: sixty-four again. When two roads meet at one number, the number is safe to hand in.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: square at the touch, twins from the peg, and every shout against the wall echoes across the chord — same size, opposite segment.
