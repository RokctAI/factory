**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: in the chord-distance calculation, several people got twenty-five under the square root but then wrote the wrong triangle down. How do you know which three lengths actually make the triangle?

**Tutor:** Build the triangle from the theorem, not from whichever numbers are lying around. The theorem says the perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord — so the moment that perpendicular is drawn, the picture contains exactly one useful right-angled triangle, and its three sides are fixed: the distance from centre to chord, half the chord, and one radius out to the chord's endpoint. For our chord of twenty-four in a circle of radius thirteen: the half-chord is twelve, the radius is thirteen, and thirteen is the hypotenuse because it sits opposite the right angle. Distance squared is one sixty-nine minus one forty-four, twenty-five, so the distance is five. The two errors I see every year are both triangle-building errors: using the whole chord of twenty-four, which ignores the bisection the theorem guarantees, and putting the radius on a leg instead of the hypotenuse, which produces a square root of a wrong number. Draw the radius to the endpoint of the chord — never to its midpoint — and label the three sides before any squaring starts. Once the triangle is honest, the arithmetic is two lines. And write the reason for the bisection: perpendicular from centre to chord. The calculation earns its marks only when the geometry that set it up is quoted.

**Bianca:** Next: the reflex angle trap. When the centre angle is two hundred degrees, some halved it to one hundred, some subtracted from three sixty first and halved one sixty to eighty. Both groups were sure. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Both calculations are internally correct — they answer different questions, and the diagram decides which question is being asked. A chord's two endpoints cut the circle into two arcs, minor and major, and each arc carries its own pairing of centre angle and circumference angle. The non-reflex centre angle of one hundred and sixty degrees stands on the minor arc, so a point sitting on the major arc — looking across at that minor arc — sees eighty degrees. The reflex angle of two hundred stands on the major arc, so a point on the minor arc sees one hundred. Notice the pleasing consistency: eighty plus one hundred is one eighty, which is exactly the supplementary relationship you will meet formally with cyclic quadrilaterals next session. So the method is never halve first, decide later. First locate the vertex of the circumference angle. Then shade the arc it stands on — the arc between the chord's endpoints on the far side from the vertex. Then halve the centre angle that stands on that same shaded arc, reflex or not. The theorem has no exceptions; every so-called exception in homework is a mis-shaded arc. One shading stroke with a pencil costs three seconds and rescues the whole question.

**Bianca:** Someone asked why we bother learning the proof of the doubling theorem when the exam might only ask us to use it. Is the proof really worth the effort?

**Tutor:** Yes, three times over. First, practically: theorem proofs are directly examinable at grade eleven, and the doubling proof is among the most commonly asked, so the effort converts straight into marks. Second, the proof is a masterclass in the topic's whole method. Look at its ingredients: draw a line through the centre, spot that two radii make an isosceles triangle, harvest the equal base angles, then apply the exterior angle theorem to double them. Those are precisely the moves you use in every rider — the proof is a guided rehearsal of the toolkit. When you prove the theorem, you are practising riders with the answers included. Third, the proof teaches you the theorem's boundaries, which is what protects you in strange diagrams. Because the proof runs through the centre point, you learn why both angles must stand on the same arc — the two isosceles triangles are built on that shared arc — and why the doubling survives even when the circumference point slides sideways and the construction subtracts instead of adds. Memorised facts crack under a weird diagram; understood facts flex. My suggestion: learn the proof as a story in three sentences — through the centre, isosceles twice, exterior angle twice — and rebuild the details from the story rather than memorising line by line.

**Bianca:** Last one: reasons. A learner says writing radii or same segment after every line feels like empty ritual when the answer is already right. What actually goes wrong if you skip the reasons?

**Tutor:** What goes wrong is that the marks vanish — and the marker is not being petty; the reasons are the mathematics. Here is the honest way to see it. A rider answer is a proof: a claim that an angle is forty degrees is worthless on its own, because the marker cannot tell whether it came from a theorem or from measuring the diagram with a protractor or from a lucky guess. The reason is the evidence that the claim was earned. That is why the mark scheme attaches marks to the reason itself — statement mark and reason mark are often separate, so a page of correct numbers with no reasons can score close to nothing while a page with one wrong number but honest reasons scores nearly full marks. There is also a selfish benefit: writing reasons audits your own logic in real time. If you cannot name the reason for a line, you have almost certainly skipped a step or used a theorem where its conditions fail — same segment quoted across two different chords is the classic one, and the act of writing the reason is what catches it. Keep the menu short and memorised: radii; perpendicular from centre to chord; centre is twice circumference; angle in semicircle; same segment; base angles isosceles; exterior angle; angle sum. Eight phrases, and every line of every rider takes one of them. Ritual it may feel — but it is the ritual the marks are paid for.
