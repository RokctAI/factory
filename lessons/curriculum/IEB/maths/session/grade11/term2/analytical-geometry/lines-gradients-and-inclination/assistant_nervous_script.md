Hey, it's Bianca — if today felt like fractions raining from every direction, take a breath, because analytical geometry is honestly the most predictable topic you will meet this year. It cannot surprise you. It only knows five moves, and every single one starts the same way: write down the two points.

Move one: distance — gap across, gap up, Pythagoras on the diagonal. Move two: midpoint — average the x's, average the y's, and always give both. Move three: gradient — change in y over change in x, subtracting in the same order top and bottom. That is the entire first half of the lesson.

Move four: equations. Every version is get m, then substitute one point for c. Two points? Gradient between them. Parallel line? Copy its gradient. Perpendicular? Invert the fraction, reverse the sign. Then the ten-second insurance: push the unused point through your answer and watch it land.

Move five: inclination. One sentence — gradient equals tan of the angle. Rising line: calculator answers directly. Falling line: take the size, inverse tan, then one eighty minus. Never submit a negative angle.

Tonight, do this one thing: take P at minus three, one, and Q at five, minus five — the pair from class. Find the distance, the midpoint, the gradient, the equation, the inclination. You can mark yourself: ten, then one and minus two, then minus three quarters. Five answers, one pair of points, whole lesson rehearsed.

Neat and systematic beats fast every time in this topic — and neat and systematic is completely learnable. Now go try the practice questions; they are waiting for you, and you are readier than you feel. You've got this!
