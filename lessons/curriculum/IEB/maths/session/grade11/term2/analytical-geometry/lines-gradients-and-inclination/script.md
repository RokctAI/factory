# Part 1 — Expert

Analytical geometry is the pact between algebra and geometry: once every point wears a pair of coordinates, every geometric claim — this length, that midpoint, this angle — can be settled by calculation alone. Grade eleven sharpens the three tools you met in grade ten, distance, midpoint and gradient, and then adds two genuinely new powers: building the equation of a straight line from whatever scraps of information a question offers, and extracting the angle a line makes with the horizontal directly from its gradient. Master the small set of recipes and this topic becomes one of the most reliable sources of marks in your final exams.

## Subtopic: Distance, Midpoint and Gradient — the Three Instruments

Every calculation in this topic begins with two points, so anchor a working pair for the whole session: P at minus three, one, and Q at five, minus five.

First instrument: distance. Walk from P to Q. The horizontal change is five minus minus three — eight. The vertical change is minus five minus one — minus six, size six. Those two changes form the legs of a right-angled triangle, and PQ is its hypotenuse, so Pythagoras closes the account: eight squared plus six squared is sixty-four plus thirty-six, one hundred, and the root is ten. PQ is ten units. Every distance formula question is this triangle in disguise — the formula is simply Pythagoras with coordinates substituted in.

Second instrument: midpoint. Average each coordinate separately. The x of the midpoint is minus three plus five, over two — one. The y is one plus minus five, over two — minus two. Midpoint: one, minus two. Nothing deeper than averages, but a warning earned from marking: give both coordinates. An answer of just one is half a midpoint and earns half the credit at best.

Third instrument: gradient. Change in y over change in x. From P to Q that is minus six over eight, which reduces to minus three quarters. Two habits keep the sign honest. Subtract in one consistent order: if Q's y minus P's y sits on top, then Q's x minus P's x must sit underneath — mixing the orders flips the sign wrongly. And always read the answer back against a rough sketch: negative means the line falls as you move right, positive means it climbs, zero means dead level, and a vertical line has no gradient at all — its run is zero, and dividing by zero is not permitted.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Fresh point pairs, all three instruments, and one probes the vertical-line exception.

## Subtopic: Parallel, Perpendicular and Collinear

Gradient measures direction, so every relationship between the directions of lines is a statement about gradients.

Parallel lines carry identical gradients — same slope, same heading, never crossing. If a line has gradient minus three quarters, so does every line parallel to it. The statement works both ways, and questions use both ways: prove two lines parallel by computing both gradients and showing them equal, or borrow a gradient because the question declares the lines parallel.

Perpendicular lines carry gradients whose product is minus one. The working shortcut: invert the fraction and reverse the sign. Perpendicular to minus three quarters is plus four thirds — and always run the check: minus three quarters times four thirds is minus twelve over twelve, minus one. Confirmed. One pair escapes the formula: a horizontal line, gradient zero, meets a vertical line, no gradient, at right angles — but their product cannot be formed, so you state the geometry rather than the multiplication.

Collinear points share a single straight line, and gradients test that too: three points P, Q and R are collinear when the gradient from P to Q equals the gradient from Q to R. Try R at nine, minus eight, against our pair. Gradient PQ: minus three quarters. Gradient QR: minus eight minus minus five is minus three, over nine minus five, which is four — minus three quarters again. Equal gradients through the shared middle point Q, so the three points queue on one line.

These three tests are the working vocabulary of proof questions. Show that a quadrilateral is a parallelogram: two pairs of opposite sides with equal gradients. Show that a triangle has a right angle: one product of gradients equal to minus one. Translate the shape's definition into gradient checks and the geometry collapses into arithmetic.

Stop for this section's questions now — equal gradients for parallel, product minus one for perpendicular, and one shared-point gradient match for collinear.

## Subtopic: Finding the Equation of a Line

Every non-vertical line answers to y equals m x plus c — gradient m, y-intercept c — and building that equation is always the same two moves: capture m, then substitute one known point to expose c.

Case one: two points supplied. Through P at minus three, one, and Q at five, minus five, the gradient is minus three quarters from earlier. Write the frame y equals minus three quarters x plus c and feed in Q: minus five equals minus three quarters times five, plus c. That is minus five equals minus fifteen quarters plus c, so c equals fifteen quarters minus five — fifteen quarters minus twenty quarters — minus five quarters. The line: y equals minus three quarters x, minus five quarters. Now the ten-second insurance: substitute the unused point P. Minus three quarters times minus three is nine quarters; nine quarters minus five quarters is four quarters, one — exactly P's y-coordinate. The equation is certified.

Case two: a point plus a parallel line. Through two, three, parallel to y equals two x plus five. Parallel donates its gradient: m is two. Substitute: three equals two times two plus c, so c is minus one. The line: y equals two x minus one.

Case three: a point plus a perpendicular line. Through four, one, perpendicular to y equals four x minus seven. Invert and reverse: m is minus one quarter. Substitute: one equals minus one quarter times four, plus c — one equals minus one plus c — c is two. The line: y equals minus one quarter x plus two.

Two lines refuse the recipe entirely. A vertical line has no m to find; its equation is x equals a constant, the shared x-coordinate — through three, seven, it is simply x equals three. A horizontal line is y equals a constant. Trying to push these through the m-and-c machine wastes minutes; recognise them on sight instead.

The questions for this section arrive now — capture the gradient, one substitution for c, and always spend the ten seconds on the unused-point check.

## Subtopic: Inclination — the Angle Inside the Gradient

The inclination of a line is the angle it opens with the positive x-axis, swept anticlockwise, and by definition it lives between zero and one hundred and eighty degrees. One sentence connects it to everything you learned in trigonometry: the gradient is the tangent of the inclination. Rise over run is opposite over adjacent — the gradient triangle and the tangent triangle are the same triangle.

A rising line has an acute inclination, and the calculator delivers it directly. Gradient one: inverse tangent of one, forty-five degrees. Gradient three: inverse tangent of three, seventy-one comma five seven degrees.

A falling line has a negative gradient, and its inclination must sit between ninety and one eighty — obtuse — which is where the reduction work from term one earns its keep. Take our gradient of minus three quarters. Strip the sign and work with the size: inverse tangent of three quarters is thirty-six comma eight seven degrees — that is the reference angle. Tangent is negative in the second quadrant, so the inclination is one eighty minus thirty-six comma eight seven: one hundred and forty-three comma one three degrees. Do not type the negative gradient into the calculator and submit the negative angle it offers — a negative number is outside the definition of inclination, and it will not be accepted, however honestly the calculator produced it.

The extreme cases lock the definition in place. Horizontal line: gradient zero, inclination zero. Vertical line: inclination ninety degrees — and tangent of ninety is undefined, perfectly matching the vertical line's missing gradient. The definitions were built to interlock.

Inclination runs backwards as well. A line inclined at one hundred and twenty degrees has gradient tangent of one twenty — minus tangent of sixty — minus root three, about minus one comma seven three. And since parallel lines share gradients, they share inclinations, which turns questions about the angle between two lines into a comparison of two inclinations — a favourite closing question in this topic.

The final questions of this part are with you now — tangent ties gradient to angle, a falling line demands an obtuse answer, and one eighty minus the reference angle delivers it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole toolkit again, but walked through a city you can picture — blocks, bicycle ramps, and directions you could text a friend — same mathematics, gentler entrance.

## Subtopic: The Town Grid

Picture a delivery cyclist's map of a city built on a perfect grid: streets run north-south, avenues run east-west, and every corner has an address made of two numbers — blocks east of the main square, blocks north of it. The corner minus three, one is three blocks west and one block north. Coordinates are nothing more mysterious than that address system.

Distance is the crow's shortcut. Cycling from minus three, one, to five, minus five, means eight blocks east and six blocks south along the streets — but a crow flies the diagonal, and Pythagoras prices it: eight squared plus six squared is one hundred, so the direct flight is ten blocks. The distance formula just automates the crow: gap east, gap north, diagonal.

Midpoint is the fair meeting spot. Two friends at those corners who agree to meet halfway must average both halves of the address: east-west average one, north-south average minus two — they meet at the corner one, minus two. Averaging only one number gets one friend to the right street and leaves the other lost; both parts of the address must be averaged.

Gradient is the steepness of the diagonal ride: blocks gained north for every block pedalled east. Here you lose six north across eight east — three quarters of a block down per block along, gradient minus three quarters. Downhill rides to the right have negative gradients, uphill rides positive, a flat avenue zero. And a route running due north makes no eastward progress at all — per block east has no meaning when there are no blocks east, so that route has no gradient, and its whole equation is its street name: x equals some constant.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. East first, north second, and let the crow audit your Pythagoras.

## Subtopic: Ramps, Twins and Corners

Treat every line as a bicycle ramp. Two ramps with identical steepness are twins: set them down anywhere and they run side by side forever without touching. That is everything parallel means, which is why the whole test is one comparison — are the gradients equal?

To build a ramp that meets another at a perfect corner, take the first ramp's steepness and give it a quarter turn: what was the run becomes the rise, what was the rise becomes the run, and the climb reverses direction. In numbers: invert the fraction, reverse the sign — minus three quarters turns into plus four thirds. And every such pair betrays itself in one multiplication: a gradient times its quarter-turned partner is always minus one. That is the corner test, and it proves right angles without a protractor: multiply the gradients of the two sides that meet at the suspected corner, and if minus one appears, the corner is square.

Collinear is the queue test. Three delivery stops sit on one straight route exactly when the heading from stop one to stop two matches the heading from stop two to stop three — same gradient through the shared middle stop. Different gradients mean the middle stop has drifted off the route.

Twins, corners and queues are the full grammar of show-that questions. Parallelogram: two pairs of twin sides. Rectangle: the twins plus one square corner. Points on a line: one queue. Rewrite the shape's name in ramp language, and what looked like geometry becomes two or three short divisions.

Your questions for this part are up now. Twins share steepness, corners multiply to minus one, and a queue holds one heading.

## Subtopic: Directions From a Landmark

An equation of a line is a compressed set of directions: y equals m x plus c says start c blocks above the main square, then climb m blocks north for every block east. To write directions of your own you always need the same two ingredients — a steepness, and one corner the route passes through — and every question supplies both, sometimes in costume.

Two corners given? The steepness is the gradient between them. One corner and a parallel route? Copy the twin's steepness unchanged. One corner and a perpendicular route? Quarter-turn the other steepness — invert and reverse. Then one substitution finishes it: push the known corner through y equals m x plus c and c drops out. Through four, one, perpendicular to a route of steepness four: new steepness minus one quarter; substitute to find c equals two; directions: y equals minus one quarter x plus two. And always the cheap insurance — run the spare corner through your finished directions and watch it land exactly.

The last idea gives every route its bearing: the inclination, the angle the route cuts with the eastward avenue, measured anticlockwise, from zero up to one eighty. The bridge is one sentence — steepness equals the tangent of the angle — because rise over run is opposite over adjacent. Steepness one is a forty-five degree route. Steepness minus three quarters is a falling route, so its angle must be obtuse: take the size, three quarters, inverse tangent gives thirty-six comma eight seven, and one eighty minus that gives one hundred and forty-three comma one three degrees. A falling route always leans past the vertical ninety, exactly as second-quadrant tangent promised in term one.

Hold the three pictures and the lesson fits in one pocket: an address system with a crow, ramps with twins and corners, and directions with a bearing. Every formula in the topic is one of those pictures translated into symbols.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: copy or quarter-turn the steepness as the question demands, substitute one corner for c, and give every falling route its obtuse bearing.
