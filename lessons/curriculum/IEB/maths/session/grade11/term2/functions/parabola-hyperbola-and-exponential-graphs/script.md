# Part 1 — Expert

In grade ten the parabola, the hyperbola and the exponential arrived in their plainest clothes. Grade eleven issues each family a complete control panel: a number a that stretches and flips, a number p that pushes the graph sideways, and a number q that lifts or lowers it. This session works those controls across all three families — turning points, asymptotes, intercepts, domain and range — and finishes with the average gradient between two points on a curve, the first quiet step towards the calculus waiting in grade twelve.

## Subtopic: The Parabola in Turning-Point Form

The grade eleven parabola arrives as y equals a, times x plus p, all squared, plus q — turning-point form, so named because the turning point can be read off without a single calculation. The squared bracket is at its smallest, zero, when x equals minus p; at that x the whole expression collapses to q. Turning point: minus p, q. Axis of symmetry: the vertical line x equals minus p.

Each control acts alone. The a governs shape: positive a opens the arms upward around a minimum, negative a hangs them downward around a maximum, and a larger size of a narrows the graph. The p works sideways — and against the visible sign: x plus three moves the graph three left, x minus three moves it three right. The q works vertically, exactly as written.

Run a complete reading of y equals two, times x plus three, all squared, minus eight. Turning point: minus three, minus eight — a minimum, because a is positive two. Axis: x equals minus three. The y-intercept comes from substituting x equals zero: two times nine, minus eight — ten. The x-intercepts come from setting y to zero: two times the bracket squared equals eight, the bracket squared equals four, so x plus three equals two or minus two, giving x equals minus one or x equals minus five. And now the free audit: minus one and minus five average to minus three, precisely the axis of symmetry. Intercepts always sit balanced about the axis; if yours do not average to it, a sign slipped somewhere and the check just caught it.

Domain and range finish the reading. Domain: all real x — every parabola, always. Range: anchored at the turning point — here y greater than or equal to minus eight, since the minimum is genuinely reached and the arms climb forever.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They read turning points straight from the form, push graphs both ways, and one hinges on the balancing check.

## Subtopic: The Hyperbola and Its Asymptotes

The hyperbola is y equals a over x plus p, plus q, and its character is defined by two refusals. The denominator refuses to be zero, so x can never equal minus p — that forbidden x is a vertical asymptote, a line the graph crowds but never crosses. And a fraction with a non-zero numerator refuses to equal zero, so y can never equal q — a horizontal asymptote. The two asymptotes intersect at minus p, q, the centre of the hyperbola, and the two branches occupy opposite corners of that cross.

Read y equals four over x plus one, minus two. Vertical asymptote: x equals minus one. Horizontal asymptote: y equals minus two. With a positive four on top, the branches take the top-right and bottom-left corners; a negative a would hand them the other diagonal.

Intercepts are plain substitution. At x equals zero: four over one, minus two — two. The y-intercept is two. At y equals zero: four over x plus one equals two, so x plus one equals two, and x equals one. The x-intercept is one. Both intercepts belong to the top-right branch, and a sketch showing the cross of asymptotes with those two points pinned is a complete answer.

Domain and range are the two refusals written formally: x is any real number except minus one; y is any real number except minus two. Omitting the exceptions is the most reliably lost mark in this section — a domain without its exception describes a different graph.

One further reading: the hyperbola's own axes of symmetry are the two diagonals through the centre with gradients one and minus one. For our example they are y equals x minus one, and y equals minus x minus three — substitute the centre, minus one, minus two, into each and both equations hold, which is the quick verification.

Stop for this section's questions now — asymptotes first, corners next, intercepts last, and never write a domain without its exception.

## Subtopic: The Exponential Graph Raised and Shifted

The exponential family is y equals a times b to the power x plus p, plus q, with base b positive and not equal to one. Its skeleton: bases beyond one climb ever faster to the right; bases between zero and one decay. The crucial structural fact: b to any power is strictly positive, so a times that power can never equal zero — meaning y can never equal q. The line y equals q is a horizontal asymptote, a floor when a is positive, a ceiling when a is negative. There is no vertical asymptote anywhere in the family: the domain is all real x, always.

The controls behave exactly as on the parabola: p slides horizontally against its sign, q slides vertically, and a stretches, its sign deciding which side of the asymptote the graph lives on.

Work through y equals four times two to the power x minus one, minus eight. Asymptote: y equals minus eight. With a positive four and base two above one, the graph hugs the floor far to the left and climbs steeply to the right. The y-intercept, at x equals zero: two to the power minus one is a half, times four is two, minus eight — minus six. The x-intercept, at y equals zero: four times two to the power x minus one equals eight, so two to the power x minus one equals two, and matching powers of the common base gives x minus one equals one — x equals two. Both intercept calculations are the exponential equation-solving of term one, wearing graph clothing.

The range: y strictly greater than minus eight. Strictly — no equals sign — because the graph approaches its floor forever without landing. Contrast the parabola, whose range includes its turning point: the parabola touches its extreme; the exponential only approaches. Questions are built specifically to see whether you keep that distinction straight.

The questions for this section arrive now — the asymptote is q, intercepts fall to common bases, and the range inequality stays strict.

## Subtopic: Average Gradient and the Slope of a Curve

A straight line owns a single gradient. A curve owns none — its steepness changes continuously — so grade eleven asks a more modest question: between two chosen points on a curve, what is the average gradient? The answer is the gradient of the straight line joining those two points: change in y over change in x, borrowed directly from analytical geometry.

Compute one on y equals x squared. Between x equals two, where y is four, and x equals four, where y is sixteen: average gradient is sixteen minus four, over four minus two — twelve over two — six. Geometrically, the joining line climbs at six; the curve itself is shallower than six at the left point and steeper than six at the right, and the average packages the whole journey as one number.

Now the experiment that grade twelve will build a subject on. Nail the left point at x equals two and walk the right point inward. From two to three: nine minus four over one — five. From two to two comma five: six comma two five minus four, over zero comma five — four comma five. From two to two comma one: four comma four one minus four, over zero comma one — four comma one. Watch the sequence: five, four and a half, four comma one — the averages are closing in on four. The steepness of the curve itself, exactly at x equals two, is the number those shrinking averages approach: four. Grade twelve will call that limit the derivative. Grade eleven asks only that you run the table and name the number being approached.

Two disciplines for the exam. First, average gradient runs on y-values you must compute by substitution — substitute both, carefully, before subtracting anything. Second, keep the two questions apart: the average gradient between two points is always a direct calculation; the gradient at a single point is a limit, signalled by wording like the gradient of the curve at x equals two. The word between means the joining line; the word at means the destination of the table.

The final questions of this part are with you now — substitute twice, subtract once, divide once, and read what the shrinking averages are telling you.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three graphs and the same controls, carried by a tent on a sports field, fences no one may touch, and tollgates on the highway — same rules, same marks.

## Subtopic: Sliding the Tent

Start with the plainest parabola as a tent pitched with its lowest point on the centre spot of a sports field — the origin. The equation's three numbers are instructions to the setup crew. The q is the crane operator: plus q hoists the entire tent q metres straight up, minus q sets it lower, shape untouched. The p is the crew pushing the tent sideways — with the famous twist that the push defies the visible sign: x plus three in the bracket moves the tent three to the left, x minus three moves it three to the right. Never argue with the sign; instead ask, what x makes the bracket zero? That x is where the tent's lowest point now stands, and the question answers itself every time.

The a is the tension in the canvas. Crank a up and the walls pull steep and narrow; slacken it to a fraction and they sag wide and shallow. Flip a negative and the whole tent inverts — lowest point becomes highest point, minimum becomes maximum.

So y equals two, times x plus three, squared, minus eight is a setup order: shift three left, lower by eight, double the canvas tension. The low point lands at minus three, minus eight — exactly the turning point the form promises. Every parabola-reading question is this work order run in your head.

And every tent stands symmetric about its centre pole. The two places the canvas meets the ground — the x-intercepts, minus five and minus one here — sit at equal distances from the pole at x equals minus three. Average your two intercepts: if they do not give the pole, one of them is wrong, and you have caught it before the marker could. That single audit rescues more parabola marks than any formula.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Read each equation as a setup order: push, hoist, tension.

## Subtopic: The Fences You Never Touch

The hyperbola and the exponential each arrive fenced. An asymptote is a fence the graph presses against forever without ever touching — and locating the fences is always the opening move.

The hyperbola owns two fences forming a cross. The vertical fence stands at the x that would divide by zero — x equals minus one for four over x plus one, minus two. No point of the graph can stand on that line, because no y-value exists there. The horizontal fence runs at the height the formula cannot manufacture: the fraction part is never exactly zero, so y is never exactly minus two. The fences cross at the centre, and the two branches curl into opposite corners of the yard like two wary cats — top-right and bottom-left for positive a, the other diagonal for negative.

The exponential has a single fence: a floor (or ceiling) at height q. Two raised to any power is always positive — vanishingly small far to the left, enormous to the right, but never zero, never negative. So four times two to a power stays strictly above zero, and our whole graph rides strictly above its floor at minus eight, skimming it westward, soaring eastward.

Why the obsession with fences? Because domain and range are fences written out as sentences. Hyperbola: x is anything except the vertical fence; y is anything except the horizontal one. Exponential: x is anything at all; y is strictly beyond the floor — strictly, because approaching is not arriving. And the tent is the honest exception: a parabola genuinely touches its lowest point, so its range carries the equals sign. Touch versus approach — examiners return to that distinction year after year because it separates readers of graphs from memorisers of formulas.

Your questions for this part are up now. Fences first, corners second, and keep touch and approach strictly separated.

## Subtopic: Average Speed Between Two Tollgates

A bakkie passes a tollgate at two in the afternoon and a second tollgate, two hundred and forty kilometres down the highway, at five. Average speed: two forty over three — eighty kilometres per hour. Nobody imagines the needle sat frozen on eighty: there was a slow crawl at the toll plaza, an overtake past a truck, an open stretch. Eighty is the honest single-number summary of a varying trip.

Average gradient is the same summary for a curve. Between two points on a graph, stretch a taut string from one to the other: the string's gradient — rise over run — is the average gradient. On y equals x squared, from x equals two to x equals four, the heights are four and sixteen: string gradient twelve over two, six. The curve loafs below six near the first point and outruns six near the second; six is the fair summary of the whole stretch.

Now drag the second tollgate towards the first. Gate two at x equals three: string gradient five. At two comma five: four and a half. At two comma one: four comma one. Read the drumbeat — five, four and a half, four comma one — the strings are converging on four. Pull the gates close enough and the string gradient sits as close to four as you like. That four is the speedometer reading at the very instant of x equals two: the curve's own steepness at one point, exposed as the limit of averages over shrinking intervals. All of grade twelve calculus grows from this one manoeuvre; grade eleven only asks you to run the table and point at the number it approaches.

Keep the road's two questions distinct, because the wording will test you. Average speed between two gates: two substitutions and a division, always available. Speedometer at an instant: the destination of shrinking averages, a limit. Between means the string; at means the speedometer. Answer the question the word asked.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: compute both heights before subtracting, divide by the gap, and when the gates crowd together, name the number the strings are chasing.
