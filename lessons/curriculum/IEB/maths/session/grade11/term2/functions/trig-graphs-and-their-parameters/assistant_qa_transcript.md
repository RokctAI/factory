**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: amplitude. Someone measured from the bottom of the wave to the top, got six for the wave we sketched, and wrote a equals six. Where exactly does that go wrong?

**Tutor:** It goes wrong at the word from — amplitude is measured from the middle line, not from the trough. The wave three sine x minus one runs from minus four up to two, and the full vertical journey is indeed six; that number is real, but it is the total swing, and the amplitude is half of it. Think of the wave as breathing around its calm middle line: the amplitude is how far it inhales above the middle, which equals how far it exhales below — three up, three down, total six. So the two reflexes always travel together: middle line equals the average of maximum and minimum — two plus minus four over two, minus one — and amplitude equals half the difference — two minus minus four over two, three. Notice how the pair audits itself: middle minus one, plus amplitude three, lands on the maximum two; middle minus amplitude lands on minus four. If your q and a do not rebuild the max and min you started from, one of the two divisions slipped. And a final guard: amplitude is a distance, so it is always positive — a flipped wave with a negative a in the equation still has positive amplitude; the minus sign lives in the equation, not in the measurement. Half the swing, from the middle: say it that way and the six-versus-three error never returns.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the k control. A learner sketched sine three x with peaks at three instead of squeezing the wave, and says the three has to do something to the height because it multiplies. What untangles this?

**Tutor:** Position untangles it — where the three stands decides what it touches. Read the equation from the inside out, the way the calculation actually happens. In sine of three x, the three lives inside the bracket, so it grabs the angle before sine ever sees it: feed in x equals forty, and sine receives one hundred and twenty. The three is spending the angle faster — the wave finishes its full cycle by x equals one twenty instead of three sixty. But the output of sine is untouched: whatever angle it receives, sine still answers with a number between minus one and one, so the peaks stay at exactly one. Compare three sine x: now the three stands outside, catching sine's answer and tripling it — heights become three, but the angle was spent at the normal rate, so the period stays three sixty. Inside changes the input, the horizontal behaviour; outside changes the output, the vertical behaviour. That inside-outside law is the same one you already use on the parabola, where the bracket works horizontally and the added constant works vertically. For sketches, the habit is anchor division: take the mother anchors — zero, ninety, one eighty, two seventy, three sixty — and divide each by k. For sine three x: zero, thirty, sixty, ninety, one twenty, with the peak at thirty and height still one. Divide the anchors, never the heights.

***

**Bianca:** This one caused an actual argument: is cosine its own graph, or just sine moved along? And if they are the same thing, why do we learn both?

**Tutor:** Both sides of the argument hold a piece of the truth. Structurally, the class is right that they are one wave: shift sine ninety degrees to the left and it lands on cosine exactly — sine of x plus ninety equals cosine of x, every point, every angle. One recording, two starting positions. When an exam asks you to describe the transformation taking one to the other, that single sentence is the complete expected answer: cosine is sine shifted ninety degrees left — and mind the direction, because saying right instead of left is the common slip; check it with one point, since sine's peak at ninety must travel to cosine's peak at zero, which is a move to the left. So why keep two names? Convenience, and it is genuine. Cosine starts at a maximum, which makes it the natural language for anything that begins at its extreme; sine starts at zero climbing, natural for anything that begins mid-swing. Questions hand you waves in both postures, and recognising each on sight — sine crosses the origin climbing, cosine opens on a peak — is faster than re-deriving one from the other under time pressure. It also matters for reading sketches: a wave opening at its top with no horizontal shift is cosine, and writing it as shifted sine, while technically correct, invites bracket errors you did not need to risk. Same wave, two costumes; know the relationship for the theory mark, and know each costume on sight for speed.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the summit sketch, two sine of x minus thirty. Some did the stretch before the shift, some the shift first, and everyone wants to know if the order actually changes the answer.

**Tutor:** For this pair of controls, the honest answer is that the final graph comes out the same either way — the vertical stretch and the horizontal shift work on different axes, so they do not interfere. The reason I still insist on the fixed order — shape, shift, stretch, check — is about error rates, not mathematics. Watch what happens in each order. Shift first: you move clean, familiar anchors — the climb at zero goes to thirty, the peak at ninety goes to one twenty — small integers, easy arithmetic, and only then do you double heights that are still just one and minus one. Stretch first: now you are sliding a wave whose heights are already two and minus two, and in the middle of managing those numbers, learners reliably drift into shifting the heights or scaling the angles — cross-contamination between the axes. The fixed order quarantines each control: horizontal business finishes completely before vertical business starts. And the fourth step is not decoration. Play the first note: substitute x equals zero into the finished equation — two sine of minus thirty is two times minus a half, minus one — and demand that your curve cross the y-axis exactly there. That one substitution tests the shift direction, the stretch factor and your anchor arithmetic simultaneously, because almost any error in any of them moves the y-intercept. Ten seconds, and the sketch either certifies itself or confesses. Same picture either way, yes — but the four-step order is the version of you that makes the fewest mistakes, and that is the version the exam should meet.
