# Part 1 — Expert

In term one, sine, cosine and tangent were single numbers pinned to single angles. This session sets those numbers in motion: plotted against every angle they become waves that repeat without end, and the waves answer to the same control panel as the parabola and the exponential — a stretch, a lift, a slide — plus one control that is brand new, a squeeze on the wave itself. Trig graphs are read, sketched and reverse-engineered with a compact toolkit, and the marks they carry in your final exams reward exactly that toolkit, applied calmly.

## Subtopic: The Mother Graphs — Sine, Cosine and Tangent

Construct sine point by point across minus three sixty to three sixty degrees. Zero at zero degrees, climbing to one at ninety, back through zero at one eighty, down to minus one at two seventy, and returning home at three sixty. Joined smoothly, the shape is a wave. Its amplitude — measured from the middle line out to a peak, never peak to trough — is one. Its period — the width of one full repeat — is three hundred and sixty degrees, and the repetition runs identically in both directions forever.

Cosine is the same wave photographed at a different moment: it opens at its maximum. One at zero, zero at ninety, minus one at one eighty, zero at two seventy, one at three sixty. Amplitude one, period three sixty, identical shape — in fact, slide cosine ninety degrees right and it becomes sine, a coincidence that will be promoted to an exam question the moment horizontal shifts arrive.

Tangent refuses to be a wave. Zero at zero, one at forty-five, and as the angle nears ninety it detonates — the term one reason: the cosine underneath is heading to zero. The graph rockets towards a vertical asymptote at ninety, re-emerges from the depths just beyond ninety, passes through zero at one eighty, and repeats. Period: one hundred and eighty degrees, half of what the others need. Amplitude: none — each branch runs from bottomless to topless. Asymptotes stand at ninety plus every multiple of one eighty: on our window, minus two seventy, minus ninety, ninety, two seventy.

Sketching is anchor work. Sine and cosine each carry five anchors per period — zeros, maxima, minima; tangent carries its zero, its one at forty-five, and its walls. Plot the anchors first, then let the curve flow through them. That order — anchors, then curve — is the entire craft of the topic.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Anchor points, periods, and why only tangent owns asymptotes.

## Subtopic: Amplitude and Vertical Shift — the Work of a and q

The equation y equals a sine x plus q recycles two controls you already trust. The a multiplies every height, so the amplitude becomes the size of a. The q lifts the entire wave, moving the middle line from the x-axis up to height q.

Read y equals three sine x minus one as a work order. Stretch first: every height triples, so the bare wave would swing between three and minus three. Lower second: everything drops one unit. The finished wave swings between two and minus four around a middle line at minus one. Maximum two, where sine peaks — ninety degrees; minimum minus four, at two seventy. Range: minus four up to two, both ends included, because a wave genuinely touches its peaks — like the parabola at its turning point, unlike the exponential at its floor.

Two reflexes fall out, and they run in both directions. Amplitude equals half the total swing — maximum minus minimum, divided by two. Middle line equals the average of maximum and minimum — and the middle line is q. Exam setters love running the machine backwards: a sketched wave with maximum two and minimum minus four has middle line at the average, minus one, so q is minus one; total swing is two minus minus four, six, half of which is three — a is three. The equation three sine x minus one is recovered from the picture in two divisions.

A negative a turns the wave upside down: minus sine x leaves zero at zero but dives first instead of climbing. The flip relocates the maxima and minima — a favourite trick in questions — but never touches the amplitude, which is a distance and stays positive.

Stop for this section's questions now — half the swing gives a, the average height gives q, and watch the opening move of a flipped wave.

## Subtopic: Changing the Period — the Effect of k

The new control sits inside, multiplying the angle: y equals sine of k x. Heights are untouched; what changes is how much x the wave needs to finish one lap. Sine of three x completes a cycle when three x reaches three sixty — that is, by x equals one twenty: the period is squeezed to a third. Sine of x over two needs x to crawl all the way to seven twenty: the period doubles. The one-line rule: new period equals the mother period divided by the size of k — three sixty over k for sine and cosine, one eighty over k for tangent.

Run the standard trio. Y equals sine three x: period one twenty, so three complete waves occupy the space where one used to live. Y equals cosine of half x: period seven twenty — a single unhurried half-wave fills the whole standard window. Y equals tangent three x: period sixty, and the walls compress along with everything else — asymptotes at thirty plus every multiple of sixty. Asymptotes obey k exactly as anchor points do.

Sketching with k is division at the anchors: take the mother graph's anchor x-values and divide each by k. Sine three x rises through zero at zero, peaks at thirty, crosses at sixty, bottoms at ninety, closes at one twenty — the sine skeleton at one-third spacing. Compressed anchors first, smooth curve after.

Now the most profitable warning in the topic: k changes the period and nothing else. A wave showing three full cycles in three sixty degrees has k equal to three — and that fact says absolutely nothing about its height. Speed and size are separate dials. When a question shows a fast, short wave, read the cycles for k and the peaks for a, independently, because setters deliberately pair unusual combinations to catch the reflex that fast means tall.

The questions for this section arrive now — divide the period by k, compress the anchors, and leave every height exactly where it was.

## Subtopic: Horizontal Shifts and Combined Sketches

The last control slides the wave along the angle axis: y equals sine of x plus p. As everywhere in this course, the visible sign points backwards: x minus forty-five moves the wave forty-five right, x plus sixty moves it sixty left. Refuse the argument with the sign and ask the bracket-zero question instead: sine of x minus forty-five behaves at forty-five the way plain sine behaves at zero — so the wave begins its climb at forty-five.

Sketch y equals sine of x minus forty-five on zero to three sixty. Slide every anchor forty-five right: climbing zero at forty-five, maximum at one thirty-five, falling zero at two twenty-five, minimum at three fifteen. Whatever slides past the window's edge re-enters from the other side if the interval requires it — the wave has no first note and no last; the window is just where we choose to listen.

The co-function fact from term one now becomes something you can watch: cosine x equals sine of x plus ninety. Slide sine ninety to the left and every point lands exactly on cosine — the two mother graphs are one wave carrying two names. When an exam asks for the relationship, the full answer is a single sentence: cosine is sine shifted ninety degrees to the left.

Your syllabus combines at most two of these controls in one equation, so the summit sketch looks like y equals two sine of x minus thirty. Work the fixed order. Shape first: the sine skeleton. Shift second: every anchor thirty right — climbing zero at thirty, peak at one twenty, falling zero at two ten, trough at three hundred. Stretch third: double all heights, peaks at two, troughs at minus two. Then the closing safety check by calculation: at x equals zero, two sine of minus thirty is two times minus a half — minus one. The sketch must cross the y-axis at minus one, and if the drawing and the arithmetic disagree, trust the arithmetic and fix the drawing. Shape, shift, stretch, check: four steps that tame any two-control equation.

The final questions of this part are with you now — bracket-zero locates every shift, and the four-step order turns equations into clean sketches.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same waves through everyday machines — a heart monitor, a fast-forward button, and a song that starts late — same controls, same sketches.

## Subtopic: The Heartbeat on the Screen

Watch the monitor beside a hospital bed: rise, dip, rise, dip — one shape gliding past forever. Every trig graph is that: a repeating wave, and two measurements pin any wave down completely. How tall it swings — the amplitude, always measured from the calm middle line out to a peak, never from the bottom of a dip to the top of a spike. And how long one beat lasts — the period, the width of a single repeat.

Sine is the gentlest possible patient: middle line on the axis, amplitude one, period three sixty. Cosine is the same heartbeat with the recording started at a peak instead of mid-swing — identical pulse, different first frame. Tangent could never appear on a hospital screen: every one eighty degrees it bolts from infinitely low to infinitely high, slams against a wall it never touches — the asymptote — and restarts from the bottom. No peaks at all, so no amplitude; just walls, evenly spaced.

Two controls you already own return unchanged. Multiplying outside — the a — is the intensity dial: three sine x swings three times as high. Adding outside — the q — carries the whole monitor to a different shelf: three sine x minus one swings around a middle line at minus one, from two down to minus four. And reading a wave backwards is two questions asked of any picture: where is the calm middle — the average of top and bottom, which is q; and how far is a peak from that middle — half the total swing, which is a. Top two, bottom minus four: middle minus one, swing six, half-swing three. The picture said three sine x minus one, and all it took was two divisions.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Middle first, half-swing second, and remember tangent has walls where others have peaks.

## Subtopic: The Fast-Forward Button

The k inside the bracket is the fast-forward button. Sine of three x is the same song at triple speed: every note arrives in a third of the time, so a full verse squeezes into one twenty degrees instead of three sixty. Cosine of half x is slow motion — one verse stretched across seven twenty, so only half of it fits in the standard window. One line covers every case: new period equals old period divided by k. And the button is thorough — for tangent three x even the walls hurry closer, standing every sixty degrees with the first at thirty.

Here is what fast-forward can never do: change the volume. Triple the speed of a song and it is not louder — only quicker. Sine three x still swings exactly from one to minus one. This is the single most examined misunderstanding on trig graphs: cycles crammed into the window tell you k, and only the heights tell you a. Two dials, two readings, no crosstalk.

Counting cycles is the practical skill. Look at a window of three sixty degrees. One full wave: k is one. Three full waves: k is three — each wave one twenty wide. Half a wave: k is a half. So the complete reading recipe for any wave picture now has three steps: middle line gives q, half-swing gives a, cycles-per-window gives k — three knobs, read independently, in any order.

Your questions for this part are up now. Divide the period, count the waves in the window, and never let speed impersonate volume.

## Subtopic: The Same Song, Started Later

The final control changes nothing about the song itself — not the speed, not the volume, only when you press play. Sine of x minus forty-five is the same track started forty-five degrees late: every rise, peak and dip shows up forty-five to the right. Sine of x plus sixty was started sixty early, so everything sits left. The bracket's sign will keep trying to trick you, so stop reading it: ask where the bracket equals zero. At x equals forty-five the late song is at its opening note, so the wave starts climbing at forty-five. That one question settles every shift, in every chapter of this course.

One pair of songs turns out to be the same recording. Start sine ninety degrees early — sine of x plus ninety — and listen to it open at a peak, fall through zero, bottom out, and return. That is cosine, note for note. Cosine was never a separate song; it is sine with an early start, and when a question asks how the two graphs relate, that sentence — cosine is sine shifted ninety degrees left — is the whole expected answer.

The summit sketch turns two knobs, in the calm fixed order. Two sine of x minus thirty: hum the sine skeleton first; start it thirty late — climbing zero at thirty, peak at one twenty, falling zero at two ten, trough at three hundred; then raise the intensity to two — peaks at two, troughs at minus two. Close with the musician's check: play the opening note. At x equals zero the equation says two sine of minus thirty — two times minus a half — minus one. Your curve must cut the y-axis at minus one. If the sketch says otherwise, the sketch is wrong and you caught it yourself, which is exactly the point of the check.

Volume, shelf, speed, start time — four knobs, and every wave question you will meet is two of them turned at once. Read them one at a time, in the fixed order, and no graph can tangle you again.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: bracket-zero finds the start time, cosine is sine started early, and always play the first note before you hand the sketch in.
