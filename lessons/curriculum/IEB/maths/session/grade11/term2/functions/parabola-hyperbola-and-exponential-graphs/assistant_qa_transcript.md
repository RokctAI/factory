**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the eternal one: why does x plus three in the bracket move the graph LEFT? Half the class still feels the sign is lying to them.

**Tutor:** The sign is not lying — it is answering a question nobody asked. Here is the honest way to see it. The basic tent y equals x squared has its lowest point where the squared thing is zero: at x equals zero. Now write y equals x plus three, all squared. The lowest point still happens where the squared thing is zero — that has not changed — but the squared thing is now x plus three, and x plus three is zero when x equals minus three. So the low point has relocated to minus three: three units left. The bracket is not telling you which way the graph moves; it is telling you which x now plays the role that zero used to play. That is why the reliable habit is bracket-zero: ignore the sign entirely and solve the little equation bracket equals zero. X plus three: zero at minus three, so left. X minus three: zero at plus three, so right. This one habit transfers everywhere — the hyperbola's vertical asymptote sits where its denominator bracket is zero, and the exponential's interesting behaviour is anchored the same way. One warning while we are here: the shift only reads cleanly when the bracket's x has coefficient one. If you meet something like two x plus six inside, factor it first — two times x plus three — and read the shift from the factored bracket. Bracket-zero, always, and the sign never fools you again.

**Bianca:** Next: domain and range. A learner wrote 'domain: all real x' for the hyperbola and lost the mark, but the same sentence was correct for the exponential. What is the system here?

**Tutor:** The system is: domain and range are lists of what the formula cannot produce, and each family has different incapacities. Interrogate the formula, not your memory. For the hyperbola, four over x plus one, minus two: ask first, is there an x I cannot feed in? Yes — x equals minus one would demand division by zero, so the domain must carry that exception: all real x except minus one. Then ask, is there a y the formula cannot output? Yes — the fraction part is never exactly zero, so y never equals exactly minus two: all real y except minus two. For the exponential, four times two to the x minus one, minus eight: any x can be fed in — powers accept everything — so the domain genuinely is all real x, and the sentence that failed for the hyperbola succeeds here. But the output side has a wall: two to any power is strictly positive, so the graph lives strictly above minus eight, and the range is y strictly greater than minus eight. And the parabola completes the pattern from the other side: domain all real x, range y greater than or equal to the turning point's y — equal to, because the parabola actually reaches its minimum. So the writing rules are: exceptions for the hyperbola, a strict inequality for the exponential, an inclusive inequality for the parabola. Not because a marker is fussy, but because each of those sentences describes a genuinely different set of points — write the wrong one and you have described the wrong graph.

**Bianca:** Someone asked about the x-intercept of the exponential: they got to four times two to the power x minus one equals eight and froze. What is the intended finish?

**Tutor:** The intended finish is the common-base technique from term one — the graph section is quietly recycling your equation-solving. From four times two to the power x minus one equals eight, first isolate the power: divide both sides by four, leaving two to the power x minus one equals two. Now write both sides as powers of the same base. The right side is two to the power one. With the bases matched, the exponents must match: x minus one equals one, so x equals two. Done — and notice the answer checks instantly: four times two to the one is eight, minus eight is zero. Two habits protect this calculation. First, isolate before matching: dividing by the four must happen before you compare exponents, because the four is not part of the power — learners who try to absorb it into the exponent invent equations that were never there. Second, if the numbers had not cooperated — say the right side were ten — the common-base route would stall, and at grade eleven that is usually the sign of an arithmetic slip earlier, so recheck rather than force it. Expect the setters to choose numbers that resolve to clean common bases: powers of two, three, five. When you see four, eight, sixteen in an exponential question, the equation is begging to be rewritten in base two. Oblige it.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the shrinking-interval table, a learner asks — if the averages just get closer and closer to four but never equal it, how can we say the gradient AT the point IS four?

**Tutor:** That is not a beginner's confusion — that is the exact question that took mathematicians two centuries to answer properly, so respect it. Here is the grade-eleven honest version. The averages five, four and a half, four comma one are measurements over intervals, and every interval, however small, still smears the curve's changing steepness into one number. So no single average equals the steepness at the point — correct. But watch what the sequence does: as the interval shrinks, the averages do not wander; they home in on one specific value, four, and they can be pushed as close to four as anyone demands by taking the gate close enough. Closer than a tenth? Take the gate at two comma zero one. Closer than a thousandth? Closer still. No number other than four survives that squeeze. The gradient at the point is defined as that survivor — the unique number the averages corner. That is what the word limit means: not the last entry of the table, but the value the table pins down beyond escape. Grade twelve will give this a name, the derivative, and a whole algebra for computing it without tables. For now, hold two things. Computationally: be able to build the table — substitute, subtract, divide, and read the trend. Conceptually: the instantaneous gradient is not one of the averages; it is the number they besiege. If that idea sits comfortably now, the first weeks of calculus next year will feel like revision instead of revelation.
