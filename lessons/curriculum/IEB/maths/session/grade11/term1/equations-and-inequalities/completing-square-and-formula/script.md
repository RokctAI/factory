# Part 1 — Expert

Factorising is a wonderful method with a fatal weakness: it only works when tidy factors exist, and most quadratics refuse to supply them. Completing the square has no such weakness. It solves every quadratic ever written, and as a bonus it rewrites the expression into a shape that hands you the turning point of the graph without any further work. In this session we build the technique from the perfect-square pattern, extend it to quadratics whose leading coefficient is not one, use it once on the general equation to produce the quadratic formula, and finish with the sign discipline and rounding discipline that turn formula questions into reliable marks.

## Subtopic: Building a Perfect Square from an Incomplete One

Start with the pattern everything rests on. Expand x plus k, all squared, and you get x squared plus two k x plus k squared. Read the answer from right to left and it becomes an instruction: in a perfect square trinomial, the constant term is the square of half the coefficient of x. Half, then square. That order never changes.

Try it on x squared plus eight x. Half of eight is four; four squared is sixteen. So x squared plus eight x plus sixteen is a perfect square, namely x plus four, all squared. Try x squared minus twelve x. Half of minus twelve is minus six; minus six squared is thirty-six. So the completed square is x minus six, all squared. Keep one detail straight: the number that appears inside the bracket is the half, not the square. Four goes in the bracket; sixteen is what you added.

Now put the pattern to work on an equation that will not factorise. Solve x squared plus eight x plus three equals zero. No pair of whole numbers multiplies to three and adds to eight, so factorising is off the table. Move the constant across: x squared plus eight x equals minus three. Half of eight is four, four squared is sixteen, so add sixteen to both sides — both sides, because an equation stays true only if you treat its two sides identically. The left side becomes x plus four, all squared. The right side becomes thirteen.

Square-root both sides, and this is the step where a whole solution goes missing if you rush. A squared bracket equal to thirteen means the bracket itself is plus root thirteen or minus root thirteen, because a negative number squares to a positive too. Write x plus four equals plus or minus root thirteen. Then x equals minus four plus root thirteen, or minus four minus root thirteen. As decimals those sit near minus nought comma three nine and minus seven comma six one, but the surd form is the exact answer, and exact is what you give unless the question says otherwise.

The identical algebra, done to an expression instead of an equation, produces the graph form. Rewrite x squared plus eight x plus three by adding sixteen and immediately subtracting it: x plus four, all squared, minus sixteen, plus three, which is x plus four, all squared, minus thirteen. The turning point of the parabola reads off directly: minus four, minus thirteen.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test whether you square the half or halve the square, and whether the plus-or-minus survived your square root.

## Subtopic: Completing the Square when a Is Not One

Halving the middle coefficient only builds the correct bracket when the coefficient of x squared is one. So when it is not one, make it one. For an equation, divide every term by the leading coefficient. Dividing an equation by a nonzero constant is always legitimate; the only division that is ever dangerous is division by a variable, because a variable might be zero.

Solve three x squared minus twelve x plus two equals zero. Divide each of the three terms by three: x squared minus four x plus two thirds equals zero. Move the constant: x squared minus four x equals minus two thirds. Half of minus four is minus two; squared, that is four. Add four to both sides. The left side collapses into x minus two, all squared. The right side is four minus two thirds, which is ten thirds.

So x minus two, all squared, equals ten thirds. Root both sides, keeping the plus or minus: x minus two equals plus or minus the square root of ten thirds. Tidy the surd — root ten over root three multiplies up to root thirty over three, because a surd is never left sitting in a denominator. Therefore x equals two plus root thirty over three, or two minus root thirty over three. Correct to two decimal places, that is three comma eight three or nought comma one seven.

An expression is a different animal, because it has no second side to balance against. If you divide y equals three x squared minus twelve x plus two by three, you have manufactured a different expression, a third of the size. Instead, factor the three out of the first two terms only: y equals three, times the bracket x squared minus four x, plus two. Complete the square inside the bracket: x squared minus four x is x minus two, all squared, minus four. So y equals three, times the bracket x minus two all squared minus four, plus two. Now the step that separates full marks from a wrong turning point: the three multiplies everything inside its bracket, the minus four included. That gives three times x minus two all squared, minus twelve, plus two — which is three times x minus two all squared, minus ten. Turning point: two, minus ten. Substitute x equals two into the original to confirm: twelve minus twenty-four plus two is indeed minus ten.

The trap in this subtopic has one shape: the leading coefficient gets multiplied onto the squared bracket and forgotten on the constant beside it. Guard that distribution and the method is safe.

Pause for this section's questions now — divide through when you are solving, factor out when you are rewriting, and let the leading coefficient reach every term inside the bracket.

## Subtopic: Deriving the Quadratic Formula

Here is the honest truth about the quadratic formula: it contains no new mathematics at all. It is completing the square carried out once, on the completely general quadratic, so that the work never has to be repeated. The derivation is regularly asked as a question in its own right, so treat these six lines as content to know, not decoration.

Begin with a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero, where a is not zero. Line one: divide every term by a, giving x squared plus b over a, times x, plus c over a, equals zero. Line two: move the constant across, giving x squared plus b over a, times x, equals minus c over a.

Line three is the completion itself. Half of b over a is b over two a. Its square is b squared over four a squared. Add that quantity to both sides. Line four: the left side is now a perfect square, x plus b over two a, all squared, while the right side is b squared over four a squared minus c over a. Put the right side over the single denominator four a squared: it becomes b squared minus four a c, all over four a squared.

Line five: take the square root of both sides, with the plus or minus. The left side gives x plus b over two a. The right side gives plus or minus root of b squared minus four a c, over two a, since the square root of four a squared is two a. Line six: subtract b over two a from both sides and write the result over the common denominator. x equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a.

Two features of that final line carry most of the marks. First, the fraction bar belongs to the entire numerator: two a divides the minus b just as much as it divides the root. Second, the expression under the root, b squared minus four a c, is called the discriminant, and it alone determines how many real solutions exist — a job so important it gets its own lesson.

The questions on this section are in front of you now. The derivation runs: divide by a, shift the constant, add the square of half, root with plus or minus, subtract, combine. Six lines, in order.

## Subtopic: Using the Formula — Exact Values and Rounding

Formula questions are lost to small habits, not big misunderstandings, so build three habits that hold under pressure.

Habit one: standard form first, then list a, b and c with their signs attached, before anything is substituted. Solve two x squared minus nine x plus three equals zero. Here a is two, b is minus nine, c is three. So minus b is plus nine — the negative b flips positive on entry into the formula, and that flip is the single most common slip in this topic. The discriminant is minus nine squared, minus four times two times three: eighty-one minus twenty-four, which is fifty-seven. Therefore x equals nine plus or minus root fifty-seven, all over four.

That is the exact answer. If the instruction reads leave your answer in simplest surd form, you are finished. If it reads correct to two decimal places, carry on: root fifty-seven is roughly seven comma five five, so the two roots are about four comma one four and nought comma three six.

Habit two: round once, at the end. Watch what early rounding does here. A learner who calls root fifty-seven simply seven comma five and then divides gets four comma one three and nought comma three eight — both second decimals wrong, both accuracy marks gone. Keep the full calculator value on screen all the way through and press round only on the final line.

Habit three: clear a negative leading coefficient before you start. Solve minus three x squared plus seven x plus six equals zero. Multiply the whole equation by minus one: three x squared minus seven x minus six equals zero. Now a is three, b is minus seven, c is minus six. The discriminant is forty-nine plus seventy-two, which is one hundred and twenty-one — eleven squared, a perfect square. So x equals seven plus or minus eleven, all over six, which gives three, or minus two thirds. And that perfect-square discriminant is a message worth reading: the quadratic would have factorised, as three x plus two, times x minus three, and factorising would have been the faster road.

So the method choice is a decision, not a reflex. Perfect-square discriminant: factorise. Anything else: formula. Question demands the turning point or names the method: complete the square.

The final questions of this part are with you now — signs listed before substituting, the whole numerator over two a, and one rounding, at the very end.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same mathematics now, but built out of pavers, a recipe card, and a plank of wood — the pictures that make the algebra feel inevitable.

## Subtopic: Paving the Corner of the Yard

Picture your family paving a square patio, x metres by x metres. A delivery arrives with extra pavers — enough to cover eight x more square metres — and the plan is for the patio to end up a perfect square again, just bigger.

The sensible move is to split the extra pavers into two equal strips, each four metres wide: one strip laid along the right edge, one along the bottom. Now step back and look at the shape. It is an L. Very nearly a big square of side x plus four — except for one empty corner where the two strips fail to meet. That gap is exactly four by four: sixteen square metres. Sixteen more square metres of paving, and the patio is a flawless square, x plus four on each side.

That gap in the corner is completing the square. The eight x was halved into two strips of four; the missing corner is that half, squared. Halve the middle number, square it, and you have named the missing piece. It works every single time, for exactly the reason the picture shows.

Now the honest bookkeeping. When the pavers belong to an equation, adding sixteen to one side without adding sixteen to the other breaks the equals sign. So x squared plus eight x equals minus three becomes x squared plus eight x plus sixteen equals thirteen, and the left side is now the tidy square: x plus four, all squared, equals thirteen.

One more step, and it is the one that quietly decides a mark. If a square equals thirteen, its side might be root thirteen — or minus root thirteen, since a negative times itself is positive too. So the plus or minus is not decoration; it is half your answer. x equals minus four plus root thirteen, or minus four minus root thirteen. Two answers, as a quadratic almost always has.

And if the equation opens with three x squared rather than x squared, divide everything by three first, so the picture of a single square patio applies again.

Quick check before we move on — the questions on this section are reaching you now. Halve the middle, square it, add it to both sides, and never drop the plus or minus.

## Subtopic: The Recipe Someone Already Cooked

Think of a family bread recipe. The first person to bake it had to experiment — too much yeast, too little salt, oven too hot — until it came out right. Then they wrote the final version on a card, and now nobody in the family ever experiments again; they read the card. The quadratic formula is that card. Someone completed the square on the general equation once, wrote down the result, and left it for every learner who came after.

The card reads: x equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a. And the phrase all over two a is doing serious work. The two a sits underneath the entire top line, the minus b included. Divide only the root by two a and you have followed half the recipe — the bread does not rise.

Using the card is mostly a reading exercise. Get the equation into the shape something equals zero, then copy out three labelled values, signs and all. For two x squared minus nine x plus three equals zero, a is two, b is minus nine, c is three. Now watch b closely. Minus b means minus of minus nine, which is plus nine. And b squared means minus nine times minus nine, which is plus eighty-one. Two negatives, two chances to slip — this is the slow-down moment of the whole calculation.

Under the root: eighty-one minus four times two times three, which is eighty-one minus twenty-four, fifty-seven. So x equals nine plus or minus root fifty-seven, all over four. Done — the card never fails.

There is even a free gift in the middle of the recipe. That number under the root, fifty-seven here, speaks before you finish. Had it come out a perfect square, like one hundred and twenty-one, the quadratic would have factorised, and factorising would have been quicker. So one glance at the discriminant tells you whether you chose the best method — and teaches you to choose better next time.

Your questions for this part are up now. Read off a, b and c with their signs, let the fraction bar carry the whole top line, and treat a perfect square under the root as a hint you could have factorised.

## Subtopic: Exact or Rounded — Say Which

One last idea, about the difference between an exact answer and a practical one.

Suppose you cut a one-metre plank into three equal shelves. Exactly, each shelf is one third of a metre long. But no tape measure shows a third of a metre; the closest you can mark is thirty-three comma three centimetres, and a tiny sliver of wood is unaccounted for. Both answers are honest — one third is exact, thirty-three comma three is practical — and mathematics simply asks you to say which one you are giving.

For quadratics, the exact answer is the one with the root sign still standing: two plus root thirty over three, or nine plus or minus root fifty-seven over four. Nothing has been lost or blurred in that form. When a question says leave your answer in simplest surd form, or give the exact value, that surd form is the required answer — and reaching for the calculator there actually costs you, because the decimal is an approximation of what you were asked for.

When a question says correct to two decimal places, it wants the practical answer, but only converted at the finish line. Here is the reason that timing matters. Root fifty-seven is seven comma five four nine eight. Round it early to seven comma five, divide by four, and your answer lands on four comma one three — but the true answer, rounded at the end, is four comma one four. One premature rounding, one wrong digit, one lost mark. Think of rounding like laminating a page: do it last, because nothing can be corrected afterwards.

And keep the method decision deliberate, because it saves minutes in every paper. Quadratic factorises easily: factorise, since it is fastest and exact. It refuses: formula, since it never fails. The question wants a turning point, or instructs you to complete the square: use the paving method, because it is the only one that delivers the turning point directly.

Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: choose the method on purpose, keep the surd when exact is asked, and round exactly once, at the very end.
