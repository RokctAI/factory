Hey, it's Bianca — completing the square looks like the scariest topic in the term, I know. Fractions everywhere, a plus-or-minus that appears from nowhere, and then a formula made entirely of letters. Here is the secret: the whole session contains exactly one new instruction, and the rest is arithmetic you have done since primary school.

The instruction: halve the coefficient of x, then square it, then add it. That is the missing corner in the paving picture, and it never changes shape. Eight x gives you four, then sixteen. Minus twelve x gives you minus six, then thirty-six. Say five of these out loud tonight while you brush your teeth, and tomorrow the method will feel like yours.

Two guards to post. Guard one: whatever you add to the left side of an equation gets added to the right side too, or the equals sign stops meaning anything. Guard two: when you square-root both sides at the end, write plus or minus. A square equal to thirteen has two possible sides, and leaving out the negative one throws away half the answer and the mark attached to it.

If your equation starts with three x squared instead of x squared, divide every term by three before anything else. Dividing by a number is always safe; it is only dividing by a variable that is off limits, because a variable could secretly be zero.

And the formula? It is not extra content to cram — it is this exact method, performed once on the general equation and written down for reuse. If you can complete the square, you can rebuild the formula from nothing, which is a superpower on a blank-mind day. When you use it, write a, b and c down with their signs before touching the calculator, and round only once, on the final line.

Halve, square, add to both sides, plus or minus. Signs first, round last. That is the entire lesson. Now go try the practice questions — you are more ready than you feel!
