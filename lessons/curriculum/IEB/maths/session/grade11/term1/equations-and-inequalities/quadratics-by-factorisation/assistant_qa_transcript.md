**Bianca:** Time for class questions! First up: someone solved a bracket equation, got two answers, checked them, and both were wrong. The equation was a product equal to ten. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly they set each bracket equal to ten, and that step is only legal when the product equals zero. The zero-product principle — a product is zero only when a factor is zero — is a special property of zero and of no other number. A product of ten pins down neither factor: ten is one times ten, two times five, twenty times a half, minus two times minus five, endlessly. So with something like x plus one, times x minus four, equals ten or equals six, the route is fixed: expand the brackets, bring the ten or the six across, reach standard form with zero on the right, and only then factorise and split into cases. Take the version equal to six: expanding gives x squared minus three x minus four equals six, so x squared minus three x minus ten equals zero, which factorises as x minus five, times x plus two, and the answers five and minus two both check in the original. The habit to build is a one-second scan before splitting any brackets: is the right-hand side genuinely zero? If it is not, you have algebra to do first.

**Bianca:** Next question: in the homework, x squared equals seven x, half the class got only x equals seven. Why is dividing by x such a big deal if it gives a right answer?

**Tutor:** Because it gives you A right answer while destroying THE other one, and it does so invisibly — nothing on your page looks wrong afterwards. Dividing an equation by x means dividing by a quantity that might be zero, and division by zero is not defined, so the step is only valid on the assumption that x is not zero. You have silently thrown away exactly the case where x is zero — which here happens to be a genuine solution, since zero squared equals seven times zero. The safe procedure never divides by any expression containing the variable: bring everything to one side, x squared minus seven x equals zero, factorise the x out, x times x minus seven equals zero, and now the zero-product principle hands you both answers, zero and seven, with full working shown. The same discipline matters later in the term: an equation like x squared minus five x equals zero, or any common-factor shape, always carries zero as one of its solutions, and the learners who divide will drop it every single time. One more safeguard: a quadratic can have at most two solutions, so if a division or a squaring step somewhere leaves you with one, or magically three, audit your steps for an illegal move.

**Bianca:** Here's one about the fraction equations: why do restrictions have to be written before we solve? A few people wrote them at the end and still got the right answers.

**Tutor:** They got lucky, because in that particular question no solution collided with a restriction. The restriction is not decoration; it records the values of x that make a denominator zero, and at those values the original equation does not even exist as a statement, so they can never be answers no matter what the algebra later says. Writing them first does two jobs. Practically, it arms you before the danger arrives: when your factorised quadratic offers you a solution that sits on the banned list, you reject it immediately and say why, and that rejection line is itself credited. Logically, it matters because the very first algebraic move — multiplying through by the lowest common denominator — is only valid when that denominator is not zero. The restriction is the licence for the multiplication, so it must be issued before the multiplication happens. In an equation with denominators x and x plus three, you write x not equal to zero and x not equal to minus three at the top, multiply every term by x times x plus three, solve the quadratic that appears, and screen both candidates against the list. If ever a candidate fails the screening, the correct final answer keeps only the survivor — and shows the rejected one, crossed out, with its reason.

**Bianca:** Last question tonight: with the simultaneous ones, the linear and the quadratic together, why must the final answers be in pairs? Someone lost marks despite having all four numbers right.

**Tutor:** Because the pairing is part of the mathematics, not part of the presentation. A system of two equations in x and y is not asking which x values are possible and which y values are possible; it is asking which POINTS satisfy both equations at once — which combinations. Solving x plus y equals seven with x squared plus y squared equals twenty-five gives x equals three or four, and the partner of each comes from the linear equation: three pairs with four, and four pairs with three. Write them as ordered pairs, three with four, and four with three. The learner who writes x equals three or four, and y equals three or four, has asserted four combinations — including three with three and four with four, which fail the first equation instantly. Right numbers, false claim, marks gone. Two working habits protect you. Always substitute back into the linear equation to find each partner, because it is one subtraction with nothing to go wrong, whereas substituting into the quadratic invites arithmetic slips and extra phantom answers. And if the geometry helps you: the linear equation is a line, the quadratic here is a circle, and your two pairs are the two crossing points — each crossing point is one x with its own y, welded together.
