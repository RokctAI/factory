Hey, it's Bianca — if factorising quadratics feels like a bag of unrelated tricks, take a breath, because the whole topic hangs off one single idea: zero talks. A product can only be zero if one of its factors is zero. That is it. Every rule in the lesson exists to put you in a position to use that one sentence.

Standard form? That is just making sure there is actually a zero on the right before you ask the brackets to confess. If the right-hand side says six or ten, the brackets tell you nothing yet — expand, move everything across, and make it zero first.

The four factorising shapes are not four new topics. Common factor, simple trinomial, split-the-middle, difference of squares — you met all of them last year. The only new muscle is choosing which shape you are looking at, and that comes from doing five or six mixed examples, not from staring at the theory. Try that tonight: pick any six, and before solving each one, just NAME its shape out loud.

Two tiny guards will save you the most marks. Never divide an equation by x — factorise the x out instead, and let it hand you the answer zero honestly. And when a squared thing equals a positive number, there are always two roots, the plus one and the minus one. Say plus or minus every time you square-root.

For the fraction ones, write the banned values down before you do anything — the x values that would make a denominator zero. It takes ten seconds and it is the difference between a full answer and a trap.

And the fancy-looking ones with a repeated chunk? Give the chunk a nickname, solve the small quadratic, then swap the real thing back in. Nicknames make giants small.

Zero talks, name the shape, never divide by x, plus or minus, banned values first. Five sentences and you own this lesson. The practice questions are waiting — go show them who's boss!
