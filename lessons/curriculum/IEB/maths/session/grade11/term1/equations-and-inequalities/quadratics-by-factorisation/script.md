# Part 1 — Expert

A quadratic equation is any equation that can be rearranged into the shape a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero, with a not zero. Of the three solving methods, factorisation comes first: whenever the expression will factorise, it is the quickest route and it delivers exact answers. This session anchors the principle that makes the method legal, works through every factorising shape the grade expects, and then stretches the same principle to cover equations with the unknown in a denominator, equations with a repeated expression hiding inside them, and a pair of simultaneous equations where one is linear and one is quadratic.

## Subtopic: Standard Form and the Zero-Product Principle

Every factorisation solution opens with the same move: bring every term to one side so that the other side reads zero. Not six, not ten — zero. There is a genuine mathematical reason, and it is a property no other number has.

The zero-product principle says that if a product A times B equals zero, then A is zero or B is zero. No third possibility exists, because two non-zero numbers can never multiply to zero. Now contrast that with a product equal to six. From A times B equals six you can conclude nothing about A. It could be one times six, or three times two, or twelve times a half, or minus one times minus six. Six keeps every option open; zero closes them all but two. That is why the equation must equal zero before any bracket may be set equal to anything.

Watch the principle abused. Solve x plus one, times x minus four, equals six. The tempting, wrong line is x plus one equals six. There is no zero on the right, so the principle does not apply, and both resulting answers will fail. The correct route is to expand: x squared minus three x minus four equals six, so x squared minus three x minus ten equals zero, which factorises to x minus five, times x plus two. The solutions are five and minus two, and both survive checking: six times one is six, and minus one times minus six is six.

A second abuse deletes an answer without leaving a trace. Solve x squared equals seven x. Divide both sides by x and you get x equals seven — but x equals zero also satisfies the original equation, and the division erased it. Dividing by a variable is dividing by something that might be zero, and that is never allowed. The legal version: x squared minus seven x equals zero, take out the common factor, x times x minus seven equals zero, so x equals zero or x equals seven. Two answers, both earned.

Make it a permanent rule: never divide an equation by a variable expression. Factorise the variable out instead and let it deliver its own solution.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Expect a product set equal to a number that is not zero, and expect at least one equation begging you to divide by x.

## Subtopic: Factorising Every Shape a Quadratic Takes

Once the right-hand side is zero, the job is factorising, and grade eleven quadratics arrive in four recognisable shapes.

Shape one, a common factor only. Three x squared minus fifteen x equals zero becomes three x, times x minus five, equals zero, so x equals zero or x equals five. Hunt for a common factor first in every single question; whatever remains after you remove it is always simpler.

Shape two, a trinomial whose leading coefficient is one. Solve x squared minus two x minus fifteen equals zero. You need two numbers that multiply to minus fifteen and add to minus two: minus five and three. The factors are x minus five, times x plus three, and the solutions are five and minus three.

Shape three, a trinomial whose leading coefficient is not one. Solve two x squared plus seven x minus four equals zero. Multiply a by c: two times minus four is minus eight. Find two numbers multiplying to minus eight and adding to seven: eight and minus one. Split the middle term: two x squared plus eight x, minus x minus four. Group the pairs: two x, times x plus four, minus one, times x plus four. Factor out the shared bracket: two x minus one, times x plus four. So x equals one half, or x equals minus four.

Shape four, a difference of two squares. Solve nine x squared minus forty-nine equals zero. It splits into three x minus seven, times three x plus seven, so x equals seven thirds or minus seven thirds. The same shape often arrives disguised, as in twenty-five x squared equals four, and the danger is writing x equals two fifths and stopping. A squared quantity equal to a positive number always produces two values — plus two fifths and minus two fifths. Dropping the negative root is one of the cheapest marks lost in the whole topic.

Finish with a habit of presentation. After factorising, set EACH bracket equal to zero on its own line and solve it separately — those lines carry marks of their own. And remember the ceiling: a quadratic has at most two solutions. If your page shows three, one of them was smuggled in by an illegal step.

Stop for this section's questions now — common factor out first, split the middle term when the leading coefficient is not one, and never surrender the negative square root.

## Subtopic: Equations with Fractions — and the Restriction Rule

When x lives in a denominator, the equation is not yet a quadratic; it becomes one only after the fractions are cleared. The procedure has a fixed order, and its first step involves no algebra at all.

Step one: write down the restrictions. Inspect every denominator and record the x values that would make it zero, because division by zero is meaningless and those values are disqualified before the race starts. Doing this first is the whole protection.

Step two: multiply every term on both sides by the lowest common denominator. Step three: rearrange into standard form and factorise. Step four: test each solution against the restriction list and reject any that appear on it.

First worked example. Solve x plus ten over x equals seven. The only denominator is x, so the restriction is x not equal to zero. Multiply every term by x: x squared plus ten equals seven x. Standard form: x squared minus seven x plus ten equals zero. Factorise: x minus two, times x minus five. So x equals two or x equals five; neither is zero, so both stand. Check x equals two: two plus ten over two is two plus five, which is seven. It balances.

Second worked example, two denominators this time. Solve two over x, plus two over x plus three, equals one. Restrictions: x not equal to zero, and x not equal to minus three. The lowest common denominator is x times x plus three. Multiply every term by it: two times x plus three, plus two x, equals x times x plus three. Expand: two x plus six, plus two x, equals x squared plus three x, so four x plus six equals x squared plus three x. Standard form: x squared minus x minus six equals zero, factorising to x minus three, times x plus two. So x equals three or x equals minus two, and neither is on the banned list.

Verify x equals three in the original: two thirds plus two sixths is two thirds plus one third, which is one. Verify x equals minus two: two over minus two is minus one, and two over one is two, and minus one plus two is one. Both answers are genuine.

The trap here is arithmetic rather than concept: when you multiply through by the lowest common denominator, EVERY term gets multiplied, including the lonely constant on the right-hand side. Leave that one untouched and every line below it is wrong.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — restrictions before anything else, the lowest common denominator onto every single term, and each answer checked against the banned list before it earns a place on your page.

## Subtopic: Substitution, and One Linear with One Quadratic

Two of the harder question types collapse back into work you have already mastered.

The first is the equation with a repeated expression. Solve the quantity x squared minus two x, all squared, minus eleven times the quantity x squared minus two x, plus twenty-four equals zero. Expanding would create a fourth-degree monster. Look instead for the chunk that appears twice: x squared minus two x, once squared and once plain. Let k stand for x squared minus two x. The equation shrinks to k squared minus eleven k plus twenty-four equals zero, which factorises to k minus eight, times k minus three. So k equals eight or k equals three.

Now undo the substitution — and concentrate, because this is where solutions are abandoned. Each value of k spawns its own quadratic. From k equals eight: x squared minus two x minus eight equals zero, factorising to x minus four, times x plus two, so x equals four or minus two. From k equals three: x squared minus two x minus three equals zero, factorising to x minus three, times x plus one, so x equals three or minus one. Four solutions in all: minus two, minus one, three and four. Stopping at k equals eight or three answers a question nobody asked.

The second type pairs a linear equation with a quadratic one. Solve x plus y equals seven together with x squared plus y squared equals twenty-five. The method is substitution, and the equation you rearrange is not a matter of taste: rearrange the LINEAR one, because it gives a clean expression free of squares. From the first equation, y equals seven minus x. Substitute into the second: x squared plus the quantity seven minus x, all squared, equals twenty-five. Expand that bracket with care: forty-nine minus fourteen x plus x squared. So two x squared minus fourteen x plus forty-nine equals twenty-five, which tidies to two x squared minus fourteen x plus twenty-four equals zero. Divide through by two — safe, because two is a constant: x squared minus seven x plus twelve equals zero, factorising to x minus three, times x minus four. So x equals three or x equals four.

Finding each partner comes next, and you substitute back into the LINEAR equation, where the arithmetic is short and nothing can go wrong. When x equals three, y equals four. When x equals four, y equals three. Present the results as ordered pairs — three with four, or four with three — because the pairing itself is what gets marked. Writing x equals three or four and y equals three or four claims four combinations, and two of them are false: three with three fails the first equation, and four with four fails it too.

The final questions of this part are with you now — nickname the repeated chunk before expanding anything, rearrange the linear equation and never the quadratic one, and let every solution arrive holding its partner.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same equations again, but told through a bake sale, a lift-club bill and a pair of gloves — same steps, same answers, with the reasons out in the open.

## Subtopic: Zero Is the Only Number That Talks

Two classmates run a koeksister table at the school market day. At the end you are told one fact: their two takings, multiplied together, come to zero. You immediately know something solid — at least one of them sold absolutely nothing. Multiplication has no other way to make zero.

Change the fact: their takings multiplied together come to six hundred rand. Now what do you know about the first classmate? Nothing whatsoever. She could have made six hundred while he made one, or thirty while he made twenty, or ten while he made sixty. Every split works. Six hundred keeps secrets; zero confesses.

That is the entire reason for standard form. A product equal to zero forces one of its factors to be zero; a product equal to anything else forces nothing. So faced with x plus one, times x minus four, equals six, you may not write x plus one equals six. Expand, gather, and bring it to x squared minus three x minus ten equals zero. Factorise into x minus five, times x plus two — and now, with a zero on the right, one bracket must be empty. The answers are five and minus two.

A second habit protects a whole solution. Never divide an equation by x. Consider x squared equals seven x. Divide by x and you get seven, one correct answer — but zero was also correct, and the division wiped it off the page. It is like rubbing a learner's name off the class register before roll call: absent or not, they will never be counted. The honest version moves everything across — x squared minus seven x equals zero — then takes x out as a factor: x times x minus seven equals zero. The x standing alone is now a bracket in its own right, and it hands you the answer zero, in public.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this are coming to you right now. For each, ask first whether the right-hand side is genuinely zero, and second, whether any solution got divided away.

## Subtopic: Clear the Fractions Before You Fight

Think of a lift club settling the month's petrol bill. Everyone's share is written as a fraction of the total — a quarter here, a sixth there — and nobody can see who owes what until someone converts the fractions into plain rands. Equations behave the same way: while x sits underneath a fraction line, the equation is unreadable. So the first fight is against the fractions, not the algebra.

The weapon is the lowest common denominator, and the rule of engagement is that EVERY term gets multiplied by it — including the plain number sitting alone on the other side of the equals sign. That lonely number is the one everybody forgets, and forgetting it poisons every line that follows.

Take x plus ten over x equals seven. One denominator, x. Multiply everything by x: x times x is x squared, ten over x times x leaves ten, and seven times x is seven x. So x squared plus ten equals seven x, which rearranges to x squared minus seven x plus ten equals zero and opens into x minus two, times x minus five. The answers are two and five, and testing two gives two plus five, which is seven. The bill balances.

Now the banned values. Before touching anything, look at each denominator and ask what would make it zero. If the denominators are x, and x plus three, then x may not be zero and may not be minus three — a share of a bill split among zero people means nothing. Write those banned values at the top of your working like a warning sign. When your answers finally arrive, hold each one against the list. If an answer is on the list, cross it out and say why. That visible crossing-out earns its own mark, because it proves you understood the restriction rather than fluked past it.

Your questions for this part are up now. Banned values first, then the lowest common denominator onto every term — every single one, especially the number standing alone.

## Subtopic: Nicknames, and Keeping the Pairs Together

Two closing ideas, and both turn a frightening problem into one you have already solved.

The first is the nickname. You are handed the quantity x squared minus two x, all squared, minus eleven lots of x squared minus two x, plus twenty-four. It looks like a page of work. But squint: the same chunk, x squared minus two x, appears twice. Give the chunk a nickname — call it k — and the line becomes k squared minus eleven k plus twenty-four, an ordinary trinomial that opens into k minus eight, times k minus three. So k is eight or k is three.

Do not stop there, because k was never the question; it was an alias. Swap the real expression back in, one k value at a time. From eight: x squared minus two x minus eight equals zero, giving four and minus two. From three: x squared minus two x minus three equals zero, giving three and minus one. Four true answers. Submitting the k values instead is like answering a delivery note with the nickname of the person who ordered — the parcel never arrives.

The second idea concerns two facts about the same pair of numbers. Say two amounts add up to seven, and their squares add up to twenty-five. The adding fact is simple; the squares fact is not. Use the simple fact to shrink the story to one unknown: if the amounts sum to seven, the second is seven minus the first. Push that into the squares fact and a single quadratic falls out, solving to three and four.

Then keep the pairs together, like gloves. A left three goes with a right four; a left four goes with a right three. Find each partner from the SIMPLE fact, because it is one subtraction and cannot go wrong. And write the final answers as matched pairs, never as two loose lists — a loose list claims pairings you never tested, and two of those claims are false.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: hunt for the repeated chunk before you expand a single bracket, and when two unknowns travel together, make sure every answer leaves with its partner.
