**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we wrap up! First one: if the formula solves every quadratic anyway, why should anyone bother learning to complete the square?

**Tutor:** Because completing the square earns you things the formula cannot. First, it is the source of the formula — the derivation from a x squared plus b x plus c equals zero is something you must be able to reproduce on demand: divide by a, shift the constant, add b squared over four a squared to both sides, root with a plus or minus, subtract b over two a. If you have only memorised the finished formula, you cannot write those lines, and you cannot rebuild the formula on the day your memory wobbles. Second, the turning point. Rewriting three x squared minus twelve x plus two as three, times x minus two all squared, minus ten, tells you immediately that the minimum of the graph sits at the point two, minus ten, with axis of symmetry x equals two — no other method delivers that. Third, some questions explicitly instruct you to solve by completing the square, and a formula answer there scores nothing even when it is numerically correct. So carry three tools with three jobs: factorise for speed when factors exist, formula when they do not, complete the square when the graph or the instruction demands it.

***

**Bianca:** Next question: when do we divide the whole thing by the leading coefficient, and when do we factor it out instead? A few of us mixed those up in the homework.

**Tutor:** The rule follows from what kind of object you are holding. An equation has two sides, so dividing both sides by the same nonzero constant keeps it true: three x squared minus twelve x plus two equals zero becomes x squared minus four x plus two thirds equals zero, and the solutions do not move, because zero divided by three is still zero. An expression has no second side. Divide y equals three x squared minus twelve x plus two by three and you now own a different expression, one third the size, whose graph is a different parabola. So for an expression you must preserve the value: factor the three out of the first two terms only, giving three, times the bracket x squared minus four x, plus two, complete the square inside the bracket, then distribute the three over everything the bracket contains — including the minus four that the completion created. That distribution is where the marks leak. Three, times x minus two all squared minus four, plus two must become three times x minus two all squared, minus twelve, plus two, which is minus ten at the end — not minus four plus two.

***

**Bianca:** This one comes from someone who says the formula keeps betraying them: how do you stop sign errors when b is negative?

**Tutor:** Sign errors come from substituting in your head, so move the substitution onto the page with a fixed three-line ritual. Line one: standard form, zero on the right. Line two: a equals, b equals, c equals, written separately with signs attached — for two x squared minus nine x plus three, that is a equals two, b equals minus nine, c equals three. Line three: the formula written out with brackets around every substituted value — minus, bracket, minus nine, close bracket, plus or minus the root of, bracket, minus nine, close bracket squared, minus four times two times three, all over two times two. The brackets then do the thinking: minus a minus nine is plus nine, and minus nine squared is plus eighty-one. Afterwards, run two sanity checks. If c is negative, then minus four a c is positive, so the discriminant must come out bigger than b squared — if yours is smaller, a sign fell off. And if the graph clearly cuts the x axis twice, the number under your root must be positive; a negative there means recheck the four a c term first, because that is where signs die.

**Bianca:** Last one for tonight: surd form or decimals — how do we know which one the question wants, and can we lose marks for giving the wrong kind?

**Tutor:** You can, and the question always tells you which it wants, in fixed wording worth memorising. If it says leave your answer in simplest surd form, or give the exact value, the root sign must survive to the final line: nine plus or minus root fifty-seven, all over four, is the complete answer, and decimalising it discards exactness and the mark with it. Simplest surd form also means the surd itself is tidy — root thirty over three rather than the root of ten thirds, because a surd may not remain in a denominator. If it says correct to two decimal places, a decimal is compulsory, to precisely that number of places. And when no instruction appears, exact form is always safe. One working habit protects every case: compute exactly all the way through, surds and fractions intact, and only convert on the very last line. Rounding root fifty-seven to seven comma five before dividing by four turns four comma one four into four comma one three — the arithmetic was fine, the timing of the rounding lost the mark.
