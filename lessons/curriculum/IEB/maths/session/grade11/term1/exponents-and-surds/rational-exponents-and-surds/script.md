# Part 1 — Expert

Until now, exponents were whole numbers. This session lets a fraction into the exponent, and with that single admission the root sign and the power sign turn out to be one instruction wearing two uniforms. Four skills follow from the definition: simplifying expressions whose bases disguise themselves as composite numbers, solving equations where the unknown lives upstairs in the exponent, handling surds as exact numbers rather than decimals, and the compulsory checking step that surd equations bring with them.

## Subtopic: Rational Exponents — a Root Written as a Power

Everything in this topic hangs on one definition. For x greater than zero and q a positive whole number, x to the power one over q means the q-th root of x. Extend it one step: x to the power p over q means the q-th root of x, raised to the power p. The bottom of the fraction names the root; the top names the power. Read every fractional exponent in that order and the topic shrinks to arithmetic.

The condition x greater than zero is doing real work. Roots of negative numbers are non-real, and if negative bases were allowed with rational exponents the exponent laws would start disagreeing with each other, so the base stays positive throughout this topic.

Every law you already trust still holds: multiply powers of one base by adding exponents, divide by subtracting, raise a power to a power by multiplying, read a negative exponent as a reciprocal, and send any non-zero base to one when the exponent is zero.

Evaluate eighty-one to the power three quarters. First move: write the base as a prime. Eighty-one is three to the fourth. Then three to the fourth, all to the power three quarters, multiplies the exponents: four times three quarters is three, so the answer is three cubed, twenty-seven. The root reading agrees: the fourth root of eighty-one is three, and three cubed is twenty-seven.

Evaluate eight to the power minus two thirds. The minus inverts: one over eight to the power two thirds. Eight is two cubed, and two cubed to the power two thirds is two squared, four. So the answer is one quarter — flipped upside down, never made negative.

Once more with variables. Eighty-one x to the twelfth, all to the power three quarters, gives twenty-seven x to the ninth.

Now the trap that recurs every year: the root of a sum. The square root of thirty-six plus sixty-four is the root of one hundred, which is ten — it is not six plus eight. Roots and powers distribute across multiplication and division, and never across a plus or a minus.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They will ask you to translate fractional exponents into roots and back again, and one of them will dangle a plus sign under a root to see if you split it.

## Subtopic: Simplifying — Prime Bases and Common Factors

Simplification questions belong to two families, and each family has its own opening move.

Family one: every base can be rewritten on a single prime. Simplify four to the power x plus two, times two to the power three x minus one, all over eight to the power x. Four is two squared, so the first factor is two to the power two x plus four. Eight is two cubed, so the denominator is two to the power three x. Add the exponents in the numerator: two x plus four, plus three x minus one, gives two to the power five x plus three. Subtract the three x below, and the answer is two to the power two x plus three.

Family two: the terms are joined by a minus or a plus, so no law applies directly — but a common factor can be pulled out. Simplify five to the power x plus two, minus five to the power x, all over eight times five to the power x. The numerator holds two powers of five, and the smaller one is the common factor. Five to the power x plus two is five to the x times twenty-five; five to the power x is itself. So the numerator is five to the x, times the bracket twenty-five minus one — that is twenty-four times five to the x. Divide by eight times five to the x: the powers cancel completely, leaving twenty-four over eight, which is three. An expression bristling with x collapsing into a plain number is exactly what this family produces.

The operator between the terms picks your method for you. Times and divide signs call for prime bases and the laws; a plus or minus calls for factorising, because no law exists for adding powers.

The costliest misreading in the topic: five to the power x plus two is NOT five to the x plus twenty-five. It is five to the x MULTIPLIED by twenty-five. Addition upstairs in the exponent is multiplication downstairs in the base.

Stop for this section's questions now — first decide which family the expression belongs to, and never let addition in an exponent leak down into the base.

## Subtopic: Exponential Equations, Including the Hidden Quadratic

When the unknown sits in the exponent, drive both sides onto the same base and set the exponents equal. The step is legal because an exponential function never repeats a value, so equal outputs force equal inputs.

Solve two to the power three x plus one equals thirty-two. Thirty-two is two to the fifth, so three x plus one equals five, and x equals four thirds.

When the unknown sits in the base under a rational exponent, raise both sides to the reciprocal power. Solve x to the power three quarters equals eight. Raise both sides to the power four thirds: x equals eight to the power four thirds. Eight is two cubed, so that is two to the fourth, sixteen. Check it: sixteen to the power three quarters is the fourth root of sixteen, cubed — two cubed, eight. It holds.

Mind the even-numerator case, because it doubles the answers. Solve x to the power two thirds equals nine. Cube both sides: x squared equals seven hundred and twenty-nine, so x equals twenty-seven or minus twenty-seven. Both survive checking, because the cube root happily accepts a negative and the squaring afterwards erases the sign. Writing only twenty-seven surrenders half the marks.

Now the shape that turns up in paper after paper: the exponential equation with a quadratic hiding inside it. Solve two to the power two x, minus six times two to the power x, plus eight equals zero. Notice that two to the power two x is two to the power x, all squared. Let k stand for two to the power x. The equation contracts to k squared minus six k plus eight equals zero, which factorises to k minus two, times k minus four. So k equals two or four. Translate back: two to the power x equals two gives x equals one; two to the power x equals four gives x equals two.

One piece of discipline rides along. If the substitution ever offers a negative value of k, reject it on paper with a reason — a positive base raised to any real power stays positive, so no real x can produce a negative exponential. The written rejection collects a mark.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — one base on both sides, eyes open for the squared exponential that wants a substitution, and a written rejection for any negative value the substitution offers.

## Subtopic: Surds — Simplify, Rationalise, and Check

A surd is a root that refuses to settle into a rational number, and it stays in root form because any decimal version is an approximation of it.

Simplifying a surd means marching every perfect-square factor out of the root. The square root of seventy-five is the root of twenty-five times three, which is five root three. The root of twenty-seven is three root three; the root of twelve is two root three. Once surds share the same root part, they add and subtract like like terms. Take two root twenty-seven, minus root seventy-five, plus root twelve. Two root twenty-seven is six root three; root seventy-five is five root three; root twelve is two root three. Six root three, minus five root three, plus two root three, is three root three.

Multiplying surds is ordinary distribution, with the extra fact that root a times root a is simply a. Expand three root two minus one, times root two plus five. Three root two times root two is six; three root two times five is fifteen root two; minus one times root two is minus root two; minus one times five is minus five. Collect: one plus fourteen root two.

Rationalising clears surds out of a denominator. Against a single surd, multiply top and bottom by that same surd: five over root three becomes five root three over three. Against a two-term denominator, multiply by the conjugate — the identical expression with its middle sign reversed — because the product forms a difference of two squares and both surds die together. Simplify eight over root seven minus root three: multiply top and bottom by root seven plus root three. The denominator becomes seven minus three, which is four, and dividing the eight by it leaves two root seven plus two root three.

Last, surd equations, where checking is not politeness but part of the method. Solve the root of x plus eleven equals x minus one. Square both sides: x plus eleven equals x squared minus two x plus one. Rearrange: x squared minus three x minus ten equals zero, which factorises to x minus five, times x plus two, so x equals five or minus two. Now check both in the ORIGINAL equation. At x equals five: the left side is the root of sixteen, four, and the right side is four. Accepted. At x equals minus two: the left side is the root of nine, three, but the right side is minus three, and a square root sign never outputs a negative. Rejected. The only solution is five. Squaring both sides manufactures impostors, so the check is where the marks live.

The final questions of this part are with you now — simplify every surd before combining, bring out the conjugate for a two-term denominator, and test every squared-equation answer against the original.

## Subtopic: Half a Doubling

Picture a clip you posted that doubles its views every day. After one day the views are multiplied by two. After two days, by two squared. After three, by two cubed. The exponent simply counts doublings.

Now ask the question that forces fractions into the exponent: what has happened by lunchtime — half a day in? Whatever the halfway factor is, applying it twice must complete one doubling. So it must be the number that squares to two: the square root of two. And we write it as two to the power one half. That is the whole secret of a fractional exponent: the bottom says how many equal steps make one full multiplication, and the top says how many of those steps you actually take.

Read eighty-one to the power three quarters that way. Chop eighty-one into four equal multiplying steps — the fourth root of eighty-one is three — then take three of the steps: three times three times three, twenty-seven. Bottom is the root, top is the power. Repeat it until it says itself.

A negative exponent is the same journey walked backwards. Eight to the power minus two thirds says: take two thirds of a step in reverse, so flip the fraction over — one over eight to the two thirds. The cube root of eight is two, squared is four, so the answer is one quarter. The minus sign turns the answer upside down; it never turns it negative.

And one warning to pocket. If one delivery weighs thirty-six kilograms and another weighs sixty-four, the root of the total is the root of one hundred — ten — and not six plus eight, which is fourteen. Roots slide happily through times and divide, and never through plus or minus.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on fractional exponents are coming to you right now. For each one, ask two things only: how many equal steps, and how many do I take?

## Subtopic: Everything in the Same Currency

Two hundred rand and two hundred dollars are not the same amount, and nobody can compare them until both are converted into one currency. Exponents work identically: two sides of an equation cannot be compared until both sit on the same base.

Solve two to the power three x plus one equals thirty-two. Convert the thirty-two: two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two — five twos, so thirty-two is two to the fifth. Both sides now trade in twos, so the exponents must agree: three x plus one equals five, and x equals four thirds.

The same conversion handles disguised bases. Four is two squared and eight is two cubed, so an expression crowded with fours, twos and eights is one currency printed on three different notes. Reissue everything in twos, then add exponents when multiplying and subtract when dividing.

Next, the trick that dissolves a whole family of scary-looking questions. A numerator like five to the power x plus two, minus five to the power x, looks stuck — but adding in the exponent means multiplying in the base, so it is five to the x times twenty-five, minus five to the x times one. That is twenty-four lots of five to the x. Set it over eight lots of five to the x and the five to the x cancels like the same item appearing on both sides of a till slip. What remains is twenty-four over eight: three.

And the favourite of setters everywhere: two to the power two x, minus six times two to the power x, plus eight. Give the awkward object a nickname. Call two to the x by the letter k, and the line becomes k squared minus six k plus eight — an ordinary trinomial, factorising into k minus two, times k minus four. So k is two or four. Now trade the nickname back in: two to the x equals two means x is one, and two to the x equals four means x is two. The monster was two lines of homework wearing a costume.

Your questions for this part are up now. One currency first, and the moment you spot a squared exponential, reach for the nickname.

## Subtopic: Tidying the Awkward Bottom

A surd is an exact number that refuses to be a tidy decimal, and only three jobs ever involve one: tidy it, combine it with its own kind, and clear it off the bottom of a fraction.

Tidying is rolling loose change. The root of seventy-five is a jumble until you spot the perfect square hiding inside: seventy-five is twenty-five times three, and the root of twenty-five walks out of the root sign as a five. Root seventy-five is five root three. Root twenty-seven is three root three, root twelve is two root three. Once they are rolled, root three becomes a coin you can count: six of them, minus five of them, plus two of them, is three of them. But you cannot count root three coins together with root two coins, any more than you can roll five-rand coins in with fifty-cent pieces.

Clearing the bottom is a currency exchange. A fraction like five over root three is worth a perfectly good amount, but the form is awkward to work with. Multiply top and bottom by root three — which is multiplying by one, so the value never moves — and the bottom becomes a clean three: five root three over three.

When the bottom carries two terms, like root seven minus root three, bring in the partner with the opposite middle sign: root seven PLUS root three. Multiplying top and bottom by the partner turns the bottom into seven minus three, four, because the crossing terms cancel each other out. Eight over root seven minus root three becomes two root seven plus two root three. That opposite-sign partner is the conjugate, and it is the only tool that clears a two-term bottom.

Finally, the habit that guards your marks. To remove a root sign from an equation you square both sides — but squaring has no memory for minus signs. Ask which numbers square to sixteen and both four and minus four answer. So squaring can smuggle in an answer the original equation never invited. Solving the root of x plus eleven equals x minus one produces five and minus two — but put minus two back and the left side reads three while the right side reads minus three. A root sign never pays out a negative, so minus two is a gate-crasher and is escorted out, in writing. Only five belongs.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: roll every surd tight before counting, call the conjugate for a two-term bottom, and walk every squared-equation answer back to the original door before letting it in.
