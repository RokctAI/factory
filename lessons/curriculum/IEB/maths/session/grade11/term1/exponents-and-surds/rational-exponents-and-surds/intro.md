Today the exponent learns fractions, and the moment it does, the root sign and the power sign become one idea written two ways: the bottom of the fraction names the root, the top names the power. From that single definition we simplify expressions whose bases hide behind composite numbers, solve equations where the unknown lives in the exponent — including the famous ones with a quadratic concealed inside — and then turn to surds: tidying them, combining them, clearing them off the bottom of fractions with the conjugate, and checking every answer a squared equation produces, because squaring is a step that manufactures impostors.
