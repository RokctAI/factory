Hey, it's Bianca — fractional exponents look like someone broke the power rules on purpose, but I promise this whole topic is one sentence long: the bottom of the fraction is the root, the top is the power. That's it. Eighty-one to the three quarters? Fourth root of eighty-one is three, then three cubed is twenty-seven. Say "bottom root, top power" out loud three times and you already own half the lesson.

The minus sign in an exponent scares people too, so let's defuse it now: a minus flips the fraction upside down. It never, ever makes the answer negative. Eight to the minus two thirds is one over four, not minus four.

For the simplifying questions, you only ever have two jobs, and the sign between the terms tells you which one. Times or divide? Rewrite every base as a prime and use the laws. Plus or minus? Factor out the smaller power, because there is no law for adding powers. And burn this into memory: five to the power x plus two means twenty-five TIMES five to the x. If you're ever unsure, test with x equals one — it takes ten seconds and it never lies.

When the equation has a squared exponential in it, give the exponential a nickname, call it k, solve the little quadratic, then swap the real thing back. And if k comes out negative, cross it out proudly and write why: a positive base can never give a negative power. That crossing-out is a mark.

Surds? Roll them tight like coin rolls — pull the perfect squares out — and only count matching coins together. Two-term bottom? Multiply by the same thing with the opposite sign. And any time you squared an equation, walk every answer back to the original before you trust it.

Bottom root, top power. Minus flips. Plus means factorise. Nickname the monster. Check after squaring. Five little sentences — now go show the practice questions that you know all five!
