**Bianca:** Class questions before we finish! First: someone wants to know why the base has to stay positive when the exponent is a fraction. Their calculator gave an answer for a negative base, so why the rule?

**Tutor:** Because with negative bases, rational exponents stop being consistent, and mathematics refuses to keep rules that contradict each other. Here is the contradiction in one example. Minus eight to the power one third should be the cube root of minus eight, which is minus two — fine so far. But one third is the same fraction as two sixths, so the same number should equal the sixth root of minus eight squared, which is the sixth root of sixty-four, and that is plus two. The same expression just produced minus two and plus two, depending only on how the fraction was written — and equivalent fractions are supposed to be interchangeable. Keeping the base positive removes the clash entirely: every rational exponent then has exactly one value, and every exponent law holds without exceptions. A calculator that returns something for a negative base is quietly choosing one interpretation for you, and different calculators choose differently, which is precisely the problem. So in this topic, when you see a rational exponent, read the restriction x greater than zero as part of the definition, and if a question does allow a negative under an odd root — like the cube root of minus twenty-seven — it will be written with the root sign, where the meaning is unambiguous.

***

**Bianca:** Next one: what actually goes wrong if you say five to the power x plus two equals five to the x plus twenty-five? Apparently half of us did exactly that this week.

**Tutor:** What goes wrong is that addition and multiplication get swapped, and everything downstream inherits the swap. The law says that multiplying powers of one base adds the exponents. Run it in reverse: an addition sitting up in the exponent must come down as a multiplication of the bases. Five to the power x plus two is five to the x, times five squared — twenty-five TIMES five to the x, not twenty-five plus it. Test it with a number you can check: let x be one. Five to the power three is one hundred and twenty-five. Twenty-five times five is one hundred and twenty-five — correct. Five plus twenty-five is thirty — nowhere close. That thirty-second check with x equals one is the habit I want you to build, because it catches the error before it costs anything. The reason this matters so much is that the whole factorising method for expressions like five to the power x plus two, minus five to the power x, depends on rewriting each term as a multiple of five to the x — get the rewrite wrong and the common factor never appears, the cancellation never happens, and a two-mark simplification becomes a page of stuck algebra.

**Bianca:** Third question: with the substitution equations, how do you know when to bring in the letter k, and does the k ever get rejected?

**Tutor:** The signal is a squared exponential travelling with a plain one. When you see something like two to the power two x, minus six times two to the power x, plus eight, notice that two to the power two x is exactly two to the power x, all squared — so the equation contains a thing and that same thing squared, which is the fingerprint of a quadratic. Substituting k for two to the x turns it into k squared minus six k plus eight, which factorises like any trinomial. Two warnings, though. First, the k values are not the answers — they are exponentials in disguise, so each one must be translated back: two to the x equals two gives x equals one, two to the x equals four gives x equals two. Handing in k values is answering the wrong question. Second, yes, k values do get rejected, and the rejection is worth a mark. An exponential with a positive base is strictly positive — two to the power anything real is never zero and never negative. So if your factorising ever offers k equals minus three, you write: rejected, since two to the x is greater than zero for all real x. If both k values come out negative, the equation simply has no real solution, and saying so with the reason IS the full answer.

**Bianca:** Last one for tonight: why must we check surd equation answers in the original equation? Isn't checking just for people who doubt their algebra?

**Tutor:** No — here the check is part of the method itself, because the algebra, done perfectly, can still deliver a wrong answer. The culprit is the squaring step. Squaring is not reversible: it maps both three and minus three onto nine, so after squaring, the equation remembers less than it did before. When you square both sides of the root of x plus eleven equals x minus one, you are also unavoidably solving the phantom equation where the right side is one minus x, and any solution belonging only to the phantom rides along into your final line. That is exactly what happens with x equals minus two: the left side of the original gives the root of nine, which is three, but the right side gives minus three. Since a square root sign, by definition, returns only the non-negative root, the original equation is not satisfied — minus two belongs to the phantom. The check is therefore a filter, not a courtesy: substitute every candidate into the original equation, keep the ones that balance, and reject the impostors in writing, with the reason that a square root cannot equal a negative number. Do it even when both answers happen to survive, because the marker is looking for evidence that you know squaring manufactures impostors.
