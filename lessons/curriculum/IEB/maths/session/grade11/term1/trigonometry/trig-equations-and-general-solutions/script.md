# Part 1 — Expert

An algebraic equation like three x minus six equals zero owns exactly one answer. A trigonometric equation like two cosine x minus root three equals zero owns infinitely many, because the trigonometric functions repeat forever and hit the same value again on every turn. This session builds the tool that holds all those answers in a single line — the general solution — then shows how to harvest the particular answers an interval asks for, and how to dismantle the equation that is secretly a quadratic wearing a disguise.

## Subtopic: The General Solution for Sine and Cosine

Turn the previous section into formulae. Cosine is tidier, because its two quadrants sit mirror-image above and below the x-axis, so its two families compress into a plus-minus: if cosine x equals a value with reference angle alpha, then x equals plus or minus alpha, plus k times three hundred and sixty degrees.

## Subtopic: Tangent, and Equations That Need Tidying First

Tangent runs on a faster clock. Its period is one hundred and eighty degrees, not three hundred and sixty, because the reduction formula for one eighty plus theta leaves tangent completely unchanged — half a turn flips both y and x, and the fraction shrugs. Now the tidying step most equations demand. They rarely arrive as a naked ratio equal to a number; usually one legal move must come first. Watch: root three sine x equals cosine x. # Part 2 — Simplifier

The same solving again, now running on a taxi timetable and a hall with two doors — same rules, same answers, and a picture of why the answers never run out.

## Subtopic: The Taxi on a Loop

A taxi drives a fixed loop through the neighbourhood, and you see it pass the school gate at eight in the morning. The loop takes exactly one hour. When else does it pass? Nine, ten, eleven — and it also passed at seven, and at six. One sighting plus the loop time generates the entire timetable, backwards and forwards: eight o'clock plus k hours, with k any whole number — positive for later laps, negative for earlier ones.

A general solution is exactly that timetable. The equation sine x equals zero comma eight asks: at which turnings does the wheel's north-south shadow measure zero comma eight? You anchor one sighting — fifty-three comma one three degrees — and because the wheel repeats every three hundred and sixty degrees, fifty-three comma one three plus k three sixty captures every such moment that will ever exist. The letter k is not algebra being difficult; it is a lap counter, fast-forwarding or rewinding whole loops.

Tangent drives a shorter loop — one hundred and eighty degrees, half a turn. The reason sits in the fraction: tangent is one shadow divided by the other, and half a turn flips the sign of both shadows at once, so the fraction lands exactly where it started. Same taxi, half-length route, and its k counts half-turns.

One habit follows immediately: never hand in the calculator's single sighting as the answer. The calculator tells you one time the taxi passed; the question wants the timetable.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. For each one, ask: how long is the loop, and what single sighting am I anchoring to?

## Subtopic: Two Doors Into the Hall

The taxi story misses one complication: within a single loop, sine usually reaches a value twice. Think of a seat on a big wheel. Riding up, your height passes the three-quarter mark once — and riding down, it passes the very same height again. One height, two moments.
