# Part 1 — Expert

Last year, trigonometry lived inside a right-angled triangle, which meant no angle could ever reach ninety degrees, let alone pass it. This session takes the ceiling off. Four pieces of equipment make it possible: two identities that tie the three ratios to one another, the sign rules that govern the four quadrants, the reduction formulae that fold any angle back to an acute one, and the co-function rules that let sine and cosine swap seats at ninety degrees. Between them they drive nearly every trigonometric simplification, proof and evaluation you will meet this year.

## Subtopic: The Two Identities Every Proof Leans On

Start by rebuilding the ratios, because the triangle definitions stop working past ninety degrees. Put an angle theta at the origin, opening anticlockwise from the positive x-axis, and choose any point on its terminal arm. Give that point coordinates x and y, and let r be its distance from the origin — a length, so always positive. Then sine theta is y over r, cosine theta is x over r, and tangent theta is y over x. Notice what is missing: no requirement anywhere that theta be acute. These definitions serve every angle there is.

The first identity drops out immediately. Divide sine by cosine: y over r, divided by x over r. The r cancels and y over x remains — which is tangent by definition. So tangent theta equals sine theta over cosine theta, valid wherever cosine theta is not zero.

The second identity is Pythagoras in disguise. The chosen point, the origin, and the foot of the perpendicular to the x-axis form a right-angled triangle, so x squared plus y squared equals r squared. Divide the whole statement by r squared: x squared over r squared, plus y squared over r squared, equals one. Read the pieces: cosine squared theta plus sine squared theta equals one. The square identity, true for absolutely every angle.

Its two rearrangements should fire automatically: sine squared theta is one minus cosine squared theta, and cosine squared theta is one minus sine squared theta. Any time an expression shows you a one minus a square, it is handing you an identity.

Use the machinery once, right now. Simplify one minus cosine squared theta, all divided by sine theta times cosine theta. The numerator is sine squared theta. Divide by sine theta cosine theta: one sine cancels, leaving sine theta over cosine theta — which is tangent theta. Three ratios walked in; one walked out.

A caution rides with the quotient identity. Tangent carries cosine in its denominator, so tangent fails to exist wherever cosine is zero — at ninety degrees, at two hundred and seventy, and at every angle a full turn away from those. An identity that involves tangent therefore holds for all angles except the ones that kill a denominator, and stating those exceptions is routinely where the last mark sits.

Pause here — the questions for this section reach you now. They ask you to rebuild both identities from x, y and r, and to recognise a one minus a square dressed up in context.

## Subtopic: Signs in the Four Quadrants — the CAST Rule

The x, y, r definitions unlock something triangles never allowed: negative ratios, with the signs read straight off the coordinates.

Tour the quadrants. First quadrant: x and y both positive, so all three ratios are positive. Second quadrant: x has turned negative, y still positive — sine, being y over r, keeps its plus, while cosine and tangent go negative. Third quadrant: x and y both negative — sine and cosine are negative, but tangent, a negative divided by a negative, comes out positive. Fourth quadrant: y negative, x positive — cosine positive, sine and tangent negative.

The memory word is CAST, planted quadrant by quadrant beginning in the fourth: C in the fourth where Cosine alone is positive, A in the first where All are, S in the second where only Sine is, T in the third where only Tangent is.

Now the working method the entire topic depends on: a sketch instead of a calculator. Suppose cosine theta equals minus five thirteenths, with theta between one hundred and eighty and two hundred and seventy degrees. That range is the third quadrant, so draw the arm there. Cosine is x over r, so set x equal to minus five and r equal to thirteen. Pythagoras recovers y: y squared equals one hundred and sixty-nine minus twenty-five, which is one hundred and forty-four, so y is twelve in size — and in the third quadrant y points down, so y equals minus twelve. Everything now reads off the sketch: sine theta is minus twelve thirteenths, and tangent theta is minus twelve over minus five — positive twelve fifths.

The sketch never rounds and it earns full marks. The one error that haunts this method is a forgotten sign on x or y, and the cure is mechanical: mark the quadrant before writing a single number, and let the quadrant dictate both signs.

Stop for this section's questions now — quadrant first, signs from the quadrant, numbers from Pythagoras.

## Subtopic: Reduction Formulae — One Eighty, Three Sixty and the Minus

A reduction formula rewrites the ratio of a large angle as the ratio of an acute one. Three families exist, and a single rule runs all of them: the SIZE of the answer comes from the acute angle, and the SIGN comes from the quadrant where the large angle lives.

Family one: one hundred and eighty degrees minus theta sits in the second quadrant, where only sine keeps its plus. So sine of one eighty minus theta is sine theta, cosine of one eighty minus theta is minus cosine theta, and tangent of one eighty minus theta is minus tangent theta.

Family two: one hundred and eighty degrees plus theta sits in the third quadrant, tangent's home ground. Sine becomes minus sine theta, cosine becomes minus cosine theta, and tangent stays tangent theta.

Family three: three hundred and sixty degrees minus theta sits in the fourth quadrant, where cosine rules. Sine becomes minus sine theta, cosine stays cosine theta, tangent becomes minus tangent theta. A negative angle, minus theta, points into that same fourth quadrant, so it wears the same signs: sine of minus theta is minus sine theta, cosine of minus theta is cosine theta. And whole turns are invisible — adding or subtracting three hundred and sixty degrees changes nothing.

Run the machine. Cosine of one hundred and fifty degrees: write one fifty as one eighty minus thirty. Second quadrant, cosine negative — minus cosine thirty, which is minus root three over two. Sine of two hundred and forty degrees: two forty is one eighty plus sixty, third quadrant, sine negative — minus sine sixty, minus root three over two. Tangent of three hundred and fifteen degrees: three fifteen is three sixty minus forty-five, fourth quadrant, tangent negative — minus tangent forty-five, which is minus one.

One combined evaluation, calculator shut. Cosine of one hundred and twenty degrees, times tangent of two hundred and ten degrees. Cosine one twenty is cosine of one eighty minus sixty: minus cosine sixty, minus a half. Tangent two ten is tangent of one eighty plus thirty: third quadrant, tangent positive, so tangent thirty, root three over three. Multiply: minus a half times root three over three is minus root three over six.

The discipline never changes: reduce to the acute angle, settle the sign from the quadrant before you touch the size, and show both decisions on paper. A guessed sign is a coin flip, and it is usually a two-mark coin.

The questions for this section are with you now — name the quadrant first, commit the sign second, and only then substitute the acute angle.

## Subtopic: Co-functions, Simplifying Chains and Where an Identity Breaks

The remaining family is built on ninety degrees, and it plays by a different rule: crossing ninety swaps the function. Sine of ninety minus theta equals cosine theta, and cosine of ninety minus theta equals sine theta — the complement pairing you first met in grade ten. With ninety plus theta the swap still happens, and the second quadrant then sets the sign: sine of ninety plus theta is cosine theta, positive since sine owns the second quadrant, while cosine of ninety plus theta is minus sine theta.

The one-line summary to memorise: one eighty and three sixty keep the function and negotiate only the sign; ninety swaps sine with cosine, and then the quadrant signs the result.

Now a full simplification chain, in the standard format. Simplify: cosine of ninety minus x, times sine of one eighty minus x, times tangent of three sixty minus x, all divided by sine of ninety plus x, times sine of one eighty plus x. Numerator, piece by piece: cosine of ninety minus x is sine x. Sine of one eighty minus x is sine x. Tangent of three sixty minus x is minus tangent x. So the numerator is minus sine squared x tangent x. Denominator: sine of ninety plus x is cosine x, and sine of one eighty plus x is minus sine x, so the denominator is minus sine x cosine x. The two minus signs cancel each other. Left over: sine squared x tangent x, over sine x cosine x. Cancel one sine: sine x tangent x over cosine x. Write the tangent as sine over cosine: sine squared x over cosine squared x — tangent squared x. A five-term chain, one clean ratio. That collapse is the design of every such question.

Finally, an identity proof with its validity question attached. Prove that one over one minus cosine x, plus one over one plus cosine x, equals two over sine squared x. Work the left side. The common denominator is one minus cosine x, times one plus cosine x — a difference of squares, one minus cosine squared x, which the square identity converts to sine squared x. The numerators add: one plus cosine x, plus one minus cosine x — the cosines cancel, leaving two. So the left side is two over sine squared x. Proved.

For which values of x does the identity hold? Wherever no denominator dies. One minus cosine x is zero when cosine x is one, at multiples of three sixty. One plus cosine x is zero when cosine x is minus one, at one eighty plus multiples of three sixty. Sine squared x is zero at every multiple of one eighty — and that condition contains both of the others. So the identity holds for all x except multiples of one hundred and eighty degrees, and writing that sentence is a mark in itself.

The final questions of this part arrive now — swap at ninety, keep the function at one eighty and three sixty, and put every denominator on trial before declaring an identity true.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same machinery again, now hung on a spinning wheel and a folded page — same rules, same answers, and not one calculator button.

## Subtopic: The Spoke and the Shadow

Picture a big wheel — a Ferris wheel seen from the side — with one arm painted bright yellow, turning anticlockwise from the three o'clock position. The angle is simply how far that arm has swung. Wherever the arm points, its tip sits some distance east or west of the centre, and some distance north or south. Cosine is the east-west shadow of the arm, sine is the north-south shadow, and tangent is the north-south shadow divided by the east-west one. The arm itself never changes length — that fixed length is r, and it is always positive.

Swing the arm to two hundred and ten degrees. It now leans down into the south-west. Its north-south shadow points south — negative. Its east-west shadow points west — negative too. Nothing spooky has occurred: the signs in this topic are nothing but compass directions. Sine negative means the tip is south of centre; cosine negative means it is west.

The square identity is also sitting in this picture. The two shadows are the two short sides of a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is the arm. Pythagoras says the shadows squared add up to the arm squared. Let the arm have length one, and the sentence becomes: sine squared plus cosine squared equals one. Not a formula to cram — Pythagoras, riding the big wheel.

Tangent is the fragile one of the three, because it is one shadow divided by the other. When the arm points straight up, the east-west shadow shrinks to zero, and division by zero is meaningless. That is the whole reason tangent does not exist at ninety degrees, nor at two hundred and seventy.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section come to you now. Each one needs just two thoughts: where is the tip of the arm, and which shadow does the question want?

## Subtopic: Four Neighbourhoods, One Rule

The wheel cuts the plane into four neighbourhoods, and each has a signature. First neighbourhood, east and north: all three ratios positive. Second, west but north: only the north-south ratio — sine — keeps its plus. Third, west and south: both shadows negative, yet their quotient, tangent, is negative over negative — positive. Fourth, east but south: cosine alone stays positive.

Walk the letters around the wheel starting in the fourth neighbourhood: C, A, S, T. Cosine, All, Sine, Tangent — in each neighbourhood, the letter names the only ratio holding a plus sign there.

Now the move that retires the calculator. You are told cosine of an angle is minus five thirteenths, and the angle lies between one eighty and two seventy. That range is the third neighbourhood, so the arm points south-west. Cosine is east-west over arm, so the tip sits five units west on an arm of thirteen. How far south? Pythagoras: thirteen squared minus five squared is one hundred and forty-four, so twelve units — south, so minus twelve. Every answer is now lying in the sketch: sine is minus twelve thirteenths, and tangent is minus twelve over minus five, positive twelve fifths. No decimals appeared, and nothing was rounded, because nothing was ever approximated.

Your questions for this part are up now. Draw the neighbourhood first, place both shadows with their compass signs, then read the answers straight off the drawing.

## Subtopic: Folding the Angle Back Home

Every big angle is an acute angle in fancy dress. Take one hundred and fifty degrees and fold the wheel along the north-south line: the arm lands exactly on thirty degrees. The fold swapped east for west and touched nothing else — so every ratio at one fifty has the same SIZE as at thirty, and only the ratios that depend on east-west change sign. That is the entire content of the reduction formulae. One eighty minus theta is that fold; one eighty plus theta is a half-turn of the arm; three sixty minus theta is a fold along the east-west line. Each move lands in its own neighbourhood, and the CAST letter of that neighbourhood decides who keeps a plus.

The recipe never varies. Step one: find the home angle — the acute gap between the arm and the east-west line. Step two: name the neighbourhood and look up its letter for the ratio in question. Step three: write the sign, then the home angle. Cosine of one fifty: home angle thirty, second neighbourhood, cosine negative there — minus cosine thirty, minus root three over two. Sine of two forty: home angle sixty, third neighbourhood, sine negative — minus root three over two. Tangent of three fifteen: home angle forty-five, fourth neighbourhood, tangent negative — minus one.

Ninety degrees plays an entirely different game: instead of flipping signs, it swaps the players. Turn the arm a quarter-turn and yesterday's east-west shadow becomes today's north-south one. Hence sine of ninety minus theta is cosine theta, and cosine of ninety minus theta is sine theta. Keep the two behaviours in separate pockets: one eighty and three sixty bargain only over signs; ninety swaps sine with cosine first, and then its neighbourhood signs the result.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: find the home angle, let the CAST letter set the sign, and whenever ninety appears, swap before you sign.
