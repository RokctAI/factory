**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every time: the three shortcut facts — 2a, 3a plus b, a plus b plus c — keep blurring together. Is there a way to genuinely remember which is which?

**Tutor:** Anchor each fact to WHERE it lives in the difference table, because each one is simply the table's own architecture rewritten as algebra. Picture the table: top row, the terms; middle row, the first differences; bottom row, the second differences. The bottom row is the deepest layer, and depth purifies: after two rounds of differencing, only the n squared part of the formula is still standing, so the constant down there belongs to a alone — it equals 2 a. The middle row's FIRST entry — term two minus term one — is 3 a plus b: derive it once by subtracting T 1 from T 2 in the general formula, then trust it forever. Notice what it contains: a survives, b has arrived, but c has already been differenced away. The top row's first entry is the formula itself standing at position one — a plus b plus c, all three letters, nothing yet removed. So the memory device is the letter count: bottom row, one letter; middle row's first entry, two letters; top row's first entry, three letters. Solve in exactly that order, because each equation feeds its result to the next. And if everything deserts you on the day, substitute n equals 1, 2 and 3 into a n squared plus b n plus c and solve three simultaneous equations — slower, but unbreakable, and it always agrees with the shortcuts.

**Bianca:** Next one: a learner's formula checked out on term one but collapsed at term five, while a friend's did exactly the opposite. What is each failure telling them?

**Tutor:** The direction of the failure is the diagnosis. A formula that works early but drifts late has a faulty a or b — the letters multiplied by n. An error in c displaces EVERY term by the same fixed amount, so it announces itself immediately, at term one; but an error in a or b grows with position, sometimes coinciding with the sequence at small n and then peeling away. So collapsing at term five with term one intact points at a misread second difference — perhaps taken from miscopied terms — or a first gap taken from the wrong pair. The friend's case, wrong at term one but fine in shape later, is the signature of a wrong c and only c: return to a plus b plus c equals the first term and repair one line. The systematic fix, in order: recompute both difference rows straight from the printed sequence, checking every subtraction; halve the second difference and confirm a; substitute a into 3 a plus b equals the first gap and confirm b; then recover c from term one. And build the habit that catches all of it before anyone else does: test the finished formula on TWO terms, one early and one late — term one and the last term given. The early test exposes c; the late test exposes a and b. Ten seconds of substitution, and every loose screw identifies itself.

**Bianca:** Third: for the membership question — is 150 a term — why is the discriminant the judge? Learners think of it as belonging to the nature-of-roots chapter.

**Tutor:** It does belong there — and a membership question IS a nature-of-roots question, once you translate it. Asking whether 150 is a term asks: does there exist a POSITION, a natural number n, with n squared plus 3 n equal to 150? Rearranged, that is n squared plus 3 n minus 150 equals zero, and membership has become the existence of a suitable root. Now the discriminant performs its usual service, with one extra filter stacked on top. Negative discriminant: no real roots, so certainly no position. Positive but not a perfect square: the roots are irrational, and no irrational number can index a sequence — that is our case, with 609 — so the answer is no. A perfect square: the roots are rational, and you still solve to apply the final two checks — the candidate must be whole, and it must be positive. Compare the equation n squared plus 3 n minus 130 equals zero: discriminant 9 plus 520, which is 529 — 23 squared — roots 10 and minus 13, and 10 passes the last filter while minus 13 is rejected in writing. The complete chain is: real, then rational, then whole, then positive. Nature-of-roots theory is not making a guest appearance here — it is the engine room of every membership question.

**Bianca:** Last one: with picture patterns, some swear by counting and differencing, others by spotting the structure. Which habit should we train?

**Tutor:** Train both, in a fixed sequence, because each protects the other. Begin with counting and differencing — the guaranteed route. Count the objects in each printed figure carefully, write the sequence, form both difference rows, and build T n from the three facts. That works on every quadratic picture ever drawn, whether or not it hides anything elegant. THEN give thirty seconds to structure-hunting: ask how the figure is assembled — rows times columns, a square with a border, two staircases interlocked. Our paving pads reveal themselves as n rows of n plus 3 bricks, so T n equals n times n plus 3 — and expanding gives n squared plus 3 n, precisely what the table built. That agreement is the prize: two independent routes converging on one formula is stronger verification than re-checking either route's arithmetic on its own. If the routes disagree, trust neither and recount the printed figures — miscounting a picture is the commonest context error, ahead of any algebra slip. Structure alone earns full credit when it is right, and it reads as insight, but it carries risk: a plausible misreading of the picture produces a confident wrong formula. The professional order is count first, structure second, agreement as proof — then answer the story's questions in the story's own words: bricks, pads, rows.
