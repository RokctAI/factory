# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the algebra and trigonometry revision sweep for the year — quadratic equations in every disguise, the three graph families with their shifts, and the trigonometry engine from identities through general solutions to the triangle rules. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed into the decisions that earn marks, rebuilt through fresh worked examples, and aimed at the traps that catch candidates in the final exams every single year.

## Subtopic: Quadratic Equations and the Discriminant

Every quadratic equation offers three weapons, and choosing quickly is half the marks. Weapon one: factorising, when the factors show themselves. X squared minus seven x plus ten equals zero: two numbers multiplying to ten, adding to minus seven — minus two and minus five. Solutions: two and five.

Weapon two: the formula, when the factors hide. X squared minus six x plus one equals zero. Minus b, plus or minus the square root of, b squared minus four a c, all over two a: six plus or minus root thirty-two, over two — which simplifies to three plus or minus two root two. As decimals: about five comma eight three, and nought comma one seven. Completing the square lands on the same pair — x minus three, all squared, equals eight — and the formula is nothing but completing the square done once, in general, forever.

The quantity under the root runs the show: the DISCRIMINANT, b squared minus four a c. Positive: two real roots. Zero: one repeated root. Negative: no real roots. A perfect square: rational roots. Here it is thirty-two — positive but not a perfect square, so two real irrational roots, exactly what appeared. Nature-of-roots questions never ask you to solve; they ask you to read delta.

The quadratic inequality adds one picture. X squared minus two x minus eight is less than zero: factorise, x minus four times x plus two; critical values minus two and four. The parabola opens upwards, dipping below the axis BETWEEN its roots, so the answer is: x between minus two and four. Not two disconnected pieces — that answer belongs to greater than zero. Sketch the parabola, shade the demanded sign, read the interval.

And the surd equation carries a sting. Root of x plus three equals x minus three. Square both sides: x plus three equals x squared minus six x plus nine, so x squared minus seven x plus six equals zero — x equals six or x equals one. Now the compulsory check: x equals six gives root nine, three, and six minus three, three — valid. X equals one gives root four, two, but one minus three is minus two — invalid. Squaring manufactures ghost solutions, and the check is not politeness; it is part of the method.

This section's questions are with you now — weapon chosen by shape, delta read before solving, and every surd answer checked against the original.

## Subtopic: Functions — Parabola, Hyperbola, Exponential

Grade eleven functions live in completed form, where every graph confesses its anatomy. The parabola: y equals a, times x plus p all squared, plus q. The turning point is minus p semicolon q — sign flipped on p, honest on q. Take y equals two, times x plus one squared, minus eighteen: turning point minus one semicolon minus eighteen, opening upwards since a is positive, minimum value minus eighteen.

Intercepts complete the sketch. Y-intercept: set x to zero — two times one minus eighteen, minus sixteen. X-intercepts: set y to zero — x plus one squared equals nine, x plus one equals plus or minus three, so x equals two or minus four. Turning point, two roots, y-intercept: four anchors, one parabola, full marks.

The hyperbola: y equals a over x plus p, plus q. Its skeleton is the asymptotes — vertical where the denominator dies, x equals minus p; horizontal at y equals q. For y equals four over x plus one, minus two: asymptotes x equals minus one and y equals minus two, and the two branches hug that cross without ever touching it. Domain: all x except minus one. Range: all y except minus two — the asymptotes ARE the domain and range, written as fences.

The exponential: y equals a times b to the x, plus q. The horizontal asymptote sits at y equals q, and the graph lives entirely on one side of it. Y equals four times two to the x, plus two: asymptote y equals two, range y greater than two, y-intercept at four plus two, six. Growth when b is greater than one, decay when b is a fraction between nought and one.

One grammar runs all three families: p slides left and right with its sign flipped, q slides up and down honestly, a stretches and flips. Learn the grammar once; every sketch and every find-the-equation question is the same sentence with new numbers.

Take this section's questions now — anatomy first, anchors second, and the asymptotes drawn before the curve.

## Subtopic: Trig Identities, Reduction and General Solutions

Two identities power everything: tan x equals sine x over cosine x, and sine squared x plus cosine squared x equals one. Every proof question is a chain built from these two — start on the messier side, convert tangents to sine over cosine, hunt for the squared pair, and simplify toward the cleaner side.

Reduction formulas shrink any angle into the first quadrant, governed by the CAST picture. Quadrant two, angles of the form one hundred and eighty minus x: only sine survives positive — cosine of one hundred and eighty minus x is MINUS cosine x. Quadrant three, one hundred and eighty plus x: only tangent positive — sine of one hundred and eighty plus x is minus sine x. Quadrant four, three hundred and sixty minus x: only cosine positive — cosine of three hundred and sixty minus x IS cosine x. Negative angles live there too: sine of minus x equals minus sine x. The procedure: locate the quadrant, read the sign from CAST, keep the reference angle. Cosine of one hundred and twenty degrees: quadrant two, cosine negative, reference sixty — minus a half.

Then the general solution, because sine and cosine repeat forever. Solve cosine x equals a half. The partners for cosine are plus and minus the reference angle: x equals sixty degrees, or minus sixty. Then the engine of repetition: add k times three hundred and sixty degrees to each, k any integer — so x equals plus or minus sixty, plus k full turns. For sine the partners sit across the vertical: sine x equals root three over two gives sixty and one hundred and twenty, each plus k times three hundred and sixty. For tangent, one solution repeats every one hundred and eighty degrees. Restricted-interval questions are the general solution with a filter: write the general form, then let k run and keep whatever lands inside.

This section's questions are ready — two identities, one CAST compass, and no solution family left behind.

## Subtopic: Trig Graphs and the Triangle Rules

The trig graphs obey the same grammar as the algebraic families. Amplitude a scales the wave vertically. The number multiplying x compresses the period: y equals cosine of three x completes its cycle in one hundred and twenty degrees, not three hundred and sixty. A horizontal shift slides the wave: y equals sine of x plus forty-five runs forty-five degrees early. Vertical q lifts the whole wave. Reading these off a drawn graph — amplitude from the height, period from one full cycle, shift from where the wave starts its climb — is the reverse question, and it pays the same marks.

Then the rules that solve ANY triangle, not just right-angled ones. The sine rule: a over sine A equals b over sine B — usable the moment you own a matching pair, a side with its opposite angle. Angles thirty-five and seventy-five, with the side opposite thirty-five equal to ten: the side opposite seventy-five is ten sine seventy-five over sine thirty-five — about sixteen comma eight four.

The cosine rule: a squared equals b squared plus c squared, minus two b c cosine A — Pythagoras with a correction term, for when the matching pair is missing. Sides six and ten embracing an angle of forty degrees: the third side squared is thirty-six plus one hundred, minus one hundred and twenty cosine forty — about forty-four comma zero seven — so the side is about six comma six four. Two sides and the included angle, or all three sides: cosine rule. Anything with a matching pair: sine rule.

The area rule completes the trio: area equals a half, a b, sine C — two sides and the INCLUDED angle, no height required. Same triangle: a half times six times ten times sine forty — about nineteen comma two eight square units.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the unchecked surd equation, a ghost root submitted as genuine. Exhibit two: the turning point read as p semicolon q without the sign flip. Exhibit three: one solution family written where sine offered two. Exhibit four: the cosine rule's correction term dropped, or the non-included angle used. Each exhibit costs the same mark every year — except to the candidates who rehearsed them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — grammar for the graphs, the right rule for the triangle, and all four exhibits dodged.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision as three machines — a root-finding machine with a warning light, graphs as machines with dials, and trigonometry as one circle telling every story.

## Subtopic: Delta as the Warning Light

Think of every quadratic as a machine that hunts for where the parabola crosses the axis — and the discriminant, b squared minus four a c, is the warning light on its front panel. Light green and bright — delta positive: two crossings, two real roots. Light glowing a single steady dot — delta zero: the parabola kisses the axis once, one repeated root. Light red — delta negative: the parabola floats clear of the axis, no real roots at all. Reading the light takes one calculation and answers the whole nature-of-roots question — no solving, no formula, just delta.

The machine has three attachments, picked by the shape of the job. Factors visible: factorise — the fastest attachment. Factors hidden: the formula — it never jams, it just needs delta under the root. Asked to rewrite rather than solve: completing the square, which also hands over the turning point for free. All three attachments find the same roots; the marks go to choosing quickly and executing cleanly.

Two jobs need extra care. The inequality job wants a REGION, not points: sketch the parabola, mark the roots, and shade where the demanded sign lives — below the axis between the roots for less than zero, outside them for greater. And the surd job ships with a mandatory quality check: squaring both sides can weld a ghost solution onto the genuine one, so every candidate answer goes back into the ORIGINAL equation, and the ghost — like x equals one claiming that root four equals minus two — is rejected in writing. The check is part of the answer, and it carries its own mark.

Your questions for this section are ready — read the light, pick the attachment, and reject every ghost by name.

## Subtopic: Graphs as Machines with Dials

Every function family is one basic machine with three dials, and the dials never change their jobs. Dial a: stretch and flip — bigger a, steeper or tighter; negative a, upside down. Dial p: the sideways slider with the flipped label — plus p in the bracket slides LEFT, minus p slides right. Dial q: the honest elevator — up for plus, down for minus, no tricks.

The parabola machine: base bowl y equals x squared; the dials move its lowest point around. Y equals two times x plus one squared minus eighteen is the bowl slid left one, dropped eighteen, doubled in steepness — turning point minus one semicolon minus eighteen without a single calculation. The hyperbola machine: two branches hugging a cross of asymptotes; p and q move the CROSS, and the branches follow. The exponential machine: a curve hugging one floor; q moves the floor, and the range reads straight off it — floor at two means y greater than two.

The sine and cosine machines are waves with the same dials plus one more: the number clamped onto x, which sets how fast the wave repeats — three x means three times the speed, period cut to one hundred and twenty degrees.

Reading a drawn graph is the machine question in reverse: find where the anchor moved — turning point, asymptote cross, floor, wave start — and the dial settings are the equation. Anchor first, dials second, every family, every time.

Here are this section's questions — name the machine, read the anchors, set the dials.

## Subtopic: One Circle, Every Triangle — and the Final Sweep

All of trigonometry is one circle wearing four costumes. An angle sweeps around; its sine is the height, its cosine is the shadow along the floor, and CAST just records where height and shadow point in each quarter. Reduction formulas are the circle's symmetry written as rules — one hundred and eighty minus x lands at the same height, so sine survives; three hundred and sixty minus x keeps the shadow, so cosine survives; one hundred and eighty plus x flips both. Nothing to memorise beyond the picture: same height, same sine; same shadow, same cosine.

General solutions are the circle refusing to stop spinning. Cosine equals a half at sixty degrees — and at its mirror below the floor line, minus sixty — and then again every full turn, forever. So the answer is a family: plus or minus sixty, plus k full turns. Sine's mirrors sit across the vertical, which is why sine answers carry two base angles — the reference and one hundred and eighty minus it; tangent repeats every half turn. Write the family, and if the question fences an interval, let k walk and keep what lands inside the fence.

The triangle rules are the circle's gift to surveying: they measure triangles no right angle ever visits. Own a side with its opposite angle — a matching pair? Sine rule. Two sides gripping the angle between them, or all three sides? Cosine rule — Pythagoras plus a correction term that never gets dropped. Want area with no height in sight? A half a b sine of the INCLUDED angle.

The final sweep — four traps, four antidotes. Ghost roots from squaring: substitute every answer back, reject the ghost in writing. The sign of p: the bracket lies, the anchor tells the truth — read the turning point or asymptote from the graph when in doubt. The lost second family: sine and cosine almost always offer two base angles; write both before adding the k turns. The non-included angle in the cosine or area rule: the angle must sit BETWEEN the two sides you used. And the closing habit that turns near-marks into marks: substitute roots back, tap special angles into your working as checks, and confirm a computed triangle side is longer opposite the bigger angle. The circle, the machine dials and delta all audit themselves — collect the free proof every time.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — one circle, three dials, one warning light, and every ghost sent home.
