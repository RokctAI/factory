# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the revision sweep for the geometry and applications half of the year — circle geometry, analytical geometry with inclination, statistics with deviation and outliers, and the finance-and-probability pair. Four topics, four toolkits: a set of circle theorems that name themselves in diagrams, one link between gradient and angle, one recipe for spread, and two decay directions for money plus one independence test for events. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — compressed, worked through fresh numbers, and pointed at the traps that cost candidates the same marks in the final exams every year.

## Subtopic: Circle Geometry — the Theorem Toolkit

The circle theorems arrive in three families, and every riders question is a chain built from them.

Family one, the centre theorems. The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord — and its converse runs both ways. The classic calculation: radius seventeen, chord sitting eight from the centre. The radius to the chord's endpoint completes a right-angled triangle: half-chord equals the root of, seventeen squared minus eight squared — the root of two hundred and twenty-five — fifteen. Full chord: thirty. Pythagoras hides inside every chord question. And the angle at the centre is TWICE the angle at the circumference on the same arc: centre one hundred and thirty degrees means circumference sixty-five. Its famous child: the angle in a semicircle is ninety degrees, because the centre angle there is a straight line.

Family two, the same-arc theorems. Angles at the circumference on the same arc, same side, are equal — the arc is the anchor; find which arc subtends each angle and equal angles reveal themselves. In a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles are supplementary: one angle of one hundred and ten degrees forces its opposite to seventy. An exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle.

Family three, the tangent theorems. A tangent meets its radius at ninety degrees — an instant right angle wherever a tangent touches. Two tangents from the same external point are equal — an instant isosceles triangle. And the tan-chord theorem: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the ALTERNATE segment — the theorem that unlocks the hardest riders, because it teleports an angle across the circle.

The working discipline: every statement in a geometry answer carries its reason, short and standard — angle at centre; angles same arc; opposite angles cyclic quad; tan-chord. A correct angle without its reason is a mark half-earned.

This section's questions are with you now — name the family, quote the reason, and let Pythagoras handle the chords.

## Subtopic: Analytical Geometry — Gradient, Inclination and Lines

Grade eleven adds one powerful link to the coordinate toolkit: the gradient IS the tangent of the inclination angle — m equals tan theta, where theta is measured from the positive x-axis, anticlockwise, between nought and one hundred and eighty degrees.

Run it forwards: a line through A, one semicolon minus two, and B, five semicolon six. Gradient: six minus minus two, over five minus one — eight quarters — two. Inclination: theta is the angle whose tangent is two — sixty-three comma four degrees. Run it backwards: a line at sixty degrees has gradient tan sixty — root three. And handle the negative case with care: a gradient of minus two gives a reference of sixty-three comma four, but inclination lives between nought and one eighty, so theta is one hundred and eighty minus sixty-three comma four — one hundred and sixteen comma six degrees. Negative gradient always means an obtuse inclination.

The line equation follows from one point and the gradient: y minus y-one equals m, times, x minus x-one. Through B with gradient two: y minus six equals two, times, x minus five. Parallel lines copy the gradient; perpendicular lines flip and negate — the partner of two is minus a half, product minus one.

The integrated question chains it all: find a gradient from two points, convert to an inclination, build a perpendicular through a third point, intersect two lines by solving simultaneously. Each step is one tool; the exam merely strings them. Sketch first, always — the sketch predicts the sign of every gradient and catches the obtuse-angle case before it costs marks.

Take this section's questions now — tan theta on the forwards run, one eighty minus on the negative run, and a sketch above it all.

## Subtopic: Statistics — Ogives, Deviation and Outliers

Grade eleven statistics upgrades spread from the crude range to the professional pair: variance and standard deviation. The recipe for a data set — three; five; nine; eleven; twelve. Mean: forty over five — eight. Deviations from the mean: minus five, minus three, one, three, four. Square them: twenty-five, nine, one, nine, sixteen. Average the squares: sixty over five — twelve. That is the VARIANCE. Standard deviation: its square root — about three comma four six. One number that hears every value, measuring how far the data typically strays from its mean. Small deviation: data huddled close. Large: data scattered wide.

The ogive — the cumulative frequency curve — answers position questions for grouped data. Plot cumulative totals against UPPER class boundaries; the curve climbs to n. Then read: half of n across, down to the axis — the median. Quarter and three-quarters — the quartiles. The ogive turns where the histogram peaks, and reading it is pure geometry: across, touch, down.

Outliers get a precise fence in grade eleven. Compute the interquartile range; the fences sit at the lower quartile minus one comma five times the IQR, and the upper quartile plus one comma five times the IQR. Lower quartile fifteen, upper twenty-five: IQR ten, fences at nought and forty. A value of forty-four stands beyond the fence — an outlier by definition, not by opinion. And outliers drag the mean while barely touching the median — which is why a mean far above its median is itself evidence of large values pulling rightward, skewing the data.

This section's questions are ready — square the strays for deviation, walk the ogive across and down, and let the fences name the outliers.

## Subtopic: Finance and Probability — Decay, Effective Rates and Independence

Money moves in two directions, each with two speeds. Growth you know; grade eleven adds DECAY. Straight-line depreciation is simple interest in reverse: A equals P, times, one minus i n. A bakkie of one hundred and twenty thousand rand losing twenty percent per year for three years: one hundred and twenty thousand times, one minus nought comma six — forty-eight thousand rand. Reducing-balance depreciation is compound in reverse: A equals P, times, one minus i, to the power n. Same bakkie, same rate: one hundred and twenty thousand times nought comma eight cubed — sixty-one thousand four hundred and forty rand. Reducing balance keeps more value, because each year's twenty percent bites a smaller amount. Same rate, thirteen thousand four hundred and forty rand apart — the formula choice IS the question.

Then the honesty test for advertised rates. A nominal rate of eighteen percent per year, compounded monthly, is not really eighteen: each month applies one comma five percent, and compounding twelve times gives one comma zero one five to the twelfth, minus one — nineteen comma five six percent. That is the EFFECTIVE annual rate, the true price of the money. Nominal is the label; effective is the contents.

Probability's grade eleven centrepiece is independence. Two events are independent when one happening tells you nothing about the other — and the test is multiplication: P of A and B must equal P of A, times, P of B. Check: P of A nought comma three, P of B nought comma six, P of both nought comma one eight. Product: nought comma one eight. Equal — independent, proven, not assumed. Contingency tables run this test on real counts; tree diagrams multiply along branches and add across them, and dependent events change the second branch's probabilities because the first draw altered the pool.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: a circle angle stated without its reason. Exhibit two: an obtuse inclination reported as its acute reference. Exhibit three: deviations averaged without squaring — they always sum to zero. Exhibit four: straight-line depreciation where the words reducing balance appeared. Each exhibit costs the same mark every year — except to the candidates who rehearsed them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — decay matched to its formula, rates converted to their true price, and independence proven by multiplication.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision as three pictures — a detective's circle, a data set telling its own story, and money on two downhill slopes — with the habits that make each automatic.

## Subtopic: The Circle as a Crime Scene

Treat every circle geometry rider like a crime scene, where the theorems are your forensic kit and each mark on the diagram is evidence. The centre is witness one: any line from it to a chord's midpoint testifies at ninety degrees, and any chord question hides a right-angled triangle — radius as hypotenuse, half-chord as a leg, Pythagoras closing the case. Radius seventeen, distance eight: half-chord fifteen, chord thirty.

Witness two is the arc. The same arc seen from the centre and from the rim tells the same story at two scales — the centre's angle is exactly double. Seen from two rim seats on the same side, the story is identical — equal angles. The skill is asking every angle: which arc are you watching? Angles watching the same arc are related; angles watching different arcs are strangers.

Witness three is the tangent: it always testifies at ninety degrees to the radius, and the tan-chord theorem lets it whisper an angle into the alternate segment — the far side of the chord — which is usually the missing link in the hardest riders.

The procedure at the scene: mark every given angle on the diagram, chase consequences one theorem at a time, and write each statement WITH its reason — the reason is half the mark, every time. When stuck, hunt for the three high-value patterns: a diameter — ninety degrees waiting; a cyclic quadrilateral — opposites summing to one eighty; and a tangent — a right angle plus the alternate-segment whisper.

Your questions for this section are ready — interrogate the arcs, trust the tangent, and file every reason.

## Subtopic: Data That Tells on Itself

A data set is a witness that cannot lie if you question it properly. The mean is its centre of gravity; the standard deviation is how far the values typically wander from home. The recipe never changes: find the mean, measure every deviation, square the strays so the negatives cannot cancel the positives, average the squares, take the root to return to the original units. Three; five; nine; eleven; twelve: mean eight, squared strays averaging twelve, deviation about three comma four six. Why square at all? Because deviations always sum to zero — the mean sits exactly where they cancel — and squaring is what stops the cover-up.

The ogive is the data climbing a staircase: each class adds its count, and the curve records the running total. Questions about position — median, quartiles, how many below sixty — are all the same move: go across from the count, touch the curve, drop to the value. Across, touch, down.

The outlier fence turns suspicion into arithmetic: one comma five interquartile ranges beyond either quartile. Inside the fences, ordinary; beyond them, outlier — by definition, not by debate. And the outlier's fingerprints show in the summary numbers: it drags the mean toward itself while the median barely moves, so a big gap between mean and median is the data telling on its own extremes. When that gap appears, report the median as the honest centre and say why — that sentence is a mark.

Here are this section's questions — square the strays, walk the staircase, and let the fences do the judging.

## Subtopic: Two Slopes, One Honest Rate — and the Final Sweep

Picture value rolling downhill in two ways. The straight-line slope is a ramp: the same rand amount lost every year — twenty percent of the ORIGINAL price, three years, sixty percent gone, the one hundred and twenty thousand rand bakkie worth forty-eight thousand. The reducing-balance slope is a ball bouncing lower: each year loses twenty percent of what REMAINS, so the losses shrink as the value shrinks — nought comma eight cubed leaves sixty-one thousand four hundred and forty rand. The ramp hits zero and keeps conceptually falling; the bouncing ball never quite reaches the floor. The words in the question choose the slope: straight line or on cost names the ramp; reducing balance or on book value names the ball.

The nominal-versus-effective story is a label-versus-contents story. Eighteen percent compounded monthly is a label; the contents are one comma five percent applied twelve times, compounding to nineteen comma five six. Whenever compounding happens more often than yearly, the effective rate beats the label — and comparing two investments fairly means converting both labels to contents first.

Probability's simplest deep idea: independence is a claim you TEST, never assume. Multiply the separate probabilities; if the product matches the joint probability, the events genuinely ignore each other — nought comma three times nought comma six landing exactly on the recorded nought comma one eight. On tree diagrams the same idea lives in the branches: independent trees repeat the same probabilities at every level; dependent trees update them, because the first event changed the pool.

The final sweep — four traps, four antidotes. A reasonless circle statement: no theorem name, no mark — file the reason every time. The acute angle reported for a falling line: negative gradient means one eighty minus the reference. Deviations averaged unsquared: they sum to zero by design — square first. The ramp used where the ball was named: circle the words reducing balance before computing. And the closing habit: audit every answer against its own picture — a chord shorter than its diameter, an effective rate above its nominal label, probabilities between nought and one, a standard deviation that shrinks when data huddles. Every topic in this lesson checks itself, free — collect the proof before the marker looks.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — two slopes told apart, every label opened, and every reason filed.
