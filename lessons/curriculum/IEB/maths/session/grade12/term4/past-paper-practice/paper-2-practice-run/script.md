# Part 1 — Expert

## Subtopic: Question One — Statistics and the Box and Whisker

The question: the marks of ten learners are eighteen, forty-one, forty-eight, fifty-five, fifty-nine, sixty-three, sixty-six, seventy-one, seventy-eight, ninety-one. One point one: calculate the mean, two marks. One point two: calculate the standard deviation, two marks. One point three: write the five-number summary and draw the box and whisker diagram, four marks. One point four: determine, with calculation, whether the data contains an outlier, three marks. One point five: how many of the marks lie within one standard deviation of the mean, two marks. Thirteen marks in total.

The attack plan: calculator statistics mode for the first two parts, ordered-list reading for the quartiles, the fence formula for the outlier, and an interval count at the end.

One point one: the sum is five hundred and ninety, over ten — the mean is fifty-nine. Setup mark, answer mark. One point two: the calculator delivers the standard deviation as nineteen comma three three — method mark for the mode entry, answer mark. One point three: minimum eighteen, lower quartile forty-eight, median sixty-one — the mean of fifty-nine and sixty-three — upper quartile seventy-one, maximum ninety-one. Two marks for the summary, two for a box correctly spanning the quartiles with whiskers to the extremes. One point four: the interquartile range is twenty-three, so the lower fence is forty-eight minus one comma five times twenty-three — thirteen comma five. Eighteen sits above thirteen comma five, so the data contains no outlier — and that verdict is the point: the eye suspects eighteen, the calculation acquits it. Formula mark, calculation mark, conclusion mark. One point five: the interval runs from thirty-nine comma seven to seventy-eight comma three; counting the ordered list places eight of the ten marks inside. Interval mark, count mark.

## Subtopic: Question Two — Regression and the Fenced Prediction

The question: a teacher records hours of practice against test percentage for six learners — two hours, thirty-five percent; four hours, forty-five; six hours, fifty; eight hours, sixty-two; ten hours, sixty-eight; twelve hours, seventy-nine. Two point one: determine the equation of the least squares regression line, four marks. Two point two: write down the correlation coefficient and comment on its strength, two marks. Two point three: predict the percentage after nine hours of practice, two marks. Two point four: a classmate uses the line to predict the mark after twenty hours; give two criticisms, two marks. Ten marks in total.

The attack plan: everything before the criticism is calculator craft — enter the six pairs once, check the entry, then read off a, b, and r. The last part is a fence argument.

Two point one: the calculator returns a equal to twenty-six comma four and b equal to four comma three, so y hat equals twenty-six comma four, plus four comma three x. One mark for data entry evidenced by correct coefficients, one for a, one for b, and one for the equation written with y hat. Two point two: r equals nought comma nine nine six — very strong positive linear correlation; the value is one mark, the strength-and-direction sentence the other. Two point three: substitute nine: twenty-six comma four plus four comma three times nine gives approximately sixty-five percent. Substitution mark, answer mark — and nine sits inside the data range of two to twelve hours, so the prediction is trustworthy interpolation. Two point four: criticism one, twenty hours lies far outside the observed range, so this is extrapolation and the linear trend is unverified there. Criticism two, the context itself bends — a test percentage cannot pass one hundred, so the straight line must fail eventually. One mark per stated criticism.

## Subtopic: Question Three — the Analytical Triangle

The question: in the Cartesian plane, P is minus four semicolon one, Q is two semicolon five, R is six semicolon minus one. Three point one: calculate the gradient of P Q, two marks. Three point two: prove that angle P Q R is a right angle, three marks. Three point three: calculate the length of P Q, leaving the answer in simplified surd form, two marks. Three point four: determine the coordinates of M, the midpoint of P R, and explain why M is equidistant from P, Q and R, three marks. Three point five: calculate the size of the angle of inclination of Q R, two marks. Twelve marks in total.

The attack plan: gradient formula twice for the right angle, distance formula for the length, and the semicircle theorem imported for the midpoint part — this paper loves the moment analytical tools and circle theorems meet. Then inclination with its obtuse correction.

Three point one: gradient P Q equals five minus one, over two minus minus four — four over six, two thirds. Substitution mark, answer mark. Three point two: gradient Q R equals minus one minus five, over six minus two — minus six over four, minus three halves. One mark. The product: two thirds times minus three halves equals minus one — one mark. Perpendicular lines, therefore angle P Q R is ninety degrees — conclusion with reason, one mark. Three point three: length P Q is the square root of, six squared plus four squared — root fifty-two — two root thirteen. Substitution mark, simplified surd mark. Three point four: M is the average of P and R: one semicolon nought — one mark. Since angle Q is ninety degrees, P R is a diameter of the circle through P, Q and R — the angle in a semicircle reversed — so the midpoint of P R is the centre, equidistant from all three vertices. Two marks for that argument, and a quick check confirms it: each of the three distances from M comes out at root twenty-six. Three point five: tan theta equals minus three halves gives a calculator angle of minus fifty-six comma three one degrees; inclination lives between nought and one hundred and eighty, so add one hundred and eighty: one hundred and twenty-three comma six nine degrees. Corrected answer with the tan statement — a mark each.

## Subtopic: Question Four — the Circle and Its Tangent

The question: given the circle x squared plus y squared plus eight x minus six y equals zero. Four point one: determine the centre and radius, four marks. Four point two: show that the point minus one semicolon seven lies on the circle, two marks. Four point three: determine the equation of the tangent to the circle at minus one semicolon seven, four marks. Four point four: calculate the length of the tangent from the external point four semicolon nine to its point of contact, three marks. Thirteen marks in total.

The attack plan: complete the square, substitute to verify, then tangent perpendicular to radius, and finally Pythagoras on the external-point triangle.

Four point one: group and complete both squares — x squared plus eight x plus sixteen, plus y squared minus six y plus nine, equals sixteen plus nine. One mark for each completed square. The form: x plus four all squared plus y minus three all squared equals twenty-five — centre minus four semicolon three, one mark; radius five, one mark. Four point two: substitute minus one and seven into the centre form: three squared plus four squared is nine plus sixteen — twenty-five, equal to the radius squared, so the point lies on the circle. Substitution mark, conclusion mark. Four point three: the radius to minus one semicolon seven has gradient seven minus three over minus one minus minus four — four thirds, one mark. The tangent is perpendicular to this radius — stating the reason is a mark — so its gradient is minus three quarters, one mark. Point-gradient form: y minus seven equals minus three quarters times x plus one, which tidies to y equals minus three quarters x plus twenty-five over four — final mark. Four point four: the distance from four semicolon nine to the centre squared is eight squared plus six squared — one hundred, one mark. The tangent, radius, and this line form a right triangle at the point of contact — tangent perpendicular to radius again — so the tangent length is the square root of one hundred minus twenty-five, one mark. Root seventy-five — five root three, approximately eight comma six six — answer mark.

## Subtopic: Question Five — Identities and General Solutions

The question. Five point one: determine, WITHOUT a calculator, the value of sin fifteen degrees in simplest surd form, four marks. Five point two: prove the identity one minus cos two x, over sin two x, equals tan x, four marks. Five point three: determine the general solution of sin x equals minus root three over two, four marks. Five point four: if cos x equals five thirteenths and x is acute, calculate sin two x and cos two x without finding x, four marks. Sixteen marks in total.

The attack plan: split fifteen into forty-five minus thirty; choose the face of cos two x that annihilates the one; reference angle then quadrants for the general solution; and a five-twelve-thirteen triangle for the last part.

Five point one: sin of forty-five minus thirty equals sin forty-five cos thirty minus cos forty-five sin thirty — the expansion off the compound angle formula, one mark. Substituting the special values — root two over two, root three over two, and a half — two marks. The tidy answer: root six minus root two, all over four — final mark, exact, no decimals. Five point two: left side numerator: choosing cos two x as one minus two sin squared x makes one minus cos two x collapse to two sin squared x — two marks, because the face choice is the examined skill. Denominator: sin two x becomes two sin x cos x — one mark. Cancel two sin x: sin x over cos x, which is tan x, equal to the right side — final mark. Five point three: the reference angle for root three over two is sixty degrees — one mark. Sine is negative in the third and fourth quadrants — one mark. General solution: x equals two hundred and forty degrees plus k times three hundred and sixty, or x equals three hundred degrees plus k times three hundred and sixty, with k an integer — two marks, and the k-statement is part of the answer. Five point four: cos x five thirteenths with x acute puts sin x at twelve thirteenths via the five-twelve-thirteen triangle — one mark. Sin two x: two times twelve thirteenths times five thirteenths — one hundred and twenty over one hundred and sixty-nine, one mark and the double angle formula quoted is another. Cos two x: twenty-five over one hundred and sixty-nine minus one hundred and forty-four over one hundred and sixty-nine — minus one hundred and nineteen over one hundred and sixty-nine — final mark, and the negative sign is the tell that two x has passed ninety degrees.

## Subtopic: Question Six — Triangles in Two and Three Dimensions

The question. Six point one: in triangle A B C, side a equals fifteen units, angle A equals seventy degrees, angle B equals forty-four degrees; calculate side b, three marks. Six point two: a triangle has sides eight and eleven enclosing an angle of fifty degrees; calculate the third side and the area of the triangle, six marks. Six point three: from a point thirty-two metres from the base of a vertical flagpole, the angle of elevation of the top is forty-two degrees; calculate the height of the flagpole, three marks. Twelve marks in total.

The attack plan: a side facing a known angle summons the sine rule; two sides trapping an angle summon the cosine rule and the area rule from the same data; the flagpole is one right triangle with tan.

Six point one: the sine rule — b over sin B equals a over sin A, one mark. Substitute: b equals fifteen sin forty-four over sin seventy — one mark. The answer: approximately eleven comma zero nine units — one mark. A quick sanity glance: the smaller angle faces the shorter side, forty-four against seventy, eleven against fifteen — consistent. Six point two: the cosine rule — the third side squared equals sixty-four plus one hundred and twenty-one, minus two times eight times eleven times cos fifty — formula and substitution, two marks. That is one hundred and eighty-five minus one hundred and thirteen comma one three — seventy-one comma eight seven, one mark — and the side is the square root, approximately eight comma four eight — one mark. The area rule: a half times eight times eleven times sin fifty — one mark — giving approximately thirty-three comma seven one square units — one mark. Six point three: the flagpole is opposite the forty-two degree angle with the thirty-two metre distance adjacent, so the height is thirty-two tan forty-two — setup and tan statement, two marks. Approximately twenty-eight comma eight one metres — answer mark, with units, because a length without units is an incomplete answer in geometry.

## Subtopic: Question Seven — the Euclidean Rider

The question: D A E is a tangent to a circle at A. Chords A B and A C are drawn, with B and C on the circle so that the angle between the tangent and chord A B — angle E A B — equals thirty-five degrees, and the angle between the tangent and chord A C on the other side — angle D A C — equals seventy-two degrees. Seven point one: write down, with a reason, the size of angle A C B, two marks. Seven point two: write down, with a reason, the size of angle A B C, two marks. Seven point three: calculate the size of angle B A C, two marks. Seven point four: prove the theorem that the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment, five marks. Eleven marks in total.

The attack plan: the tan-chord theorem twice — once on each side of the tangent — then the angle sum of a triangle. The proof is one of the classic performances every candidate should have rehearsed, delivered with its construction.

Seven point one: the angle in the alternate segment to angle E A B is angle A C B, so angle A C B equals thirty-five degrees — statement mark, and the reason, tan chord theorem, is the second mark. Seven point two: on the other side, angle D A C corresponds to the angle in its alternate segment, angle A B C, so angle A B C equals seventy-two degrees — again statement mark plus the tan chord reason mark. Seven point three: the angles of triangle A B C sum to one hundred and eighty degrees, so angle B A C equals one hundred and eighty minus thirty-five minus seventy-two — seventy-three degrees. Calculation mark, and the reason — angle sum in triangle — the second mark. Notice the check: seventy-three plus thirty-five plus seventy-two restores one hundred and eighty exactly.

Seven point four, the proof, delivered as a performance. Construction: draw the diameter A O F from the point of contact, and join F to B — construction mark. The angle between the tangent and the diameter is ninety degrees, since the tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact — one mark. The angle A B F, subtended by the diameter, is ninety degrees, angle in a semicircle — one mark. So the angle F A B equals ninety minus angle A F B, from the angle sum in triangle A B F; and the angle between the tangent and chord A B equals ninety minus angle F A B — which makes it equal to angle A F B — one mark. But angle A F B and angle A C B are angles in the same segment on chord A B, therefore equal — and so the tangent-chord angle equals the angle in the alternate segment — final mark.
