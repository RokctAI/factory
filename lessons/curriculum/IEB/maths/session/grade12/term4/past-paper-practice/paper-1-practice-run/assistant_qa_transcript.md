**Mandy:** Questions from the practice run! First: on the real day, is it better to work the paper strictly in order, or hunt for the easiest questions first?

**Tutor:** In order, with a disciplined ejector seat — that is the strategy strong scripts almost always share. A well-set paper tends to open with its most routine work: the algebra opener is there to be banked quickly and calmly, and skipping around costs time in page-turning and costs composure in fragments. So start at question one, work at examination pace, and carry one rule: any subquestion that stalls for more than a self-honest while gets a margin mark and is abandoned mid-sentence — not wrestled. The return trip at the end, with the routine marks already secured, is a different psychological country: you know the paper, the panic is gone, and stalled questions often open on the second visit. Two refinements. Within a long question, later parts frequently do not depend on the stalled part — a part four often uses the part one answer, not the part three one, so read on before abandoning the whole question. And follow-through marking is your safety net: when an earlier slip is carried into later working that is otherwise correct, the later reasoning can still earn — which is exactly why you never leave a part blank when a method is available. A wrong number worked correctly is worth more than a blank space.

**Mandy:** The first-principles question is five marks for a derivative we could write down instantly. Why does the paper insist on the long way, and where do the five marks actually sit?

**Tutor:** The question is examining the definition, not the answer — first principles is calculus's birth certificate, and this is the year you prove you have seen it. The five marks sit on five visible commitments. One: the definition itself, f prime of x equals the limit as h tends to nought of, f of x plus h minus f of x, over h — written with the limit symbol, not paraphrased. Two: the correct expansion of f of x plus h, where the common casualty is the middle term of the square — two times x plus h squared must produce the four x h. Three: the subtraction, in which every term without an h must die; if anything survives without an h, the expansion above was wrong, which makes this line a free self-check. Four: division by h, legitimate precisely because h is approaching nought rather than equal to it. Five: the limit taken, four x plus two h becoming four x, with the limit symbol having survived on every line until this one. The same two losses appear over and over in practice scripts: the limit symbol vanishing after line one, and candidates who write the power-rule answer first and then reverse-engineer the working — which reads exactly like what it is. Perform the ritual honestly, top to bottom, and it is the most predictable five marks you will ever earn.

**Mandy:** In the finance question, what separates the scripts that get all four payment marks from the ones that get two?

**Tutor:** Three separators, all visible before any arithmetic starts. First, formula selection: a loan being repaid is a PRESENT value annuity — the bank gives money now, the payments discharge it — while savings growing toward a target is future value; both formulas exist, and choosing between them is the examined skill. Scripts that reach for future value on a loan lose the plot immediately. Second, the rate and count conversion: nine comma six percent per annum compounded monthly means i is nought comma zero nine six over twelve, and twenty-five years of monthly payments means n is three hundred. The pair must convert together — an annual i with a monthly n is the most common two-mark donation in the whole finance section. Third, the timeline: payments starting one month after the loan is the formula's home scenario; any deviation — a delayed start, a deposit, a final short payment — shifts the exponent, and only a sketched timeline makes the shift visible. The scripts that score four from four write the formula, declare i and n explicitly on their own line, substitute once, and round only the final answer to the cent. The scripts that score two substitute a half-converted rate into a half-remembered formula and round in the middle. The mathematics is identical; the bookkeeping is the difference.

**Mandy:** Last one: after finishing this practice run, what is the highest-value way to use the remaining practice papers?

**Tutor:** Rehearse, mark, autopsy — in that cycle, every time. Rehearse: one full paper in one sitting at examination pace, formula summary beside you only if you would have it on the day, no notes, no pausing to check — the point is recreating the real conditions, including the discomfort. Mark: against a full worked solution, mark by mark, the same way a marker would — method marks, accuracy marks, follow-through where an earlier slip fed a later part. Scoring yourself generously is the most common way candidates lie to themselves in the final stretch; score like a stranger. Autopsy: every dropped mark gets classified into one of three bins. Knowledge gaps — a method you could not produce — send you back to the relevant lesson for a targeted rebuild. Execution slips — sign errors, early rounding, a missing second root — get written into the error log as one-line rules, because slips recur until named. Strategy losses — time spent camping, parts left blank that had scorable method — get fixed by adjusting the ejector-seat rule next run. Then the next paper measures whether each bin shrank. Three or four honest cycles beat ten papers worked casually with the solutions open, because the exam is a performance, and performances improve through rehearsal conditions, not through reading the sheet music.
