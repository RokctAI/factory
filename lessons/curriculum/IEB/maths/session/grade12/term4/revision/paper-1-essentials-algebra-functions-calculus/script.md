# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the whole algebra side of the course folded into one revision sweep — algebra and equations, sequences and series, functions with their inverses, finance, differential calculus, and probability. Every mark you will meet on that material belongs to a method you have already used this year; today the methods get reorganised into a revision order, each one carrying a fresh worked example and its classic trap, so that nothing on the day arrives as a stranger.

## Subtopic: Algebra and Equations — the Opening Moves

An algebra section usually opens a paper, and the opening question is deliberately the friendliest — factorise if the factors show themselves, formula if they do not. Solve five x squared plus two x minus four equals zero, correct to two decimal places. The phrase two decimal places is itself the instruction: the trinomial will not factorise, so go straight to the quadratic formula. X equals minus b, plus or minus the square root of b squared minus four a c, all over two a. Substitute: minus two, plus or minus the square root of four plus eighty, over ten — the square root of eighty-four. Two answers: x is approximately nought comma seven two, or minus one comma one two. Keep every decimal in the calculator until the final line; rounding the square root early drags the second decimal off target and costs the accuracy mark.

The k-method, this year's own trick: solve two to the power two x, minus twelve times two to the power x, plus thirty-two, equals zero. Let k equal two to the power x. The equation becomes k squared minus twelve k plus thirty-two equals zero, which factorises as k minus four, times k minus eight. So two to the x equals four, giving x equals two, or two to the x equals eight, giving x equals three. The substitution turns an alien equation into a familiar quadratic — that is the whole method.

Surd equations demand a check. Solve: the square root of three x plus one, equals x minus three. Square both sides: three x plus one equals x squared minus six x plus nine. Bring everything left: x squared minus nine x plus eight equals zero, so x minus eight times x minus one equals zero, giving x equals eight or x equals one. Now test both in the original. X equals eight: square root of twenty-five is five, and eight minus three is five — valid. X equals one: square root of four is two, but one minus three is minus two — rejected, because a square root is never negative. Squaring manufactures candidates; the original equation vets them, and a mark waits on the vetting.

Quadratic inequalities live in the world of graphs: solve x plus one, times x minus six, less than zero. The parabola through roots minus one and six opens upward, so it is negative between the roots: minus one less than x less than six. Sketch the parabola every time — the picture chooses between outside and between.

Last of the set, simultaneous equations, one linear and one not: y equals two x plus one, and x y equals ten. Substitute the linear into the product: x times two x plus one equals ten. Tidy: two x squared plus x minus ten equals zero, factorise to two x plus five times x minus two. X equals two with y equals five, or x equals minus five halves with y equals minus four. Pairs, always — an x without its y is half an answer.

## Subtopic: Sequences and Series — Patterns With a Formula

Patterns reward recognition by differences. The pattern four; eleven; twenty-two; thirty-seven has first differences seven, eleven, fifteen — not constant — and second differences four, four — constant. Constant second difference means quadratic: T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c, with two a equal to the second difference, so a equals two. Then three a plus b equals seven gives b equals one, and a plus b plus c equals four gives c equals one: T n equals two n squared plus n plus one. Check it: two plus one plus one is four, eight plus two plus one is eleven — the formula is verified against the data before it is trusted. Which term equals one hundred and seventy-two? Set two n squared plus n minus one hundred and seventy-one equal to zero, factorise as n minus nine times two n plus nineteen, and since n counts terms, n equals nine. Reject the negative candidate out loud — that rejection is a mark.

Geometric sequences run on a constant ratio. The series two hundred, plus one hundred, plus fifty, and so on, has a equal to two hundred and r equal to one half. Because r sits strictly between minus one and one, the sum to infinity exists: a over one minus r, which is two hundred over a half — four hundred. The convergence condition is not decoration; state it, because the marker looks for minus one less than r less than one before accepting the sum.

The favourite twist: for which smallest n does S n first exceed three hundred and ninety-nine? S n equals four hundred times, one minus a half to the power n. Exceeding three hundred and ninety-nine forces a half to the power n below one over four hundred. A half to the eighth is one over two hundred and fifty-six — not small enough; a half to the ninth is one over five hundred and twelve — small enough. So the smallest n is nine. Check: S nine is about three hundred and ninety-nine comma two — past the target, as promised.

Sigma notation is a set of instructions, not a monster: the sum from k equals one to twenty-five of two k plus three means substitute k from one to twenty-five and add. Arithmetic with first term five, common difference two, twenty-five terms: S n equals n over two, times two a plus n minus one d — twelve comma five times, ten plus forty-eight — seven hundred and twenty-five. The classic sigma trap is the count: a sum from k equals four to twenty-five has twenty-two terms, not twenty-one — upper minus lower, plus one.

Take this section's questions now — differences to diagnose, formulas to fit, and every count of terms done twice.

## Subtopic: Functions, Inverses and Finance

Every graph question walks the same checklist: shape, asymptotes, intercepts, then the special points. Take f of x equals four over x plus three, minus two. Hyperbola. Vertical asymptote where the denominator dies: x equals minus three. Horizontal asymptote from the shift: y equals minus two. Y-intercept at x equals nought: four over three, minus two, equals minus two thirds. X-intercept where f is zero: four over x plus three equals two, so x plus three equals two, x equals minus one. Four facts, four marks, before any sketching begins.

Inverses are this year's upgrade. The inverse undoes the function, and graphically it is the reflection across the line y equals x — every point a semicolon b becomes b semicolon a. For the exponential g of x equals two to the power x, the inverse is the logarithm: g inverse of x equals log base two of x. The point three semicolon eight on g becomes eight semicolon three on g inverse. Domain and range swap wholesale: g takes all real x to positive y, so g inverse takes positive x to all real y. And remember the restriction ritual for the parabola: y equals x squared only has an inverse function once the domain is cut to x greater than or equal to nought, or x less than or equal to nought — otherwise the reflection fails the vertical line test.

## Subtopic: Calculus and Probability — the Big Finish

Calculus opens with first principles, and the definition earns its marks verbatim: f prime of x equals the limit as h tends to nought of, f of x plus h, minus f of x, all over h. For f of x equals four x squared plus one: f of x plus h is four times x plus h squared plus one, which expands to four x squared plus eight x h plus four h squared plus one. Subtract f of x, and the surviving terms are eight x h plus four h squared. Divide by h: eight x plus four h. Let h tend to nought: f prime of x equals eight x. Writing the limit symbol on every line until it is used is not pedantry — it is a mark.

Then the rules, after rewriting: y equals two x to the fifth, minus three times the square root of x, plus four over x squared. Rewrite the square root as x to the half and the fraction as four x to the minus two. Differentiate term by term: ten x to the fourth, minus three halves x to the minus a half, minus eight x to the minus three. The rewriting step is where the marks hide — the rule only applies to powers of x, never to roots and fractions left in disguise.

Cubic graphs, the centrepiece. F of x equals x cubed plus three x squared minus four factorises as x minus one times x plus two squared: x-intercepts at one and at minus two, the double root meaning the graph touches at minus two; the y-intercept sits at minus four. Turning points where f prime, three x squared plus six x, is zero: x equals nought and x equals minus two. F of minus two is nought — local maximum sitting exactly on the intercept; f of nought is minus four — local minimum. The point of inflection, where concavity changes, at x equals minus one, y equals minus two — the midpoint of the turning points for any cubic. Optimisation questions are this same machinery pointed at a story: write the quantity as a function, differentiate, set the derivative to zero, and answer in the units of the story.

Probability closes with friendly marks. Independent events A and B with P of A equal to nought comma two five and P of B equal to nought comma four: independence means P of A and B is the product, nought comma one. The addition rule gives P of A or B: nought comma two five, plus nought comma four, minus nought comma one — nought comma five five. The counting principle: the letters of the word BANANA, six letters with A appearing three times and N twice, arrange in six factorial over, three factorial times two factorial, ways — sixty. Repeated letters divide; that division is the whole trick.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same material as a route map — where the effort should go, which question shapes come back again and again, and the short list of donations that weaker scripts make to the markers.

## Subtopic: The Map of Paper One

Picture this half of the course as a city with six districts: algebra and equations, number patterns, functions and graphs, finance, calculus, and probability. The districts are not equal. Functions and calculus are the two biggest — they carry the heaviest questions, they connect to each other, and almost every other district eventually routes through them: a pattern question ends in an equation, a finance question is an exponential function wearing money, an optimisation question is calculus wearing a story. Revision effort should lean toward those two districts, because depth there pays everywhere.

But the algebra district holds the most routine work: solve, solve, solve, inequality, simultaneous. Those opening methods should be banked calmly and quickly on the day, because momentum is a real mark-earner — a clean opening settles the hand for the harder districts ahead.

The map also says what every question is allowed to ask. Patterns can only be linear, quadratic, arithmetic or geometric — four species, no more. Finance is growth, decay, nominal versus effective, and the two annuity formulas. Probability is Venn diagrams, tree diagrams, the addition rule, independence, and the counting principle. Districts, not wilderness: each one has a fence around what can be examined, and revision is walking the fenced ground until it is familiar.

At examination pace the rule is simple: never camp in one district. A question that stalls gets a mark in the margin and a promise to return; the districts ahead hold marks that need no help from the one that stalled.

Your questions for this section are ready — six districts, their weights, and the route through them.

## Subtopic: Five Question Shapes That Repeat Every Year

Same shapes, new numbers — that is what a final paper is. Shape one: solve to two decimal places. Translation: the quadratic formula, because two decimal places is code for does not factorise. Shape two: an equation with x in the exponent twice, like two to the two x minus twelve times two to the x plus thirty-two — the k-substitution, turning it into a quadratic wearing a costume.

Shape three: here is a pattern, find the general term, then find which term equals some number. Differences diagnose the species — constant first difference, linear; constant second difference, quadratic; constant ratio, geometric. Then the second half is always the same move in reverse: set the formula equal to the number and solve for n, rejecting any negative or fractional n because term numbers count.

Shape four: the graph question, and its checklist never changes — shape, asymptotes, intercepts, special points, in that order. A hyperbola question and an exponential question ask the same four things wearing different equations. Inverses add one move: swap x and y, then make y the subject, and on the sketch reflect across the line y equals x.

Shape five: the cubic. Factorise for intercepts, differentiate and solve for turning points, and remember the double root touches while a single root cuts. Year after year the same five shapes carry the bulk of the marks; a learner who can perform them cold walks in owning most of the paper before the first page turns.

Here are this section's questions — five shapes, five reflexes, no surprises.

## Subtopic: The Donation List — Marks Given Away and How to Keep Them

Markers speak of donations: marks the script gives away that the mathematics never required. The donation list for this material is short and stable, and knowing it is as valuable as knowing a theorem.

Donation one: rounding too early. The square root of eighty-four gets rounded to nine comma two on line two, and by the final line the answer misses the expected nought comma seven two. Keep everything in the calculator; round once, at the end, to exactly the accuracy the question names.

Donation two: the unchecked surd answer. Squaring an equation invents candidates, and one of them usually fails the original. The check is one substitution per candidate — a single line that carries a full mark and, better, certainty.

Donation three: the missing second answer. Quadratics have two roots; simultaneous systems have two pairs; a general term question wants the n rejected as well as the n accepted. Whenever a square appeared anywhere in the working, ask where the second answer went.

Donation four: finance without a timeline. Payments that start one month after the loan, or a deposit that grows for a different number of months than the question implies — one small timeline sketch, dots for months, arrows for payments, prevents nearly every finance error worth preventing.

Donation five: notation in calculus. The limit written once and then abandoned, or d y d x used as if it were a fraction of its own accord. Write the definition in full, keep the limit symbol until h actually goes to nought, and the notation marks stay home.

The habit that collects them all: after every answer, one check that costs a line. Substitute back, multiply back, count again, or test a value. The paper rewards accuracy it can see, and the check makes accuracy visible.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the donation list read twice, and every check performed while the marks are still yours.
