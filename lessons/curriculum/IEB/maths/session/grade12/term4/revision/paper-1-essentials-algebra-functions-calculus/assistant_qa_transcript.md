**Mandy:** Revision questions from the class! First: with six topics on one paper, how do we split the remaining revision days without just doing everything thinly?

**Tutor:** Weight by connection, not by fear. Functions and calculus deserve the lion's share of the remaining days, for a structural reason: they are the districts every other district routes through. A pattern question ends in an equation; a finance question is an exponential function wearing money; an optimisation question is calculus wearing a story. Deep fluency there pays out across the whole paper, which is why thin, equal slices are the weakest possible plan. A workable split for a ten-day stretch: three days on functions and inverses, three on calculus, and one each on algebra, patterns, finance, and probability — with the algebra day early, because clean equation-solving feeds everything after it. Within each day, do not reread notes: work examples cold, from a blank page, and only open the notes when a method refuses to arrive. Rereading feels like progress and measures nothing; a blank-page attempt is an honest test that doubles as practice. And end every day with fifteen minutes in a different district from the one you studied — interleaving is uncomfortable and slightly slower, which is exactly why it sticks.

**Mandy:** Next: the k-substitution came up again today. How do we recognise, in the exam room, that an equation wants it?

**Tutor:** Look for the same base wearing two different exponents, one of them double the other — that is the fingerprint. Two to the two x alongside two to the x; three to the two x alongside three to the x; even nine to the x alongside three to the x, because nine to the x is three to the two x in disguise. The moment you see the double-and-single pair, name the single one k, and the equation collapses into a quadratic in k: today's two to the two x minus twelve times two to the x plus thirty-two became k squared minus twelve k plus thirty-two, factorising to k equals four or k equals eight. Then — and this is where scripts leak — remember that k was never the answer. Each k value must be translated back: two to the x equals four gives x equals two, two to the x equals eight gives x equals three. Two translations, two marks, and a candidate who stops at k equals four and eight has solved a different question. One more vetting instinct: since k stands for an exponential, k must be positive, so a negative k candidate is rejected with a sentence, exactly like a failed surd candidate. The substitution is mechanical; the translation back and the vetting are where the thinking — and the marks — live.

**Mandy:** The cubic keeps being called the centrepiece. What is the full list of things a cubic question can ask, so we can rehearse the whole anatomy once?

**Tutor:** The anatomy has six organs, and a full question simply tours them. One: intercepts — factorise for the x-intercepts, read the constant for the y-intercept, and classify each root: single cuts, double touches. Two: turning points — differentiate, set to zero, solve, then substitute back for the y-coordinates, because a turning point without its height is half a point. Three: classification — left of a maximum the function rises, or use the second derivative's sign if you prefer; either argument earns, but one must appear. Four: the point of inflection — second derivative zero, and for any cubic it sits exactly midway between the turning points, a fact that doubles as a check. Five: the sketch — which now assembles itself from the first four organs, and the marks are for the labelled features, not artistic beauty. Six: the interpretive add-ons — for which x is the graph increasing, for which x is it concave down, for which values of k does f of x equals k have three roots. That last one is answered entirely from the turning point heights: three roots exactly when k lies strictly between the minimum and maximum values. Rehearse the tour on today's example, x cubed plus three x squared minus four, and every future cubic is the same tour with new numbers.

**Mandy:** Last one: any advice for the night before and the morning of the paper?

**Tutor:** The night before is for consolidation, never for conquest — no new topics, because a half-learned method displaces three settled ones. Run one gentle circuit: the five question shapes, one example each, worked slowly with the checklist visible. Then read your donation list out loud — round at the end, check the surd, second answer, timeline, limit symbol — and go to sleep at a normal hour; a rested brain retrieves, a tired one reconstructs, and retrieval is faster and safer. The morning wants routine, not revision: eat, arrive early, and give the brain one warm-up — a single quadratic solved on scrap paper settles the hands the way scales settle a pianist. In the room, spend the reading time mapping the paper: mark the questions that look routine, and start there. First fifteen minutes at a deliberate, almost slow pace — early errors cost double, because they shake confidence as well as marks. And carry one sentence for the moment a question looks alien: it is one of the five shapes wearing a costume. Find the shape, and the method follows. That sentence has rescued more scripts than any formula, because it happens to be true.
