## Subtopic: Statistics and Regression — Method You Can Bank

Statistics holds the most calculator-driven marks of the year. Take ten test scores: fifteen, forty-two, forty-seven, fifty-three, fifty-seven, sixty, sixty-four, sixty-nine, seventy-four, eighty-nine. The mean: the sum, five hundred and seventy, over ten — fifty-seven. The standard deviation, straight from the calculator's statistics mode: nineteen comma zero three. Then the five-number summary for the box and whisker diagram: minimum fifteen, lower quartile forty-seven, median fifty-eight comma five, upper quartile sixty-nine, maximum eighty-nine.

Outliers are policed by the fence rule: lower quartile minus one comma five times the interquartile range. The interquartile range is sixty-nine minus forty-seven — twenty-two. The lower fence: forty-seven minus thirty-three, which is fourteen. Now the verdict that surprises people: fifteen sits above fourteen, so fifteen is not an outlier — the eye suspected it, the calculation acquitted it, and only the calculation earns. One more favourite: how many scores lie within one standard deviation of the mean? The interval runs from thirty-seven comma nine seven to seventy-six comma zero three, and counting the ordered list gives eight of the ten scores.

Regression pairs two variables. Daily rainfall in millimetres against umbrellas sold: six pairs, from five millimetres with eight umbrellas up to thirty millimetres with thirty-eight umbrellas. The calculator delivers the least squares line: y hat equals two comma zero seven, plus one comma one eight x, with correlation coefficient r equal to nought comma nine nine six — very strong positive linear association. Interpret the gradient in context: about one comma two more umbrellas sold for each extra millimetre of rain. Predict at twenty-two millimetres: about twenty-eight umbrellas — trustworthy, because twenty-two sits inside the data range. Predict at sixty millimetres and the duty changes: calculate if asked, then flag it as extrapolation beyond the observed range and decline to trust it. In statistics the refusal is a scoring answer.

## Subtopic: Trigonometry — the Largest Kingdom

Trigonometry is the broadest single territory on this side of the course, and it splits into identities, equations, and triangle rules. First the compound and double angle machinery. Cos of one hundred and five degrees, exactly: split it as sixty plus forty-five. Cos of A plus B equals cos A cos B minus sin A sin B. Substituting the special values — a half, root two over two, root three over two — gives root two minus root six, all over four. Negative, as it should be: one hundred and five degrees lives in the second quadrant, where cosine is negative, and noticing that is a free check. The exactness is the point: no calculator decimal earns the marks.

Double angles compress: sin two x equals two sin x cos x, and cos two x wears three faces — cos squared minus sin squared, two cos squared minus one, one minus two sin squared. Choosing the right face is the skill. Given cos x equals three fifths with x acute, the three-four-five triangle gives sin x equals four fifths, so sin two x equals two times four fifths times three fifths — twenty-four over twenty-five — and cos two x equals nine over twenty-five minus sixteen over twenty-five — minus seven over twenty-five. Identities reward the same discipline: prove sin two x, over one plus cos two x, equals tan x. Replace sin two x with two sin x cos x, and one plus cos two x with two cos squared x — the face chosen as two cos squared x minus one, precisely because it kills the one. Cancel two cos x, and the left side becomes sin x over cos x — tan x. Start from one side, state restrictions if asked, never multiply across the equals sign.

Equations want all solutions. Sin x equals a half: the reference angle is thirty degrees, sine is positive in the first and second quadrants, so the general solution is x equals thirty degrees plus k times three hundred and sixty, or x equals one hundred and fifty degrees plus k times three hundred and sixty, k an integer. The general solution first, always — then restrict to the demanded interval by choosing k.

Triangles close the kingdom. Sine rule when a side faces a known angle; cosine rule when two sides trap an angle or three sides are known. A side facing forty-eight degrees, with a side of fourteen facing seventy-five degrees: the sine rule gives fourteen sin forty-eight over sin seventy-five — approximately ten comma seven seven. Two sides six and ten with a one hundred and twenty degree angle between them: the cosine rule gives the third side squared as thirty-six plus one hundred, minus two times six times ten times cos one hundred and twenty — and since cos one hundred and twenty is minus a half, that is one hundred and thirty-six plus sixty: one hundred and ninety-six, a perfect square, third side fourteen exactly. The area rule from the same data: a half times six times ten times sin one hundred and twenty — about twenty-five comma nine eight square units. In three-dimensional problems, solve one triangle at a time and carry the shared side across.

## Subtopic: Euclidean Geometry — Theorems, Reasons, Riders

Euclidean geometry pays its marks in a currency of reasons: every statement in a proof earns only when its reason is stated in the accepted wording. A handful of classic theorem proofs deserve rehearsal until they are word-perfect — the angle at the centre, the tangent-chord theorem, the proportionality theorem, and equiangular triangles being similar are the performances that repay the practice most. Learn each one as a performance — diagram, construction, statements, reasons — because a proof question is the closest thing to free marks a prepared candidate will meet.

The working theorems, with their reason wording. Angle at centre: a central angle of one hundred and forty degrees forces seventy degrees at the circumference on the same arc — reason, angle at centre equals two times angle at circumference. Angles in the same segment are equal. The angle in a semicircle is ninety degrees. Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary, and the exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral equals the interior opposite angle. Tangents from a common point are equal. The tangent-chord theorem: the angle between a tangent and a chord equals the angle in the alternate segment. Then the proportionality pair that rules the similarity questions: a line parallel to one side of a triangle divides the other two sides proportionally, and equiangular triangles are similar.

Riders are solved backwards and forwards at once. Read the diagram and mark everything given — equal sides, tangents, diameters — because each given is a theorem invitation: a diameter whispers ninety degrees, a tangent whispers tan-chord, a cyclic quadrilateral whispers supplementary. Then read the target and ask what would prove it: equal angles come from the same segment, from tan-chord, from isosceles triangles, from parallel lines. The proof is the corridor where the two searches meet, and it is written statement by statement, each with its reason, no steps assumed. When a similarity question asks for a ratio, name the triangles with letters in matching order — the order is the mapping, and a scrambled order scrambles the ratio.

## Subtopic: Reasons, Rounding and the Paper Two Habits

The habits that decide geometry-side scripts are small, repeatable, and all drillable in the final stretch.

Habit one: the reason column. In Euclidean geometry a correct statement with a missing or invented reason is a mark lost, every time. Markers accept specific wordings — tan chord theorem, angle at centre equals two times angle at circumference, opposite angles of cyclic quad — and revision means writing those exact phrases until they arrive without thought. A useful drill: cover the reasons in a worked rider, and supply them from memory.

Habit two: degrees on the calculator. One general solution computed in radian mode poisons every answer downstream. Check the mode at the start of the trigonometry work — a two-second glance that protects a dozen marks.

Habit three: exact versus decimal. When a question says show that, or asks for cos one hundred and five degrees in surd form, a calculator decimal earns nothing — the exact value is the answer. When a question names an accuracy, round exactly there and nowhere earlier. Analytical lengths stay as simplified surds unless decimals are demanded.

Habit four: the diagram as ledger. Every fact discovered — an angle of seventy degrees, a pair of equal tangents — gets written onto the diagram immediately. Later subquestions almost always spend what earlier subquestions earned, and the ledger keeps the earnings visible.

Habit five: the general solution reflex. Trigonometric equations produce families, not single answers; write the family with its k, then harvest the members inside the demanded interval. Order matters — family first, harvest second — because the family earns even when the harvest slips.

Five habits, none of them mathematics, all of them marks. The territory knowledge is already in your hands; the habits are how it reaches the page intact.
