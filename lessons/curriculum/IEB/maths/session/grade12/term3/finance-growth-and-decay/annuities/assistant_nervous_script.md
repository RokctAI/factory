Hey, it's Mandy — annuities look like the scariest formulas in the book, all brackets and exponents. Here is the secret: there are only two of them, they are twins, and both are just the geometric series you already know from term one wearing a bank uniform.

The saving twin: envelopes on a table. You drop equal amounts in every month; each envelope earns for however long it sits there; add them up with the series formula and you have the future value. R1 500 a month for five years at 0,75 percent a month grows to about R113 000 — you put in 90, compounding added the rest.

The borrowing twin: the same series run backwards. A lump today equals all your future payments shrunk back to today. That is how a bank turns a R180 000 loan into an instalment of R4 608,61 — and how you catch what borrowing truly costs: forty-eight of those payments total about R221 000.

Choosing between the twins is one word in the question. SAVE — future value, positive exponent. OWE — present value, negative exponent. That single check rescues more marks than any amount of formula-cramming.

Two habits and you are safe. First, draw the timeline: dots for payments, a cross for the valuation date, and count every dot's months to the cross — that catches all the first-payment-today traps. Second, in sinking funds, keep the three rates in three lanes: inflation prices the new machine, depreciation prices the scrap, and only the fund's own rate touches your payments.

The formulas will always be there for you once you can rebuild them from the envelope row — the real thinking is dots, a cross, and one verb. Now head over to the practice questions and let the timeline do the work. You've got this!
