# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven priced growth and decay and pulled the mask off nominal rates. Grade twelve finishes the toolkit: the four formulae revised and drilled, and then the two questions grade eleven had to leave open — given the start and the end, what was the RATE, and, the genuine novelty of this year, how LONG does money need? The second question belongs to logarithms, and answering it is the reason logarithms sit in this course at all.

## Subtopic: The Four Formulae on One Timeline

The complete kit shares one cast: P the present or starting value, A the accumulated or ending value, i the rate per period as a decimal, n the number of periods. Simple growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i n. Compound growth: A equals P times the quantity one plus i, raised to the power n. Simple decay: A equals P times the quantity one minus i n. Compound decay, also called reducing balance: A equals P times the quantity one minus i, raised to the power n.

Behind the algebra sits term one's geometry: simple interest adds the same rand amount every period — an arithmetic sequence — while compound interest multiplies by the same factor every period — a geometric sequence with ratio one plus i. Term one and term three are one subject in two costumes.

Drill a worked case. Invest 20 000 rand at 7 percent per annum, compounded annually, for 6 years. A equals 20 000 times 1,07 to the power 6. The power is 1,500730 to six decimals, so A is 30 014,61 rand — the money has grown by half as much again and then some. Two grade-eleven disciplines still govern everything: match i and n to the compounding period — monthly compounding divides the annual rate by twelve and counts n in months — and carry every decimal to the final line, because rounding 1,07 to the sixth too early throws away rand-level accuracy.

When compounding beats the year, convert honestly: 6 percent per annum compounded monthly is zero comma zero zero five per month, and three years is thirty-six months.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Choose the formula by growth or decay, simple or compound, then match the rate and the clock to the same period.

## Subtopic: Solving for the Rate

A perennial favourite: the start and end values are known, the time is known, and the RATE hides. The compound formula then holds an unknown base raised to a known power, and the tool is the n-th root — the inverse of raising to the power n.

Worked case. A delivery van bought for 320 000 rand is worth 135 000 rand after 3 years on reducing balance. Find the annual depreciation rate. Set up: 135 000 equals 320 000 times the quantity one minus i, cubed. Divide first: one minus i cubed equals 135 000 over 320 000, which is 0,421875. Take the cube root: one minus i equals 0,75 — exactly, since 0,75 cubed is 0,421875. So i is 0,25: the van sheds a full 25 percent of its current value every year.

The growth twin runs on identical rails. If 8 000 rand grows to 10 941,05 rand in 3 years compounded annually, then one plus i cubed equals 1,367631, whose cube root is 1,11 — the rate was 11 percent per annum. On the calculator, a cube root is the power one third: raise 1,367631 to the power of a third.

Two disciplines. First, divide BEFORE rooting: the root acts on the ratio A over P, never on A alone. Second, sign sense: a reducing-balance question must deliver one minus i between zero and one — a root above one in a decay setting means the fraction went in upside down. And close with a percentage, because the question asked for a rate, not a decimal.

Stop for this section's questions now — isolate the bracket, root it with the reciprocal power, and read decay rates off one minus i, never off the root itself.

## Subtopic: Solving for Time — Enter the Logarithm

Now the year's true newcomer. How long will 4 000 rand take to double at 7,5 percent per annum compounded annually? Set up: 8 000 equals 4 000 times 1,075 to the power n, so 1,075 to the n equals 2. The unknown now lives in the EXPONENT, and no root can fetch it down. This is exactly the question the logarithm was built to answer: the log of a number, to a given base, is the exponent that base needs to reach that number. Term one's definition: y equals log base b of x means b to the y equals x.

Apply it: n equals log base 1,075 of 2, and the change-of-base rule turns that into log 2 divided by log 1,075. Compute: 0,30103 over 0,031408 in common logs — or the same ratio in natural logs — and n is 9,58 years to two decimals.

Now interpret, because the decimal is not yet an answer. Under ANNUAL compounding, interest lands only at year ends: after 9 years the factor is 1,075 to the ninth, about 1,9172 — still short of double — while after 10 years it is about 2,0610. The money first exceeds double at the END OF YEAR 10. The calculation locates the crossing inside the tenth year; the crediting schedule says the balance actually leaps past double when year ten's interest arrives. With monthly compounding, the same reasoning rounds n UP to the next month.

Inflation obeys the same mathematics: at 5 percent per annum, prices double in log 2 over log 1,05 years — about 14,2 years. Every doubling question — money, prices, populations — is the same logarithm in different clothing.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — unknown exponent summons the logarithm, change of base turns it into one division, and the decimal must be rounded to the crediting schedule before it becomes an answer.

## Subtopic: Changing Conditions on One Timeline

Real accounts never sit still: rates shift midstream, deposits arrive late, withdrawals interrupt. The master technique is the TIMELINE — mark every event at its moment, grow each amount through each regime separately, and add value only at the target date.

Worked case, in full. Deposit 12 000 rand at 7 percent per annum compounded annually. At the end of year 3, add 6 000 rand. At the end of year 4, the rate rises to 8 percent. Find the value at the end of year 6. Walk the line. Years zero to three: 12 000 times 1,07 cubed is 14 700,52. Add the deposit: 20 700,52 sitting at time three. Year three to four, still at 7 percent: 20 700,52 times 1,07 is 22 149,55. Years four to six under the new rate: 22 149,55 times 1,08 squared. Since 1,08 squared is 1,1664, the value is 25 835,24 rand.

The alternative bookkeeping grows each deposit separately to the end — 12 000 through four years at 7 percent then two at 8, plus 6 000 through one year at 7 and two at 8 — and adds. The distributive law guarantees the same answer; choose whichever keeps your page cleaner.

The rules that make timelines safe. Every amount travels through every period between its arrival and the target date — nothing skips a segment. A rate change slices the line into regimes; an amount crossing the boundary carries its accumulated value over, then grows by the new rules. A withdrawal subtracts at its own date and simply stops growing for the amount that left. Draw the line, mark the arrows, and a four-event question turns from acrobatics into bookkeeping.

The final questions of this part are with you now — draw the timeline before touching the calculator, grow through each regime in turn, and make every rand answer for every period it was present.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same growth and decay told from a savings book and a second-hand van yard — same formulae, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Escalator Up, Escalator Down

Money under compound interest rides an escalator: each year multiplies by the same factor. At 7 percent the factor is 1,07 — this year's WHOLE balance, past interest included, times 1,07. After six years, six multiplications: 20 000 rand becomes 20 000 times 1,07 to the sixth — 30 014,61 rand. Simple interest is the staircase instead: the same fixed step of 1 400 rand each year, worked out forever on the original 20 000 alone. Stairs add; escalators multiply. That is the entire distinction between simple and compound — the arithmetic-versus-geometric split from term one, now wearing a bank uniform.

Decay is the identical escalator, pointed downhill. A bakery oven losing 12 percent a year on reducing balance keeps 88 percent of its CURRENT value each year: times 0,88, times 0,88, over and over. It never quite touches zero — 88 percent of something is always something — whereas straight-line decay, the downward staircase, descends in equal steps and does strike the floor.

Choosing the formula is two quick questions. Up or down — growth or decay? Stairs or escalator — simple or compound? Four combinations, four formulae, and no memorising beyond the pattern: stairs put i n inside the bracket with a plus or minus; escalators raise the bracket to the power n.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the four formulae are coming to you right now. Ask up or down, stairs or escalator, and the formula picks itself.

## Subtopic: The Car Lot Detective

A second-hand dealer announces: this delivery van cost 320 000 rand new, three years back; it is on the floor today at 135 000. What yearly percentage did it shed? That is detective work on a rate, and the method is to un-multiply.

Three years of reducing balance means the original price was multiplied by one mystery factor, three times over. Total damage: 135 000 over 320 000 — 0,421875 of the value kept. One year's factor is the number that multiplies by itself three times to give 0,421875: the cube root. The cube root of 0,421875 is 0,75, because 0,75 times 0,75 is 0,5625, and 0,5625 times 0,75 is 0,421875. Each year the van kept three quarters of itself — it shed 25 percent per annum.

The detective's two habits. Habit one: divide end by start FIRST, to expose the total factor, before any rooting — the root undoes the repeated multiplication, so it must act on the factor, never on the raw price. Habit two: sense-check which side of one the factor lands. Decay keeps less than everything, so the yearly factor must sit below one; growth's must sit above. A decay answer above one means the fraction went in upside down — catch that yourself before anyone else does.

Growth detection is the same trick on the other side of one: money that turned 8 000 into 10 941,05 over three years kept a yearly factor of the cube root of 1,367631 — exactly 1,11, an 11 percent per annum growth rate.

Your questions for this part are up now. Divide end by start, root by the years, and read the percentage off the factor's distance from one.

## Subtopic: How Long Until It Doubles

The last question money ever asks: how long? At 7,5 percent compounded annually, when does 4 000 rand become 8 000? The setup boils down to 1,075 to the power n equals 2 — and here ordinary tools run dry, because the unknown n stands upstairs in the exponent. Roots pull down known powers; they are helpless against unknown ones.

The logarithm is the tool built for precisely this. A log answers a single question: how many times must I multiply by the base to reach this number? Log base 1,075 of 2 asks: how many rides on the 7,5 percent escalator double my money? Your calculator serves it as a division: log of 2 over log of 1,075 — about 9,58.

But 9,58 is a raw reading, not an answer, because the bank pays interest only at year ends. Check the ledger: after 9 years the factor is about 1,92 — close, not double. The balance only crosses double when year ten's interest lands. So the honest answer is: at the end of year 10. Round to the crediting schedule, never to the nearest number — under monthly compounding, round the months up instead.

The same tool settles every how-long question. Prices doubling at 5 percent inflation: log 2 over log 1,05, about 14,2 years — leave money under a mattress for fourteen years and it buys half. A population, a debt, a rumour spreading at a fixed percentage: same logarithm, same division, same careful rounding at the finish.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: an unknown exponent calls the logarithm, the log of the target factor over the log of the yearly factor, and the calendar answer honours when interest actually arrives.
