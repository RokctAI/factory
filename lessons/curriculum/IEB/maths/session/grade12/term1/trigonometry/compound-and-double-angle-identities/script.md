# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven trigonometry closed with reduction formulae and general solutions. Grade twelve installs the final major tools of the identity toolbox: formulae for the sine and cosine of a SUM or DIFFERENCE of two angles, and their celebrated special case, the double angle. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the four compound-angle identities, the craft of exact values without a calculator, the double-angle identities with the three faces of cos of 2 alpha, and the deployment of double angles inside proofs and equations.

## Subtopic: The Compound-Angle Identities

Open with a warning shot. Sine of a sum is NOT the sum of the sines. Test it with real numbers: sine of 90 degrees is exactly 1, but sine of 45 degrees plus sine of 45 degrees is about 0,71 plus 0,71, which is 1,41. Distributing a trig function across a plus sign is the most heavily punished error in this topic. The genuine expansions carry more structure.

Here they are, spoken with care. Sine of alpha plus beta equals sine alpha cos beta plus cos alpha sine beta. Sine of alpha minus beta equals sine alpha cos beta minus cos alpha sine beta. Cosine of alpha plus beta equals cos alpha cos beta MINUS sine alpha sine beta. Cosine of alpha minus beta equals cos alpha cos beta PLUS sine alpha sine beta.

Two patterns carry all four. For sine, the functions mix — sine cos, then cos sine — and the middle sign copies the sign in the bracket. For cosine, the functions travel in matched pairs — cos cos, then sine sine — and the sign FLIPS: plus in the bracket becomes minus between the terms. The parent of the family is cosine of alpha minus beta, derived from the distance formula on the unit circle; the other three follow by substituting minus beta or invoking co-functions. Know that the derivation exists; know the four results without hesitation.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Sine mixes and copies the sign; cosine pairs up and flips it.

## Subtopic: Exact Values Without a Calculator

The compound identities' first job is manufacturing exact values for awkward angles out of familiar ones. The special angles 30, 45 and 60 degrees carry known surds: sine of 30 is a half, cos of 30 is root 3 over 2, sine and cos of 45 are both root 2 over 2, sine of 60 is root 3 over 2, cos of 60 is a half.

Now take 15 degrees, which is 45 minus 30. Sine of 15 equals sine 45 cos 30 minus cos 45 sine 30. Substitute: root 2 over 2, times root 3 over 2, minus root 2 over 2, times a half. That is root 6 over 4 minus root 2 over 4, giving root 6 minus root 2, all over 4. As a decimal that is about 0,26, and the calculator concurs — but the exam wants the surd, and the identity is the only route to it.

The same machinery serves 75 degrees: cos of 75, which is cos of 45 plus 30, expands to cos 45 cos 30 minus sine 45 sine 30, and lands on the identical surd, root 6 minus root 2 over 4. No accident — cos of 75 and sine of 15 are co-functions of complementary angles. Questions also run the machine in reverse: meet cos 50 cos 20 plus sine 50 sine 20 and read it as the expansion of cos of 50 minus 20, which is cos 30, which is root 3 over 2. Compressing a four-term expansion back into one function earns as many marks as expanding ever does.

Stop for this section's questions now — split the awkward angle into two special ones, expand, substitute the surds, and stand ready to run every identity backwards.

## Subtopic: Double Angles and the Three Faces of cos 2 Alpha

Set beta equal to alpha in the sum identities and the double angles drop out. Sine of 2 alpha equals 2 sine alpha cos alpha. Cosine of 2 alpha equals cos squared alpha minus sine squared alpha — and this one owns two further costumes, courtesy of the Pythagorean identity sine squared plus cos squared equals 1. Swap out the sine squared: cos of 2 alpha equals 2 cos squared alpha minus 1. Swap out the cos squared: cos of 2 alpha equals 1 minus 2 sine squared alpha. Three faces, one identity — and selecting the convenient face is the real skill being examined.

Worked case. Given cos alpha equals minus 5 over 13, with alpha between 90 and 180 degrees, find sine of 2 alpha and cos of 2 alpha. First recover sine alpha: from the Pythagorean identity, sine squared alpha is 1 minus 25 over 169, which is 144 over 169, so sine alpha is plus or minus 12 over 13 — and in the second quadrant sine is positive, so sine alpha equals 12 over 13. Sketch the triangle in the correct quadrant whenever that sign call wobbles. Now sine of 2 alpha is 2 times 12 over 13 times minus 5 over 13, which is minus 120 over 169. And cos of 2 alpha, wearing the face that needs only cosine, is 2 times 25 over 169, minus 1, which is minus 119 over 169. Run the checks: both values sit legally between minus 1 and 1, and with sine 2 alpha negative AND cos 2 alpha negative, the angle 2 alpha lands in the third quadrant — consistent, since alpha a little over 90 degrees doubles to a little over 180.

Quick pause — the questions on double angles are with you now. Two sine cos for sine; for cos 2 alpha, pick the face that matches the information in your hand.

## Subtopic: Double Angles in Identities and Equations

Proving identities is choosing faces. Prove that sine 2 alpha, over 1 plus cos 2 alpha, equals tan alpha. Work the left side: for cos 2 alpha choose the face 2 cos squared alpha minus 1, so the denominator becomes 1 plus 2 cos squared alpha minus 1, which is 2 cos squared alpha — the constant is gone by design. The numerator is 2 sine alpha cos alpha. Divide: sine alpha over cos alpha, which is tan alpha. The strategy generalises to nearly every such proof: select the face of cos 2 alpha that kills the constant or matches the squared function already on stage.

Equations obey the same logic. Solve cos 2 x plus 3 sine x minus 2 equals 0 for x in 0 to 360 degrees. A double angle and a single angle cannot negotiate, so convert the double: choose 1 minus 2 sine squared x, because the other trig term is a sine. The equation becomes 1 minus 2 sine squared x plus 3 sine x minus 2 equals 0, which tidies to 2 sine squared x minus 3 sine x plus 1 equals 0. Factorise as a quadratic in sine x: 2 sine x minus 1, times sine x minus 1, equals 0. So sine x equals a half, or sine x equals 1. Sine x equals 1 gives x equals 90 degrees. Sine x equals a half is positive in quadrants one and two, reference angle 30 degrees: x equals 30 degrees or 150 degrees. Three solutions: 30, 90 and 150. Substitute one back to certify: at 150 degrees, cos of 300 is a half, and 3 times sine of 150 is three halves — a half plus three halves minus two is zero. Correct.

The final questions of this part are with you now — dress the double angle to match the single one, factorise the quadratic, and solve each simple equation in its own quadrants.

## Subtopic: Why the Shortcut Fails

Here is the tempting shortcut: surely sine of 45 plus 45 is sine 45 plus sine 45. Put numbers on it. Sine of 90 is exactly 1. Sine 45 plus sine 45 is about 1,41. The shortcut overshoots by forty percent, and the picture explains why: angles combine by rotation, not by stacking rulers. Tilt a ramp by 45 degrees and then tilt it 45 further, and the second tilt does not add the same height as the first — it starts from a slope already leaning, so part of its effort spends itself sideways.

The true expansions honour that geometry. Sine of a sum is a blend of BOTH functions of BOTH angles: sine alpha cos beta plus cos alpha sine beta — each angle offers its sine, scaled by how upright the other angle leaves the situation, which is its cosine. Cosine of a sum pairs the functions instead — cos cos minus sine sine — and carries the sign flip: further rotation can only eat away horizontal reach, so plus inside the bracket turns to minus outside.

Two memory hooks, and only two. Sine mixes the functions and copies the bracket's sign. Cosine pairs the functions and flips it.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the expansions are coming to you right now. Never distribute across the bracket; blend for sine, pair and flip for cosine.

## Subtopic: Making 15 Degrees From Scratch

The angles 30, 45 and 60 are old companions with exact surd values. But a question demands sine of 15 degrees, exactly — no decimals allowed. The move: assemble 15 from companions. Fifteen is 45 minus 30, so sine 15 is sine of 45 minus 30, and the difference identity unpacks it: sine 45 cos 30 minus cos 45 sine 30.

From here it is surd bookkeeping. Root 2 over 2 times root 3 over 2 gives root 6 over 4. Root 2 over 2 times a half gives root 2 over 4. Subtract: root 6 minus root 2, all over 4. That expression IS sine 15 — exact, calculator-free, and worth full marks where 0,26 earns nothing. Think of it as mixing paint: the shop stocks no fifteen-degree tin, but it stocks 45 and 30, and the identity is the mixing recipe.

The reverse skill weighs just as much: recognising a mixture already made. Meet cos 50 cos 20 plus sine 50 sine 20 in a question and read it as a completed recipe — it is exactly cos of 50 minus 20, which is cos 30, which is root 3 over 2. Spotting that a sprawling four-term expression collapses into one special angle converts an ugly question into a single line.

Your questions for this part are up now. Split the strange angle into two friendly ones, follow the recipe, and learn to read a recipe backwards.

## Subtopic: One Angle, Folded Double

Set both angles in the sum identities to the same alpha and the compound formulae fold onto themselves. Sine of 2 alpha is 2 sine alpha cos alpha — the blend's two identical terms merge. Cosine of 2 alpha is cos squared minus sine squared — and because sine squared plus cos squared is always 1, that single face rewrites twice: 2 cos squared alpha minus 1, or 1 minus 2 sine squared alpha. One actor, three costumes: the same identity underneath, dressed for different scenes.

Which costume? Read the scene. Holding only cos alpha — say cos alpha is minus 5 over 13 in the second quadrant? For cos 2 alpha, wear 2 cos squared minus 1: two times 25 over 169, minus 1, is minus 119 over 169, and no sine was ever needed. For sine 2 alpha there is no dodging sine alpha, so fetch it through the Pythagorean identity — 144 over 169, square root, and CHOOSE the sign by quadrant: second quadrant, sine positive, 12 over 13. Then sine 2 alpha is minus 120 over 169.

In equations, the costume matches whatever else stands on stage. Cos 2 x plus 3 sine x minus 2 equals zero: the other actor is a sine, so cos 2 x wears 1 minus 2 sine squared x, and the whole line becomes a quadratic in sine x — factorise, solve sine x equals a half or 1, and read the quadrants: 30, 90, 150 degrees. The double angle never argues with the single angle; it changes costume and joins it.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: fold the compound identities to reach the doubles, keep all three faces of cos 2 alpha within reach, and dress the double angle to match whoever shares the stage.
