**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First: is there a quick way to DECIDE between the sine rule and the cosine rule without staring at the triangle for five minutes?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is one question: do you hold a complete pair? A complete pair means a side AND the angle directly opposite it, both known. If yes — and you have any single extra piece of information — the sine rule turns immediately, because its fractions only connect sides to their opposite angles. If no complete pair exists anywhere in the data, the sine rule cannot even start, and the cosine rule takes over: it is built precisely for the pairless hands — two sides with the clamped angle between them, or all three sides. Run the drill on our own examples. Angle A 35 degrees with side a equal to 12: complete pair, sine rule, done. Sides 6 and 11 with the 50 degree angle wedged between them: no pair — the 50 degrees is not opposite either known side — so cosine rule. Sides 4, 7 and 9 with no angles at all: no pair possible, cosine rule in its rearranged form. Train the reflex until the stocktake takes ten seconds, because choosing correctly is where this topic's marks are actually decided; the arithmetic afterwards is routine.

***

**Mandy:** Next: why does the cosine rule look like Pythagoras with an extra bit stapled on? Is that a coincidence?

**Tutor:** No coincidence — the cosine rule IS Pythagoras, generalised to corners that are not square. Look at the anatomy: a squared equals b squared plus c squared, which is pure Pythagoras, minus 2 b c cos A, which is the correction for the lean of the angle. Test the hinge at three settings. Set the corner to exactly 90 degrees: cos of 90 is zero, the correction evaporates, and the rule collapses to a squared equals b squared plus c squared — Pythagoras, exactly as it must, because now the triangle genuinely is right-angled. Pinch the corner tighter, like our 50 degree field: the cosine is positive, the correction subtracts, and the far side comes out SHORTER than the Pythagorean 12,53 — only 8,49 — because narrowing the hinge pulls the far ends together. Open the corner past 90: the cosine turns negative, subtracting a negative adds, and the far side stretches LONGER than Pythagoras would say, because a splayed hinge pushes the ends apart. So read the formula as a sentence: square the two arms, then correct for the hinge. Once you hear it that way, you can even catch errors — a far side longer than the Pythagorean value with an acute angle means a sign slipped somewhere.

***

**Mandy:** Third one: when the sine rule finds an angle, why do we have to worry about a second possible answer, but with the cosine rule we never do?

**Tutor:** Because of where each function hides its negatives. Sine is positive in BOTH the first and second quadrants — an acute angle and its obtuse supplement share the same sine. So when the sine rule reports sine of the angle equals some positive value, the calculator returns only the acute candidate, and the obtuse supplement, 180 minus that answer, is silently equally consistent with the equation. Whether it is consistent with the TRIANGLE is your job to check: add the obtuse candidate to the other known angle, and if the total still sits under 180 degrees, a second legitimate triangle exists and both answers must be reported — that is the ambiguous case. If the total bursts past 180, the obtuse candidate dies and the acute answer stands alone. Cosine plays no such game: it is positive for acute angles and negative for obtuse ones, so every possible angle between 0 and 180 owns a UNIQUE cosine value. When the rearranged cosine rule hands the calculator 19 over 21, only one angle in the triangle range fits, and a negative value would have simply and honestly announced an obtuse angle. Practical consequence: given all three sides, prefer the cosine rule for angles — it can never ambush you.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the mast problem, several people found the right numbers but multiplied them in the wrong triangle. How do we keep the two triangles from blurring together?

**Tutor:** Name the spine out loud before any calculation, and write each triangle as its own flat sketch. The blur happens because a 3D drawing forces both triangles onto one picture, and angles from different pages start looking like neighbours. Separate them. Page one, lying flat: the ground triangle P Q R, holding the 80 metre baseline and the ground angles 65 and 40 — everything in it is horizontal, and the mast's height does not exist here at all. Page two, standing up: the right-angled triangle from Q to the mast's foot to its top, holding the 32 degree elevation. The ONLY object in both pages is the spine P Q, the flat run from observer to mast foot — so the spine is the only number allowed to travel. The routine writes itself: solve the page rich in data first, which is the ground page, extracting P Q equals 80 sine 40 over sine 75, about 53,24 metres. Then carry exactly that one number into the standing page and finish: height equals 53,24 tan 32, about 33,27 metres. If ever you cannot say which page an angle lives in, you are not ready to compute — label the sketch until you can. Two clean pages and one travelling number: that discipline is the entire 3D topic.
