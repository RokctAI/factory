# Part 1 — Expert

Right-angled triangles yield to sine, cosine and tangent directly. But the triangles of working life — survey baselines, roof frames, radio mast sightlines — are rarely right-angled. Grade eleven introduced the three rules that govern ANY triangle; grade twelve revises their proofs and marches them into three dimensions. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the decision procedure that selects a rule, the sine rule in action, the cosine rule in action, and the two-triangle strategy that unlocks every 3D problem.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Rule

The standard labelling first, because everything hangs off it: in triangle A B C, the side called little a sits OPPOSITE the angle called capital A, little b opposite capital B, little c opposite capital C. Every rule is phrased in that pairing.

The sine rule states: a over sine A equals b over sine B equals c over sine C. It trades in PAIRS — each side with the angle staring at it. The cosine rule states: a squared equals b squared plus c squared minus 2 b c cos A. It trades in three sides plus ONE angle, the angle clamped between the two known sides. The area rule states: area equals a half a b sine C — two sides and the INCLUDED angle, no height required.

Choosing is stocktaking. List what the question supplies. Holding a complete pair — a side with its opposite angle — plus any one extra piece? Sine rule. Holding two sides with the angle wedged between them, or all three sides while hunting an angle? Cosine rule. Asked for area from two sides and the angle between? Area rule. All three proofs are examinable, and each begins identically: drop a perpendicular height inside the triangle, cut it into right-angled pieces, and assemble the rule from basic ratios — with Pythagoras joining in for the cosine rule.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Pair each side with its opposite angle, count your data, and let the stocktake pick the rule.

## Subtopic: The Sine Rule at Work

Deploy the sine rule when a matched pair is on the table. In triangle A B C, angle A is 35 degrees, angle B is 75 degrees, and side little a is 12 units. The pair a and A is complete, so the rule turns. B over sine B equals a over sine A: b equals 12 times sine 75, over sine 35, which computes to 20,21 units correct to two decimals. Estimate before calculating: angle B is far bigger than angle A, so side b MUST come out well beyond 12 — larger angles face longer sides — and 20,21 obeys the geometry.

Angle C arrives free: the angles of a triangle total 180 degrees, so C is 70 degrees, and side c follows from the same rule if the question wants it. Harvest that third angle by subtraction before chasing anything else — it is the cheapest fact in the whole triangle.

One caution inherited from grade eleven: when the sine rule hunts an ANGLE, the calculator hands back an acute answer, yet sine stays positive into the second quadrant, so an obtuse triangle may conceal a second candidate. This ambiguous case arises when the given data genuinely permits two different triangles; test whether the obtuse alternative still fits beneath the 180 degree ceiling, and report both when both survive.

Stop for this section's questions now — complete pair plus one more piece, longest side faces biggest angle, and cross-examine the calculator's angle before believing it.

## Subtopic: The Cosine Rule at Work

No matched pair anywhere? Then the cosine rule, in one of its two trades. Trade one: two sides and the included angle, find the far side. Sides 6 and 11 with a 50 degree angle clamped between them. A squared equals 6 squared plus 11 squared, minus 2 times 6 times 11 times cos 50. That is 36 plus 121, minus 132 times 0,6428: 157 minus 84,85, which is 72,15. The side is the square root of 72,15, about 8,49 units. Study the formula's anatomy: Pythagoras plus a correction. At exactly 90 degrees the cosine dies and the correction vanishes — the cosine rule IS Pythagoras, adjusted for how far the angle leans away from square.

Trade two: all three sides known, find any angle. Rearranged: cos A equals b squared plus c squared minus a squared, all over 2 b c. Sides 4, 7 and 9, hunting the angle opposite the 4: cos A equals 49 plus 81 minus 16, over 2 times 7 times 9 — that is 114 over 126, which is 19 over 21. Inverse cosine gives 25,21 degrees. The rearranged rule involves no quadrant debate: cosine, unlike sine, goes negative for obtuse angles, so the calculator's one answer is always the honest one — a negative cosine simply reports an obtuse angle without disguise.

And the area rule rounds out the kit: the same 6 and 11 sides around the 50 degree included angle enclose a half times 6 times 11 times sine 50, about 25,28 square units. Two sides and the wedge — no height, no base-hunting.

Quick pause — the questions on the cosine and area rules are with you now. Included angle for a side, three sides for an angle, and read a negative cosine as an obtuse verdict, never a malfunction.

## Subtopic: The Two-Triangle Strategy in Three Dimensions

Every 3D examination question is two flat triangles sharing an edge, and that shared edge is the bridge. The routine: identify the horizontal triangle lying on the ground, identify the vertical triangle standing upright, name the side they share, then carry a number across the bridge.

Worked case. A vertical mast stands at point P. Two observers stand at ground points Q and R. The angle P Q R is 65 degrees, the angle P R Q is 40 degrees, and the distance Q R is 80 metres. From Q, the angle of elevation of the mast's top is 32 degrees. Find the mast's height.

Step one: the ground triangle P Q R. Its third angle, at P, is 180 minus 65 minus 40, which is 75 degrees. The bridge is the side P Q — the horizontal run from observer Q to the mast's base — and it faces the 40 degree angle at R. Sine rule: P Q equals 80 times sine 40, over sine 75, which is 53,24 metres.

Step two: the vertical triangle. It stands on P Q, right-angled at the mast's foot, with the 32 degree elevation at Q. The height stands opposite that angle while the bridge P Q lies adjacent, so height equals P Q times tan 32, which is 53,24 times 0,6249 — 33,27 metres.

The discipline is bookkeeping: mark every right angle — vertical structures meet level ground at 90 degrees even when the sketch hides it — write the elevation angle inside the vertical triangle, and name the bridge side aloud before computing anything. Nearly every mark lost in 3D trigonometry dies from solving the wrong triangle first: the bridge must be computed in the triangle that holds enough data, then ferried to the other.

The final questions of this part are with you now — flat triangle, standing triangle, shared bridge, and cross it in the correct direction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three rules from a card game, a marshy field and a radio mast — same rules, same answers.

## Subtopic: Which Tool Comes Out of the Box

Three tools live in the triangle toolbox, and each grip fits one specific hand of data. Before any arithmetic, lay your cards on the table and look at what you were dealt.

The sine rule is the matching-pairs tool. It runs on fractions: each side over the sine of the angle watching it from across the triangle, all equal to each other. To turn its handle you need one COMPLETE pair — a side together with its opposite angle — plus any single extra card. The cosine rule is the hinge tool: two sides with the angle clamped between them delivers the far side, or all three sides delivers any angle. The area rule is the carpet quote: two sides and the clamped angle, half a b sine C, and nobody climbs a ladder to measure a height.

The labelling convention keeps the pairs visible: small letters for sides, capitals for angles, and small a always faces capital A across the room. Break the pairing and every rule misfires — the most common zero in this topic is a side divided by the sine of the WRONG angle.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on tool choice are coming to you right now. Lay out the cards, find a pair or find a hinge, and pick the tool whose grip fits.

## Subtopic: A Field You Cannot Walk Across

A farmer owns a triangular field. Two fences leave the same corner post: one runs 6 units, the other 11, opening at 50 degrees to each other. The third boundary crosses a marsh nobody can wade. How long is that unreachable fence?

This is the hinge tool's hour: two sides with the angle clamped between them. The far side squared equals 6 squared plus 11 squared minus 2 times 6 times 11 times cos of 50. Work in pieces: 36 plus 121 is 157; the correction is 132 times 0,6428, about 84,85; and 157 minus 84,85 is 72,15. Square root: roughly 8,49 units of fence.

Watch what the correction term is doing, because it is the entire concept. If the corner opened at exactly 90 degrees, cos would be zero and the rule would shrink to Pythagoras — root 157, about 12,53. At 50 degrees the corner is pinched tighter than square, the far side shortens, and the minus 84,85 measures exactly how much. Open the corner wider than 90 and the cosine turns negative, the correction ADDS, and the far fence stretches. Pythagoras with a hinge — that is the cosine rule in one phrase.

And the marsh's area, since the farmer will ask: half times 6 times 11 times sine 50 — about 25,28 square units — measured entirely from the dry corner.

Your questions for this part are up now. Two fences and a hinge angle: square both, subtract the swing correction, and take the root.

## Subtopic: The Flagpole and Its Two Triangles

A mast problem in 3D defeats everyone until they see the secret: there is no such thing as a 3D triangle question — only two flat triangles pretending, one lying on the ground, one standing upright, joined along a single shared side like two pages of an open book meeting at the spine.

The mast stands at P. Two friends stand at Q and R on level ground, 80 metres apart. The ground angles they measure at Q and R are 65 and 40 degrees. Friend Q sights the top of the mast at 32 degrees above the horizontal. The height hides in the standing page; the given distances live on the lying page. The spine — the side both pages share — is P Q, the flat run from friend Q to the mast's foot.

Solve the lying page first, because that is where the data sits. Its angles are 65, 40, and by subtraction 75 at the mast. P Q faces the 40 degree angle, so the matching-pairs tool gives P Q equals 80 sine 40 over sine 75 — 53,24 metres. Now carry that number up the spine into the standing page: a right-angled triangle, base 53,24, elevation 32 degrees. Height equals 53,24 times tan 32, which is 33,27 metres.

Every 3D question is this same dance: find the spine, solve the page that holds the data, ferry the spine's length to the page that holds the answer. If the 3D sketch swims before your eyes, redraw the two pages as separate flat triangles — flattening the book loses nothing and rescues everything.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: lying page, standing page, one shared spine, and always solve the data-rich page first.
