**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! The big one first: what actually IS a logarithm? Several people say they can compute them but still don't know what they are.

**Tutor:** A logarithm is an answer that finally got its own name. Ask: 3 to the power what gives 81? You can answer that — four — but before this lesson there was no way to WRITE the question's answer as an object you could carry into further algebra. Log base 3 of 81 is exactly that object: the exponent that takes the base 3 up to 81. That is the whole definition, and it means every log statement is an exponential statement in different clothes. The statement y equals log base b of x and the statement x equals b to the power y are one fact wearing two costumes: the log costume isolates the exponent, the exponential costume isolates the power. When any log confuses you, change its costume out loud. Log base 3 of one twenty-seventh: 3 to the what gives one over 27? Three to the minus three does, so the log is minus 3. Log base 10 of 10 000: ten to the what gives ten thousand? Four. Once the translation reflex is automatic, there is nothing left in this topic to be afraid of — only counting questions in formal dress.

***

**Mandy:** Next: why can't we take the log of zero or of a negative number? The calculator just says error, but why?

**Tutor:** Because the log's question has no answer there, and the exponential explains why. Log base 3 of minus 9 asks: 3 to the power what gives minus 9? Now run through the candidates. Positive exponents give 3, 9, 27 — positive and growing. The zero exponent gives 1 — positive. Negative exponents give a third, a ninth — positive and shrinking. Every possible exponent leaves the answer strictly positive, because a positive base multiplied or divided by itself any number of times cannot change sign, and cannot reach zero either — it only creeps toward zero without arriving. So there is no exponent to report, and the log does not exist. That is why the calculator refuses: it is not being unhelpful, it is being honest. The same fact drawn as a picture: the exponential graph lives entirely above the x axis, so its reflection, the log graph, lives entirely to the right of the y axis — domain x strictly greater than zero. Keep the two statements linked in your mind: outputs of the exponential are positive, therefore inputs of the log must be positive. One fact, seen from both sides of the mirror.

***

**Mandy:** Third one: for 2 to the x equals 11, why does the calculator recipe log 11 over log 2 work? Where does the division come from?

**Tutor:** The recipe is the change of base, and you can trust it without ceremony once you see what it does. The honest answer to 2 to the x equals 11 is x equals log base 2 of 11 — the definition says so directly. The snag is practical: many calculators only offer log base 10. So we reach 11 in two stages via base 10. It takes log 11 in base ten steps of ten to reach 11, and log 2 of those steps to build a single doubling. Dividing log 11 by log 2 counts how many doublings fit into the journey to 11 — which is exactly log base 2 of 11. Numerically: log 11 is about 1,0414 and log 2 about 0,3010, and the quotient is 3,46. Then the estimate that protects you from typing errors: 2 cubed is 8, 2 to the fourth is 16, and 11 sits between them, so x must lie between 3 and 4. It does. Make that bracketing estimate a habit for every exponent you solve — it costs five seconds and catches swapped digits, inverted fractions and slipped decimal points before they cost marks.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: in sketches, people keep letting the log graph touch the y axis. What is the one-line way to remember it never does?

**Tutor:** Remember whose reflection it is. The exponential graph never lands on its floor — the x axis — because doubling backwards halves forever without hitting zero. Reflect the whole picture across the diagonal y equals x and the floor tips upright into a wall: the y axis. A curve that never touched the floor cannot, after reflection, touch the wall. So the log graph slides down alongside the y axis, closer and closer, never arriving — and every sketch should show that gap, with the asymptote dashed to declare it. The one-line memory: the exponential's floor becomes the log's wall, and neither is ever touched. Practical sketching drill for log base 3: plant the anchor at 1 and 0, which every log graph shares, add the friendly points 3 and 1 and, if there is room, 9 and 2, dash the wall up the y axis, and draw the curve diving down beside the wall on its right. Everything stays strictly in x greater than 0. If your pencil ever crosses to the left of the y axis in a log sketch, stop — you have walked through a wall that the mathematics says is solid.
