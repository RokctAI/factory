# Part 1 — Expert

The inverse machinery just built runs into the one graph ordinary algebra cannot unpick: the exponential. Reversing y equals b to the power x calls for an entirely new word — the logarithm — and grade twelve is where that word gets defined, drawn and put to work. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the exponential graph with its laws and its asymptote, the definition of a logarithm as an exponential rewritten, the log graph with its own features, and the skill everything later depends on — digging an unknown out of an exponent.

## Subtopic: The Exponential Function y Equals b to the x

The exponential function is y equals b to the power x, with base b positive and not equal to 1. Take b equal to 3 and read a quick table: 3 to the power 0 is 1, 3 to the power 4 is 81, and 3 to the power minus 2 is one ninth, about 0,11. Three features leap out. The graph always passes through the point 0 and 1, because any legal base to the power 0 is 1. The outputs are always positive — no exponent can drag b to the x down to zero or past it. And as x marches leftward, the outputs shrink toward zero without ever arriving: the x axis, the line y equals 0, is a horizontal asymptote.

The base sets the direction. With b greater than 1 the graph climbs — each step right multiplies by b. With b between 0 and 1 the graph falls: take b equal to one third, and one third squared is one ninth, so every step right cuts the output to a third. The falling graph is the climbing one reflected in the y axis, because one third to the x equals 3 to the minus x.

Domain and range in one breath, asked constantly: domain all real x, range y strictly greater than 0. And the exponent laws travel with the function: same base multiplied means add the exponents, same base divided means subtract them, and a power raised to a power multiplies them.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Through 0 and 1, asymptote along the x axis, and the base decides climb or fall.

## Subtopic: The Logarithm Defined

Now the question the exponential cannot voice: 3 to the power WHAT gives 81? The answer is clearly 4, but until now there was no notation for that answer as a thing in itself. The logarithm is that notation. The statement y equals log base b of x means precisely x equals b to the power y. One fact, two costumes: the log costume isolates the exponent, the exponential costume isolates the power. Sliding between the costumes is the most examined skill in this entire topic.

Read values straight off the definition. Log base 3 of 81 asks 3 to the what gives 81 — answer 4. Log base 10 of 10 000 asks 10 to the what gives 10 000 — answer 4. Log base 3 of 1 asks 3 to the what gives 1 — answer 0, and indeed log of 1 is 0 in every base. Logs go negative without drama: log base 3 of one twenty-seventh is minus 3, because 3 to the minus 3 is one over 27. And a base between 0 and 1 flips the sign: log base one third of 9 is minus 2, because one third to the minus 2 is 9.

Two boundary rules come straight from the definition. The base b must be positive and not 1, inherited from the exponential it reverses. And the input x must be strictly positive: b to the power y is always positive, so no base can ever manufacture log of zero or log of a negative number. Asking for log base 3 of minus 9 is asking 3 to the what gives minus 9, and no exponent will ever answer.

Stop for this section's questions now — rewrite every log statement as an exponential one, and the answer usually rolls out on its own.

## Subtopic: The Graph of y Equals log Base b of x

Because y equals log base b of x is the inverse of y equals b to the x, its graph is the exponential reflected in the line y equals x — and every feature crosses the mirror with it. The exponential passed through 0 and 1; the log passes through 1 and 0, so every log graph cuts the x axis at 1. The exponential hugged the horizontal asymptote y equals 0; the log hugs the vertical asymptote x equals 0, sliding down beside the y axis without touching it. The exponential owned domain all real x and range y greater than 0; the log swaps them — domain x strictly greater than 0, range all real y.

The base tells the same directional story as before. For b greater than 1, say log base 3, the graph climbs — patiently, with no ceiling, reaching 2 at x equals 9 and 4 only at x equals 81. For b between 0 and 1, say base one third, the graph falls, the mirror image in the x axis of the base 3 graph, because log base one third of x equals minus log base 3 of x.

To sketch: plant the anchor point 1 and 0, add one friendly point — for log base 3, the point 3 and 1 or the point 9 and 2 — dash the vertical asymptote along the y axis, and let the curve dive down that asymptote on its right side. A log graph drawn touching or crossing the y axis is the classic dropped mark: the curve lives entirely in x greater than 0.

Quick pause — the questions on the log graph are with you now. Through 1 and 0, asymptote up the y axis, domain strictly positive.

## Subtopic: Solving for the Exponent

Everything funnels into one practical power: when the unknown hides in an exponent, the logarithm digs it out. Cleanest case first: solve 4 times 3 to the power x equals 108. Isolate the power: 3 to the x equals 27. Recognise 27 as 3 cubed, and x equals 3 — no calculator required when a shared base exists.

With no shared base, the definition takes command. Solve 2 to the power x equals 11. In log costume, x equals log base 2 of 11. The calculator evaluates it as log 11 divided by log 2, which is 3,46 correct to two decimals. Estimate before accepting: 2 cubed is 8 and 2 to the fourth is 16, so the exponent had to fall between 3 and 4 — and 3,46 does.

The inverse story now closes its circle. To invert y equals 2 to the x, swap: x equals 2 to the y. The unknown y sits in an exponent, so the definition rewrites it: y equals log base 2 of x. The logarithm IS the inverse of the exponential — that single sentence is worth marks on its own, and the two graphs, mirrored across y equals x, agree in silence.

One forward pointer for the finance work ahead: solving for time under compound growth means facing an equation like 1,06 to the power n equals 3, and the answer, n equals log 3 over log 1,06, about 18,85, is exactly this skill with money attached.

The final questions of this part are with you now — isolate the power, match bases when you can, and summon the log when you cannot.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same exponentials and logs through a video that keeps getting shared and a cooling cup of tea — same rules, same answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Doubling on Repeat

A short video is posted and one person has seen it. Each day, the view count doubles: 1, then 2, then 4, then 8. After x days the count is 2 to the power x. That is the exponential function — doubling on repeat, written as a power.

Read the graph straight out of the story. On day 0 the count is 1, so the graph passes through 0 and 1. The count is always positive — a video cannot be seen by zero people once it exists, let alone by minus three. Run the days backwards and the count halves each step: a half, a quarter, an eighth — sinking toward zero forever without landing on it. That endless descent is the horizontal asymptote along the x axis.

Now flip the story: a cup of tea loses half of its extra heat each hour. That is base one half — the same curve mirrored, falling instead of climbing, because halving on repeat shrinks. Climbing or falling, the shape laws hold: through 0 and 1, always positive, hugging the x axis at one end.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the doubling graph are coming to you right now. Start at one, double or halve each step, and never touch the floor.

## Subtopic: The Question the Log Asks

Here is the logarithm with its mystery removed. The video doubles daily, and you ask: how many DAYS until 64 people have seen it? Not how many people — how many days. The answer is 6, and the notation for that answer is log base 2 of 64. A logarithm is the how-many-steps question, aimed at a doubling process.

Every log value reads this way. Log base 2 of 32: how many doublings climb from 1 to 32? Five. Log base 10 of 100 000: how many times does 10 multiply to reach 100 000? Five. Log base 2 of 1: how many doublings to remain at 1? Zero — the counting has not started. And log base 2 of one eighth: how many doublings from 1 down to an eighth? Minus 3 — three steps backwards, three halvings. Negative logs are steps in reverse, nothing spookier.

Some questions simply have no answer, and the story shows why. How many doublings take one viewer to minus 9 viewers? No count of steps, forwards or backwards, produces a negative audience — so the log of a negative number does not exist, and neither does the log of zero. The log only answers questions about positive amounts.

Your questions for this part are up now. Read every log as how many steps, count forwards for large targets, backwards for fractions, and refuse the question when the target is zero or below.

## Subtopic: Mirror Twins Across the Diagonal

The exponential and the log are one table of facts read in opposite directions. The exponential row says: after 6 days, 64 viewers. The log row says: 64 viewers means 6 days have passed. Input and output have traded seats — and trading input with output is exactly what an inverse does. So y equals log base 2 of x is the inverse of y equals 2 to the x, and the two graphs hang as mirror twins across the diagonal line y equals x.

Every feature crosses the mirror with coordinates swapped. The exponential's anchor 0 and 1 becomes the log's anchor 1 and 0 — every log graph cuts the x axis at 1. The exponential's floor, the horizontal asymptote y equals 0, tips upright into the log's wall, the vertical asymptote x equals 0. Domain and range change seats too: the exponential accepted any x and produced only positive y; the log accepts only positive x and produces any y.

Sketching a log graph is therefore a reflection exercise. Plot 1 and 0, then 2 and 1, then 16 and 4 if the page allows, dash the wall along the y axis, and keep every part of the curve strictly right of it. The most common sketch error is letting the log curve lean back and rest against the y axis — the wall is never touched, exactly as the exponential never lands on its floor.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: swap the coordinates, tip the floor up into a wall, and keep the log strictly right of the y axis.
