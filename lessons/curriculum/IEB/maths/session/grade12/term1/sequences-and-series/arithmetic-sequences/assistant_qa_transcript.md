**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and I saw it three times in the chat: why is the general term a plus n minus one times d, and not simply a plus n d?

**Tutor:** Count what happens on the way to a term. Term one is where the sequence starts — nothing has been added yet. Term two carries one addition of d. Term three carries two. The additions always lag the position by exactly one, because the first term is a departure point, not a jump. So term n holds n minus one additions, and the formula records precisely that. Write a plus n d instead and every answer is one step too far — your term two hundred is actually term two hundred and one in disguise. The protection is cheap: after building the formula, test it on a term you can see. For 4; 11; 18; 25, the formula T n equals 7 n minus 3 must return 25 at position four, and it does — seven fours minus three. If your version had returned 32 there, the extra step is exposed and fixed before it ever costs a mark. Ten seconds of checking retires the most common error in this entire topic.

**Mandy:** Next one: when a question asks whether some number is a term and n comes out as a decimal, a few people want to just round it. Why can't we?

**Tutor:** Because n is a position in a list, not a measurement, and positions come only in whole numbers. There is a thirteenth term and a fourteenth term, but no term twenty-one comma eight five seven — the sequence has no slot between positions. When 7 n minus 3 equals 150 hands you n equal to 21,857, the algebra is reporting that 150 falls BETWEEN two genuine terms: term twenty-one is 144 and term twenty-two is 151, and the sequence leaps over 150 entirely. Rounding n to 22 and answering yes would declare that 151 and 150 are the same number. The full-credit answer is a sentence: since n is not a natural number, 150 is not a term of the sequence. Contrast that with the threshold word problems, where a decimal n means the target is passed partway through a step — there you take the next whole n up, because the question asks when a level is first reached, not whether it is hit exactly. Read which kind of question you are inside before touching the decimal.

**Mandy:** Third: with T4 equal to 19 and T9 equal to 39, why does subtracting the two equations spit out d so fast?

**Tutor:** Because subtraction measures the climb between the two known positions and nothing else. Write both facts with the general term: a plus 3 d equals 19, and a plus 8 d equals 39. Both lines carry the identical a, so subtracting erases it and leaves 5 d equals 20 — five jumps lifted the value by twenty, so each jump is 4. Keep that picture, because it works without equations too: position four to position nine is nine minus four, five jumps, and the value moved 39 minus 19, twenty, so d is twenty over five. Once d is in hand, substitute into either equation to get a — here a plus 12 equals 19, so a is 7. Then run the safety check on the OTHER given term: T9 should be 7 plus eight fours, which is 39. It agrees, so both letters are certified. The general fact underneath: the difference between any two terms equals d times the number of jumps between their positions. That one sentence dissolves every two-terms-given question you will meet.

**Mandy:** Last question: in the fence post picture, why do four planted posts make five spaces? Somebody divided by four and lost the mark.

**Tutor:** Draw it and the mistake evaporates. The two corner posts already stand — those are the given numbers, 3 and 18. Plant four new posts between them and walk the line counting spaces: corner to first post, one; then two, three, four; and fourth post to the far corner, five. Four posts, five spaces — the walk always contains one more space than the posts you planted, because both ends were occupied before you started. In sequence language, inserting four means between 3 and 18 creates a six-term sequence, and six terms hang on five jumps, so 18 equals 3 plus 5 d and d is 3 — not 15 over 4. Dividing by four is really forgetting that the far corner also demands a jump to reach it. The rule worth memorising: k inserted means produce k plus one jumps, so d equals the total gap over k plus one. And after computing d, write out the full list — 3; 6; 9; 12; 15; 18 — because watching all six numbers climb evenly is a free check that confirms everything.
