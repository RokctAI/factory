# Part 1 — Expert

Grade twelve begins by settling unfinished grade eleven business that final examinations return to again and again: the quadratic number pattern, the one whose SECOND difference holds constant. It usually stands right at the gate of the sequences work, and it surrenders to a fixed drill. Four pieces of equipment by the end: the difference table that separates linear patterns from quadratic ones, the three equations that recover a, b and c in T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c, the algebra that locates the position holding a given value, and the two classics — an unknown buried in the terms, and the term where a pattern turns.

## Subtopic: The Difference Table Verdict

Every pattern question opens the same way: write the terms in a row and subtract neighbours. Take 5; 9; 13; 17. First differences: 9 minus 5 is 4, 13 minus 9 is 4, 17 minus 13 is 4. Constant at the first level, so the pattern is linear, with general term shaped T n equals d n plus c. The grade eleven recipe still applies: the coefficient of n is the constant difference, 4, and the constant is the first term minus that difference, 5 minus 4, which is 1. So T n equals 4 n plus 1, and a test on a visible term seals it: T4 is four fours plus one, 17. Correct.

Now take 4; 11; 22; 37. First differences: 11 minus 4 is 7, 22 minus 11 is 11, 37 minus 22 is 15. Not constant, so not linear. Descend one level and subtract the differences themselves. Second differences: 11 minus 7 is 4, and 15 minus 11 is 4. Constant at the SECOND level: the pattern is quadratic, and its general term takes the shape T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c.

That is the entire verdict procedure. Constant first difference: linear. Constant second difference: quadratic. Two disciplines protect it. Subtract in order — each term minus its predecessor — so a falling pattern shows negative differences honestly. And insist on at least four terms before declaring a quadratic, because four terms produce three first differences and two second differences: a matching PAIR at the second level, not one number standing alone.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Build the table, subtract in order, and let the level where the numbers settle deliver the verdict.

## Subtopic: Three Equations Recover a, b and c

With the pattern certified quadratic, the shape T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c stands ready, and three facts nail down its three letters. Substitute the first three positions: T1 is a plus b plus c. T2 is 4 a plus 2 b plus c. T3 is 9 a plus 3 b plus c. Subtracting down the column turns these into three working tools. The second difference equals 2 a. The first of the first differences, T2 minus T1, equals 3 a plus b. And T1 itself equals a plus b plus c. Solve in that order — a, then b, then c — and each line surrenders exactly one letter.

Run it on 4; 11; 22; 37. The second difference is 4, so 2 a is 4 and a is 2. The first of the first differences is 7, so 3 a plus b is 7; three twos are 6, so b is 1. The first term is 4, so a plus b plus c is 4; two plus one is three, so c is 1. The general term is T n equals 2 n squared plus n plus 1.

Never leave that formula untested. Aim it at a term it has not met: T3 should be 22, and two nines are 18, plus 3, plus 1 — and 18 + 3 + 1 = 22. Certified. Now let it earn its wages on a position no table reaches: T50 is two times 2 500, which is 5 000, plus 50, plus 1 — and 5000 + 50 + 1 = 5051. Term fifty, computed in one line.

Stop for this section's questions now — half the second difference gives a, the first gap gives b through 3 a plus b, the first term gives c, and one test on a visible term certifies the lot.

## Subtopic: Which Position Holds a Given Value

The reverse question carries the heaviest marks: a value is announced, and its position must be hunted. The method is rigid — set T n equal to the value, solve the quadratic equation, then interrogate what comes out.

Which term of 2 n squared plus n plus 1 equals 172? Set up: 2 n squared plus n plus 1 equals 172, so 2 n squared plus n minus 171 equals 0. It factorises: two n plus nineteen, times n minus nine, equals zero. So n is 9, or n is minus 9,5. A position must be a natural number, so the negative fraction is dismissed without mourning, and the answer is term nine. Confirm: T9 is two times 81, which is 162, plus 9, plus 1 — and 162 + 9 + 1 = 172. Correct.

Now the trap version: is 250 a term of the same pattern? Set 2 n squared plus n plus 1 equal to 250, so 2 n squared plus n minus 249 equals 0. The discriminant is 1 plus 1992, which is 1993 — and 1993 is not a perfect square, since 44 squared is 1936 and 45 squared is 2025. So n is irrational, roughly 10,91, and certainly not a natural number. Conclusion, written as a sentence: since n is not a natural number, 250 is not a term of the pattern. The terms agree. T10 is 200 plus 10 plus 1, which is 211. T11 is 242 plus 11 plus 1, which is 254. The pattern strides from 211 to 254 and never touches 250. Rounding n to 11 would claim 254 and 250 are the same number — the sentence, not a rounded n, earns the mark.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — set T n equal to the value, solve the quadratic, and admit n only when it proves itself a natural number.

## Subtopic: Hidden Unknowns and the Smallest Term

Two exam favourites close this part. The first buries an unknown in the terms. The pattern x; 9; 22; 41 is quadratic with a constant second difference of 6 — find x. Build the difference table with the letter riding inside. First differences: 9 minus x; 13; 19. Second differences: 13 minus the quantity 9 minus x, which is 4 plus x, and 19 minus 13, which is 6. A constant second difference forces 4 plus x equal to 6, so x is 2. Substitute back and read the honest table: terms 2; 9; 22; 41, first differences 7; 13; 19, second differences 6 and 6. Now the recovery machine: 2 a is 6, so a is 3. Then 3 a plus b is 7; three threes are 9, so b is minus 2. Then a plus b plus c is 2; three minus two is one, so c is 1. The general term is T n equals 3 n squared minus 2 n plus 1, and the check on T4: three times 16 is 48, and 48 − 8 + 1 = 41. Certified.

The second favourite is the turning pattern. The pattern 18; 11; 6; 3; 2; 3; 6 falls, touches bottom and climbs again — its first differences run minus 7; minus 5; minus 3; minus 1; 1; 3, rising by a constant 2. Its general term is T n equals n squared minus 10 n plus 27, and the question asks for the smallest term. A quadratic in n reaches its minimum where n equals minus b over 2 a, here 10 over 2, which is position 5. Five is a natural number, so it names a genuine term, and T5 is 25 minus 50 plus 27 — and 25 − 50 + 27 = 2. The smallest term is 2, at position five. When the vertex lands between whole positions, test the natural numbers either side and keep the smaller — the pattern exists only at natural positions, whatever the parabola does in between.

The final questions of this part are with you now — let the unknown travel through the difference table, and pass the vertex position through the natural-number gate before crowning the smallest term.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same quadratic patterns built from tins in a supermarket display and paving stones in a courtyard — same rules, same answers, with pictures attached.

## Subtopic: Stacks Where the Gaps Grow

Picture tins stacked in a display at the front of a supermarket. The smallest display uses 2 tins. The next uses 6, then 12, then 20. Write the pattern: 2; 6; 12; 20. Measure the gaps: 4, then 6, then 8. The gaps are NOT equal — no staircase with even steps here. But look at the gaps themselves: each one is exactly 2 more than the last. The gap of the gaps holds constant.

That is the whole idea of a quadratic pattern. A linear pattern climbs like a staircase with identical steps. A quadratic pattern climbs like a staircase that steepens — every step taller than the one before, and always taller by the same extra amount. That steepening amount is the second difference; here it is 2.

The picture predicts: the next gap must be 10, so the next display needs 20 plus 10, which is 30 tins. And the picture defends: if someone insists 2; 6; 12; 20 is linear because it keeps growing, the gaps end the argument in one line — 4, 6, 8 are growing steps, not equal ones. Always measure the gaps, then the gaps of the gaps; the level where the numbers calm down names the family.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on spotting the growing gaps are coming to you right now. Gaps first, then gaps of gaps, and a steady second level means quadratic.

## Subtopic: Three Facts Build the Whole Formula

A paving contractor lays courtyard designs in a growing range: 9 stones, then 20, then 37, then 60. Gaps: 11, 17, 23. Gaps of gaps: 6 and 6 — quadratic. Now assemble T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c from three facts, each a single sentence long.

Fact one: a is HALF the second difference. The second difference is 6, so a is 3. Fact two: the first gap equals 3 a plus b. The first gap is 11, and three threes are 9, so b is 2. Fact three: the first term equals a plus b plus c. The first term is 9, and three plus two is five, so c is 4. The formula is T n equals 3 n squared plus 2 n plus 4 — built in three sentences.

Now make it face the evidence. Design four should take 60 stones: three times 16 is 48, and 48 + 8 + 4 = 60. The formula tells the truth. So let it speak about a design too large to sketch: design twenty takes three times 400, which is 1 200, plus 40, plus 4 — and 1200 + 40 + 4 = 1244 stones. No drawing, no counting, one line.

Your questions for this part are up now. Half the second difference, first gap through 3 a plus b, first term for c — and always test the formula on a design you can still count by hand.

## Subtopic: Which Stack Holds 212 Stones

The contractor's question runs in reverse: a client orders a courtyard of exactly 212 stones — which design is that? Set the formula equal to the order: 3 n squared plus 2 n plus 4 equals 212, so 3 n squared plus 2 n minus 208 equals 0. The discriminant is 4 plus 2 496, which is 2 500 — square root 50. So n is minus 2 plus 50, over 6, which is 8, or a negative value that no design number can be. Design eight. Check: three times 64 is 192, and 192 + 16 + 4 = 212. Exactly the order.

Back to the supermarket tins 2; 6; 12; 20; 30, whose formula is T n equals n times n plus one — n squared plus n. A manager wants a display of exactly 240 tins. Solve n squared plus n equals 240: that is n squared plus n minus 240 equals 0, factorising as n minus fifteen, times n plus sixteen. So n is 15 — display fifteen holds exactly 240 tins, because fifteen times sixteen is 240.

But a display of exactly 150 tins? Display eleven holds eleven times twelve: 132. Display twelve holds twelve times thirteen: 156. The displays leap from 132 straight to 156 — none holds 150. The algebra concurs: n squared plus n minus 150 equals 0 has discriminant 601, not a perfect square, so no natural-number solution exists. When n will not come out whole, the order cannot be built, and saying so in a sentence is the answer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: set the formula equal to the order, solve, and only a natural number n names a real design — anything else means the pattern skips that number completely.
