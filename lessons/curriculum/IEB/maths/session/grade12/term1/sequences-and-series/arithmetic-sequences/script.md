# Part 1 — Expert

Grade eleven closed with quadratic patterns, where the second difference held steady. Grade twelve begins by going back to the plainest pattern in the book and equipping it fully: the arithmetic sequence, whose FIRST difference never changes. Four tools will be in your hands by the end: a test that certifies a sequence as arithmetic, a general term that reaches any position instantly, algebra for a variable buried inside the terms, and the habit of converting a story into a term number.

## Subtopic: The Constant Difference Test

An arithmetic sequence is a list in which every term equals the term before it plus one fixed number. That fixed number is the common difference, called d, and subtraction reveals it: take any term and subtract its predecessor. Consider 4; 11; 18; 25. Eleven minus four is seven. Eighteen minus eleven is seven. Twenty-five minus eighteen is seven. The gap holds steady, so the sequence is arithmetic and d equals 7.

The subtraction carries two rules of conduct. Rule one is direction: d is always the later term minus the earlier one, never the other way round. The sequence 31; 26; 21 gives d equal to minus 5 — a negative difference breaks nothing; it simply says the sequence is falling. Rule two is persistence: one matching gap is not evidence. The list 3; 6; 12 opens with a gap of 3, but the next gap is 6, so the pattern is not arithmetic at all. The definition demands the same gap along the entire list, so measure it at least twice before signing the certificate.

Standard notation runs through every question: a names the first term, d the common difference, and T n the term sitting in position n. For 4; 11; 18; 25, a is 4, d is 7, T1 is 4 and T4 is 25.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Subtract in the right order, test the gap more than once, and let a falling sequence keep its negative d.

## Subtopic: The General Term T n Equals a Plus n Minus One, Times d

Writing out terms one at a time collapses the moment a question wants term two hundred. The general term is the shortcut that lands there directly. Watch how the terms are built: T1 is a alone. T2 is a plus one copy of d. T3 is a plus two copies. The count of d's always runs one behind the position, so T n equals a plus the quantity n minus one, times d.

Run it forwards. For 4; 11; 18; 25, T n equals 4 plus the quantity n minus one, times 7, which tidies to 7 n minus 3. Certify the formula on a term you can point at: T4 should be 25, and seven fours minus three is 25. That ten-second check catches nearly every slip, so make it a reflex. Term two hundred is now seven times two hundred, minus three: 1397. No listing required.

Run it backwards, where most of the marks sit. Is 88 a term of this sequence, and if so which one? Set 7 n minus 3 equal to 88, so 7 n is 91 and n is 13. Yes — 88 is the thirteenth term. Now ask the same of 150: 7 n minus 3 equals 150 gives n equal to 153 over 7, which is 21,857 and change. A position must be a natural number, and there is no term twenty-one comma eight five seven, so 150 does NOT belong to the sequence. That written sentence — n is not a natural number, therefore 150 is not a term — is what the final mark pays for.

Third job: rebuilding a and d from two known terms. Say T4 is 19 and T9 is 39. Then a plus 3 d equals 19, and a plus 8 d equals 39. Subtract the equations: 5 d equals 20, so d is 4 and a is 7. Sense-check the arithmetic of it: position four to position nine is five jumps, and the value rose by twenty — five jumps of four. The general term is T n equals 4 n plus 3, and testing on the other given fact, T9 is four nines plus three, which is 39. Confirmed.

Stop for this section's questions now — assemble T n, prove it on a visible term, and accept a value as a member only when n arrives as a natural number.

## Subtopic: Unknowns in the Terms and Arithmetic Means

A favourite exam construction plants a variable inside the sequence. The terms x plus 3; 2 x plus 1; 4 x minus 5 are consecutive terms of an arithmetic sequence — find x. One sentence solves it: in an arithmetic sequence, neighbouring gaps are equal. Middle term minus first term equals last term minus middle term. So 2 x plus 1, minus the quantity x plus 3, equals 4 x minus 5, minus the quantity 2 x plus 1. The left side reduces to x minus 2. The right side reduces to 2 x minus 6. Equate them: x minus 2 equals 2 x minus 6, so x is 4.

Do not stop at x. Substitute back: the terms become 7; 9; 11, gaps of 2 and 2 — a genuine arithmetic sequence, and writing that check usually banks a mark. The classic wound in this question type is a dropped sign while subtracting a bracket: minus the quantity x plus 3 means minus x minus 3, with both signs flipped.

Equal gaps also drive arithmetic means — numbers inserted between two given values so the whole list climbs evenly. Insert four arithmetic means between 3 and 18. Four insertions make 3 the first term and 18 the sixth, so 18 equals 3 plus 5 d, giving d equal to 3. The sequence reads 3; 6; 9; 12; 15; 18, and the means are 6, 9, 12 and 15. Count with care: four means create a six-term sequence with FIVE jumps. The jumps always outnumber the means by exactly one.

A single arithmetic mean between two numbers is simply their halfway mark — between 6 and 20 it is 13, their average. That is the reason the word mean appears here at all.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — equal gaps build the equation, substitution supplies the check, and inserted means always bring one extra jump.

## Subtopic: From Word Problem to Term Number

Anything that grows by a fixed amount per step is secretly arithmetic: a wage with a fixed yearly rise, rows of seedlings, seats added per row, a fee that climbs by the same amount each month. Translation always asks the same three questions. What is the starting value? That is a. What is the fixed change per step? That is d. And is the question after a term's VALUE, or after the POSITION where a value is reached?

Worked case. A tree nursery plants its beds in rows: the first row holds 12 seedlings, and every following row holds 5 more than the row before. How many seedlings stand in row nine, and which row is the first to hold 92? Here a is 12 and d is 5, so T n equals 12 plus the quantity n minus one, times 5. Row nine: T9 equals 12 plus eight fives, which is 52 seedlings. For the second part, set T n equal to 92: the quantity n minus one, times 5, equals 80, so n minus one is 16 and n is 17. Row seventeen is the first to hold 92 seedlings.

Two traps stalk this territory. The off-by-one: row nine carries eight increases, not nine, because the first row starts the pattern without an increase — the formula's n minus one manages this automatically if you trust it. And the inequality version: if a question asks when the count first PASSES a value that no term equals exactly — say more than 100 seedlings, where solving gives n equal to 18,6 — the answer is the next whole position up, row nineteen. Positions round up to the next term when a threshold is being crossed, never to the nearest.

The final questions of this part are with you now — name a, name d, write the general term, and make n answer the question that was actually asked.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same arithmetic sequences again, seen from a staircase and a delivery scooter — identical rules, identical answers, built from things you can picture.

## Subtopic: Steps of the Same Size

Think of a staircase where every step has exactly the same rise. That picture IS the arithmetic sequence. The bottom step is the first term. The rise of each step is the common difference d. Climb, and the numbers you stand on are the terms: 4, then 11, then 18, then 25 — every step lifting you by 7.

Checking a staircase means measuring the gaps. Subtract each number from the one after it. Every gap identical: arithmetic. One rebel gap — like 3; 6; 12, where the first gap is 3 and the next is 6 — and the staircase is condemned; the sequence fails the test. A builder never signs off after measuring a single step, and neither should you.

Staircases also descend. The list 31; 26; 21 drops 5 at a time, so d is minus 5. A negative d is not a mistake — it is a stairway heading down to the basement, and only the correct order of subtraction shows it honestly: later number minus earlier number. Twenty-six minus thirty-one is minus five. Swap the order and you would think you were climbing while sinking.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on spotting the constant step are coming to you right now. Measure the gap, measure it again, and keep the subtraction pointing forwards.

## Subtopic: Jumping Straight to Term Two Hundred

A delivery scooter charges 4 rand for the first kilometre and 7 rand for every kilometre after that. One kilometre costs 4. Two kilometres cost 11. Three cost 18. You could tick your way to any distance — or think like the rider: begin with 4, then add 7 for each kilometre AFTER the first. Two hundred kilometres is 4 plus one hundred and ninety-nine sevens: 1397 rand.

That thought, written down, is the general term. T n equals a plus the quantity n minus one, times d. The n minus one is doing real work — it records that the first kilometre is the starting fee, not an add-on; the adding begins only after it. Test it small: term four should be the start plus THREE additions, and seven fours minus three is 25. It checks out.

The formula runs in reverse too, and that is the version exams love. You carry 88 rand — how far can you ride? Set 7 n minus 3 equal to 88: n is 13, so exactly thirteen kilometres. Now try 150 rand: 7 n minus 3 equals 150 puts n at 21,857 and a bit. There is no kilometre twenty-one comma eight five seven. The price display never shows 150 — it steps from 144 straight to 151. When n refuses to be whole, the amount was never on the meter, and stating that fact IS the answer.

Your questions for this part are up now. Start plus jumps times step, prove the formula on a term you can see, and let a broken n tell you the number never appears.

## Subtopic: The Missing Rung and the Fair Middle

Sometimes a question presents a ladder with one rung obscured: three terms in a row, each hiding an x, such as x plus 3; 2 x plus 1; 4 x minus 5. The rescue line is a single fact — on a straight ladder, the space below any rung equals the space above it. Middle minus first must equal last minus middle. Write that as an equation, solve, and x is 4, so the true rungs read 7; 9; 11. Then look at what you built: gaps of 2 and 2, a straight ladder, answer confirmed. Always finish by looking.

The same fairness explains arithmetic means. Insert four numbers between 3 and 18 so the list rises evenly — like planting four fence posts between two corner posts so all the spacing agrees. Six posts standing means five equal spaces, and a run of 15 shared across five spaces is 3 per space: 3; 6; 9; 12; 15; 18. The planted numbers 6, 9, 12 and 15 are the arithmetic means. The counting trap lives right here — four planted posts make FIVE spaces, always one more space than posts, because both corners were already standing.

And one mean between two numbers is nothing but the fair middle: halfway between 6 and 20 sits 13, the same average you have computed since primary school. Same word, same fairness — a value placed so the spacing favours neither side.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: equal spaces above and below the middle rung, substitute x back to see the real numbers, and count one more space than the posts you planted.
