Hey, it's Mandy — I watched that sigma symbol land on screen and felt half the class flinch. Let me shrink it back to size. Sigma is not a new branch of mathematics; it is a packing slip. Bottom: where the counting starts. Top: where it stops. Right: the recipe for each item. Read it out loud like an instruction and it does exactly what it says.

Three numbers to keep within reach. First, the count of terms: top minus bottom, plus one — never forget the plus one, it is fence posts all over again. Second, the arithmetic sum: fold the list, pair first with last, and S n equals n over 2 times the quantity a plus l. If the last term hides, wear the other costume: n over 2, times 2 a plus n minus one d. Third, the geometric sum: a times r to the n minus one, over r minus one — and when the ratio is a genuine fraction, the infinite version collapses to a over one minus r.

When a question asks HOW MANY terms make a total, expect a quadratic in n or a same-base exponent equation. Solve it calmly, and throw away any root that could not count real things — negative or fractional n dies with a written sentence.

And the sum to infinity always opens with a bouncer's check at the door: is r strictly between minus one and one? Only then does the total exist.

Packing slip, fold in half, telescope shut, check the ratio. That is the whole lesson, and the practice questions ahead use nothing beyond it. Go and pack those crates!
