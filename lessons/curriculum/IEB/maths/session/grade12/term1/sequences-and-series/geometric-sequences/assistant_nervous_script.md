Hey, it's Mandy — if the exponents in this lesson made your shoulders climb towards your ears, let me shrink it back down. A geometric sequence is a photocopier with a dial. One setting, applied again and again. Every question is about that dial.

First question: is one machine responsible? Divide each term by the one before it, all the way along. Same quotient everywhere means geometric; one different quotient means someone touched the dial. Fractions, negatives and shrinking are all legal settings — only zero is banned.

Second question: what comes out after n copies? T n equals a times r to the power n minus one. The exponent counts multiplications, and it runs one behind the position because the first term is the original, not a copy. Build the formula, then test it on a term you can see — if it reproduces term four, it will not lie about term eight.

Third question: two terms known, machine unknown? Divide the bigger fact by the smaller. The start cancels and a clean power of r remains — three positions apart means r cubed. Read the exponent as the number of jumps.

And for three-in-a-row questions with an x inside, one identity does everything: middle squared equals the product of the neighbours. Solve, substitute back, and divide to see the ratio with your own eyes. If an even root hands you r squared equals 16, keep both signs alive until the question kills one.

Divide, count the jumps, respect both signs. The practice questions waiting for you now use exactly those three moves and nothing else. Go and take them apart!
