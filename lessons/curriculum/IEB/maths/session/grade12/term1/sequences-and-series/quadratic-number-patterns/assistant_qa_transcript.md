**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! The most upvoted one: why does half the second difference give the value of a? It feels like a magic trick.

**Tutor:** It is bookkeeping, not magic, and one substitution exposes it. Write the shape T n equals a n squared plus b n plus c at positions one, two and three: a plus b plus c, then 4 a plus 2 b plus c, then 9 a plus 3 b plus c. Take the first differences: the first is 3 a plus b, the next is 5 a plus b. Now the second difference: 5 a plus b, minus 3 a plus b, equals 2 a. Everything else — the b's, the c's — has already cancelled itself on the way down the table, and only 2 a survives to the second level. So the constant you see at the bottom of the table IS 2 a, which is why halving it recovers a. The same working shows why the first gap equals 3 a plus b, handing you b next, and why the first term hands you c last. The order matters: a, then b, then c, each equation surrendering exactly one letter. Once you have seen the cancellation happen on paper, the recipe stops being a spell and becomes three lines of subtraction you could rebuild in the margin of any exam.

**Mandy:** Next one: in the 250 question, the discriminant came out as 1993 and some people froze. What is the discriminant actually telling us there?

**Tutor:** It is telling you what kind of n is about to emerge, before you finish the calculation. Setting 2 n squared plus n plus 1 equal to 250 gives 2 n squared plus n minus 249 equals 0, and the discriminant is 1 plus 1992, which is 1993. Now interrogate it. A perfect square discriminant means the quadratic factorises and n comes out rational; here 44 squared is 1936 and 45 squared is 2025, so 1993 sits between consecutive squares and is not a perfect square. That single observation already settles the question — n will be irrational, an irrational number is certainly not a natural number, and so 250 is not a term. No further decimals needed, though computing n as roughly 10,91 does no harm. The freeze happens when people expect the discriminant to behave; the skill is realising an awkward discriminant IS the answer, wearing work clothes. Confirm with the terms if you like: term ten is 211, term eleven is 254, and the pattern steps over 250 entirely. Then write the sentence that earns the mark: n is not a natural number, so 250 is not in the pattern.

**Mandy:** Third: in the hidden x question, why do we push the x through the difference table instead of substituting positions into the formula?

**Tutor:** Because the difference table is where the given information actually lives. The question hands you x; 9; 22; 41 and one extra fact: the second difference is constant at 6. You do not yet know a, b or c, so the formula route has three unknowns and one equation — a dead end. The table route has one unknown and one equation. Let x ride along: first differences are 9 minus x, then 13, then 19. Second differences: 13 minus the quantity 9 minus x, which simplifies to 4 plus x, and 19 minus 13, which is 6. The constancy fact snaps the trap shut: 4 plus x equals 6, so x is 2. Notice the sign discipline inside that subtraction — minus the quantity 9 minus x becomes minus 9 plus x, both signs flipped, and that is where this question type collects most of its casualties. Once x is known, the table becomes honest numbers — 2; 9; 22; 41 with gaps 7; 13; 19 — and only THEN does the three-fact machine start up to deliver T n equals 3 n squared minus 2 n plus 1. Table first to find x, formula second. Never the other way round.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the smallest term, someone answered with the vertex value from the parabola instead of a term. What is the difference?

**Tutor:** The parabola is a smooth curve that exists everywhere; the pattern exists only at the natural numbers sitting on it, and the question asks about the pattern. For T n equals n squared minus 10 n plus 27, the vertex formula minus b over 2 a gives n equal to 5, and because 5 happens to be a natural number, the smallest term really is T5, which is 25 minus 50 plus 27, equal to 2. But shift the pattern slightly and the vertex can land at, say, n equal to 5,4 — a point on the curve where NO term lives. The parabola's minimum value down there is not a term of anything. The procedure then is to test the natural numbers on either side, n equal to 5 and n equal to 6, evaluate both, and keep the smaller. Two evaluations, one comparison, done. The habit to build is a final question to yourself before writing any answer in this topic: is this n a position that actually exists? The vertex gives you the neighbourhood; only a natural number names a resident. Answer with a term the pattern truly contains, never with a coordinate the curve merely passes through.
