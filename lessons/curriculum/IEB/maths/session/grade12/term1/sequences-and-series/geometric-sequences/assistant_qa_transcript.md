**Mandy:** Class questions before we wrap! The one I saw most: in T n equals a r to the power n minus one, why is the exponent n minus one and not n?

**Tutor:** Count the multiplications on the road to a term. Term one is the original — no multiplication has happened yet. Term two carries one multiplication, term three carries two, and the count always trails the position by exactly one, because the first term is a launch pad, not a jump. Term n therefore wears the exponent n minus one. Write r to the n instead and every answer is one multiplication too big — for the sequence 3; 12; 48; 192 your version of term four would be 768 instead of 192, wrong by a whole factor of the ratio. The insurance policy costs ten seconds: build the formula, then aim it at a term you can see. Three times four cubed must give 192, and it does. If it had given 768, the extra exponent is caught and corrected before it reaches any marker. Same off-by-one, by the way, as the arithmetic n minus one — the two families share the same skeleton, with multiplication swapped in for addition.

***

**Mandy:** Next: when two terms are given, like T2 is 10 and T5 is 80, why do we divide the equations instead of subtracting like we did for arithmetic sequences?

**Tutor:** Because the operation you use to strip away the unknown must match the operation that builds the sequence. Arithmetic sequences are built by adding, so subtraction peels them cleanly. Geometric sequences are built by multiplying, so division does the peeling. Write the two facts: a r equals 10, and a r to the fourth equals 80. Subtracting those would leave a tangled expression with a still inside it. Dividing them cancels a completely and leaves r cubed equals 8 — pure, solvable, done: r is 2, and then a is 5 from either equation. The exponent three is not an accident: it is the number of jumps from position two to position five, and the quotient 8 is how much the sequence grew across those jumps. That reading works as a general tool — the quotient of any two terms equals r raised to the number of positions between them. Finish the way you always should: check the unused fact. T5 equals 5 times 2 to the fourth, which is 80. Certified.

***

**Mandy:** Third one: in the question with x; x plus 4; x plus 6, the answer came out negative and half the class assumed they had made an error. Why is a negative sequence fine?

**Tutor:** Because the definition of geometric never mentions the word positive. It demands one thing only: a constant quotient between neighbours. Solving the middle-squared identity gives x equal to minus 8, so the terms are minus 8; minus 4; minus 2. Now run the test honestly: minus 4 over minus 8 is a half, and minus 2 over minus 4 is a half. Constant ratio, certificate issued — a perfectly healthy geometric sequence that happens to live below zero, climbing towards it by halving. Notice the ratio is positive even though every term is negative, because a negative divided by a negative is positive. Keep that separate from the alternating case: a sequence like 5; minus 10; 20 has a negative RATIO, minus 2, and its signs flip forever. Negative terms with a positive ratio, or sign-flipping terms with a negative ratio — both legal, both certified by the same division test. The only rejection the definition ever issues is a zero term, which would poison every division after it.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: when r squared came out as 16, someone wrote r equals 4 and lost a mark. What was missing?

**Tutor:** The other machine. An even root always has two answers: if r squared is 16, then r is 4 or minus 4, and here both are real sequences, not algebraic ghosts. Say the first term is 5. With r equal to 4 the sequence runs 5; 20; 80 — steady growth. With r equal to minus 4 it runs 5; minus 20; 80 — same sizes, alternating signs. Divide the terms of either list and the test passes in both, so mathematics alone cannot choose between them; only the question can. Sometimes it does: a phrase like all terms are positive, or a context where the quantities are heights or amounts of money, executes the negative option immediately, and then writing r equals 4 alone is complete. But with no such clause, the full answer names both ratios and ideally both sequences, and the mark you mention pays precisely for the second one. The discipline to build: every time a ratio or any unknown emerges from an even power, pause and ask what happened to the negative root — either present it, or state in writing why the question forbids it.
