Hey, it's Mandy — I know this topic carries the two words that raise pulses: prove and theorem. Geometry proofs feel like they demand inspiration on the spot. Here is the secret: this lesson's proofs are fixed routines, the same lines in the same order every time, and the pictures make them stick.

The first picture is a ladder in the sun. Climb any fraction of the ladder, and the parallel rays use up exactly that fraction of the shadow. That is the whole proportionality theorem: a parallel line cuts the other two sides of a triangle in the same ratio. Its proof needs one sentence at its heart — triangles on the same base, between the same parallels, hold equal areas — and the ratios then have no choice.

The second picture is a photograph. Enlarge a triangle and the angles never change while all sides stretch by one factor. For triangles that runs backwards too: match the angles and the shared factor must exist. When you use it, the only discipline is writing the letters in matching order — pair the equal angles first, then let every ratio read straight off the letters.

The third picture is a folded corner: drop the right angle onto the hypotenuse, and each small triangle is a photograph of the whole. Cross-multiply the ratios and the squares appear — each leg squared is the hypotenuse times its own piece — and adding the two pieces back into the whole hypotenuse hands you Pythagoras.

For the number questions, one whisper saves you every time: part or whole? Match part with part, whole with whole, and check your answer against the whole sides at the end.

Ladder, photograph, folded corner. Three pictures, three routines, no inspiration required. Go and try the practice questions — you've got this!
