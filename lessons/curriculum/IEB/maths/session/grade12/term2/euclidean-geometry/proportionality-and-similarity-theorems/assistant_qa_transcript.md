**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: in the proportionality proof, the step everyone accepts without seeing is that triangles B D E and C E D have equal areas. Can you slow that moment right down?

**Tutor:** Gladly, because that single moment is the entire proof — everything else is bookkeeping. Area of a triangle is half base times height, so two triangles agree in area the instant they agree in base and in height. Look at B D E and C E D. They visibly share the base: both stand on the segment D E. Now the heights. The height of B D E is the perpendicular distance from the apex B down to the line through D E; the height of C E D is the perpendicular distance from C to that same line. And here the given fact finally does its work: D E is parallel to B C. Two parallel lines stay the same distance apart along their whole length — that is practically the definition of parallel — so B and C, both sitting on the line B C, hang at exactly the same perpendicular distance from the line D E. Same base, same height, equal areas, even though the two triangles look nothing alike. Notice how the logic distributes: the parallelism is spent at precisely this step and nowhere else, while the two ratio equations upstream use only the shared-height fact. In an exam proof, write the reason in words — same base D E, between the same parallel lines — because that sentence is the mark. Lose it and the proof reads as assertion.

**Mandy:** Next: the costume-change confusion. When are we allowed to swap A D over D B for A D over A B, and when is the swap illegal?

**Tutor:** The swap is pure fraction algebra, and seeing it once kills the confusion. Suppose A D over D B equals A E over E C — the theorem's own statement, part against part. Add one to each side, writing one as D B over D B on the left and E C over E C on the right. The left side becomes A D plus D B, all over D B — and A D plus D B is the whole side A B, so the left is A B over D B. Same on the right: A C over E C. Flip both fractions and you hold D B over A B equals E C over A C; run a similar manipulation and A D over A B equals A E over A C. So parts proportional, wholes proportional, parts-to-wholes proportional — the costumes are all equivalent, and you choose whichever one contains your three knowns and one unknown. What is ILLEGAL is wearing two costumes in one equation: a part-to-part fraction on the left equated to a part-to-whole fraction on the right. Eight over six against twelve over twenty-one — the numbers came from today's figure, and both fractions are individually correct statements about it, yet equating them is false, because they measure different comparisons. The safeguard costs five seconds: before writing the equals sign, point at each of your four segments and say part or whole. If the pattern on the left matches the pattern on the right, proceed. If not, change costume, not the theorem.

**Mandy:** Third: why is the letter order in a similarity statement worth marks by itself? Some of us write the triangles in any order and fix things later.

**Tutor:** Because the order IS the content of the statement — the letters are not names so much as a seating plan. When you write triangle A B C similar to triangle D E F, you are asserting three angle equalities in one breath: the angle at A equals the angle at D, B equals E, C equals F, first with first, second with second, third with third. Every ratio you may legally write descends from that seating: A B over D E, because A B connects seats one and two while D E connects the matching seats. Now scramble it — say triangle A B C similar to triangle E F D — and you have asserted DIFFERENT angle equalities, most likely false ones, and every ratio you derive inherits the falsehood even if your mental pairing was right all along. Fixing it later rarely survives exam pressure, because the wrong ratios look just as tidy as the right ones. So build the statement in two moves, always. Move one: identify the equal angles and say the pairing out loud — the right angle here matches the right angle there, the shared angle matches itself. Move two: write each triangle's letters walking through the paired seats in the same sequence. Do that, and in the Pythagoras figure the awkward-looking statement, triangle A B D similar to triangle C B A, assembles itself correctly — and its ratios hand you A B squared equals B D times B C without a moment of doubt. The order is not etiquette. It is the mathematics, compressed.

**Mandy:** Last one: the altitude relation, fold squared equals piece times piece. Where does it come from, and how would a numeric question use it?

**Tutor:** It comes from the third similarity hiding in the folded figure — the one between the two SMALL triangles. You have already seen each small triangle proved similar to the whole; but similarity passes through: two triangles each similar to a third are similar to each other. So the left-hand small triangle and the right-hand small triangle are the same shape. Set up the ratio between them with the fold as the bridge: in the left triangle, the fold A D plays the role of upright leg; in the right triangle it plays the role of base leg. Writing the matching ratio, B D over A D equals A D over D C — the fold appears on both sides, once upstairs, once downstairs. Cross-multiply and A D squared equals B D times D C: the fold squared equals the product of the two hypotenuse pieces. Numerically it is a gift. Pieces of 9 and 16: the fold squared is one hundred and forty-four, fold 12, no other side needed. And the relation runs in reverse: told the fold is 12 and one piece is 9, divide to find the other piece is 16, and the whole hypotenuse is 25. Then the leg relations join in — each leg squared is the hypotenuse times its own piece, so the left leg squared is nine times twenty-five, leg 15, and the right leg squared is sixteen times twenty-five, leg 20. One figure, three product relations, and any two given numbers unlock all the rest. That is why this figure earns its own questions: it is a small machine for manufacturing lengths.
