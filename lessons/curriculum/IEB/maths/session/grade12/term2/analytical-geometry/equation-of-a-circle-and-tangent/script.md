# Part 1 — Expert

Until now, analytical geometry has lived entirely on straight lines: distances, midpoints, gradients, angles of inclination. Grade twelve introduces its first and only curve — the circle — and immediately pairs it with the tangent, the straight line that touches the curve at a single point. By the end of this lesson you will own four tools: reading centre and radius straight off the centre-radius equation, completing the square to recover a circle from its expanded form, the right angle between a radius and its tangent, and a four-step method that produces any tangent's equation on demand.

## Subtopic: The Circle as a Distance Statement

Strip away the mystery first: the circle equation is the distance formula with a condition attached. Choose a centre, call it a and b, and a radius r. A roaming point x and y belongs to the circle precisely when its distance from the centre equals r. Set the distance formula equal to r, square both sides to clear the root, and you have it: x minus a, all squared, plus y minus b, all squared, equals r squared.

Practise reading one immediately. Take x plus 2, all squared, plus y minus 1, all squared, equals 25. The centre is minus 2 and 1. Watch the sign reversal carefully: x plus 2 is really x minus negative 2, so the centre's x coordinate is minus 2, not 2. The most reliable habit is to ask what value makes each bracket zero — x equals minus 2 kills the first bracket, y equals 1 kills the second, and those two killers are the centre. The radius is the square root of the right side: root 25 is 5. Writing the radius as 25 is the other classic slip, and both slips together account for most lost marks on this opener.

A centre at the origin collapses the equation to x squared plus y squared equals r squared — nothing to subtract, so no brackets survive.

The equation also works as a position tester. Does the point 2 and 4 lie on our circle? Two plus two is 4, squared is 16. Four minus one is 3, squared is 9. Sixteen plus 9 is 25, exactly r squared, so the point sits on the circle. Try minus 2 and minus 3 instead: zero plus 16 is 16, below 25, so that point lies strictly inside. Any total above 25 lands outside. One substitution, three possible verdicts.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Kill the brackets for the centre, root the right side for the radius, substitute to place a point.

## Subtopic: Completing the Square

Test setters seldom hand you the tidy form. Expect x squared plus y squared plus 4 x minus 2 y minus 20 equals 0, followed by the instruction: determine the centre and the radius. The recovery tool is completing the square, applied twice — once to the x terms, once to the y terms.

Organise first: gather x squared plus 4 x, gather y squared minus 2 y, and carry the loose minus 20 to the right as plus 20. Now complete each square. Half of 4 is 2, squared gives 4, so x squared plus 4 x rewrites as x plus 2, all squared, minus 4. Half of minus 2 is minus 1, squared gives 1, so y squared minus 2 y rewrites as y minus 1, all squared, minus 1. Move the minus 4 and minus 1 across and collect: x plus 2 squared, plus y minus 1 squared, equals 20 plus 4 plus 1, which is 25. The expanded mess was our circle all along — centre minus 2 and 1, radius 5.

Hold on to the accounting principle: every number a completed bracket introduces must be compensated on the other side of the equals sign. Each bracket, once expanded, contains an extra constant the original never had; failing to pay for it leaves the centre intact but corrupts the radius without any visible warning. The cheapest audit is to expand your finished form and compare it with the original term by term, or to substitute one known point into both versions.

Two structural checks before you even start: a legitimate circle equation carries x squared and y squared with IDENTICAL coefficients and contains no x y term. A shared coefficient on both squares must be divided out first. And if completing the square leaves zero or a negative number on the right, there is no circle — the equation describes one lonely point, or no points at all, and stating that IS the answer.

Stop for this section's questions now — gather, halve, square, pay the balance across, and read off centre and radius.

## Subtopic: Radius Meets Tangent at Ninety Degrees

A tangent touches a circle at exactly one point, called the point of tangency. Picture a line slicing through a circle at two points, then sliding steadily outward: the two intersection points drift together and merge into one at the instant of touching. The theorem that powers every tangent calculation is this: the radius drawn to the point of tangency stands PERPENDICULAR to the tangent. A rolling wheel on a level road tells the same story — one contact point, and the spoke down to it meets the road at exactly ninety degrees.

Algebra receives the theorem through gradients: perpendicular lines have gradients whose product is minus 1. Therefore the tangent's gradient is the negative reciprocal of the radius gradient at the touching point. A radius gradient of 3 over 4 forces a tangent gradient of minus 4 over 3. A radius gradient of minus 5 forces a tangent gradient of one fifth.

That single right angle resolves whole families of questions with almost no work. Is a given line tangent at a given point? Multiply its gradient by the radius gradient there and demand minus 1. What is the shortest distance from the centre to a tangent line? Exactly one radius, measured along the perpendicular. How many tangents can be drawn from a point outside the circle? Precisely two, arranged symmetrically about the line joining the external point to the centre.

Quick pause — the questions on the radius-tangent right angle are with you now. One touch, one perpendicular radius, a gradient product of minus 1.

## Subtopic: The Tangent Equation Routine

Assemble everything into a routine of four steps. Task: find the tangent to the circle x plus 2 squared, plus y minus 1 squared, equals 25, at the point 2 and 4.

Step one: verify the point actually lies on the circle. We did: 16 plus 9 is 25. Never skip this check — a tangent at a point off the circle is nonsense, and question setters do plant such points deliberately. Step two: compute the radius gradient from the centre minus 2 and 1 to the point 2 and 4. Change in y: 4 minus 1 is 3. Change in x: 2 minus negative 2 is 4. Gradient 3 over 4. Step three: flip and negate for the tangent gradient: minus 4 over 3. Step four: write the line through 2 and 4 with gradient minus 4 over 3. Y minus 4 equals minus 4 over 3, times x minus 2. Expanding: y equals minus 4 over 3 x, plus 8 over 3, plus 4, which settles at y equals minus 4 over 3 x plus 20 over 3.

Ten seconds buys certainty: the touching point must satisfy the finished line. Minus 4 over 3 times 2 is minus 8 over 3; add 20 over 3 to get 12 over 3, which is 4. It balances.

Origin-centred circles run the routine even faster. Tangent to x squared plus y squared equals 100 at the point 6 and 8: the radius gradient comes straight from the origin as 8 over 6, which is 4 over 3; the tangent gradient is minus 3 over 4; the line is y equals minus 3 over 4 x plus 25 over 2. And notice the shortcut worth memorising: for an origin circle, the tangent at the point x one and y one always has gradient minus x one over y one.

The final questions of this part are with you now — point on circle, radius gradient, negative reciprocal, point-gradient form, and the ten-second certificate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same circles, same tangents — this time with a goat in a camp and a wheelbarrow wheel. The pictures change; the answers do not.

## Subtopic: Every Point the Rope Can Reach

Picture a goat clipped to a steel peg by a rope 5 metres long, and suppose the goat keeps the rope stretched tight while it walks. The full lap it walks is every point sitting exactly 5 metres from the peg — and that lap is the circle. Its equation is only the distance formula insisting the rope stays tight: x minus a, squared, plus y minus b, squared, equals 5 squared, with a and b marking where the peg is hammered in.

Read the equation like a map of the camp. In x plus 2 squared, plus y minus 1 squared, equals 25, the peg sits at minus 2 and 1. The signs reverse because the equation stores the SUBTRACTIONS used to measure distance — x plus 2 is the roaming point minus a peg coordinate of minus 2. And the rope is the square root of 25, which is 5, because the equation keeps the rope length SQUARED. Root it before you write anything down.

The equation will also referee any point in the camp. Feed in 2 and 4: the left side adds up to exactly 25, so the goat reaches that point with the rope dead tight — it lies ON the circle. Feed in minus 2 and minus 3: the total is 16, under 25, so that spot is inside easy reach. Anything totalling over 25 stands beyond the rope entirely. One substitution and the verdict is read out.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on reading the circle are coming to you right now. Peg from the reversed signs, rope from the square root, substitution for the verdict.

## Subtopic: The Circle in Disguise

Sometimes the camp record arrives scrambled: x squared plus y squared plus 4 x minus 2 y minus 20 equals 0. Nothing about the camp changed — someone multiplied everything out and shook the pieces loose. Completing the square is the unpacking that finds the peg and the rope again.

Treat it like packing a school bag: x items in one pocket, y items in another, loose numbers across the equals sign. Then the halving move, twice. For x squared plus 4 x: halve the 4 to get 2, and write x plus 2 squared — but that bracket quietly brings along an extra 4, so the 4 must be recorded on the other side as well. For y squared minus 2 y: halve the 2 to get 1, write y minus 1 squared, and record its stowaway 1 across. The right side collects 20 plus 4 plus 1, which is 25. Peg at minus 2 and 1, rope of 5 — the camp restored.

The entire skill is refusing to let a stowaway ride free: every constant a bracket smuggles in gets declared on both sides of the equation. Miss one, and the rope length quietly changes — your record now describes some other goat in some other camp. Unsure? Multiply your tidy version back out and watch it match the scrambled original piece by piece.

Your questions for this part are up now. Pack the pockets, halve, square, and declare every stowaway on both sides.

## Subtopic: Where the Wheel Kisses the Road

A wheelbarrow wheel rolling over smooth concrete touches it at just one point. That is tangency — the concrete is a tangent to the wheel. Now sight along the spoke from the axle straight down to that contact point: it hits the concrete at a perfect right angle, every time, no matter where the wheel rolls. Axle, spoke, concrete: centre, radius, tangent. That perpendicular spoke carries the entire topic.

In coordinates, a right angle means gradients multiplying to minus 1, so the tangent's gradient is the spoke's gradient flipped over and negated. Spoke gradient 3 over 4? Concrete gradient minus 4 over 3. Finding a tangent's equation then becomes a four-stop trip. Stop one: confirm the contact point genuinely sits on the wheel by substituting it into the circle equation. Stop two: work out the spoke's gradient from axle to contact point, rise over run. Stop three: flip and negate to get the road's gradient. Stop four: push a line through the contact point with that gradient, and the equation appears.

Run it for the wheel with axle at minus 2 and 1, touching at 2 and 4: the spoke rises 3 for every 4 across, so the concrete falls 4 for every 3 across, and the finished line reads y equals minus 4 over 3 x plus 20 over 3. Last courtesy before moving on: substitute the contact point back into the line, watch both sides agree, and the answer is sealed.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: one contact point, one square spoke, flip and negate, and let the point-gradient form write the line for you.
