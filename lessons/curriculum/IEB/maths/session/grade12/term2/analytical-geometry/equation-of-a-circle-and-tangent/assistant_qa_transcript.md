**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one is the perennial: why do the centre's signs come out backwards in the circle equation? Someone read x plus 2 squared today and confidently wrote the centre at positive 2.

**Tutor:** The cure is to remember what the brackets are doing, because then the reversal stops being a memorised rule. The equation is the distance formula, and distance is built from subtractions: roaming coordinate minus centre coordinate. If the centre's x coordinate is minus 2, subtracting it gives x minus negative 2, which the equation writes as x plus 2 — the sign you see has ALREADY absorbed the subtraction. To read it back, ask the question that never lies: what value of x makes this bracket zero? For x plus 2, the answer is x equals minus 2, and that is the centre's x coordinate. Do the same for the y bracket. Two bracket-killing questions, asked every single time, and the sign trap cannot touch you. While you are at it, guard the other end of the equation too: the right side stores the radius squared, so 25 is a radius of 5. Bracket-killers on the left, square root on the right — that pair of habits covers the two commonest lost marks in the whole topic.

***

**Mandy:** Next one: in completing the square, a few of us can do the mechanics but genuinely do not understand why extra numbers appear on the right side. Where do the plus 4 and plus 1 in today's example actually come from?

**Tutor:** They are repayments, and the brackets are the borrowers. When you trade x squared plus 4 x for the bracket x plus 2 squared, expand that bracket honestly: it contains x squared plus 4 x PLUS 4. The bracket carries a 4 that the original expression never owned — the rewriting borrowed it. An equation only stays true if the borrowing is repaid, so you either write minus 4 beside the bracket or add 4 to the opposite side; those are the same repayment written in two places. The y bracket borrows a 1 the same way. That is exactly why today's right side climbed from 20 to 20 plus 4 plus 1, which is 25. Skip a repayment and nothing looks broken — the equation still resembles a circle — but the radius it describes has quietly changed, and every subsequent answer inherits the error. The audit costs under a minute: expand your completed form and match it against the original term by term, or push one convenient point through both versions. If both agree, your books balance and the circle is genuine.

***

**Mandy:** Third question, straight from the homework thread: the tangent never appears alone — every method starts with the radius. Why is the radius always the way in?

**Tutor:** Because the radius owns the only right angle in the picture, and that right angle is what touching once MEANS. The theorem says the radius to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent. Here is why it cannot be otherwise: the shortest segment from a point to a line is always the perpendicular one. If the radius met the tangent at any angle other than ninety degrees, then some other point on that line would lie closer to the centre than the touching point does — closer than one radius. A point of the line inside the circle forces the line to cross the circle twice, and it stops being a tangent. So single contact and perpendicularity are the same fact wearing two outfits. Coordinates simply cash the theorem in: perpendicular gradients multiply to minus 1, so one cheap rise-over-run from centre to touching point hands you the radius gradient, and flipping and negating hands you the tangent gradient. The radius is the way in because it is the only thing you can compute directly — the tangent then costs nothing extra.

**Mandy:** Last one: an exercise asked us to PROVE that y equals minus 4 over 3 x plus 20 over 3 is a tangent to today's circle, and answers ranged from one line to a full page. What does a complete argument actually need?

**Tutor:** Two facts, each demonstrated, and a sentence saying so. Route one works when you have a candidate touching point. First show the point is shared: 2 and 4 satisfies the circle, since 16 plus 9 is 25, and satisfies the line, since minus 8 over 3 plus 20 over 3 is 4. Then show the right angle: the radius gradient from minus 2 and 1 up to 2 and 4 is 3 over 4, the line's gradient is minus 4 over 3, and their product is minus 1. Shared point plus perpendicular radius — tangency established, and you say both in words. Route two needs no candidate point: substitute the line into the circle equation and show the resulting quadratic has exactly one root, a zero discriminant, because meeting once is the very definition of a tangent. What does NOT suffice is either fact alone. A perpendicular gradient without a shared point proves nothing — a line can be perpendicular to some radius while missing the circle entirely. A shared point without the right angle could just as well be a secant passing through. Touch once, stand square: prove both, state both.
