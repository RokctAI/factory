Hey, it's Mandy — the word calculus comes with a reputation, and the h's and the limit signs can make it feel like maths just left the planet. It hasn't. This whole lesson holds exactly two ideas, and you already use both whenever you glance from a trip timer to a speedometer.

Idea one: the limit. Some expressions have a pothole — a single input where they answer zero over zero and go quiet. The limit is the value all the neighbouring outputs point at. The routine is two moves: substitute, and if zero over zero appears, factorise, cancel, substitute again. That is genuinely the whole limits section.

Idea two: the derivative. Average speed across a trip is easy — distance over time. The speedometer reading is the same fraction taken over a trip shrunk to nothing. In symbols: f at x plus h, minus f at x, over h, then let h shrink away. Four steps, always in order. Write f of x plus h. Subtract f of x. Divide by h and cancel it — it ALWAYS cancels, and when it refuses, an algebra slip is hiding in your expansion, usually a dropped bracket. Then set h to zero in what is left.

Rehearse the recipe on the four functions it gets asked about: a constant gives zero, a straight line gives its own slope, x squared gives two x, and one over x gives minus one over x squared. The reciprocal needs a common denominator and pays you back with a minus sign — check it against the falling hyperbola and enjoy the agreement.

One tactical habit: write the definition at the top of every first-principles answer before touching the specific function. It earns its own credit and settles your hands for the rest.

Pothole, speedometer, four steps. That is the entire lesson, and by your third practice run it will feel automatic. Now go take it out on the practice questions!
