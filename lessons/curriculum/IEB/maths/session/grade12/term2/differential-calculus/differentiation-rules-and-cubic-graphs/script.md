# Part 1 — Expert

First principles built the derivative by hand; this session mechanises it. A single rule differentiates every power of x in one line, and armed with it the cubic function — the headline graph of grade twelve — surrenders completely: turning points, shape, tangents, and the running commentary its derivative provides on every climb and descent.

## Subtopic: The Power Rule and Rewriting First

The rule itself: the derivative of x to the power n is n times x to the power n minus one. The exponent drops down in front as a multiplier, and the power itself is reduced by one. It works for every real exponent — positive, negative, or a fraction. Two companion rules travel with it: a constant multiplying a function simply multiplies the derivative, and a sum differentiates term by term, each piece handled independently.

A worked run. For y equals two x to the fifth, minus four x, plus seven: the first term gives ten x to the fourth, the second gives minus four, and the lone seven differentiates to zero. So d y by d x is ten x to the fourth minus four.

Since the rule only recognises powers of x, the genuine skill is REWRITING before you differentiate. Three disguises recur endlessly. Surds: four root x is four x to the power one half, whose derivative is two x to the power minus one half — two over root x. Reciprocals: five over x cubed is five x to the power minus three, with derivative minus fifteen x to the power minus four. And quotients that are secretly sums: three x to the fourth plus x, all over x squared, splits into three x squared plus one over x — rewrite the tail as x to the minus one, and the derivative is six x minus x to the minus two. This course contains no quotient rule and needs none: every quotient you will meet either splits term by term or simplifies first.

Take care with negative exponents: reducing minus three by one gives minus four, not minus two. Down the number line means more negative.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Rewrite every surd and quotient as powers of x first, then drop the exponent forward and reduce the power.

## Subtopic: Tangents to a Curve

The derivative evaluated at a point is the gradient of the tangent there — so tangent questions collapse into a fixed routine. Find the tangent to f of x equals x cubed minus four x squared plus three, at x equals 2. Step one, the point: f of 2 is eight minus sixteen plus three, which is minus 5 — the point is two, minus five. Step two, the gradient function: f prime of x is three x squared minus eight x. Step three, the gradient number: f prime of 2 is twelve minus sixteen — minus 4. Step four, the line: y plus five equals minus four times x minus two, which tidies to y equals minus four x plus three.

Every variation shuffles the same four cards: point, derivative, gradient, line. When the question hands you the gradient instead — find where the tangent to this curve has gradient 3 — set the derivative equal to 3 and solve: three x squared minus eight x equals three gives three x squared minus eight x minus three equals zero, factorising to three x plus one, times x minus three: x is 3 or minus one third. Two places on the curve carry that tangent slope, one on each rising arm.

A tangent parallel to the x axis has gradient zero — hold on to that translation; it is the standard disguise for the turning-point condition arriving in the next section. A tangent parallel to a given line borrows that line's gradient, so read the gradient off the given line before anything else.

Stop for this section's questions now — point, derivative, gradient, line, dealt in whatever order the question chooses.

## Subtopic: The Cubic Graph — Stationary Points and Sketch

The cubic f of x equals a x cubed plus b x squared plus c x plus d is the canvas for the remainder of the term, and its landscape is governed by its derivative. Wherever f prime equals zero the tangent lies flat: a stationary point — a local maximum or a local minimum.

A complete worked sketch: f of x equals x cubed minus three x squared minus nine x plus twenty-seven. Stationary points first. F prime of x is three x squared minus six x minus nine — set to zero and divide by three: x squared minus two x minus three equals zero, factorising to x minus three, times x plus one. Stationary at x equals minus 1 and x equals 3. Heights from the original: f of minus 1 is minus one minus three plus nine plus twenty-seven — 32. F of 3 is twenty-seven minus twenty-seven minus twenty-seven plus twenty-seven — zero. The stationary points are minus one, thirty-two and three, zero.

Which is the peak? Test the sign of f prime on either side, or trust the shape: a positive x cubed coefficient climbs, turns, dips, and climbs again — first stationary point the local maximum, second the local minimum. Now the intercepts. The y intercept: f of zero is 27. The x intercepts: factorise f itself by grouping — x squared times x minus three, minus nine times x minus three, which is x minus three times x squared minus nine, and finally x minus three, squared, times x plus three. Roots at minus three and at three, the second one doubled. A double root means the graph TOUCHES the axis and turns back — and see the agreement: the local minimum three, zero sits exactly on the axis, touching it.

Assemble the sketch: rising from deep lower left, crossing at minus three, up to the summit at minus one, thirty-two, down to touch the axis at three, zero, then rising forever. When a cubic will not factorise by inspection, call on the factor theorem: test small values until one gives zero, then divide that factor out.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — derivative for the turning points, function for the intercepts, and a double root touches rather than crosses.

## Subtopic: Reading f Through f Prime, and Concavity

The derivative does more than locate turning points; it narrates the entire graph. Where f prime of x is positive, f climbs; where negative, f falls. For our cubic, f prime is three times x plus one, times x minus three — positive outside the roots, negative between them. So f increases up to x equals minus 1, decreases between minus 1 and 3, and increases beyond 3. Questions ask this directly: for which values of x is the function decreasing? Answer: minus one strictly less than x strictly less than three — an interval, lifted straight from the derivative's sign.

Differentiate once more for the second derivative, f double prime — the gradient of the gradient. Here: six x minus six, zero at x equals 1. The point one, sixteen is the point of INFLECTION: where the curve trades concavity, from arching over like a dome to opening upward like a bowl. For any cubic with two distinct turning points, the inflection sits exactly halfway between them — minus one and three average to one. The second derivative also classifies stationary points without a sign table: negative at a stationary point means concave down, a local maximum; positive means a local minimum. At x equals minus 1: minus six minus six is negative — maximum, as the shape promised. At x equals 3: eighteen minus six is positive — minimum.

The complete reading list for any cubic: zeros of f give the intercepts; zeros of f prime give the turning points; the sign of f prime gives increase and decrease; the zero of f double prime gives the inflection; and the sign of a, the leading coefficient, sets the direction of the whole journey — positive ends climbing to the right, negative ends falling.

The final questions of this part are with you now — f for position, f prime for direction, f double prime for curvature: three functions, one story.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same derivatives and cubics from a mountain footpath and a wardrobe rule for lazy differentiators — same machinery, same answers, built from pictures.

## Subtopic: The Exponent Falls Forward

First principles was the honest apprenticeship, and it is now complete — you never repeat it for routine work. The power rule is the reward: to differentiate x to any power, the exponent falls forward to become a multiplier, and the power steps down by one. X squared becomes two x. X to the fifth becomes five x to the fourth. X to the twelfth becomes twelve x to the eleventh. In a sum, every term is processed separately, constants in front simply tag along, and a lone number — plus seven, minus thirteen — differentiates to zero, because a constant changes at no rate whatsoever.

The rule's single demand is that everything arrive DRESSED as a power of x. Most of the marks lost in this topic are lost in the changing room, before differentiation even starts. Four root x is four x to the half. One over x to the fourth is x to the minus four. And a fraction like three x to the fourth plus x, over x squared, is not one garment but a sum wearing a coat: split it term by term into three x squared plus one over x, then dress the last piece as x to the minus one. Only now does the rule fire: six x minus x to the minus two.

One habit guards the negative exponents: stepping DOWN from minus three lands on minus four. Down the number line, every time — more negative, never less.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the rule are coming to you right now. Dress everything as powers, let the exponent fall forward, step the power down.

## Subtopic: The Hiking Trail

A cubic graph is a mountain footpath crossing a ridge and a valley, and the derivative is your legs' report — the steepness underfoot at every stride. Walk the cubic x cubed minus three x squared minus nine x plus twenty-seven from the far left: the path climbs, flattens at a lookout, drops into a valley, brushes the valley floor, and climbs out for good. Every landmark on that walk is a derivative fact.

Lookouts and valley floors are where the steepness reads exactly zero — level ground, a flat tangent. Set the derivative to zero: three x squared minus six x minus nine factorises as three times x plus one, times x minus three, so level ground occurs at x equals minus 1 and x equals 3. For altitudes, feed those values into the ORIGINAL function: height 32 at the lookout, height 0 at the valley floor. Lookout before valley — a right-way-up cubic always climbs, dips, climbs, in that order.

Where the path climbs, the derivative is positive; where it drops, negative. Asked where the function is decreasing, answer with the stretch between lookout and valley floor: x strictly between minus 1 and 3. And partway down the descent comes a spot every walker recognises — where the path stops rolling over the crest and starts curving into the valley's bowl. That is the inflection point, here at x equals 1, always midway between a cubic's two level spots. It is also the steepest stride of the entire descent.

Your questions for this part are up now. Level ground means derivative zero, altitude comes from the original function, and the path's rhythm — climb, drop, climb — is written in the derivative's sign.

## Subtopic: Laying a Ruler on the Curve

A tangent is a ruler pressed against the curve at a single point, matching the path's direction exactly there. Every tangent question needs the same two facts — where the ruler touches, and what slope it holds — and the derivative dispenses slopes on demand.

The routine, on f of x equals x cubed minus four x squared plus three at x equals 2. Touch point: feed 2 into the original function — height minus five, so the ruler rests at two, minus five. Slope: feed 2 into the derivative three x squared minus eight x — minus four. Line: take y equals m x plus c with m equal to minus four, push the touch point through it, and the tangent comes out as y equals minus four x plus three. Two feeds, one line, always — the only discipline is choosing the correct machine for each feed: the FUNCTION dispenses heights, the DERIVATIVE dispenses slopes, and swapping them is the classic wrong turn of the chapter.

Questions also run backwards: where on the curve does a ruler hold a slope of 3? Set the derivative equal to 3 and solve the quadratic — x equals 3 or minus one third — two spots on the path, one on each climbing stretch, equally steep. And a ruler lying dead level, parallel to the x axis? Slope zero: the level-ground condition again, which reveals that tangent questions and turning-point questions are one question wearing two jackets.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: function for the touch point, derivative for the slope, and translate every parallel clue into a statement about gradient.
