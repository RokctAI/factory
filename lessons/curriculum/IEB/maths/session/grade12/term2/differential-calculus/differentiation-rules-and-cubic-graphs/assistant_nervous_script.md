Hey, it's Mandy — those cubic sketch questions can look like a marathon: differentiate, factorise, turning points, intercepts, all stacked into one long block. Here is the steadying truth: it is the same five steps in the same order, every single time, and the rule that drives the whole thing is one short sentence.

The sentence: exponent falls forward, power steps down. X to the fifth becomes five x to the fourth. Before the rule can fire, everything must be dressed as a power of x — roots become fraction exponents, one over x cubed becomes x to the minus three, and a quotient splits term by term. Only two snakes hide in this grass: the falling exponent carries its minus sign with it, and stepping down from minus three lands on minus four.

The five sketch steps: differentiate; set the derivative to zero and solve for the turning x values; feed those x values into the ORIGINAL function for their heights; factorise the original for intercepts, calling the factor theorem when it resists; then draw, letting the leading coefficient set the direction — positive climbs away to the right. See a double root? The graph touches the axis and turns there, and that touch point will match one of your turning points. When those two facts agree, the whole page has certified itself.

Before every substitution, whisper the sorting question: height or slope? Heights come from f. Slopes come from f prime. That whisper alone blocks the most common error in the chapter.

Tangent questions are just two feeds — the function for the touch point, the derivative for the slope — then one straight line through both facts.
