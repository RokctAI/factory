# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the gateway to the biggest single source of marks in senior mathematics: trigonometry. Two exam-style tasks carry the whole hour. First, without a calculator, evaluate sine forty-five degrees times cosine forty-five degrees, plus tangent sixty degrees times tangent thirty degrees. Second, find the angle x in a right-angled triangle whose hypotenuse is twenty-five and whose side opposite x is seven. The three ratios, the special angles, and the inverse move — the complete starter kit.

## Subtopic: The Three Ratios and SOH CAH TOA

Take any right-angled triangle and choose one of its acute angles — call it theta. The three sides now take names relative to theta. The hypotenuse: the longest side, always facing the right angle — it never changes. The opposite: the side facing theta across the triangle. The adjacent: the arm of theta that is not the hypotenuse.

Now the definitions at the heart of all trigonometry. Sine of theta equals opposite over hypotenuse. Cosine of theta equals adjacent over hypotenuse. Tangent of theta equals opposite over adjacent. The memory aid the whole world shares: SOH CAH TOA.

Why do these three fractions deserve permanent names? Here is the deep fact: every right-angled triangle containing the same angle theta produces IDENTICAL values for these ratios, regardless of the triangle's size. Scale every side by ten and the ratio seven over twenty-five becomes seventy over two hundred and fifty — the same number. Similar triangles guarantee it. So sine of thirty degrees is a constant of the universe, not a fact about one drawing — which is why a calculator can store it, and why surveyors and engineers can compute unreachable distances from a single measured angle.

One discipline before anything else: opposite and adjacent belong to the ANGLE, not to the triangle. Shift your attention to the other acute angle and the two labels trade places. Label AFTER choosing your angle, every time — premature labelling is the single largest source of trig errors in this grade.

This section's questions are with you now: the three definitions stated exactly, the hypotenuse identified independently of any angle, and the reason a ratio belongs to an angle rather than to one particular triangle.

## Subtopic: The Special Angles — Values Without a Calculator

The words "without a calculator" mean the values at thirty, forty-five and sixty degrees must be available in your head. Do not memorise them blindly — build them from two triangles you can redraw in ten seconds whenever memory fails.

Triangle one: an equilateral triangle with sides of two — every angle sixty degrees. Drop a perpendicular from the apex: it halves the base into one and one, and splits the apex angle into thirty and thirty. Pythagoras on either half: height squared equals four minus one — three — so the height is root three. Read the sixty-degree corner at the base: opposite root three, adjacent one, hypotenuse two. Sine sixty: root three over two. Cosine sixty: one half. Tangent sixty: root three. Now read from the thirty-degree corner at the top, where the roles swap: sine thirty is one half, cosine thirty is root three over two, tangent thirty is one over root three.

Triangle two: a right-angled isosceles triangle with legs of one — both acute angles forty-five degrees. Pythagoras gives the hypotenuse: root two. So sine forty-five and cosine forty-five are both one over root two, and tangent forty-five is one over one: exactly one.

Now the evaluation, term by term. Sine forty-five: one over root two. Cosine forty-five: one over root two. Their product: one over two — root two times root two is exactly two, no decimals involved. Tangent sixty: root three. Tangent thirty: one over root three. Their product: root three over root three — exactly one.

The expression: one half plus one, which is three halves.

Two professional habits on display there. Surds multiplied exactly — root two times root two IS two, root three over root three IS one; no decimal ever appeared. And the final answer stands as an exact fraction: three halves. A no-calculator question is an exactness question; decimals are surrender.

Work this section's questions before continuing — the two triangles rebuilt from scratch, the six values read off them, and the products carried through in exact surd form.

## Subtopic: Finding the Angle — the Inverse Move

Now the triangle problem: hypotenuse twenty-five, side opposite the unknown angle x equal to seven. Find x.

Step one: choose the ratio. Interrogate the givens relative to x. The side facing x: opposite, seven. The longest side: hypotenuse, twenty-five. Opposite and hypotenuse — SOH — sine. So sine of x equals seven over twenty-five.

Step two: understand what that sentence delivers. It gives the sine OF the angle — but the question wants the angle itself. Sine must be run backwards: from a known ratio, recover the angle. The reverse gear is inverse sine — written sine to the minus one, also called arcsine — reached on the calculator with shift, then sine.

Step three: x equals inverse sine of seven over twenty-five. In DEGREE mode — check the D on the display — the calculator returns sixteen comma three degrees, to one decimal place.

Step four: defend the answer with two sense checks. First: seven over twenty-five is zero comma two eight, well under a half — and sine thirty is exactly a half — so the angle must sit below thirty degrees. Sixteen comma three does. Second: this is the seven, twenty-four, twenty-five triangle — twenty-four squared plus seven squared is five hundred and seventy-six plus forty-nine, six hundred and twenty-five, which is twenty-five squared. The other acute angle is inverse sine of twenty-four over twenty-five: seventy-three comma seven degrees. And sixteen comma three plus seventy-three comma seven is exactly ninety. The triangle closes.

Hold the inverse as a question-flip. Sine answers: given the angle, what is the ratio? Inverse sine answers: given the ratio, what was the angle? One relationship, two directions — the same machine driven in reverse.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Right Ratio — and the Complete Method

The mark-deciding skill is not calculation — it is CHOICE. Every right-triangle question hands you two named sides, or one side and a mission. The routine: one, mark the angle you care about. Two, label the sides relative to it — hypotenuse first, since it never moves; then opposite, facing the angle; then adjacent, the remaining arm. Three, circle the two sides that are known or wanted. Four, take the ratio whose letters match the circle: opposite-hypotenuse, sine; adjacent-hypotenuse, cosine; opposite-adjacent, tangent.

Run the routine across variations of our triangle. Given the angle sixteen comma three degrees and hypotenuse twenty-five, find the opposite side: SOH forwards — opposite equals twenty-five times sine of the angle, returning seven. Given opposite seven and adjacent twenty-four, find the angle: TOA — tangent of x equals seven over twenty-four, and inverse tangent returns the same sixteen comma three degrees. Different givens, same triangle, same routine every time.

The error museum for trigonometry, three exhibits. Exhibit one: labels attached to the wrong angle — one angle's opposite is the other angle's adjacent; always label AFTER choosing. Exhibit two: radian mode — a calculator claiming sine thirty is something other than zero comma five is not broken, it is mis-set; find the D before trusting a digit. Exhibit three: the inverse applied in the wrong direction — ratio wanted, plain function; angle wanted, inverse function. The direction of the question decides.

The complete method: label relative to your angle; choose through SOH CAH TOA; write the ratio equation; solve — forwards for a side, backwards through the inverse for an angle; and verify with a size check, plus Pythagoras whenever three sides are in play.

Where this gateway leads: Grade 11 extends these ratios past ninety degrees onto the Cartesian plane, through reduction formulae into general solutions; the final year adds the sine, cosine and area rules and three-dimensional problems. Every one of those questions opens with what you built today: three ratios, two triangles, one routine. Drill it until SOH CAH TOA stops being a phrase and becomes a reflex.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the routine run from step one, the museum errors recognised on sight, and the choice between a ratio and its inverse justified out loud.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three ratios from a playground slide — one triangle, and names that change depending on where you sit.

## Subtopic: Where You Stand Decides the Names

Picture the big slide at the park. The ladder up, the slide down, the ground between them: a triangle, with the square corner where the ladder meets the ground. Three sides, three jobs.

The slide itself is the long slanted side, lying straight across from the square corner. That is the hypotenuse — the easy one, because its name never changes. It belongs to the square corner, not to you.

The other two names depend on where you sit. Sit at the bottom of the slide, at the angle between slide and ground. The ladder stands across the triangle, facing you: that is the opposite. The ground runs from you to the square corner: that is the adjacent. Now climb to the top and use the angle between slide and ladder instead. Suddenly the GROUND faces you — the ground becomes the opposite, and the ladder becomes the adjacent. The triangle never moved; your seat did. Hence the golden habit: choose your angle first, name the sides second.

With the names set, the three ratios are only pairings. Opposite over hypotenuse: sine. Adjacent over hypotenuse: cosine. Opposite over adjacent: tangent. First letters: SOH CAH TOA.

And here is what makes the whole subject possible. A small slide and a giant slide, built at exactly the same angle, feel identical to ride — because the ratio of height to length is the same number for both. It works like airtime per rand: a R10 voucher and a R100 voucher load different amounts, but the rate per rand is one fixed number. Scale the seven-twenty-five triangle by ten and seventy over two hundred and fifty is still the same value. That is why sine of thirty degrees is one fixed number in the universe, not a fact about somebody's sketch.

## Subtopic: Two Triangles You Can Rebuild Anywhere

The exam phrase "without a calculator" for thirty, forty-five and sixty degrees sounds like a memory test. It is not — it is two shapes you can rebuild on scrap paper in ten seconds.

Shape one: an equilateral triangle — picture a triangular road sign — with all three sides two units and all three corners sixty degrees. Cut it straight down the middle from the top. The base halves into one and one; the top corner halves into thirty and thirty; the cut itself is the height. Pythagoras: two squared minus one squared is three, so the height is root three.

Sit at the sixty-degree corner of one half. Facing you: the cut, root three. Beside you: the half-base, one. The slant: two. So sine sixty is root three over two, cosine sixty is one half, tangent sixty is root three. Move up to the thirty-degree corner and the short sides swap jobs: sine thirty is one half, cosine thirty is root three over two, tangent thirty is one over root three.

Shape two: a square serviette of side one, folded corner to corner. That fold makes a square corner and two forty-five-degree corners, with short sides one and one and a diagonal of root two. So sine forty-five and cosine forty-five are both one over root two, and tangent forty-five is one over one: exactly one.

Two shapes, six values, zero memorising. Now the exam expression: sine forty-five times cosine forty-five, plus tangent sixty times tangent thirty. First product: one over root two, times one over root two — root two times root two is two — so one half. Second product: root three times one over root three — exactly one. One half plus one: three halves.

Leave it exactly there. A no-calculator question wants an exact answer, and three halves IS the finished product — pushing it into decimals throws away the very mark the question was testing.

## Subtopic: Forward Gear, Reverse Gear

One last idea, and it unlocks half of all trig questions.

Think of sine as a taxi in forward gear: feed in an angle, and a ratio comes out. Inverse sine is the same taxi in reverse: feed in a ratio, and the angle comes out. Same vehicle, opposite direction — and on the calculator, reverse gear is shift, then sine.

Try it on the slide. The slide is twenty-five metres of slanted steel, and its top sits seven metres above the ground. What angle does it make with the ground? Sit at the bottom: the height faces you — opposite, seven; the slide is the slant — hypotenuse, twenty-five. Opposite and hypotenuse: sine. So sine of the angle is seven over twenty-five. That sentence hands you the ratio and hides the angle — so reverse gear: the angle is inverse sine of seven over twenty-five, which is sixteen comma three degrees.

Check it twice, because a checked answer is a defended answer. First: seven over twenty-five is about zero comma two eight, clearly under a half — and sine thirty is exactly a half — so the angle must be under thirty degrees. It is. Second: this is the famous seven, twenty-four, twenty-five triangle, so the slide's foot stands twenty-four metres from the base of the ladder, and the top angle must be inverse sine of twenty-four over twenty-five: seventy-three comma seven degrees. Sixteen comma three plus seventy-three comma seven: exactly ninety. The triangle closes, and the answer stands.

The routine in working order: mark your angle; name the three sides from there; pick out the two sides the question actually mentions; let their letters choose the ratio. Then ask what is being hunted — a side, with the angle known, is forward gear; an angle, with two sides known, is reverse gear.

Two warnings to keep. The names swap the moment you change angles, so never carry old labels into a new part of a question. And if the calculator ever claims sine thirty is anything but zero comma five, the machine is in the wrong mode — find the D on the screen and reset it. You know the true value; make the calculator prove itself before trusting it with your marks.
