# Part 1 — Expert

The three ratios now leave the definitions page and go to work. Solving a right-angled triangle means finding every unknown side and angle in it — and then aiming that skill at the two-dimensional problems examiners actually set: towers, ladders, ramps and lighthouses, reached through angles of elevation and depression. Four worked problems carry this session, and every one of them turns on the same two decisions: which ratio, and where the unknown sits in the fraction.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Ratio, and Finding a Side on Top

Every solution starts with labelling, and labelling starts with choosing an angle. Fix the acute angle you are working from. The hypotenuse is the side facing the right angle — it never moves, whatever you choose. The opposite side faces your chosen angle across the triangle. The adjacent side is the remaining arm of your angle. Switch to the other acute angle and opposite and adjacent trade places; the hypotenuse stays put.

Then the choosing routine, four steps. One: mark your angle. Two: label the three sides relative to it. Three: circle the two sides that matter — the one you know and the one you want; everything else is noise. Four: pick the ratio whose letters match the circle. Opposite with hypotenuse: sine. Adjacent with hypotenuse: cosine. Opposite with adjacent: tangent.

Problem one. A ladder fifteen metres long leans against a warehouse wall in Durban, making an angle of fifty degrees with the ground. How far up the wall does it reach?

Label from the fifty-degree angle at the foot. The ladder is the hypotenuse, fifteen. The wall height faces the angle — opposite. Opposite and hypotenuse: sine. So sine of fifty degrees equals the height over fifteen.

The unknown sits on top of the fraction, so free it by multiplying: height equals fifteen times sine fifty degrees. Calculator in DEGREE mode: sine fifty is zero comma seven six six zero. Multiply: eleven comma four nine metres, to two decimal places.

Sense-check before moving on — always. At forty-five degrees, a fifteen-metre ladder reaches about ten comma six metres; fifty degrees is a steeper lean, so the answer must land a little higher. Eleven comma four nine does. And notice the structural guarantee: sine of any acute angle is less than one, so the opposite side is always shorter than the hypotenuse. Any answer longer than the ladder itself is impossible and means the unknown was misplaced.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Label from your chosen angle first, then let the two circled sides name the ratio for you.

## Subtopic: When the Unknown Sits Underneath

The second structural case separates careful workers from careless ones: the unknown in the denominator.

Problem two. A guy wire runs from a ground anchor to a point on a radio tower, making an angle of fifty-five degrees with the ground. The anchor sits twenty metres from the tower's base. How long is the wire?

Label from the fifty-five-degree angle. The twenty metres along the ground lies beside the angle — adjacent. The wire is the hypotenuse, unknown. Adjacent with hypotenuse: cosine. So cosine of fifty-five degrees equals twenty over the wire length.

The unknown is underneath, and exactly one move is correct: multiply both sides by the unknown, then divide by the cosine. Wire length equals twenty divided by cosine fifty-five degrees. Cosine fifty-five is zero comma five seven three six. Twenty divided by that: thirty-four comma eight seven metres.

The error this layout invites is multiplying instead of dividing — twenty times cosine fifty-five, which gives eleven comma four seven. The sense check catches it instantly: the hypotenuse is the longest side of any right-angled triangle, so a wire shorter than the twenty metres it must beat is impossible. Say the rule until it is reflex: unknown on top, multiply; unknown underneath, divide the known side by the ratio.

Stop for this section's questions now — decide where the unknown sits BEFORE touching the calculator, and test every hypotenuse against the sides it must exceed.

## Subtopic: Finding Angles, and Solving the Whole Triangle

Finding a side runs a ratio forwards. Finding an angle runs it backwards, through the inverse functions — sine to the minus one, cosine to the minus one, tangent to the minus one — reached with the shift key.

Problem three. A skateboard ramp in East London rises one comma two metres over a horizontal run of four metres. Find the angle of the ramp.

Check the units first — both already in metres, good. Relative to the ramp's angle at the bottom, the rise faces it — opposite — and the run lies beside it — adjacent. Opposite with adjacent: tangent. Tangent of theta equals one comma two over four, which is zero comma three. So theta equals inverse tangent of zero comma three: sixteen comma seven zero degrees.

Now solve the WHOLE triangle — every side, every angle. The third angle: a triangle's angles sum to one hundred and eighty, and one is ninety, so the two acute angles sum to ninety. Ninety minus sixteen comma seven zero: seventy-three comma three zero degrees. The ramp surface itself comes from Pythagoras, not from a rounded trig answer: one comma two squared is one comma four four; four squared is sixteen; the sum is seventeen comma four four; the square root is four comma one eight metres.

Two disciplines live in that paragraph. Prefer Pythagoras when three sides are in play — it is exact and inherits no rounding error. And keep full calculator accuracy through the working, rounding only at the end: round the angle to seventeen degrees mid-stream and the third side visibly drifts.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — inverses when an angle is wanted, the ninety-degree complement for the third angle, and Pythagoras for the third side.

## Subtopic: Angles of Elevation and Depression

Both angles are measured from the HORIZONTAL — never from the vertical — and that definition alone is worth marks.

The angle of elevation sits between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look UP. The angle of depression sits between the horizontal and your line of sight when you look DOWN. And because the horizontal at your eye and the horizontal at the target are parallel, the angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom — alternate angles between parallel lines. That equality is the most useful sentence in the whole application.

Problem four, elevation. A learner stands thirty metres from the base of a water tower in Polokwane. Her eyes are one comma five metres above the ground, and the angle of elevation from her eyes to the top of the tower is fifty-eight degrees. How tall is the tower?

The triangle sits on her eye line: a horizontal side of thirty metres, and a vertical side climbing from eye level to the top. The vertical part faces the fifty-eight degrees — opposite; the thirty metres lies beside it — adjacent. Tangent. Vertical part equals thirty times tangent fifty-eight degrees. Tangent fifty-eight is one comma six zero zero three; the vertical part is forty-eight comma zero one metres.

But that is not the tower. The triangle began at her eyes, so the one comma five metres below it still counts: forty-eight comma zero one plus one comma five is forty-nine comma five one metres. Omitting the observer's height is the classic elevation error, and it costs a mark every single time.

Problem four, depression. From the top of a sixty-metre lighthouse at Port Shepstone, the angle of depression to a ship is twenty degrees. How far is the ship from the base of the lighthouse?

Draw the horizontal at the top; the twenty degrees hangs between it and the line of sight down to the ship. By alternate angles, the angle at the ship — between the sea and the line of sight — is also twenty degrees. Work from the ship: the lighthouse, sixty metres, faces the angle — opposite; the sea distance lies beside it — adjacent. Tangent. Tangent of twenty degrees equals sixty over the distance — unknown underneath, so divide: distance equals sixty over tangent twenty degrees. Tangent twenty is zero comma three six four zero. The ship is one hundred and sixty-four comma eight five metres out.

The trap museum, four exhibits. One: the calculator in radian mode — find the D on the display before trusting anything. Two: planting the depression angle inside the triangle at the top vertex, where the interior angle is actually its complement, seventy degrees. Three: the forgotten eye height. Four: rounding mid-calculation. The full method: sketch, label from your angle, choose the ratio, note whether the unknown is on top or underneath, solve, then add back whatever the triangle left out.

The final questions of this part are with you now — elevation and depression measured from the horizontal, the alternate-angle equality put to work, and every observer height accounted for.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four problems from a street corner and a leaning ladder — same answers, but the ratio will choose itself from now on.

## Subtopic: Naming the Sides from Where You Stand

Stand in one corner of a rectangular yard and look at the far corner along the diagonal. Three lines concern you: the fence straight ahead, the fence running along beside you, and the diagonal you are looking down. Three sides — and their names depend entirely on where you stand.

A right-angled triangle is exactly that. First choose your angle — that is where you stand. The longest side, facing the square corner, is the hypotenuse, and it is the loyal one: it never changes name, because it belongs to the right angle, not to you. The side directly across from you is the opposite. The side running from your feet to the square corner is the adjacent.

Walk to the other corner and the two swap: what faced you now runs beside you. That is why the golden habit is angle first, names second. Nearly every wrong trig answer in existence started with labels written before an angle was chosen.

Choosing the ratio is then almost embarrassingly simple. Look at only two sides: the one you know and the one you want. The rest is noise. Opposite and hypotenuse together: sine. Adjacent and hypotenuse: cosine. Opposite and adjacent: tangent. That is the entire decision.

Run it on the Durban ladder. Fifteen metres long, leaning at fifty degrees, and you want the height it reaches. Stand at the bottom angle. The ladder is the diagonal — hypotenuse, fifteen. The wall faces you — opposite. Opposite and hypotenuse: sine. Height equals fifteen times sine fifty: eleven comma four nine metres. And it feels right: at forty-five degrees the ladder would reach about ten and a half metres, and fifty degrees stands it a little straighter, so a little higher.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions are coming to you right now. For each one, stand at the angle first, name only the two sides that matter, and let those two names pick the ratio.

## Subtopic: Upstairs You Multiply, Downstairs You Divide

Here is the step that trips people who understand everything else: WHERE the unknown sits.

Think about airtime. If one voucher costs R30, twelve vouchers cost twelve times thirty — you multiply. But if twelve vouchers cost R360 and you want the price of one, you divide. Same relationship, opposite moves — and the missing number decides which.

Trig fractions behave identically. Write sine fifty equals height over fifteen and the unknown is upstairs, on top. To free it: multiply. Height equals fifteen times sine fifty.

Now the tower wire. Anchored twenty metres from the base, rising at fifty-five degrees. The twenty is beside the angle — adjacent. The wire is the diagonal — hypotenuse. Adjacent and hypotenuse: cosine. Cosine fifty-five equals twenty over the wire. This time the unknown is downstairs, underneath. To free it: divide the known side by the ratio. Wire equals twenty divided by cosine fifty-five: thirty-four comma eight seven metres.

And your free safety net: the hypotenuse is always the longest side in the triangle. A wire that must beat twenty metres has to come out bigger than twenty. Multiply by mistake and you get eleven comma four seven — shorter than a side it must exceed — and you know instantly, without any memo, that the fraction was flipped.

Unknown upstairs: multiply. Unknown downstairs: divide. Then confirm the hypotenuse is the biggest number on the page. Three seconds, and that entire family of errors is extinct.

Your questions for this part are up now. Before pressing any button, say out loud where the unknown sits — upstairs or downstairs. That one sentence decides the move.

## Subtopic: Looking Up and Looking Down

Last piece — and it is the reason trigonometry exists: measuring what you cannot touch.

Stand still and look straight ahead: that level line from your eyes is the horizontal, and everything is measured from it. Tilt your chin up to the top of a water tower: the angle your gaze swings through is the angle of elevation. Stand at the top of a lighthouse and tilt down to a ship: the angle your gaze drops through is the angle of depression. Both measured from the level line — never from a wall, never from a pole.

And one beautiful fact rescues every confusing depression question: the angle you look DOWN at the ship equals the angle the sailor looks UP at you. Your level line and the sea are parallel, so the two angles must match — alternate angles. Whenever a depression angle tangles you, walk yourself to the bottom of the picture and use the same number as an elevation.

Now the Polokwane water tower. The learner stands thirty metres away, eyes one comma five metres up, gazing at fifty-eight degrees to the top. Her triangle starts at her EYES, not her feet: thirty metres flat, the unknown climbing. Beside the angle: thirty. Facing it: the rise. That pairing is tangent. Rise equals thirty times tangent fifty-eight: forty-eight comma zero one metres.

Nearly finished — but her eyes are not on the ground. Add the one comma five metres of learner standing under the triangle: the tower is forty-nine comma five one metres. That little addition is the mark nearly everyone drops. Ask it every time: did my triangle start at the ground, or above it?

And the Port Shepstone lighthouse: sixty metres up, looking down twenty degrees at a ship. Move to the ship and look up twenty degrees instead. The lighthouse faces you — opposite, sixty. The sea runs beside you — adjacent, unknown. Tangent again — but the unknown is downstairs, so divide: sixty over tangent twenty is one hundred and sixty-four comma eight five metres. A distance across open water, measured by someone who never left the tower — that is the entire point of this subject.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: find the level line first, walk to the bottom of the picture when a depression angle confuses you, and add back the height you stand on. This skill is yours now.
