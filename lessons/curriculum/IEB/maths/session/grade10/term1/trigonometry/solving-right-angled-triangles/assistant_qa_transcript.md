**Thandi:** Class questions! First up: sketching. A learner says the word problems fall apart for them before the maths even starts — they cannot turn the story into a picture. Any structure for that?

**Tutor:** A strict drawing recipe, four moves, same order every time. Move one: draw the VERTICAL thing first — the tower, the wall, the lighthouse — as a straight up-and-down line, because almost every problem at this level has one. Move two: draw the HORIZONTAL — the ground, the sea, the eye-level line — meeting it at a square corner, and mark that right angle immediately; it anchors the whole triangle. Move three: connect the slanted line — the ladder, the wire, the line of sight — and suddenly the triangle exists. Move four: write every number from the story onto its side or angle, and put a letter on the thing being asked. Only now do you label opposite and adjacent, standing at the marked angle. The discipline that makes this work is refusing to touch the calculator until the sketch is complete — thirty seconds of drawing converts a paragraph of words into a triangle you have solved a dozen times. And if the story mentions eye height, draw the observer as a stick figure with the triangle starting at the head, so the extra bit below is visible from the start and cannot be forgotten at the end.

**Thandi:** Next: the radian question. Someone's calculator gave a bizarre answer for sine fifty, and they lost ten minutes hunting an algebra error that never existed. How do they protect themselves?

**Tutor:** With a two-second ritual at the start of every trigonometry session: look for the D on the display, and if anything else is showing — R for radians, G for gradians — fix the mode before writing a single line. Radian mode is the classic silent saboteur, because the calculator does not error; it answers a different question fluently. Sine of fifty DEGREES is about zero comma seven seven, but sine of fifty RADIANS is about minus zero comma two six — a negative number for an acute angle, which is your loudest alarm bell. So carry a known value as a test key: you know sine thirty is exactly zero comma five, so type sine thirty whenever you sit down or borrow a calculator. Correct answer, proceed; anything else, fix the mode. The deeper habit is owning a handful of anchor values — sine thirty, cosine sixty, tangent forty-five equals one — precisely so the machine can be audited by you, rather than trusted blindly. A calculator is a brilliant employee and a terrible manager: you set the mode, you check its first answer, then it works for you.

**Thandi:** Third one: the eye-height business. A learner wants to know why the answer needs one comma five metres added — surely the triangle already measured the tower?

**Tutor:** The triangle measured exactly what it was built on, and it was built on the learner's eye line, not on the ground. Walk the geometry. Her line of sight starts at her eyes, one comma five metres up. The horizontal side of the triangle runs from her eyes, level, to the tower — thirty metres. The vertical side of the triangle runs from THAT level line up to the top: thirty times tangent fifty-eight, forty-eight comma zero one metres. So the triangle has delivered, faithfully, the distance from eye level to the summit. But the tower does not start at eye level; it starts at the ground, and between the ground and her eye line stands one comma five metres of tower that the triangle never saw. Total: forty-nine comma five one. The reason this trap works so well is that the triangle FEELS like the whole problem, and the answer it gives looks respectable on its own. So install the closing question as a ritual: did my triangle start at ground level, or above it? If above, the gap gets added back. And notice the exam's mirror version: sometimes the height of the INSTRUMENT is given and the question asks for eye-level height — then you subtract. Read where the triangle sits; the sketch with the stick figure makes it impossible to miss.

**Thandi:** Last question: depression angles. Half the class put the twenty degrees inside the triangle at the top of the lighthouse and got the wrong answer. What actually goes wrong there?

**Tutor:** They planted the angle in the wrong corner of the picture, and the geometry quietly punished them. The angle of depression lives between the HORIZONTAL at the top and the line of sight going down. But the triangle's interior angle at the top vertex is between the VERTICAL lighthouse wall and the line of sight — and that is the complement: ninety minus twenty, seventy degrees. Use twenty in that corner and every subsequent number is wrong. Two clean escapes exist. The careful route: mark the horizontal at the top explicitly, place twenty degrees below it, and recognise the triangle's top angle as seventy — workable, but it invites the very confusion we are trying to kill. The elegant route: leave the top of the lighthouse entirely. The horizontal up there and the sea down below are parallel lines, and the line of sight cuts both — so the angle of depression at the top equals the angle of ELEVATION at the ship, alternate angles, exactly twenty degrees. Stand at the ship, where the geometry is friendly: the lighthouse faces you, sixty metres, opposite; the sea distance runs beside you, adjacent; tangent of twenty equals sixty over the distance, unknown downstairs, divide — one hundred and sixty-four comma eight five metres. One sentence to keep: when depression confuses, move to the bottom of the picture and look up.
