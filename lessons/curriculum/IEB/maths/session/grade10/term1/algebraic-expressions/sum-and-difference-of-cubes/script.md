# Part 1 — Expert

One identity carries this whole session: the sum and difference of two cubes. On first meeting it looks like the hardest factorisation in Grade 10; by the end of the hour it will be the most mechanical. Our anchor task is to factorise fully x cubed plus eight — two quiet terms hiding a technique that examiners return to year after year, because learners who own it collect the marks in seconds and learners who do not cannot even begin.

## Subtopic: Recognising a Cube When You See One

No cubes question ever introduces itself. The expression x cubed plus eight simply sits on the page; the recognition has to come from you.

A perfect cube is anything that can be written as some quantity raised to the power three. Start with the numbers, because there are only six you will ever need at this level: one is one cubed; eight is two cubed; twenty-seven is three cubed; sixty-four is four cubed; one hundred and twenty-five is five cubed; two hundred and sixteen is six cubed. Six numbers. Drill them until each one announces its own cube root the instant you read it.

Letters can be cubes too, and so can whole products. X cubed is the obvious case. Y to the nine is a cube as well, because it is y cubed, taken three times — the block is y cubed. Twenty-seven a cubed b cubed is a cube, because it is three a b, all cubed. Sixty-four p to the six is four p squared, cubed. The universal test never changes: can this be written as something to the power three? If yes, write down the something — that something is a building block of the answer.

Apply the test to the anchor. X cubed is x, to the power three. Eight is two, to the power three. So x cubed plus eight has the shape a cubed plus b cubed, with a equal to x and b equal to two. Naming a and b in writing, before anything else, is the professional opening move — every later line of the solution is read straight off those two names.

Why demand a brand-new identity here at all? Audit the tools you already carry. Common factor: x cubed and eight share only one. Difference of squares: the sign between the terms is a plus, and neither term is a square in any case. Trinomial method: there are two terms, not three. Every existing tool fails — and that triple failure is itself the signal. Two terms, both perfect cubes, nothing else working: the cubes identity is the only key that fits.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six perfect cubes named on sight, a and b identified for given expressions, and the reasons the older methods cannot touch this one.

## Subtopic: The Identity and the SOAP Mnemonic

The sum of cubes: a cubed plus b cubed equals the quantity a plus b, times the quantity a squared minus a b plus b squared.

The difference of cubes: a cubed minus b cubed equals the quantity a minus b, times the quantity a squared plus a b plus b squared.

Study the architecture before memorising a syllable. The first bracket is short: the two cube roots with a sign between them. The second bracket is long: three terms manufactured from a and b in a fixed order — a squared, then a times b, then b squared. Say the order as three words: square the first, multiply the pair, square the last. Notice that the terms are the same in both identities. Only the signs differ between the sum version and the difference version.

And one mnemonic governs all the signs: SOAP — Same, Opposite, Always Positive. The short bracket takes the SAME sign as the question. The long bracket's middle term takes the OPPOSITE sign. The long bracket's final term is ALWAYS POSITIVE.

Test it against both identities. Sum: a cubed PLUS b cubed. Same gives a plus b; opposite gives minus a b; always positive gives plus b squared. That is the identity, letter for letter. Difference: a cubed MINUS b cubed. Same gives a minus b; opposite gives plus a b; always positive gives plus b squared again. One mnemonic, both identities, no guessing left anywhere.

Never accept an identity on faith — earn it once by expansion. Multiply a plus b into a squared minus a b plus b squared. The a distributes to give a cubed, minus a squared b, plus a b squared. The b distributes to give plus a squared b, minus a b squared, plus b cubed. Now watch the interior: minus a squared b meets plus a squared b and vanishes; plus a b squared meets minus a b squared and vanishes. Only a cubed plus b cubed survives. The identity works because it was built for the middle to self-destruct — nothing about it is magic.

Pause for this section's questions: both identities written in full, SOAP run sign by sign across each, and the expansion in which the middle terms wipe each other out.

## Subtopic: Factorising x Cubed Plus Eight

Now execute the anchor problem like a checklist, step by numbered step.

Step one: recognise and name. X cubed plus eight is a cubed plus b cubed, with a equal to x and b equal to two. Write that line down — on many marking memos it carries its own mark.

Step two: the short bracket, by SOAP. The question's sign is plus, so Same gives x plus two.

Step three: the long bracket — terms by the fixed order, signs by SOAP. Square the first: x squared. Multiply the pair, with the opposite sign: minus two x. Square the last, always positive: plus four, because two squared is four.

Assemble: x cubed plus eight equals the quantity x plus two, times the quantity x squared minus two x plus four.

Step four: verify by expanding, because the collapse we proved in general must now happen in the specific. X into the long bracket: x cubed, minus two x squared, plus four x. Two into the long bracket: plus two x squared, minus four x, plus eight. Collect: the two x squared terms cancel; the four x terms cancel; the survivors are x cubed plus eight. The original returns exactly, so the factorisation is proven, not hoped for.

Step five — the step where marks quietly disappear: ask whether the long bracket factorises further. Learners see x squared minus two x plus four, recognise a trinomial, and go hunting for two numbers with product four and sum minus two. Run the hunt honestly: minus one and minus four sum to minus five; minus two and minus two sum to minus four. Nothing works — and nothing ever will, because the quadratic factor produced by a cubes identity never factorises at this level. Fully factorised means stopping in the right place, and the right place is x plus two, times x squared minus two x plus four.

One recurring wreck deserves its own warning. The middle term of the long bracket is a times b — here two x — not b squared. Learners who write minus four x have squared the two instead of multiplying it by x. Recite the order once more: square, MULTIPLY, square. The middle position always holds a product.

This section's questions are with you now — the assembled factorisation, the middle term defended as a product, and the decision to leave the quadratic bracket standing.

## Subtopic: The Difference Twin and the Full Method

The difference version runs on identical rails. Factorise x cubed minus sixty-four. Recognise: sixty-four is four cubed, so a is x and b is four. SOAP: Same gives x minus four; Opposite gives plus four x; Always Positive gives plus sixteen. Answer: the quantity x minus four, times the quantity x squared plus four x plus sixteen. And the long bracket stays — product sixteen with sum four has no pair, and never does.

Now the combination examiners love most: factorise fully five x cubed minus forty. Neither five nor forty is a cube, and panic starts — until the standing discipline speaks: common factor FIRST. Both terms share five: five, times the quantity x cubed minus eight. And the bracket is a difference of cubes, with a equal to x and b equal to two: five, times x minus two, times x squared plus two x plus four. Two techniques in sequence — common factor, then cubes — and the word fully is honoured. A cube hiding behind a common factor is the exam's favourite disguise; strip the factor and the cube steps forward.

Keep the squares twin separate, because confusing squares with cubes is expensive. X squared minus sixteen: SQUARES — two brackets, x minus four times x plus four, both linear, finished. X cubed minus sixty-four: CUBES — one linear bracket and one quadratic bracket, and the quadratic remains. Squares split symmetrically; cubes split short-and-long. Read the exponent before choosing: squared summons squares, cubed summons cubes.

The complete method in six moves. One: common factor first. Two: confirm both terms are cubes and name a and b in writing. Three: short bracket — cube roots, Same sign. Four: long bracket — square, multiply, square, with Opposite then Always Positive. Five: verify by expansion and watch the middles cancel. Six: declare the quadratic factor final.

And the identity travels with you. In Grade 11 it cancels inside algebraic fractions. In your final year the factor theorem hands you one factor of a cubic and expects you to produce exactly this short-and-long structure. Six cubes, one mnemonic, one collapsing expansion — carry them and this question type pays you for three years.

The last questions of this part are with you now: the difference twin executed, the common factor extracted before the identity, and the exponent checked so squares and cubes never trade places.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same identity from the tuckshop storeroom — sugar cubes, sealed cartons, and one bar of soap that keeps every sign honest.

## Subtopic: Cubes Are Just Boxes You Can Stack

Put the algebra down for a second and think about packing. A cube number is a number of blocks that fills a perfect box — length, width and height all equal. Stack sugar cubes two along, two deep, two high: eight cubes. So eight is a cube number. Three each way gives twenty-seven. Four each way, sixty-four. Five, one hundred and twenty-five. Six, two hundred and sixteen. And a single lonely cube in the corner of the shelf: one. That list — one, eight, twenty-seven, sixty-four, one hundred and twenty-five, two hundred and sixteen — is the entire vocabulary of this topic. Know it the way you know your own phone number: without deciding to remember.

Letters stack too. X cubed — x³ — is x times x times x: a box measuring x in every direction. Y to the nine is a cube because y cubed, stacked three times over, builds it — the block being stacked is y cubed. Even twenty-seven a cubed b cubed is a cube: the block is three a b.

So the first skill is pure spotting, and spotting needs only the list. Given two terms with a plus or minus between them, ask each term: are you something, boxed? X cubed plus eight — x³ + 8 — says yes twice: x boxed, and two boxed. Speak the two names aloud — x and two — because the whole answer is built from nothing but those names.

Also notice what does not work, so you stop wasting exam minutes there. No common factor. Not a difference of squares — the sign is wrong and the terms are not squares. No middle term to play with. Every familiar move bounces off, and that bouncing is not failure — it is the arrow pointing at the cubes method.

Take the spotting questions waiting for you now: which numbers are cubes, and what each one is a cube of. Win these and the rest of the lesson cannot hide anything from you.

## Subtopic: Three Signs, One Bar of Soap

Here is the good news up front: every cubes answer has one fixed shape — a short bracket and a long bracket. The short bracket holds the two names, x and two. The long bracket holds three parts made from the same names in a fixed order: square the first, multiply the pair, square the last — x squared, then two x, then four. You can build that whole skeleton before thinking about a single sign.

Then the soap handles the signs. SOAP: Same, Opposite, Always Positive. Same — the short bracket copies the sign in the question. Opposite — the middle of the long bracket flips it. Always Positive — the last part is plus, every time, no exceptions.

Run it on x cubed plus eight. The question says plus, so the short bracket says plus: x plus two. Opposite of plus is minus: minus two x. Always positive: plus four. Answer: the quantity x plus two, times the quantity x squared minus two x plus four — (x + 2)(x² − 2x + 4).

Now that you can build it, give it its proper name: the sum of two cubes — a cubed plus b cubed equals a plus b, times a squared minus a b plus b squared.

Why should anyone trust it? Multiply the brackets back and watch the bookkeeping. The x contributes x cubed, minus two x squared, plus four x. The two contributes plus two x squared, minus four x, plus eight. Minus two x squared and plus two x squared settle each other like a debt repaid the same afternoon; four x and minus four x do the same. What is left standing is x cubed plus eight — exactly the question.

One trap to defuse for good: the middle of the long bracket is two x, never four x. Square, TIMES, square — the middle position multiplies the names; it never squares the second one.

Your questions for this section are ready — say the three SOAP signs out loud as you answer, and enjoy how little there is to actually remember.

## Subtopic: The Curtain, the Twin and Knowing When to Stop

Three finishing ideas, and the topic is yours permanently.

The twin. A minus in the question changes only signs, never structure. Take x cubed minus sixty-four — x³ − 64. The names: x, and four, because sixty-four is four boxed. Same: x minus four. Opposite: plus four x. Always positive: plus sixteen. Answer: x minus four, times x squared plus four x plus sixteen — (x − 4)(x² + 4x + 16).

The curtain. Examiners like to hang a common factor in front of a cube, the way a curtain hides a window. Five x cubed minus forty — 5x³ − 40 — looks hopeless, because neither five nor forty is on the cube list. Pull the curtain: both terms share five, giving five, times x cubed minus eight. And there stands the cube in full view. SOAP finishes it: five, times x minus two, times x squared minus two x plus four. Rule zero of every factorisation: common factor first, pattern second.

And the full stop, where careful learners protect their marks. Leave the long bracket alone. X squared plus four x plus sixteen tempts the trinomial reflex — hunt two numbers with product sixteen and sum four. Two and eight give ten; four and four give eight; one and sixteen give seventeen. No pair exists, and no pair ever exists for the long bracket of a cubes answer. Searching for it is queueing at a counter that never opens. Fully factorised includes knowing where the answer ends.

One last sorting habit, because squares and cubes dress alike. Read the exponent first. X squared minus sixteen is squares: x minus four times x plus four, two short brackets, both done. X cubed minus sixty-four is cubes: one short bracket, one long bracket, and the long one stays. Squared calls squares; cubed calls cubes — the exponent chooses the tool before you do.

And here come the final questions of the lesson: the curtain pulled aside, the twin factorised, and the full stop placed exactly where it belongs. Answer them and this identity works for you all the way to your final exams.
