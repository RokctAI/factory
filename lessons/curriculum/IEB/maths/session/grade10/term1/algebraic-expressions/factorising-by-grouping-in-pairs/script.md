# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the tool for four-term expressions: factorising by grouping in pairs. The anchor problem is to factorise fully three m p plus three m q minus n p minus n q — four terms, two families of letters, and a tidy two-bracket answer waiting behind three disciplined moves. Grouping also powers the second half of the trinomial method, so mastering it here strengthens the entire factorisation toolkit.

## Subtopic: Why Four Terms Demand a Different Tool

Count the terms: three m p, plus three m q, minus n p, minus n q. Four of them — and in factorisation, the term count chooses the tool. Two terms send you to the difference of squares or the cubes identities. Three terms send you to the trinomial method. Four terms send you here, to grouping in pairs.

Confirm that nothing simpler works. An overall common factor — always the first check: the four terms share nothing, because the first two carry m and no n, the last two carry n and no m, and no number beyond one divides all four. An identity? There is no difference of squares here and no cube in sight. Every familiar door is closed.

Now look again, more locally. The FIRST two terms, three m p and three m q, share a great deal: both contain three m. The LAST two, minus n p and minus n q, both contain n. So while nothing is common to all FOUR terms, plenty is common to each PAIR. That local sharing is the entire foundation of the method: split the four terms into two pairs, factor each pair down to its own common factor, and then — this is the destination — watch the two pairs reveal an identical bracket that can be factored out in turn.

The plan in one line: pair up, factor each pair, factor out the shared bracket. Three moves. The second move hides a sign trap that catches an enormous number of Grade 10 learners, so we will take it slowly and defuse it permanently.

Take the questions for this section now: the term count that selects the method, the failed hunt for an overall common factor, and the pairwise sharing that grouping is designed to exploit.

## Subtopic: Factoring Each Pair — and the Negative Factor Trap

Move one: form the pairs as the terms stand, keeping every sign glued to its term. First pair: three m p plus three m q. Second pair: minus n p minus n q. The minus signs belong to n p and n q and must travel with them into the pair.

Move two on the first pair: three m p and three m q both contain three and m, so take out three m. Dividing three m p by three m leaves p; dividing three m q by three m leaves q. The first pair becomes three m, times the quantity p plus q.

Now the second pair — and here is the trap. Minus n p minus n q clearly shares n. But which n comes out: positive or negative? Try positive n first. Dividing minus n p by n leaves minus p; dividing minus n q by n leaves minus q. Result: n times the quantity minus p minus q. Nothing illegal — but compare brackets. The first pair produced p plus q; this pair produced minus p minus q. They do not match, and without a shared bracket the third move cannot happen. The method stalls.

Now try NEGATIVE n. Dividing minus n p by minus n leaves POSITIVE p, because a minus divided by a minus turns positive. Dividing minus n q by minus n leaves POSITIVE q. Result: minus n, times the quantity p plus q. Every sign inside the bracket flipped — dividing by a negative flips signs — and the bracket now reads p plus q, a perfect twin of the first pair's bracket.

Here is the rule, and it deserves a box in your notes: when a pair BEGINS with a minus sign, factor out a NEGATIVE common factor, so the signs inside flip into agreement. This one habit removes the single most common grouping failure in the grade.

The expression now reads: three m times the quantity p plus q, MINUS n times the quantity p plus q.

This section's questions are with you now: each pair reduced correctly, the dead end that the positive factor creates, and the negative factor demanded by a leading minus.

## Subtopic: The Common Bracket — Finishing the Factorisation

Move three. Look at what stands on the page: three m times p plus q, minus n times p plus q. Two large terms now — and they share a factor. Not a single letter this time, but an entire BRACKET: p plus q appears in both.

A bracket is a factor like any other, and it comes out the same way. If that feels abstract, rename it: let the bracket p plus q be called B. Then the expression is three m times B, minus n times B — and factoring B out of both terms leaves three m minus n. So the answer is B times the quantity three m minus n, which is: the quantity p plus q, times the quantity three m minus n.

The factorisation: three m p plus three m q minus n p minus n q equals p plus q, times three m minus n.

Verify by expanding — the short proof that costs a minute. P times three m is three m p; p times minus n is minus n p; q times three m is three m q; q times minus n is minus n q. Collect: all four original terms return, each wearing its correct sign. Proven.

Understand what the matching bracket really is: a built-in checkpoint. Matching brackets mean the pairing and the signs were right, and the answer is one move away. Mismatched brackets — like p plus q against minus p minus q — are the method waving a flag: usually a positive factor where a negative belonged, occasionally a pairing that needs rearranging. Never force different brackets together; read the flag, trace the cause, repair it.

One further skill: sometimes the terms arrive in an order that refuses to pair. Consider three m p minus n q minus n p plus three m q. Pair it as given and the first pair, three m p minus n q, shares nothing useful. The remedy is rearrangement — addition lets terms change seats. Rewrite as three m p plus three m q minus n p minus n q, reuniting the m-family and the n-family, and the grouping then runs exactly as before. When pairs refuse to factor, reseat the terms so that kin sits beside kin, and try again.

Turn to this section's questions: the bracket handled as a factor in its own right, the expansion that proves the result, and the reshuffle that rescues a stubborn ordering.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Checks, and Where Grouping Hides

The complete method, five steps. One: overall common factor first — the opening move of every factorisation, no exceptions. Two: count four terms and pair them with signs attached, rearranging first if the families are separated. Three: factor each pair, taking out a negative factor whenever a pair leads with a minus. Four: confirm the brackets match, then factor the shared bracket out, gathering the leftovers into the second bracket. Five: verify by expanding, and inspect both final brackets for further factorisation — fully factorised is a contract, not a suggestion.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the positive factor where a negative belonged — n instead of minus n — leaving mirror-image brackets that can never merge; forcing them anyway turns one error into three. Exhibit two: sign amnesia in transit — copying the second pair as n p plus n q because the minus signs fell off along the way; signs travel welded to their terms. Exhibit three: stopping at three m times p plus q minus n times p plus q — that is the halfway house, not the answer; the shared bracket must still come out. Exhibit four: pairing blindly, failing, and declaring the expression unfactorisable when a simple reshuffle would have paired the families correctly.

A bonus example to cement the habits: factorise two c d plus six c minus d minus three. Overall common factor? None. First pair: two c d plus six c — take out two c, leaving d plus three. Second pair: minus d minus three — it leads with a minus, and the only shared content is one, so take out MINUS ONE, leaving d plus three. Matching brackets. Answer: the quantity d plus three, times the quantity two c minus one. Even when minus one is all there is to extract, the negative-factor rule earns its place.

And where does grouping resurface? Inside the trinomial method: splitting the middle term produces four terms that grouping then finishes — today's skill is that method's engine room. In your final year, the factor theorem reduces cubic polynomials to exactly this finishing work. In Grade 11, algebraic fractions cancel whole grouped brackets at a stroke. Four terms, two pairs, one shared bracket — the pattern earns marks for years, and it is yours now.

The closing questions of this part are in front of you: the five steps in order, the four museum exhibits recognised, the minus-one bonus example, and the future topics that grouping quietly runs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four terms from the till at a corner shop — two carrier bags, four items, and one rule about the minus sign that never lets you down.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Shopping Into Two Bags

Four terms: three m p, plus three m q, minus n p, minus n q. Picture them as four grocery items standing on the counter, waiting to be packed.

Before any clever packing, ask the standard opening question of all factorising: does one thing divide into all four items? Check honestly. The first two carry an m and no n. The last two carry an n and no m. No number beyond one divides everything. So there is no single bag that fits the whole shop — the usual doors are shut.

Now look at the items two at a time. The first two, three m p and three m q, both carry a three and an m. The last two, minus n p and minus n q, both carry an n. This is exactly how you pack real shopping: nothing fits in one bag, but the frozen things go together and the tinned things go together. Grouping in pairs is packing: sort the four terms into two sensible bags, take out what each bag shares, then see what the two bags themselves have in common.

Open bag one: three m p plus three m q. Both items contain three m, so lift it out. Three m p divided by three m leaves p; three m q divided by three m leaves q. Bag one becomes three m, times the bracket p plus q.

Notice what the bracket holds: p and q — the parts the two items did NOT share. A bracket always carries the leftovers.

And remember what sent us down this road: the count. Two terms call the squares or cubes identities; three terms call the product-and-sum hunt; four terms call grouping. Count first, choose second, and the wrong tool never ends up in your hand.

Your questions for this section are with you now — counting terms, testing for an overall common factor, and sorting four terms into two workable pairs.

## Subtopic: The Minus That Must Come Out

Bag two: minus n p minus n q. This bag is where the marks go missing across the country, so slow right down.

Both items carry an n. Lift out a plain positive n and watch what happens. Minus n p divided by n leaves minus p. Minus n q divided by n leaves minus q. You get n, times the bracket minus p minus q. Legal — but hold it next to bag one, which gave p plus q. Minus p minus q and p plus q are not the same bracket, and the next move needs identical twins. Dead end.

Now lift out negative n instead. Minus n p divided by minus n leaves positive p — two minuses dividing make a plus, the same way two wrongs in a double negative make the sentence positive. Minus n q divided by minus n leaves positive q. Bag two becomes minus n, times the bracket p plus q. The signs inside flipped, and now the two brackets match exactly.

Say the rule until your pen believes it: when a bag starts with a minus, take out a negative. The signs inside flip, and the brackets become twins.

One serious warning, because a tempting shortcut lurks here. You may NOT simply erase the minus signs inside a bracket to force a match. Changing n times minus p minus q into n times p plus q alters the value of the expression — multiply it out and the difference is exposed immediately. A bracket's insides may only change if the factor outside changes to pay for it. Want the signs flipped inside? Make the factor outside negative. Same value, honestly bought.

The expression now reads: three m times the bracket p plus q, minus n times the bracket p plus q.

That single habit — leading minus means negative factor — has just removed the most common grouping error in the grade from your future. Your questions for this section are ready: factoring each bag, and what must come out when a bag leads with a minus.

## Subtopic: The Same Bracket in Both Hands

Look at what you are holding: three m times the bracket p plus q in one hand, minus n times the same bracket p plus q in the other. Two big pieces, one shared bracket.

Here is the idea that finishes the job: a bracket is a factor like any other factor. If that feels slippery, give the bracket a nickname. Call p plus q simply B. Then you hold three m B minus n B — and anyone in the class can factorise that: the B comes out the front, leaving three m minus n behind. Restore the real name and the answer stands: the bracket p plus q, times the bracket three m minus n.

Check it by multiplying out — it takes seconds. P times three m is three m p. P times minus n is minus n p. Q times three m is three m q. Q times minus n is minus n q. Gather them: three m p plus three m q minus n p minus n q. Every original item back on the counter, every sign intact.

Two extras worth keeping. First, the matching bracket is a free error alarm. Twin brackets mean the sorting and the signs were right. Different brackets mean something upstream broke — and the honest response is to walk back and fix it, never to force the brackets together.

Second, sometimes the four items simply arrived in the wrong queue. Take three m p minus n q minus n p plus three m q: the first two share nothing worth taking. Addition allows terms to swap seats, so reshuffle until family sits with family — three m p plus three m q minus n p minus n q — and the packing runs exactly as before. A grouping question that looks impossible is usually a seating problem, not a maths problem.

And this pattern keeps returning: split the middle term of a trinomial and four terms appear, finished by grouping; cubic factorisations in your final year end the same way. Four items, two bags, one shared bracket — that is the whole method.
