# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the busiest factorisation tool in algebra: the trinomial method. The anchor problem carries the classic complication — factorise fully four x squared minus four x minus fifteen, a trinomial whose leading coefficient is not one. By the end you will own the full procedure: hunting the two governing numbers, splitting the middle term, finishing with grouping, and proving the answer by expansion — enough machinery to open any trinomial this syllabus can set.

## Subtopic: From Expanding to Factorising

Start from what you can already do. Expand the binomials x plus two and x plus five: the first terms give x squared; the outer and inner products give five x plus two x, which is seven x; the last terms give ten. Result: x squared plus seven x plus ten — a trinomial, three terms.

Factorising is that process run in reverse: handed x squared plus seven x plus ten, recover the brackets x plus two and x plus five. Why does the reverse matter? Because factors expose what a trinomial conceals — the values that make it zero, the cancellations that simplify a fraction, the structure underneath the surface. Next year, factorised trinomials crack quadratic equations; in your final year they underpin calculus. This tool never goes out of service.

Watch the reverse closely, because it dictates the method. In the expansion, the ten arrived as two TIMES five — a product. The seven arrived as two PLUS five — a sum. So to reverse x squared plus b x plus c, hunt two numbers whose product is c and whose sum is b. For x squared plus seven x plus ten: product ten, sum seven — the numbers two and five. Brackets: x plus two, times x plus five. Finished.

One discipline stands before every factorisation you will ever perform: check for a common factor FIRST. Two x squared plus fourteen x plus twenty surrenders a common factor of two immediately: two, times the quantity x squared plus seven x plus ten — and the bracket then factorises further into the friendly trinomial above. Skip the common factor and the word fully has already cost you marks.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now: expansion read backwards, the product-and-sum rule for a leading coefficient of one, and the common factor that must exit before anything else happens.

## Subtopic: The Product-and-Sum Hunt — When a Is Not One

Now the anchor: four x squared minus four x minus fifteen. The leading coefficient is four, not one, and that changes the hunt in exactly one place.

Name the coefficients against the standard shape a x squared plus b x plus c — signs attached, always. Here a is four, b is NEGATIVE four, c is NEGATIVE fifteen. Lose a sign at this line and every line after it is wrong.

The adjusted rule for a bigger than one: hunt two numbers whose product is a TIMES c — not c alone — while the sum stays b. Compute the target product: four times negative fifteen is negative sixty. Target sum: negative four.

Before listing a single factor pair, read the signs like a detective. The product is negative, so the two numbers carry OPPOSITE signs. The sum is negative, so the larger number, in size, wears the minus. Two deductions made before any hunting — this sign-reading cuts the search in half every single time.

Now the factor pairs of sixty: one and sixty; two and thirty; three and twenty; four and fifteen; five and twelve; six and ten. Opposite signs means the SUM is really a DIFFERENCE, so hunt the pair whose difference is four. One and sixty: fifty-nine. Two and thirty: twenty-eight. Three and twenty: seventeen. Four and fifteen: eleven. Five and twelve: seven. Six and ten: four — found it.

The pair is six and ten, opposite signs, the bigger one negative: NEGATIVE ten and POSITIVE six. Verify both conditions like a professional: negative ten times six is negative sixty — product correct. Negative ten plus six is negative four — sum correct. Both keys turn; the lock is open.

The questions on this section stand ready: coefficients named with signs, the target product a times c against the target sum b, and the sign-reading that shapes the pair before the list is written.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Middle Term and Grouping

What do the two numbers actually do? They split the middle term. Negative four x is rewritten as negative ten x plus six x, so the trinomial becomes: four x squared minus ten x plus six x minus fifteen.

Pause on what changed: nothing. Negative ten x plus six x IS negative four x — same value, new outfit. But the new four-term outfit submits to grouping, the method you already own.

Group into pairs. First pair: four x squared minus ten x. Both terms share two and an x — two x. Factor it out: four x squared over two x leaves two x; negative ten x over two x leaves negative five. The pair becomes two x, times the quantity two x minus five.

Second pair: plus six x minus fifteen. Both share three. Factor it out: six x over three leaves two x; negative fifteen over three leaves negative five. The pair becomes three, times the quantity two x minus five.

Look at the page: two x times two x minus five, plus three times two x minus five. The identical bracket in both groups — two x minus five. That match is the method's heartbeat and its built-in checkpoint. Matching brackets certify the split and the signs; mismatched brackets accuse them. Never force mismatched brackets — trace back, repair, rerun.

Factor out the shared bracket: the quantity two x minus five, times the leftovers gathered from each group — two x plus three.

The factorisation: four x squared minus four x minus fifteen equals the quantity two x minus five, times the quantity two x plus three.

Does the split order matter? Run it the other way: minus four x as plus six x minus ten x gives four x squared plus six x minus ten x minus fifteen, grouping as two x times two x plus three, minus five times two x plus three — landing on two x plus three, times two x minus five. Same destination through the other gate. Either order works when the signs stay honest.

Work this section's questions now: the split written in full, each pair reduced to its factor, and the matching bracket read as the checkpoint it is.

## Subtopic: Verification, Sign Patterns and the Full Method

Never submit an unverified factorisation — expansion is a proof that costs thirty seconds. Expand two x minus five, times two x plus three: two x times two x is four x squared; two x times three is six x; negative five times two x is negative ten x; negative five times three is negative fifteen. Collect: four x squared plus six x minus ten x minus fifteen — four x squared minus four x minus fifteen. The original stands restored. Marks banked.

Now compress the sign-reading into the permanent pattern. Product a c positive with sum positive: both numbers positive. Product positive with sum negative: both numbers negative. Product negative: opposite signs, and the sum's sign rides on the larger number. Three lines that convert guessing into deduction for every trinomial you will ever meet.

The complete method, seven steps. One: common factor first, always. Two: name a, b, c with signs. Three: compute target product a times c and target sum b. Four: read the signs and predict the pair's pattern. Five: hunt the pair — sums for same signs, differences for opposite signs. Six: split the middle term and finish by grouping, expecting matched brackets. Seven: verify by expansion, and inspect both brackets for further factorisation.

The error museum, three exhibits. Exhibit one: hunting product c instead of a times c when a exceeds one — the four x squared collapses the moment the brackets are tested. Exhibit two: sign amnesia — hunting with four instead of negative four, finding a pair that adds the wrong way; the mismatched brackets downstream will flag it if you let them. Exhibit three: stopping at two x times two x minus five plus three times two x minus five — half-factorised is unfactorised; the shared bracket must come out.

One honest boundary: not every trinomial factorises over the integers. If no pair passes both tests — product AND sum — the trinomial is irreducible for now, and stating that IS the answer. Next year the quadratic formula collects those cases; this year, recognising them protects you from inventing numbers that do not exist.

Finally, place this tool in the kit. Two terms call the squares or cubes identities. Four terms call grouping. Three terms — after today — call the product-and-sum hunt, split, and group. The term count asks the question; you now hold every answer.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: verification by expansion, the three sign patterns kept distinct, the seven steps in order, and the trinomial that honestly refuses to factorise.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same trinomial from the taxi rank — two fares, one route board, and every move of the method said in ordinary words.

## Subtopic: Two Numbers That Multiply and Add

Picture paying for two short taxi hops: one costs R2, the other R5. Together they cost R7 — that is the two fares added. Multiplied, they make 10. Now play the game backwards: I am thinking of two amounts that multiply to 10 and add to 7. You would land on 2 and 5 in seconds. That backwards game is trinomial factorising, whole and entire.

Forwards in algebra it looks like this: multiply the bracket x plus two by the bracket x plus five, letting each term in the first meet each term in the second. X times x is x squared. X times five is five x. Two times x is two x. Two times five is ten. Merge the middle: five x plus two x is seven x. Result: x squared plus seven x plus ten — three terms, a trinomial.

Backwards: handed x squared plus seven x plus ten, you want the brackets back. The ten was born as a product of two numbers; the seven was born as their sum. So hunt: two numbers, product ten, sum seven. One and ten add to eleven — no. Two and five add to seven — yes. Brackets: x plus two, times x plus five. Done.

One free habit before any hunting, and it pays every time: check whether one thing divides into all three terms. In two x squared plus fourteen x plus twenty, a two divides everything — pull it to the front, leaving two times the bracket x squared plus seven x plus ten, and that bracket is the friendly trinomial you just cracked. Forget the pulled-out two and the word fully quietly takes your marks.

That is already real power: find two numbers that multiply and add to order, and half the trinomials in any exam are yours. The questions for this section are with you now — the multiplying, the adding, and the shared factor that leaves first.

## Subtopic: When the Number in Front Is Not a One

Sometimes a number rides in front of the x squared: four x squared minus four x minus fifteen. The game survives; one detail updates.

Say the three parts aloud with their signs welded on. In front of x squared: four. In front of x: NEGATIVE four. Standing alone: NEGATIVE fifteen. A minus is part of the number the way a debt is part of a balance — let one slide off and everything downstream is broken.

Here is the single update: the multiply-target is no longer the last number alone. It is the front number TIMES the last number. Four times negative fifteen: negative sixty. The add-target stays what it always was: negative four.

Read the signs before hunting. A negative multiply-target happens only when a plus meets a minus, so the two numbers carry opposite signs. A negative add-target means the heavier number carries the minus. You know the pair's shape before writing anything down.

Now the ways of making sixty: one and sixty, two and thirty, three and twenty, four and fifteen, five and twelve, six and ten. With opposite signs, adding really means subtracting, so hunt the pair that differs by four. Work the list: fifty-nine, twenty-eight, seventeen, eleven, seven... four. Six and ten differ by four — the pair.

So the numbers are negative ten and positive six. Check both promises like checking change at the window: negative ten times six is negative sixty; negative ten plus six is negative four. Both hold. The pair is right, and the sign-reading turned a wrestle into a short walk.

Your questions on this section are ready — front number times last number, and what the two signs tell you before the list exists.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Bill and Checking Your Change

Now for what the two numbers are FOR. Take the middle term, negative four x, and split it into the two pieces the numbers name: negative ten x plus six x. Nothing changed hands — a R100 note split into R60 and R40 is still R100. The expression becomes four x squared minus ten x plus six x minus fifteen. Four terms — which feels like going backwards and is exactly the road forward.

Take the terms two at a time. First two: four x squared minus ten x. A two and an x go into both, so two x steps out front, leaving the bracket two x minus five. Second two: plus six x minus fifteen. A three goes into both, so three steps out, leaving the bracket two x minus five.

Look at that — the same bracket, twice: two x times two x minus five, plus three times two x minus five. The match is your proof that everything upstream was right, like two tills ringing up the same total from different queues. If your brackets disagree, do not force them into one — walk back, re-test the two numbers, and check that every minus survived the journey.

The bracket two x minus five sits in both pieces, so it comes out front, and the leftovers — the two x and the three — form the second bracket. Answer: two x minus five, times two x plus three.

Now check your change. Multiply back: two x times two x is four x squared; two x times three is six x; minus five times two x is minus ten x; minus five times three is minus fifteen. Six x minus ten x is minus four x, and there stands four x squared minus four x minus fifteen — the exact expression you were handed. That is certainty, not hope.

And when no pair ever passes both tests? That is allowed. Some trinomials simply do not break into whole-number brackets, and writing that it does not factorise is a correct, full-marks answer — not giving up. The last questions of the lesson are with you now: the split, the two pairs, the shared bracket, and the change checked to the last cent.
