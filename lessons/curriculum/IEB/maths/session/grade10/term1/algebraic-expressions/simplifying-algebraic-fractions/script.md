# Part 1 — Expert

Every factorising technique of the past weeks reports for duty today. An algebraic fraction is a fraction whose numerator and denominator are polynomials, and every operation on one — simplifying, multiplying, dividing, adding, subtracting — opens with the same command: factorise first, cancel second. Four worked expressions structure this session, and running alongside them is the habit examiners reward and learners neglect — stating the restrictions on the variable.

## Subtopic: Factors, Not Terms — the Cancelling Law

The law comes before any example, because the law itself is examined. You may cancel a FACTOR shared by the whole numerator and the whole denominator. You may never cancel a TERM.

A factor multiplies the whole expression; a term is added or subtracted within it. The moment a plus or minus stands between parts of your numerator, those parts are terms, and nothing inside may be struck out against anything below.

Take the expression: x squared minus twenty-five, all over x squared plus eight x plus fifteen. The temptation: x squared appears upstairs and downstairs, so strike both. Convict that idea with a number. Let x equal one. The true value: one minus twenty-five over one plus eight plus fifteen — minus twenty-four over twenty-four, which is minus one. The illegal cancellation leaves minus twenty-five over eight x plus fifteen at best — at x equal to one, minus twenty-five over twenty-three. Different numbers, destroyed expression, zero marks.

Now the lawful route. The numerator, x squared minus twenty-five, is a difference of squares: x minus five, times x plus five. The denominator, x squared plus eight x plus fifteen, is a trinomial: two numbers with product fifteen and sum eight — three and five — so x plus three, times x plus five.

As products, the fraction reads: x minus five, times x plus five, over x plus three, times x plus five. Now x plus five is a genuine factor of the whole top and the whole bottom, and it divides out honestly. The simplified fraction: x minus five, over x plus three. Test at x equal to one: minus four over four — minus one, exactly the original value. Factorising changed nothing; it only revealed what top and bottom truly shared.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They separate factors from terms, and they will hand you cancellations to convict or acquit.

## Subtopic: Multiplying, Dividing, and Denominators Hiding Cubes

Multiplying algebraic fractions carries one mercy: no common denominator is required. Factorise every numerator and every denominator, cancel any factor that appears above and below anywhere across the product, and record the survivors.

Division adds one prior move, and it must come before any cancelling: invert the second fraction and multiply. Take x squared minus one, over x plus four, divided by x plus one, over x squared minus sixteen. Flip the divisor first: x squared minus one, over x plus four, times x squared minus sixteen, over x plus one. Now factorise everything: x minus one, times x plus one, over x plus four, times x minus four, times x plus four, over x plus one. The factor x plus one cancels top and bottom; so does x plus four. Survivors: x minus one, times x minus four. That product is a complete answer — expanding it is optional labour, not a requirement.

Denominators and numerators up to the sum and difference of cubes belong to this topic, so recognise them instantly. Simplify x cubed plus eight, over x squared minus four. The numerator is a sum of cubes — x cubed plus two cubed — factorising to x plus two, times x squared minus two x plus four. The denominator is a difference of squares: x minus two, times x plus two. The factor x plus two divides out, leaving x squared minus two x plus four, over x minus two.

Then stop moving. The trinomial x squared minus two x plus four does not factorise — no pair multiplies to four and sums to minus two — and nothing inside it may be cancelled against the x minus two below, because those are terms now, not factors. When a factor is irreducible, it stands.

Stop for this section's questions — invert before cancelling, factorise before inverting, and greet every cube by name the moment it appears.

## Subtopic: Adding and Subtracting — the Lowest Common Denominator

Addition and subtraction refuse to begin until the denominators match, exactly as with number fractions. The route never varies: factorise every denominator, assemble the lowest common denominator from all distinct factors, rewrite each fraction over it, then combine only the numerators.

Simplify four over x minus two, minus three over x squared minus four. Factorise the second denominator: x squared minus four is x minus two, times x plus two. The LCD is now visible: one x minus two and one x plus two — so the LCD is x minus two, times x plus two. Note what it is NOT: the product of the original denominators, which would drag in a second, useless copy of x minus two.

The first fraction lacks an x plus two, so multiply its top and bottom by x plus two: four times x plus two is four x plus eight. The second fraction already sits on the LCD; its numerator stays three. Combine: four x plus eight, minus three, over x minus two times x plus two — which is four x plus five, over x minus two times x plus two. The numerator does not factorise, so the answer is final. Leave the denominator in factors; it reads better and it displays the restrictions.

Now the subtraction trap in its purest form. Simplify three x plus two, over x minus five, minus x minus four, over x minus five. The denominators already match, so the numerator is three x plus two, MINUS the whole quantity x minus four. Bracket it before touching it: three x plus two, minus, open bracket, x minus four, close bracket. Distribute the minus across BOTH terms: three x plus two, minus x, PLUS four — giving two x plus six. Factorise the result: two, times x plus three. Answer: two times x plus three, over x minus five. Learners who skip the bracket write three x plus two minus x minus four, reach two x minus two, and lose every mark that follows. Whenever you subtract a numerator of more than one term, bracket it first — no exceptions.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — build the LCD from distinct factors, and bracket every numerator you subtract.

## Subtopic: Restrictions, and the Error Museum

A fraction with zero in its denominator is undefined — not zero, not infinity, undefined — so every algebraic fraction carries a silent condition: the denominator may not be zero. Writing that condition down is the restriction, and it is worth a mark of its own.

Read restrictions from the ORIGINAL denominators, before anything cancels. Return to the opening expression: x squared minus twenty-five, over x squared plus eight x plus fifteen. The denominator factorises to x plus three, times x plus five — so x may not be minus three, and may not be minus five. The simplified fraction, x minus five over x plus three, appears to ban only minus three. But minus five was banned the instant the original was written, and cancelling a factor cannot pardon it. Both restrictions stand. For the division example, restrict on every denominator that ever appeared AND on the numerator of the divisor — dividing by a zero fraction is still dividing by zero. There: x may not be minus four, four, or minus one.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: cancelling terms across a plus or minus — the commonest crime in the topic, always convictable by substituting one number. Two: cancelling before factorising — the same crime in a different jacket. Three: the unbracketed subtraction, leaving a minus four that never became plus four. Four: adding denominators — four over x plus three over x plus one is not seven over two x plus one; denominators are never added.

The compressed method: factorise every numerator and denominator; multiply by cancelling across, divide by inverting first; add and subtract via the LCD of distinct factors, bracketing every subtracted numerator; factorise the new numerator to hunt one final cancellation; and state restrictions from the original denominators, always.

The final questions of this part are with you now — expect the restriction to carry its own mark, and read it from the original expression every time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same fractions from the corner shop and the kitchen — crates, slices, and the one number a fraction will never allow.

## Subtopic: You Can Only Cancel a Whole Crate

Walk into the shop's storeroom. On the left: eight crates of cooldrink. On the right: twelve crates, same size. Compare the piles: eight over twelve — and because both piles are built purely of identical crates, you may shrink the comparison honestly. Divide both by four crates: two over three. Nothing lost.

Now change one detail. The left pile is eight crates PLUS one loose bottle standing on top. May you still divide crates out of both piles? No — because the loose bottle belongs to no crate, and any crate-chopping now has to answer for it. That loose bottle is a plus sign. The moment a plus or a minus sits in your top line, the parts are separate items — terms — and nothing may be cancelled out of them.

That is the entire cancelling law in one storeroom: cancel only what the WHOLE top and the WHOLE bottom are built from. And factorising is the repacking job — taking a messy pile with loose bottles and rewriting it as sealed crates multiplied together.

Watch the repack. The top, x squared minus twenty-five, packs into x minus five, times x plus five. The bottom, x squared plus eight x plus fifteen, packs into x plus three, times x plus five — three and five multiply to fifteen and add to eight. Both piles now contain an x plus five crate. Out it comes, whole, leaving x minus five over x plus three.

And check the way a shopkeeper balances a till. Put x equal to one into the original: minus twenty-four over twenty-four, minus one. Into your answer: minus four over four, minus one. Same number, till balanced, work trusted.

Quick check before we move on — questions on crates and loose bottles are coming to you now. For each, ask one thing: are the whole top and the whole bottom built of the same crate?

## Subtopic: Cut the Slices the Same Size Before You Add

Try adding half a loaf of bread to a third of a loaf. Nobody answers "two fifths" by adding tops and bottoms. You cut both into the same slice size — sixths — and then add: three sixths plus two sixths is five sixths.

Algebraic fractions behave identically. The denominator IS the slice size, and nothing can be added until both fractions are cut to the same size. The lowest common denominator is simply the smallest slice size both can be cut into cleanly.

Take four over x minus two, minus three over x squared minus four. Repack the second bottom: x squared minus four is x minus two, times x plus two. So one fraction is sliced by x minus two alone, and the other by x minus two AND x plus two together. The smallest size that suits both takes each different crate once: x minus two, times x plus two.

The first fraction is missing the x plus two, so multiply its top and bottom by it — the value does not change, only the slicing. Four times x plus two gives four x plus eight. Now both fractions match, and the tops may combine: four x plus eight, take away three, gives four x plus five. The answer sits over x minus two times x plus two, and it is finished.

Now the warning that outranks all others in this topic. When you subtract a top with more than one piece, you are removing the WHOLE parcel. If a customer returns a parcel containing a loaf and a fifty-rand voucher, you take back both items — not just the loaf. So three x plus two, minus the parcel x minus four, removes the x AND removes the minus four — and removing a minus four means adding four back. Result: three x plus two minus x plus four, which is two x plus six, and that repacks as two times x plus three. Put a bracket around every parcel you subtract, and the sign can never slip away.

Your questions for this part are up now. Cut to the same slice size first, then work only with the tops — and bracket every parcel you take away.

## Subtopic: The Numbers the Fraction Will Not Allow

The last idea quietly earns a mark on almost every fractions question. Suppose R360 must be shared equally among the learners in a lift club. Among four learners: R90 each. Among three: R120 each. Among zero learners? The question collapses. There is nobody to share among, and no answer exists — not R0, not infinity. No answer.

The bottom of a fraction is exactly that "how many to share among". So the bottom may never be zero, and every value of x that would zero it is banned before the question even starts. Those bans are the restrictions, and writing them is part of the answer. Interrogate each ORIGINAL bottom: what value would make you zero? In our opening expression the bottom packed into x plus three, times x plus five — so x may not be minus three, and x may not be minus five.

Here is where the marks leak. After cancelling, the x plus five vanished from the page — but minus five stays banned, because the ban was issued by the original question, and tidying the shop afterwards does not lift it. A number refused at the door stays refused. Hunt restrictions in the ORIGINAL bottoms, always before cancelling.

Assemble the whole session into four moves you now own. Repack everything into crates — factorise top and bottom. Cancel whole crates only. To add or subtract, cut both fractions to the same slice size and bracket any parcel you remove. And before writing the final answer, walk back to the original bottoms and ban every value that would empty them.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: name the banned numbers first, then simplify with clean hands — every move you need is already yours.
