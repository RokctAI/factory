# Part 1 — Expert

Two families of equation rule the algebra questions at this level, and both surrender to the same strategy: convert the unfamiliar into something already solved. A quadratic equation is driven into standard form and factorised, so that a product equal to zero can be read apart. An exponential equation is rewritten onto a single base, so that the exponents can be equated. Six worked equations carry this session, together with the two costliest errors in the topic: losing a solution by dividing, and equating exponents across different bases.

## Subtopic: Standard Form and the Zero-Product Law

A quadratic equation is one whose highest power of the unknown is two. Its entire solving method rests on one arithmetical fact, and the fact must be quoted exactly: if a product of two quantities equals ZERO, then at least one of those quantities is zero. That is the zero-product law — and it belongs to zero alone. No such law exists for eight: a product equal to eight says nothing certain about either factor.

That exclusivity dictates the procedure. Before factorising anything, move every term to one side so the other side reads zero. Only then does a factorisation pay out.

Solve x squared minus seven x plus twelve equals zero. The right side is already zero, so factorise the left: two numbers with product twelve and sum minus seven — minus three and minus four. So x minus three, times x minus four, equals zero. By the zero-product law, either x minus three is zero or x minus four is zero: x equals three or x equals four.

Write BOTH, joined by "or". A quadratic owns up to two solutions, and each satisfies the equation on its own. Check them, because checking is cheap: at x equal to three, nine minus twenty-one plus twelve is zero; at x equal to four, sixteen minus twenty-eight plus twelve is zero. Both confirmed.

One more, to weld the standard-form habit. Solve three x squared equals twenty-seven. Bring everything across: three x squared minus twenty-seven equals zero. Common factor three: three, times x squared minus nine, equals zero. The bracket is a difference of squares: three, times x minus three, times x plus three, equals zero. The three can never be zero, so the brackets speak: x equals three or x equals minus three. Learners who square-root both sides at the first line and write x equals three have silently discarded the negative root — half the answer, gone in one careless move.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test standard form first, the zero-product law second, and whether both solutions survived.

## Subtopic: Quadratics in Disguise — Brackets, Fractions, and the Lost Solution

Setters rarely hand you standard form; they hand you something that BECOMES standard form after honest rearrangement.

First disguise: a product equal to something other than zero. Solve x plus two, times x minus five, equals eight. The temptation is to set each bracket equal to eight — meaningless, because eight has no product law. Expand instead: x squared minus three x minus ten equals eight. Drive everything across: x squared minus three x minus eighteen equals zero. Factorise: minus six and plus three — so x minus six, times x plus three, equals zero, giving x equals six or x equals minus three.

Second disguise: a fraction. Solve eight over x equals x minus two. State the restriction first: x may not be zero. Multiply both sides by x: eight equals x squared minus two x. Rearrange: x squared minus two x minus eight equals zero. Factorise: minus four and plus two — x minus four, times x plus two, equals zero, giving x equals four or x equals minus two. Neither is the forbidden zero, so both stand.

Third disguise — the one that quietly eats marks: no constant term. Solve x squared equals seven x. Instinct says divide both sides by x, leaving x equals seven. Half right, and in mathematics half right is wrong: dividing by x silently assumes x is not zero, yet zero solves the original — substitute it and zero equals zero. The correct road: x squared minus seven x equals zero; common factor — x, times x minus seven, equals zero; so x equals zero or x equals seven. Both roots, both kept. Engrave the rule: never divide an equation by an expression containing the unknown. Division destroys solutions; factorisation preserves them.

Stop for this section's questions now — expand or clear the fraction first, drive everything to one side, and never divide the variable away.

## Subtopic: Exponential Equations — the Same-Base Method

An exponential equation hides its unknown in the exponent. Its method rests on a second single fact: if two powers with the SAME base are equal, their exponents are equal. With different bases, no conclusion follows — which is why the whole art is rewriting until one base rules both sides.

Solve two to the power x equals sixty-four. Sixty-four is two to the six. Two to the x equals two to the six: x equals six.

Solve five to the power x minus two equals one hundred and twenty-five. One hundred and twenty-five is five cubed, so equate the WHOLE exponent: x minus two equals three, giving x equals five.

Solve four to the power x equals thirty-two. The bases look mismatched until you notice both are powers of two. Four is two squared, so the left side is two squared, to the power x — two to the two x, by the power law. Thirty-two is two to the five. Equate: two x equals five, so x equals five over two. A fractional answer is perfectly respectable; leave it exact.

Solve three to the power two x plus one equals one over eighty-one. Eighty-one is three to the four, and the reciprocal turns the exponent negative: three to the minus four. Equate: two x plus one equals minus four, so two x equals minus five, and x equals minus five over two.

The habit behind all the speed: know the small powers on sight — twos up to sixty-four, threes up to eighty-one, fives up to one hundred and twenty-five.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — one shared base, then the exponents equated in full, brackets and all.

## Subtopic: Common Factors, Rational Exponents, and the Trap Museum

Two harder types complete the Grade 10 picture.

Type one: an exponential equation with two terms. Solve two to the power x, plus two to the power x plus two, equals forty. Nothing can be equated yet — the left side is a sum, not a single power. Split the second term with the multiplication law: two to the x plus two is two to the x, times two squared. The left side becomes two to the x, times the quantity one plus four — five times two to the x. So five times two to the x equals forty, giving two to the x equals eight, which is two cubed: x equals three. The move to remember: extract the common power as a factor, exactly as you would extract a common algebraic factor.

Type two: a rational exponent, with the unknown in the base. Solve x to the power three over two equals eight. Raise both sides to the reciprocal power, two over three: x equals eight to the power two over three — the cube root of eight, squared: two squared, four. Check: four to the power three over two is the square root of four, cubed — two cubed — eight. Confirmed.

Now a context problem, because quadratics arrive dressed in stories. A school vegetable bed in Kimberley is two metres longer than it is wide, and its area is thirty-five square metres. Let the width be x metres; the length is x plus two. Then x times x plus two equals thirty-five, which rearranges to x squared plus two x minus thirty-five equals zero and factorises to x plus seven, times x minus five: x equals minus seven or x equals five. Now the context rules: a width of minus seven metres does not exist, so that root is rejected WITH its reason written down. The width is five metres; the length is seven metres. A silently discarded root looks identical to a missed one — reject in writing, always.

The trap museum, four exhibits. One: brackets set equal to a non-zero number — the product law works for zero only. Two: the unknown divided away, and a root lost with it, as in x squared equals seven x. Three: exponents equated across different bases — four to the x equals thirty-two does not give x equals thirty-two over four; convert to a shared base first. Four: an exponential asked to reach zero or below — two to the power x equals zero has NO solution, because a positive base raised to any real power stays positive forever.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the common-factor move, the reciprocal power, and a context root rejected with its reason on the page.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same six equations from the market and the till — same answers, built out of ideas you can hold in one hand.

## Subtopic: Two Things That Multiply to Nothing

Start from a money fact you already believe. A vendor's takings equal the price of one item times the number sold. If the day's takings are R0, what happened? Either the price was zero — everything given away — or nothing was sold. No third story exists. Two numbers multiply to zero only when at least one of them IS zero.

Now see how special zero is. Takings of R80 explain nothing: R8 times ten items, R80 once, R4 times twenty — endless stories. Takings of zero tell one story. That is why every quadratic is pushed until one side reads zero before factorising: zero is the only number that talks.

So: x squared minus seven x plus twelve equals zero. Factorise the left into two brackets — two numbers multiplying to twelve, adding to minus seven: minus three and minus four — giving x minus three, times x minus four. Two things multiplying to nothing. So x minus three is zero, meaning x is three; or x minus four is zero, meaning x is four. Two answers, joined by "or", both true.

Check them like counting change. Three in: nine minus twenty-one plus twelve — nothing left. Four in: sixteen minus twenty-eight plus twelve — nothing left. Both work, and you proved it yourself.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions are coming to you right now. For each one, ask first: does one side read zero yet? If not, move everything across before touching anything else.

## Subtopic: Get Everything Into the Same Currency

A quick shopping puzzle: which is more, seven hundred and fifty grams of rice or one and a half kilograms? You cannot compare until both are in the same unit. Convert — one and a half kilograms is fifteen hundred grams — and the answer is immediate.

Exponential equations are that conversion, wearing algebra. In two to the power x equals sixty-four, the left side speaks "powers of two" and the right side speaks plain numbers. Convert the right: sixty-four is two doubled six times — two to the six. Same currency now: two to the x equals two to the six, so x is six. Nothing clever happened; the units changed.

A harder pair: four to the power x equals thirty-two. Neither is a power of the other — but both are built from twos. Four is two times two, so four to the x is two to the two x. Thirty-two is two to the five. Same currency: two x equals five, so x is five over two. Leave the fraction; it is the exact answer.

One with a reciprocal: three to the power two x plus one equals one over eighty-one. Eighty-one is three to the four, and the one-over flips the exponent negative: three to the minus four. Equate: two x plus one equals minus four, so x is minus five over two.

And one with two terms: two to the x plus two to the x plus two equals forty. Two to the x plus two is two to the x times four, so the left side is two to the x times five. Five of something equals forty, so the something is eight — two cubed — and x is three. Five identical parcels weighing forty kilograms together: each parcel weighs eight.

The one skill underneath: recognising the family a number belongs to. Learn the small powers like your own address: twos to sixty-four, threes to eighty-one, fives to one hundred and twenty-five. Spot the family, and the equation solves itself.

Your questions for this part are up now. For each equation, name the shared base first, then rewrite both sides in it before comparing anything.

## Subtopic: The Answer You Threw in the Bin

Last idea, and it is the difference between full marks and half marks.

Look at x squared equals seven x. Nearly everyone divides both sides by x and writes x equals seven. It is not wrong — seven genuinely works — but it is not the whole truth, because zero works too. Put zero into the original: zero equals zero. Perfectly true.

Why did dividing lose it? Because dividing by x quietly assumes x is not zero. Dividing by five is safe — five is definitely not zero. But x is not a known number; it might BE zero, and the instant you divide by it, that possibility lands in the bin.

The safe route: move everything to one side — x squared minus seven x equals zero. Take out the common factor: x, times x minus seven, equals zero. Now the takings rule speaks: either x is zero, or x minus seven is zero. Two answers, both kept. Make the rule permanent: never divide an equation by anything containing the unknown. Dividing destroys answers; factorising keeps them all.

The mirror-image habit matters just as much: sometimes a true answer must be turned away, with its reason stated. The Kimberley vegetable bed is two metres longer than it is wide, with an area of thirty-five square metres. Call the width x: x times x plus two equals thirty-five, so x squared plus two x minus thirty-five equals zero, factorising to x plus seven, times x minus five. The algebra offers minus seven or five. But no bed is minus seven metres wide — so you write that the negative root is rejected because a width cannot be negative, and answer: five metres wide, seven metres long. Keep every root the algebra produces; reject only what reality refuses — out loud, on paper.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: hunt the hidden root in each equation, and wherever a story appears, name the root you reject and say why. This one is yours now.
