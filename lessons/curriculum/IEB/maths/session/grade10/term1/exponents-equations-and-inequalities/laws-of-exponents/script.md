# Part 1 — Expert

This session installs the laws of exponents — the grammar in which the rest of algebra is written. Two tasks organise everything. First, simplify the expression: open bracket, three x y squared, close bracket, all squared, multiplied by two x to the power negative three, all divided by six x y to the power four. Second, solve the exponential equation: three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven. One expression, one equation — and between them, every law this topic can ask of you.

## Subtopic: The Laws — and Why They Are True

You met these laws in Grade 9; today they become permanent, because each one will be justified rather than memorised. An exponent counts repeated multiplication — x to the power four means four x's multiplied together — and every law is a consequence of that single sentence.

Law one: multiplying powers of the same base ADDS the exponents. X to the four times x squared is six x's in one product: x to the six. Same base, add.

Law two: dividing powers of the same base SUBTRACTS the exponents. X to the seven over x cubed: three x's cancel, four remain — x to the four. Same base, subtract.

Law three: a power raised to a power MULTIPLIES the exponents. X to the four, squared, means the pile of four taken twice: eight x's — x to the eight. Multiply.

Law four: a bracketed product raised to a power hands that power to EVERY factor inside. Three x, all squared, is three squared times x squared — nine x squared. This is the most-violated law in the grade: learners square the x and leave the three untouched. Every factor takes the power, without exception.

Two definitions complete the toolkit, and both follow from the laws rather than from decree. Anything nonzero to the power ZERO equals one — because x to the four over x to the four is certainly one, and by law two it is also x to the zero; two names, one value. And a NEGATIVE exponent means reciprocal: x squared over x to the five leaves x to the negative three by subtraction, yet by direct cancelling it plainly leaves one over x cubed — so x to the negative three IS one over x cubed. Read every negative exponent as an address, not a sign: it tells the power to live on the other side of the fraction line. It never makes anything negative.

The questions for this section are with you now: each law tied to its operation, the zero exponent justified from a division, and the negative exponent read as an address rather than a minus.

## Subtopic: Simplifying the Expression — Brackets First

Now the expression: three x y squared, all squared, times two x to the negative three, over six x y to the four. Strategy in three words: brackets, gather, divide.

Step one — the bracket. Three x y squared, all squared. Law four gives the square to every factor inside. Three squared is nine. X squared stays x squared. Y squared, squared: law three multiplies the exponents — y to the four. The bracket becomes nine x squared y to the four.

Step two — gather the numerator. Nine x squared y to the four, times two x to the negative three. Coefficients first, as ordinary numbers: nine times two is eighteen. The x's: law one adds exponents — two plus negative three is negative one; x to the negative one. The y to the four rides along untouched. Numerator: eighteen x to the negative one y to the four.

Step three — the division, base by base, deliberately. Coefficients: eighteen over six is three. The x's: law two subtracts — negative one minus one is negative two; x to the negative two. The y's: four minus four is zero — y to the zero is one, and the y's leave the stage entirely.

That leaves three x to the negative two — and one finishing move remains, because a polished final answer carries no negative exponents. The negative two is an address: move the x squared downstairs. The answer: three over x squared.

Verify with a number test, the professional's habit. Let x equal one and y equal one, purely as a check. Original: the bracket is three, squared is nine; times two times one — eighteen; over six — three. Our answer at x equal to one: three over one — three. Agreement. One substitution never proves an identity, but it catches nearly every slip for the price of twenty seconds.

The discipline worth copying: one law per line, coefficients treated as ordinary numbers, each base processed on its own. Exponent marks die when three laws are rushed into one line — slow is smooth, and smooth scores.

Take this section's questions before the equation: the bracket cleared with every factor paid, the bases gathered separately, and the y's dismissed through the zero exponent.

## Subtopic: Solving Three to the x Plus One Equals Twenty-Seven

The equation: three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven. An exponential equation — the unknown lives upstairs, in the exponent. The entire method is one move: make the bases match.

Why does matching bases work? Because if two powers are equal AND their bases are equal — the same positive base, not equal to one — their exponents must be equal too. Three to the something equals three to the something-else forces the somethings to agree. That agreement is the bridge from an exponential equation to an ordinary linear one.

Is twenty-seven a power of three? Count up: three, nine, twenty-seven — yes, three cubed. Rewrite: three to the x plus one equals three cubed.

Bases match, so equate the exponents — the WHOLE exponents: x plus one equals three. Solve: x equals two.

Verify by substitution, always: three to the power two plus one is three cubed, twenty-seven. Confirmed.

That worked because twenty-seven cooperated, so meet the exam's favourite costume changes now. If the right side were eighty-one: three to the four, so x plus one equals four and x equals three. If it were one: rewrite one as three to the zero, so x plus one equals zero and x equals negative one. If it were one over nine: three to the negative two, so x plus one equals negative two and x equals negative three. If the equation were two to the x plus one equals thirty-two: thirty-two is two to the five — same machine, different base, x equals four. The move never changes: write both sides as powers of one shared base, then equate exponents.

And refuse the tempting wrong turn: do not divide both sides by three hoping the exponent falls off. Dividing by three merely lowers the exponent by one — law two — and learners who "divide" and write x plus one equals nine have broken the mathematics entirely. The base-matching road is the only safe road.

This section's questions are in front of you now — the base-matching move executed, the costume changes with eighty-one, one, and one over nine handled, and the reason equal bases force equal exponents stated in full.

## Subtopic: The Error Museum — and the Full Method

Walk the error museum: four exhibits, each one a recurring mark-loser you will now recognise on sight.

Exhibit one: the bracket power that skips the coefficient. Three x y squared, all squared, written as three x squared y to the four — the three escaped unsquared. Law four applies to EVERY factor: the three becomes nine. When a bracket takes a power, tick each factor inside as it pays.

Exhibit two: adding exponents across DIFFERENT bases. X squared times y cubed is not x y to the five. The laws compress only powers that share a base — x's with x's, y's with y's, numbers with numbers, always in separate piles.

Exhibit three: the negative exponent read as a negative number. Two x to the negative three is not negative two x cubed, and not minus two over x cubed. The negative exponent relocates only the x cubed to the denominator; the two stays upstairs: two over x cubed. Address, not sign.

Exhibit four: multiplying the base by the exponent — three to the x plus one rewritten as three times x plus one. An exponent counts multiplications; it never multiplies the base. If that move were legal, three cubed would equal nine rather than twenty-seven — one absurdity that inoculates you forever.

Now the full method, assembled. For simplifying: clear bracket powers first, paying every factor; gather like bases; add exponents when multiplying, subtract when dividing; evict negative exponents to the other side of the fraction line for the final answer; and close with a quick number check. For exponential equations: express both sides as powers of one base; equate the whole exponents; solve the ordinary equation that remains; substitute back.

These laws never retire. Grade 11 hands you surds and fractional exponents — the same five laws wearing fraction clothes. Functions and financial mathematics run on them; logarithms in your final year are these laws read in reverse. The practice you do tonight compounds for three years — fittingly, exactly like an exponent.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four museum exhibits named, the simplifying sequence recited in order, and the equation method kept cleanly separate from it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same laws from the taxi rank — counting heads, sorting piles, and one rule about who pays the fare.

## Subtopic: Exponents Just Count How Many Times

First, remove the fear. An exponent is a counter — nothing more. X to the four — x⁴ — means "four x's multiplied together". X to the seven means seven of them. The small number counts how many times the big letter appears in the multiplication, and every law in this topic is bookkeeping on that count.

Multiplying? Push two piles together. Four x's times two x's makes one pile of six, so x to the four times x squared is x to the six. You added four and two because you were counting members of a pile, not adding values. Same base multiplied: add the counters.

Dividing? Cancel one against one. Seven x's upstairs, three downstairs: each one below knocks out one above, leaving four upstairs. X to the seven over x cubed is x to the four. Same base divided: subtract the counters.

A power on a power? Repeat the whole pile. X to the four, squared, means the pile of four taken twice — eight x's, x to the eight. Multiply the counters.

Now the two strange ones, and both fall straight out of counting. Anything nonzero to the power zero is one: x to the four over x to the four is a thing divided by itself, which is one — and by the subtracting rule it is also x to the zero. Same object, two names, so x to the zero is one. And a negative counter? Divide two x's by five x's: the subtracting rule says x to the negative three, but plain cancelling shows two knocked out above and three left standing below — one over x cubed. So x to the negative three and one over x cubed are the same thing. The minus is not a sign; it is an address. It sends the power downstairs, to the other side of the fraction line, and nothing becomes negative on the way.

Your questions for this section are with you now — answer them by counting piles rather than reciting rules, and the laws will turn into common sense in front of you.

## Subtopic: Everyone in the Taxi Pays the Fare

Now the big expression: three x y squared, all squared, times two x to the negative three, all over six x y to the four. It looks like one giant problem; it is four small ones standing in a queue.

Start with the bracket, and here is the rule that rescues more marks than any other in this grade: when a bracket takes a power, everyone riding inside pays the fare. Nobody travels free. Three passengers sit in this taxi — the three, the x, and the y squared — so all three get squared. Three squared is nine. X squared is x squared. Y squared, squared, is y to the four — repeat the pile. The bracket becomes nine x squared y to the four. The classic error lets the three ride unsquared, and the marker spots that fare-dodger instantly.

Next, sort the washing: numbers with numbers, x's with x's, y's with y's. Across the top: nine x squared y to the four times two x to the negative three. Numbers: nine times two, eighteen. X's: two plus negative three is negative one — x to the negative one. Y's: y to the four, riding along. The top reads eighteen x to the negative one y to the four.

Now divide by six x y to the four, pile by pile. Numbers: eighteen over six is three. X's: negative one minus one is negative two. Y's: four minus four is zero — and anything to the power zero is one, so the y's politely leave. Three x to the negative two remains — and the negative two is an address, so the x squared moves downstairs: three over x squared.

Then check, because you can. Put x equal to one and y equal to one into the original: the bracket is three, squared is nine; times two is eighteen; over six is three. Your answer at x equal to one: three over one — three. They agree, and the agreement cost you twenty seconds.

Take this section's questions now — one pile per line, every passenger paying, and the monster dismantles itself politely.

## Subtopic: Speak Both Sides in the Same Language

Last piece: the equation. Three to the power x plus one equals twenty-seven. The unknown is up in the counter, which feels wrong the first time — so here is the way in.

Two people can only agree when they speak the same language. The left side speaks "powers of three". The right side, twenty-seven, is sitting there in plain numbers. Translate it: three, nine, twenty-seven — twenty-seven is three cubed. Now both sides speak powers of three: three to the x plus one equals three to the three.

Once both sides are powers of the same base, the counters must match — there is no other way for the sides to be equal. So x plus one equals three, and x equals two. Check by putting it back: three to the power two plus one is three cubed, twenty-seven. Correct.

The same translation handles every costume the exam owns. Right side eighty-one? That is three to the four, so x plus one equals four and x is three. Right side one? One is three to the zero, so x plus one equals zero and x is negative one. Right side one over nine? That is three to the negative two, so x plus one equals negative two and x is negative three. And two to the x plus one equals thirty-two? Thirty-two is two to the five, so x is four. Different clothes, same move: one shared language, then let the counters talk.

Two wrong turns to refuse out loud. Do not divide both sides by three hoping the power falls off — dividing lowers the counter by one and leaves you with a true statement that helps nobody. And never rewrite three to the x plus one as three times x plus one: the counter counts multiplications of three by itself; it does not multiply the three. If it did, three cubed would be nine — and you know it is twenty-seven.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: counting, sorting, translating. Answer them and you own the grammar every formula between here and your final exams is written in.
