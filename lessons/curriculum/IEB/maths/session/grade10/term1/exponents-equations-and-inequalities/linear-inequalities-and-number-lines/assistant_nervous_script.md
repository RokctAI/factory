Hey, it's Thandi — I know why inequalities feel nerve-wracking: they look exactly like the equations you already know, so every tiny difference feels like a hidden trap. Here's the honest truth: there is only ONE new rule in this whole chapter.

Solve it like an equation. Add, subtract, multiply by a positive, divide by a positive — the sign never moves. The single exception: multiply or divide by a NEGATIVE, and the sign flips over. That's it. That's the entire difference. And you never have to trust your memory, because one test value in the original inequality tells you instantly whether your flip was right.

The dots and brackets are just spelling, and they follow one chain: a sign with the bar means the boundary is included, which means a filled dot, which means a square bracket. No bar means excluded, hollow dot, round bracket. Say the chain out loud as you draw and it can't break.

For word problems, read like a lawyer: "at least" includes the number, "more than" leaves it out. Then give your final answer in the story's language — vetkoek, rands, learners — not just symbols.

Now go do the practice questions. Flip only for negatives, test one value, match the dot to the bar. Three habits, and this chapter becomes one of the friendliest in the book. You've got this!
