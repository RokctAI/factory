**Thandi:** Time for the class questions! First one: a learner says the flip rule feels random — sometimes you flip, sometimes you don't — and they can never decide in the moment. Can you draw the line sharply for them?

**Tutor:** The line is razor sharp, and it runs through exactly two operations. You flip when you MULTIPLY or DIVIDE both sides by a negative number. That is the complete list. Adding a negative? No flip. Subtracting a negative? No flip. Multiplying by a positive, however large or small? No flip. The confusion usually comes from the phantom flip — learners see a minus sign anywhere in the working and panic-reverse the inequality. So run this checklist at each line: what operation am I performing, and what is the SIGN of the number performing it? Only the combination "times or divide" plus "negative" triggers the reversal. And when doubt survives the checklist, the test value settles it in ten seconds: pick any number from your answer, substitute into the ORIGINAL inequality, and demand that it works. Then pick a number just outside your answer and demand that it fails. Two substitutions, complete certainty, no memory required. The rule is narrow; the test is universal — carry both.

***

**Thandi:** Next: somebody solved a compound inequality by splitting it into two separate inequalities, got the right answer, and wants to know if the three-part method is compulsory or just the fashionable way.

**Tutor:** Splitting is completely legitimate — the compound statement minus five is less than or equal to three x plus one, which is less than ten, genuinely IS two conditions joined by "and": three x plus one is at least minus five, and three x plus one is under ten. Solve each separately and intersect the answers, and you land on the same strip, minus two up to but excluding three. The three-part method is preferred for two practical reasons. First, economy: every operation is written once instead of twice, and in an exam, fewer written lines mean fewer chances for a sign to die in transit. Second, safety: the format physically reminds you that the middle and both ends must all receive every operation — the classic error in split solutions is finishing one branch and forgetting the other. But the deep requirement is understanding what the sentence MEANS: one strip of numbers satisfying both fences at once. Whichever layout you choose, the final answer must be a single statement with the smaller value on the left, matching dots on the sketch — one solid, one hollow here — and the interval written square bracket, round bracket. Method optional; meaning compulsory.

***

**Thandi:** Third question, from the sketching side: does it actually matter whether the dot is filled or hollow? One learner lost a mark for it and thinks it's pedantic.

**Tutor:** It is the opposite of pedantic — the dot IS the answer at its most delicate point. Consider the vetkoek stall. The inequality said n is at least forty. If the dot at forty is filled, you are claiming the fortieth vetkoek satisfies her target — and it does: forty times fifteen is six hundred, minus the R150 fee leaves exactly R450, precisely the "at least" she asked for. If you draw the dot hollow, you are claiming forty fails and she needs forty-one — a different business decision, a different answer, wrong by one vetkoek and by one whole concept. The dot records whether the boundary case is admitted or turned away, and boundary cases are exactly where real decisions sit: the person aged exactly sixteen at the gate, the learner scoring exactly the pass mark, the stallholder hitting her target to the rand. That is why the marking distinguishes them. The habit that makes it automatic: every time you write an inequality sign, say aloud whether it has the bar. Bar means included means filled dot means square bracket. No bar means excluded means hollow dot means round bracket. One chain, four links, zero lost marks.

**Thandi:** Last one: the translation table. A learner asks why "more than forty" and "at least forty" get different treatment when they sound like the same thing in everyday talk.

**Tutor:** Because everyday talk is generous and mathematics is exact — and the exam lives in the gap between them. "At least forty" sets the floor AT forty: forty itself qualifies, and the inequality reads n greater than or equal to forty, with the bar. "More than forty" sets the floor just ABOVE forty: forty fails and forty-one is the first success — n strictly greater than forty, no bar. One vetkoek of difference, one mark of difference, and in context questions often the whole interpretation. The table to bank: "at least" and "no less than" mean greater than or equal. "At most", "no more than" and "a maximum of" mean less than or equal. "More than" and "exceeds" are strict greater-than. "Fewer than" and "under" are strict less-than. And when the counting object is discrete — vetkoek, learners, tickets — remember the final translation step: the algebra may say n is greater than or equal to forty, but the ANSWER says she must sell at least forty vetkoek, in words, in the story's own currency. Read like a lawyer, answer like a person — that is the full-marks combination.
