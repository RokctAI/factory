**Thandi:** Class questions coming in! First: someone wants to know why they should bother with the "why" behind each law. Their plan is to just memorise the five rules and move on — what's wrong with that?

**Tutor:** Nothing is wrong with it in week one; everything is wrong with it in week six, when the five rules have multiplied into fifteen half-remembered variations and the exam is asking which one applies. The counting picture is not decoration — it is compression. Hold one sentence, "an exponent counts repeated multiplication", and every law becomes something you can rebuild at the desk. Forgotten whether multiplying adds or multiplies the exponents? Picture four x's and two x's pushed into one pile: six x's — adding, obviously. Unsure about a power on a power? A pile of four taken twice is eight — multiplying. Even the strange pair falls out: x to the four over x to the four is a thing over itself, one, so x to the zero is one; and two x's over five x's leaves three below, so the negative exponent lives downstairs. Memory can be lost under pressure; reconstruction cannot. Learners who memorise own five facts. Learners who understand own the machine that manufactures the facts.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the bracket question — this one had the most wrong answers on the board today: why does the three in three x y squared, all squared, become nine? A lot of people squared the letters and left the three alone.

**Tutor:** Because the bracket is a taxi, and the power is the fare: everyone inside pays. Look at what the notation actually says. Three x y squared, all squared, means the entire product — three times x times y squared — multiplied by itself. Write it out longhand: three times x times y squared, times three times x times y squared. Now count what you have: two threes, which is nine; two x's, which is x squared; two y squareds, which is y to the four. The nine was never optional — it fell straight out of the longhand. The error happens because the letters LOOK like the algebra and the three looks like scenery, so the eye gives the power to the letters and waves the coefficient through. Train the counter-habit: every time a bracket takes a power, touch each factor inside with your pen — three, x, y squared — and confirm each one has paid. Three passengers, three fares, and the marker has nothing to circle.

***

**Thandi:** Third question: negative exponents. Someone wrote two x to the negative three as minus two over x cubed, and they're genuinely confused about which part the minus applies to.

**Tutor:** The confusion dissolves once the minus is read as an address instead of a sign. A negative exponent gives a relocation instruction to ONE tenant: the power it is attached to. In two x to the negative three, the exponent negative three is attached to the x alone — so the x cubed packs its bags and moves downstairs, and the two, which was never part of that power, stays exactly where it lives. Result: two over x cubed. Nothing became negative, because nothing about relocation changes value in that way — one over x cubed at x equal to one is one, at x equal to two is an eighth: positive numbers throughout. Your classmate's version, minus two over x cubed, turned a moving instruction into a minus sign and dragged the innocent two into it — at x equal to one it gives minus two where the truth is two. If ever unsure, run that one-second test: substitute x equal to one, where powers vanish, and see whether your rewritten expression matches the original. Address, not sign — and only the attached power moves.

**Thandi:** Last one: on the equation, a learner asks — what happens when the number on the right refuses to be a power of the base? Like three to the x equals twenty. Is the question broken?

**Tutor:** Not broken — just beyond this year's tools, and recognising that boundary is itself a skill worth marks. The base-matching method works precisely when both sides can be written as powers of one shared base, and exam questions at this level are built so they can: the twenty-sevens, eighty-ones, thirty-twos and one over nines are all chosen from the small powers you have drilled. If you meet three to the x equals twenty in an exam this year, the overwhelming likelihood is that a slip upstream produced the twenty — a dropped common factor, a sign error — so the first response is to audit your own working. But the honest mathematical answer is worth hearing too: a solution EXISTS, since three to the two is nine and three to the three is twenty-seven, so x sits somewhere between two and three — the equation is fine; it simply has no whole-number answer. The tool that names that number exactly is the logarithm, and it arrives in your final year, built directly on the laws you learned today. So: exam this year, expect cooperative numbers and audit when they misbehave; mathematics in general, know that the answer exists and its name is coming. Your small-powers vocabulary — twos to sixty-four, threes to eighty-one, fives to one hundred and twenty-five — is what makes the cooperative questions fast.
