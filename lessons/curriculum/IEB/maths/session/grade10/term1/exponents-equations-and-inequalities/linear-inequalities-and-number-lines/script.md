# Part 1 — Expert

An equation asks for the single value that balances two sides. An inequality asks a wider question — which values satisfy a condition — and its answer is almost always a stretch of the number line rather than a point. The algebra is the algebra you already own, with exactly one new rule, and around that rule sit two presentation skills the examination marks on their own: the number-line sketch and interval notation. Four worked inequalities carry this session.

## Subtopic: Solving Like an Equation — With One Exception

Name the signs precisely first: less than, greater than, less than or equal to, greater than or equal to. The two carrying the small bar include the boundary value; the two without exclude it — and that one distinction survives all the way to the final dot on your sketch.

Nearly every equation move is legal here. Add the same quantity to both sides; subtract the same quantity; multiply or divide both sides by a POSITIVE number — the inequality keeps pointing the same way through all of it.

Solve five x minus two is less than or equal to thirteen. Add two to both sides: five x is less than or equal to fifteen. Divide by five — positive — and the sign holds: x is less than or equal to three.

Now the exception, the reason this topic exists as its own skill: multiplying or dividing both sides by a NEGATIVE number reverses the inequality sign.

Watch it happen with plain numbers. Seven is greater than four — no argument. Multiply both by minus one: minus seven against minus four. Is minus seven still greater? No — minus seven is colder, deeper in debt, further left on the line. The statement only stays true if the sign flips: minus seven is LESS than minus four. Multiplying by a negative reflects both numbers through zero, and reflection reverses order.

So solve minus three x is greater than twelve. Divide both sides by minus three — negative — and flip: x is less than minus four. Prove it to yourself with one test value: take x equal to minus five. Minus three times minus five is fifteen, and fifteen is greater than twelve — the answer works. Now test the unflipped claim, x greater than minus four, with x equal to zero: minus three times zero is zero, which is not greater than twelve. The unflipped answer fails its own inequality — the flip was not optional.

One escape route worth carrying: avoid negative coefficients entirely by moving the variable to the side where it stays positive. Minus three x is greater than twelve becomes minus twelve is greater than three x, then minus four is greater than x — which, read from the x's point of view, says x is less than minus four. Same answer, no flip required.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. Every division by a negative in them is quietly asking one thing: did you remember to reverse the sign?

## Subtopic: The Number Line and Interval Notation

An inequality's answer is a set, and this course expects it presented three ways: in symbols, as a number-line sketch, and in interval notation.

The sketch first. Draw a horizontal line with arrows at both ends and mark the boundary value. If the boundary is INCLUDED — the sign carries the bar — draw a solid, filled dot. If it is EXCLUDED — a strict less-than or greater-than — draw an open, hollow dot. Shade the side where the answers live, and finish the shaded end with an arrowhead to say it runs forever.

For x less than or equal to three: solid dot at three, shading to the left, arrow on the left end. For x less than minus four: open dot at minus four, shading left.

Interval notation next. A round bracket excludes its endpoint; a square bracket includes it. Infinity can never be reached, so infinity ALWAYS takes a round bracket. The South African convention separates endpoints with a semicolon.

So x less than or equal to three becomes the interval from negative infinity to three — round bracket at infinity, SQUARE bracket at three. And x less than minus four becomes the interval from negative infinity to minus four, round brackets at both ends.

Two disciplines guard your marks here. First: the smaller value always stands on the left; an interval written backwards describes nothing at all. Second: state the domain. Unless the question restricts it, x is an element of the real numbers and the shading is continuous. If the question says x is an integer, the answer becomes separate dots, not a shaded stretch — and for x less than or equal to three, the largest integer allowed is three itself.

Stop for this section's questions now — solid dot or hollow, square bracket or round, and the smaller value on the left every time.

## Subtopic: Compound Inequalities — Trapped Between Two Fences

A compound inequality issues two conditions in one sentence, with the variable held in the middle.

Solve minus five is less than or equal to three x plus one, which is less than ten. Read it properly before touching it: three x plus one is at least minus five AND at the same time strictly less than ten. Two fences, one strip of land between them.

The method: whatever you do, you do to all THREE parts at once. Subtract one from each part: minus six is less than or equal to three x, which is less than nine. Divide each part by three — positive, no flipping: minus two is less than or equal to x, which is less than three.

Read the answer in its natural direction: x is at least minus two and strictly less than three. On the number line: a SOLID dot at minus two — the bar includes it — a HOLLOW dot at three, and shading only between them. A finite segment, no arrows. In interval notation: from minus two to three, square bracket at minus two, round bracket at three.

Where the marks leak: operating on only two of the three parts, so the middle drifts out of step with the ends — every operation visits all three, every time. And should you ever divide a compound inequality by a negative, BOTH signs reverse, and the finished statement must be rewritten so the smaller value stands on the left again.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — three parts moving together, one dot solid, one dot hollow.

## Subtopic: Word Problems, Integers, and the Trap Museum

Inequalities earn their keep in context, and the marks live in the translation.

A stallholder at a Soweto street market pays R150 for her stand for the day and sells vetkoek at R15 each. How many must she sell to make a profit of at least R450? Translate phrase by phrase. Let n be the number sold. Profit is income minus costs: fifteen n minus one hundred and fifty. "At least R450" means greater than or equal to four hundred and fifty. The inequality: fifteen n minus one hundred and fifty is greater than or equal to four hundred and fifty.

Solve it: add one hundred and fifty to both sides — fifteen n is greater than or equal to six hundred. Divide by fifteen — positive, no flip: n is greater than or equal to forty.

Now interpret in context, which carries its own mark. Nobody sells a fraction of a vetkoek, so n is a whole number, and the answer is a sentence: she must sell at least forty vetkoek. Check the boundary: forty times fifteen is six hundred; subtract the R150 stand fee and the profit is exactly R450. "At least" is satisfied by forty itself — which is precisely why the dot at forty is solid.

Bank the translation table, because setters rotate the phrasing endlessly. "At least" and "no less than": greater than or equal to. "At most", "no more than", "maximum": less than or equal to. "More than" and "exceeds": strictly greater than. "Fewer than" and "under": strictly less than. Read like a lawyer: "she sells more than forty" excludes forty; "at least forty" includes it. One word, one dot, one mark.

The trap museum, four exhibits. One: the unreversed sign after dividing by a negative — the topic's signature crime, always detectable by testing one value in the original. Two: the phantom flip — reversing the sign merely because a negative number was added or subtracted, where no flip belongs. Three: a solid dot drawn for a strict inequality with no bar. Four: an answer abandoned as algebra in a context question — the examiner asked about vetkoek, so the final line speaks vetkoek, not symbols.

The final questions of this part are with you now — translate precisely, solve carefully, and answer in the language of the problem.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four inequalities from a wallet and a queue — same answers, but this time the flipping rule will feel like something you have always known.

## Subtopic: Debt Turns Everything Upside Down

An equation is a scale in balance. An inequality is a scale that tips — one side genuinely heavier — and your job is to describe every number that keeps it tipping that way.

Almost anything you do to both sides leaves the tip alone. Add R20 to both pockets: the richer pocket stays richer. Take R50 from both: same. Double both: same. Halve both: same. No drama anywhere.

Then comes the one move that turns the world over, and money explains it best. Having R400 beats having R250 — obviously. Now flip both into debt: OWING R400 versus OWING R250. Which position wins now? Owing R250, without question. The bigger number became the worse position the moment it crossed into debt. In symbols: four hundred is greater than two hundred and fifty, but minus four hundred is LESS than minus two hundred and fifty.

That is the entire rule. Multiplying or dividing by a negative drags every amount through zero into debt, and the comparison must turn over with it.

So when minus three x is greater than twelve crosses your page, dividing by minus three gives x is LESS than minus four. Not greater. Less.

And memory is never your only defence, because one test settles everything. Your answer says minus five should work: minus three times minus five is fifteen, bigger than twelve — it works. The unflipped answer would allow zero: minus three times zero is zero, not bigger than twelve — it fails. Ten seconds of testing beats an hour of doubting.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions are on their way to you now. Wherever a negative divides out, flip the sign, then prove yourself right with one test number.

## Subtopic: Dots, Shading, and the Word "Or"

Your answer is not one number — it is a whole neighbourhood, and the sketch shows exactly who lives there.

Think about the sign at a stadium gate: "sixteen and older admitted". The sixteen-year-old at the front of the queue walks in. Now change one word: "older than sixteen". The same sixteen-year-old stays outside. Identical boundary, opposite fate for the person standing exactly on it.

That is all the two dots mean. A solid, filled dot says this boundary number is admitted — it comes from a sign wearing the bar. A hollow, open dot says the boundary number is turned away — a strict less-than or greater-than. Then shade the side where the answers live and let an arrow say it continues forever.

For x less than or equal to three: filled dot on three, shade left, arrow off the left end. For x less than minus four: hollow dot on minus four, shade left.

Interval notation is the same sketch compressed into one line, with brackets doing the dots' work. A square bracket is a filled dot: admitted. A round bracket is a hollow dot: turned away. Infinity always takes a round bracket, because no traveller ever arrives there. So x less than or equal to three reads: from negative infinity — round bracket — up to three — square bracket.

One ordering rule: the smaller number is written first, left to right, exactly as the number line itself runs. And when a question restricts x to whole numbers, stop shading: mark individual dots instead, because between the whole numbers nobody is home.

Your questions for this part are up now. For each boundary, decide first: admitted or turned away? Then draw the dot that says so.

## Subtopic: Between Two Fences, and the Vetkoek Test

Some answers are fenced on both sides. A bursary form says applicants must be at least fourteen but under nineteen: the fourteen gate stands open to the person exactly fourteen, while the nineteen gate is shut to the person exactly nineteen. A strip of allowed ages, one gate welcoming, one gate closed.

Algebra writes such a strip as one sentence with x in the middle. Minus five is less than or equal to three x plus one, which is less than ten. Handle it by doing each move to all three parts at once — like serving all three plates at the table together. Take one from each part: minus six, three x, nine. Divide each by three — friendly and positive, no flip: minus two, x, three. So x lives between minus two and three: allowed to BE minus two — the bar says so — but never three. Filled dot at minus two, hollow dot at three, shading only in between, no arrows.

Now the money question that shows why any of this matters. A stallholder at a Soweto street market pays R150 for her stand and sells vetkoek at R15 each. She wants a profit of at least R450. Profit is what comes in minus what went out: fifteen n minus one hundred and fifty, where n counts vetkoek sold. "At least R450" means reach four hundred and fifty or pass it. So fifteen n minus one hundred and fifty must be at least four hundred and fifty. Return the R150: fifteen n must be at least six hundred. Share by fifteen: n must be at least forty.

Then answer like a person, because that is where the mark sits: she must sell at least forty vetkoek. And run the boundary through the till yourself: forty vetkoek at R15 is R600; take away the R150 stand fee and exactly R450 remains. The fortieth vetkoek is the one that lands her target — which is exactly why forty is included and its dot is filled.

Two phrases to hold apart for life. "At least" and "at most" INCLUDE their number — filled dot. "More than" and "fewer than" leave it out — hollow dot. One small word decides whether the fortieth vetkoek counts, and you will never misread it again.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: translate the words, solve the algebra, then answer in the story's own language — vetkoek, rands, learners. That is what a full-marks answer sounds like, and it is yours.
