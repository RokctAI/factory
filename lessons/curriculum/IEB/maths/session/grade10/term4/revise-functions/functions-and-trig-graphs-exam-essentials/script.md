# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the functions revision sweep for the end-of-year examinations: every graph of the Grade 10 year — straight line, parabola, hyperbola, exponential, and the three trigonometric graphs — gathered into one framework. The framework is a single sentence: every function you know is a basic shape, stretched or flipped by a, then slid up or down by q. Own that sentence and the sketch questions, read-off questions and interpretation questions all submit to the same routine.

## Subtopic: One Sentence, Five Graph Families

Parade the five families and watch the same two letters perform the same two jobs in each. The straight line: y equals a x plus q. The parabola: y equals a x squared plus q. The hyperbola: y equals a over x, plus q. The exponential: y equals a times b to the power x, plus q. The trig graphs: y equals a sine x plus q, and likewise for cosine and tangent.

The letter q is the height control. It raises or lowers the entire graph without disturbing its shape. The parabola y equals x squared minus four is the basic bowl dropped four units; its lowest point slides from the origin down to nought, minus four. On a trig graph, q shifts the whole wave: the midline of y equals three sine x minus one runs along y equals minus one instead of the x-axis. On the hyperbola and exponential, q relocates the horizontal asymptote — the graph's forbidden height — from zero to q.

The letter a is the stretch control, with a mirror switch built in. On the parabola, a larger positive a builds a narrower, steeper bowl. On the trig waves, a is the amplitude — midline to peak. And a negative a throws the mirror switch: the bowl becomes a dome with a highest point, the sine wave opens by diving instead of climbing, the hyperbola trades its quadrant pair for the opposite one.

Exam skill one: read a and q straight off the equation before touching a calculation — y equals three sine x minus one announces amplitude three and a midline at minus one, no flip. Exam skill two is the reverse: given a labelled sketch, recover a and q. The height control and the stretch control are the two settings every later question adjusts.

This section's questions are with you now — five families, two controls, both directions.

## Subtopic: Line Meets Parabola — the Full Routine

The sketching routine for lines and parabolas runs in three moves: intercepts, special point, shape. Apply it to the classic pair — the parabola y equals x squared minus four and the line y equals x plus two.

The parabola. Y-intercept: set x to nought — minus four, the point nought, minus four. X-intercepts: set y to nought — x squared minus four equals nought, a difference of squares, x minus two times x plus two, so x equals two or minus two. Turning point: with no x term, the bowl rests symmetrically at its q value — nought, minus four, which doubles as the y-intercept here. Shape: a is positive one, an upright bowl. Range, read from the lowest point: y greater than or equal to minus four. Sketch: bowl through minus two and two on the x-axis, floor at minus four.

The line. Y-intercept: two. X-intercept: x plus two equals nought, so minus two. Gradient one, rising. Two points, one ruler.

Now the interpretation question that always follows: where do the graphs intersect? Intersection means equal heights at the same x, so set the formulas equal: x squared minus four equals x plus two. Sweep to one side: x squared minus x minus six equals nought. Factorise: x minus three, times x plus two. So x equals three or x equals minus two. Substitute into the line: at three, y is five — the point three, five; at minus two, y is nought — the point minus two, nought, which is also the shared x-intercept. Notice the machinery: an intersection question dissolved into the previous session's quadratic equation. The paper is one subject, not ten.

Read-off skills close the routine: for which x does the parabola sit below the line? Between the crossings — minus two to three, strict. Length of a vertical segment between the graphs at a chosen x: top formula minus bottom formula, evaluated there.

Take this section's questions now — the full routine on both graphs, and the intersection recovered by algebra.

## Subtopic: Forbidden Lines — Hyperbola and Exponential Asymptotes

Two families carry asymptotes — lines the graph approaches forever without touching — and questions test whether you can find them, draw them, and say what they forbid.

The hyperbola: y equals four over x, minus two. Two asymptotes. Vertical: x equals nought, because division by nought is impossible — the y-axis is barred. Horizontal: y equals minus two, the q value, because four over x fades toward nought as x grows enormous, leaving y just above or below minus two — never exactly there. The graph occupies two opposite quadrants of the asymptote cross. Anchor it with points: at x equals one, y is two; at x equals four, y is minus one; at x equals minus two, y is minus four. X-intercept: set y to nought — four over x equals two, so x equals two. Domain: all x except nought. Range: all y except minus two.

The exponential: y equals three to the power x, minus one. One asymptote, horizontal: y equals minus one — again the q. The power three to the x is forever positive — it never reaches nought — so y stays strictly above minus one. Y-intercept: three to the nought is one, minus one — the point nought, nought: this graph passes through the origin. More anchors: at one, two; at two, eight. Travelling left, the curve sinks toward its asymptote: at minus one, y is minus two thirds. Range: y strictly greater than minus one.

The asymptote discipline for sketches: rule the asymptotes first, dotted, then drape the curve over them. A hyperbola or exponential drawn without its dotted lines drops marks even when the curve itself is right.

And the recognition reflex, equation to family: x in a denominator — hyperbola, two asymptotes. X in an exponent — exponential, one. Lines and parabolas carry none. The asymptote count is each family's fingerprint.

This section's questions are ready — asymptotes stated, dotted, and honoured by every plotted point.

## Subtopic: Sine, Cosine and Tangent on the Full Domain

The three trig graphs across their examination domain, nought to three hundred and sixty degrees — fingerprints first, then the two controls.

Sine: departs from nought, crests at one over ninety, recrosses nought at one-eighty, troughs at minus one over two-seventy, returns home at three-sixty. One complete smooth wave. Cosine: the same wave on an earlier schedule — it OPENS at its peak: one at nought, nought at ninety, minus one at one-eighty, nought at two-seventy, one at three-sixty. Tangent: the maverick — no wave, no amplitude; it rises from nought through one at forty-five, races to infinity at its vertical asymptotes, x equals ninety and x equals two-seventy, and repeats. Sine and cosine live between minus one and one; tangent is unbounded.

Now the controls. Y equals three sine x minus one: amplitude three, midline sunk to minus one. Maximum: midline plus amplitude, two, still at ninety degrees. Minimum: midline minus amplitude, minus four, still at two-seventy. Y-intercept: at nought, sine is nought, so y equals minus one. Range: minus four up to two, inclusive. The vertical slide never moves the peaks left or right — q changes heights, never timing.

A mirrored example: y equals minus two cosine x. The opening peak becomes an opening valley: minus two at nought, climbing through nought at ninety to plus two at one-eighty, and back to minus two at three-sixty. Reading a and q off a given trig sketch reverses the machinery: amplitude is half the gap between maximum and minimum; q is their average — the midline.

Read-off questions on trig graphs: how many solutions does three sine x minus one equals one have on the domain? Draw the horizontal line at one; it slices the wave's rising and falling arms — two solutions. Where is the graph decreasing? Sine-shaped graphs fall from ninety to two-seventy. The picture answers counting and interval questions that pure algebra only reaches in Grade 11.

The final questions of this part are with you now — three fingerprints, two controls, and the read-off moves rehearsed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole gallery again, hung on one wall — five shapes, two sliders, and a routine that sketches anything.

## Subtopic: The Stretch Slider and the Height Jack

Every function equation is a machine fitted with two controls, and revision turns simple the instant you notice the controls are identical on every machine.

Control one: q, the jack. Whatever the basic shape does, plus q hoists the whole picture; minus q lowers it. The basic parabola bowls at the origin; minus four, and the identical bowl settles four units lower. The basic sine wave breathes around the x-axis; minus one, and the same wave breathes around the height minus one. For hyperbolas and exponentials the jack lifts something extra: the forbidden height — the horizontal asymptote — rides along, always parking exactly at q.

Control two: a, the stretch slider with a mirror switch. Push the slider up and the shape intensifies: steeper line, narrower bowl, taller wave. On waves the setting has a name worth using — amplitude, midline to peak, exactly the size of a. Flick the switch to negative and the picture reflects: bowls become domes, rising lines fall, waves open downward.

The revision power move: announce the controls aloud before doing anything. Y equals three sine x minus one — say it: no flip, amplitude three, midline minus one. Y equals four over x minus two — say it: hyperbola, asymptote cross at the y-axis and the height minus two. Ten seconds of control-reading and the sketch is half drawn, the range half stated, the interpretation half finished.

Your questions for this section are ready — two controls, said aloud, across every family on the wall.

## Subtopic: Four Verbs for Reading Any Graph

A large share of the functions marks needs no sketching at all — a finished graph is displayed and you are asked to read it. Reading is its own skill, built on four verbs.

Verb one: locate. Intercepts are the crossings of the axes — on the x-axis the height is nought; on the y-axis the x is nought. Turning points are the floor of a bowl or the ceiling of a dome. Asymptotes are the dotted no-entry lines.

Verb two: compare. Where does the parabola lie below the line? Between their crossing points — minus two to three in our worked pair. Which graph rides higher at x equals nought? The line at two sits above the parabola at minus four. Above means greater y, below means smaller — the phrase for which x is f above g translates into an interval on the x-axis.

Verb three: measure. The vertical distance between two graphs at a chosen x is top minus bottom evaluated there. At nought, line minus parabola: two, minus, minus four — six units. Setters favour this verb because it quietly examines substitution and signs.

Verb four: count. How many times does the horizontal line at one cut y equals three sine x minus one? The wave tops at two and bottoms at minus four, so the line at one passes through the climbing and the falling arms — count the crossings on the sketch: two. Counting questions are Grade 11 equation-solving, previewed by eye.

The discipline beneath all four verbs: every feature you claim must trace back to the equation or to a labelled point. The graph is the evidence; read from it, never from hope.

Here are this section's questions — locate, compare, measure, count.

## Subtopic: Six Lines That Sketch Everything

One checklist sketches every function in the Grade 10 gallery, and it is six lines long.

One: name the family from the equation's fingerprint — plain x, line; x squared, parabola; x downstairs in a denominator, hyperbola; x upstairs in an exponent, exponential; sine, cosine or tangent of x, a trig graph.

Two: read the controls — a for steepness, amplitude or the mirror flip; q for the lift and, where the family has one, the asymptote or midline.

Three: rule the skeleton — dotted asymptotes for hyperbola and exponential, the midline for a shifted wave, the axis of symmetry for the parabola.

Four: compute the anchors — y-intercept with x at nought, x-intercepts with y at nought solved by this week's algebra, the turning point where the family owns one, and the standard angles for trig: nought, ninety, one-eighty, two-seventy, three-sixty.

Five: sweep the curve through the anchors while honouring the skeleton — curves approach dotted lines and never cross them; waves keep within midline plus and minus amplitude.

Six: state the passport details when asked — domain, range, and any forbidden values of x.

Run the checklist mentally along the wall you now own: the line and the bowl crossing at minus two and three; the hyperbola hugging its lowered cross; the exponential sliding through the origin above y equals minus one; the deep wave peaking at two over ninety degrees. Same checklist, five families, every sketch mark collected en route.

Functions return in Grade 11 with sideways shifts joining the vertical ones — the two-control machine grows a third control, and everything rehearsed today transfers straight across.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — checklist from line one, family by family.
