# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the algebra revision sweep for the end-of-year examinations — the core of the year's algebra reorganised into the four decisions that carry the marks: how to factorise, how to solve each equation species, how to handle an inequality, and how to drive the exponent laws. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day edition — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to spring the classic traps in practice, where they cost nothing.

## Subtopic: Factorising in the Right Order

Every factorising question surrenders to the same four questions asked in the same order. Question one, always first: is there a common factor? Take five x squared minus forty-five. Both terms divide by five: five, bracket, x squared minus nine. Extracting the common factor first is not a stylistic preference — the leftover bracket is simpler, and frequently only becomes factorable once the extraction is done.

Question two: is the leftover a difference of two squares? X squared minus nine is precisely that: x minus three, times x plus three. Full answer: five, times x minus three, times x plus three. Hold the one-way rule: a DIFFERENCE of squares splits; a sum of squares, such as x squared plus nine, does not factorise at this level. And the pattern wears disguises: four x squared minus forty-nine is two x minus seven times two x plus seven — squares of expressions qualify just as well.

Question three: is it a trinomial? For x squared plus three x minus twenty-eight, hunt two numbers multiplying to minus twenty-eight and adding to plus three: plus seven and minus four. Factors: x plus seven, times x minus four. When the leading coefficient is not one, as in three x squared plus ten x minus eight, the hunt runs through the factor pairs of the outer product: the answer is three x minus two, times x plus four — and expanding confirms it: three x squared plus twelve x minus two x minus eight, which is three x squared plus ten x minus eight. That expand-back check is the professional habit: factorising is reversible, so reverse it and know for certain.

Question four, for four terms: grouping in pairs. And for the cube patterns: x cubed minus eight is a difference of cubes — x minus two, times, x squared plus two x plus four. First bracket from the cube roots; second bracket square, flip the sign, square.

The order in one breath: common factor, difference of squares, trinomial, grouping or cubes. Run it the same way every time and no factorising question can catch you unprepared.

## Subtopic: Three Equation Species, Three Strategies

Grade 10 keeps three equation species, and identifying which one is on your desk is half the solution.

Species one: linear — the variable appears to the first power only. Six x minus five equals two x plus nineteen. Gather x on one side, numbers on the other: four x equals twenty-four, x equals six. One solution, always.

Species two: quadratic — an x squared term is present. The golden sequence: everything to one side, zero on the other; factorise; set each factor to zero. Solve x squared equals three x plus ten. Do not divide by x, do not take square roots — sweep left: x squared minus three x minus ten equals zero. Factorise: x minus five, times x plus two. Solutions: x equals five or x equals minus two. Two solutions, and the marker expects both. The watched-for trap: dividing both sides of x squared equals seven x by x to reach x equals seven — that division quietly discards the solution x equals zero. Never divide by the variable; factorise instead: x times x minus seven equals zero gives x equals zero or seven.

Species three: exponential — the variable lives upstairs in the exponent. Two to the power x equals thirty-two. Strategy: force both sides onto the same base. Thirty-two is two to the fifth, so x equals five. The dressed-up version: three times five to the power x equals three hundred and seventy-five. Isolate the power first: five to the x equals one hundred and twenty-five, so x equals three. Order matters — divide away the three before touching the exponent, because three times five to the x is not fifteen to the x. Coefficients and bases never merge.

The recognition drill: variable squared — zero, factorise, two answers. Variable upstairs — same base. Variable plain — gather and divide. Classify first, then solve; classification is where the marks begin.

Take this section's questions now — three species, three strategies, and both forbidden moves refused.

## Subtopic: Inequalities — Mind the Mirror

An inequality solves exactly like a linear equation, with one explosive exception: multiplying or dividing both sides by a NEGATIVE number reverses the inequality sign. Minus three x plus four is greater than nineteen. Subtract four: minus three x is greater than fifteen. Now divide by minus three, and the sign flips: x is LESS than minus five.

Why the flip? Watch what negation does to order: two is less than six, but minus two is GREATER than minus six — multiplying by a negative mirrors the entire number line, and every left-right relationship swaps. The flip is not a convention; it is the mirror. Adding and subtracting never flip anything; only negative multiplication and division do.

Second skill: the compound inequality — a value fenced between two bounds. Minus five is less than three x plus one, which is less than or equal to ten. Work all three parts simultaneously. Subtract one throughout: minus six is less than three x, which is less than or equal to nine. Divide by three throughout: minus two is less than x, which is less than or equal to three.

Third skill: presenting the answer in the requested costume. Interval notation: from minus two to three, round bracket on minus two because it is excluded, square bracket on three because it is included. Number line: an open dot at minus two, a filled dot at three, shading between. Filled means included, open means excluded — the equal-to part of the sign chooses the dot. And when the question restricts x to integers, the answer becomes a list: minus one, nought, one, two, three.

Read the sign language exactly: strictly less than excludes; less than or equal to includes. One dot's filling, one bracket's shape — real marks.

This section's questions are ready — one flip justified, one fence solved, and the same answer written in every costume.

## Subtopic: Exponent Laws and Chained Questions

The exponent laws, rapid-fire, one example each. Same base multiplied: add the exponents — x to the fourth times x squared is x to the sixth. Same base divided: subtract — eight x to the sixth over four x squared is two x to the fourth; coefficients divide on their own. Power of a power: multiply — three x squared, all cubed, is twenty-seven x to the sixth; the coefficient gets cubed too. Anything nonzero to the power zero: one. Negative exponent: a reciprocal — x to the minus three is one over x cubed.

The law-level trap: the laws serve same-base products and quotients only. X to the fourth plus x squared does not become x to the sixth — addition obeys no exponent law; only like terms combine. And four times three to the x can never become twelve to the x.

Now the chained question — the favourite way of testing everything at once. Solve: three to the power, x minus two, equals twenty-seven. Same-base strategy: twenty-seven is three cubed. So x minus two equals three, and x equals five. Every exponential equation collapses into a smaller equation of a species you already own — topics chain together, and the chain is the design of the whole paper.

One more chain, factorising inside a fraction. Simplify: x squared minus twenty-five, over, x minus five. The top is a difference of squares: x minus five, times x plus five. Cancel the shared bracket with the bottom: x plus five, provided x is not five, because the original fraction outlaws a zero denominator. Factorising, fractions, restrictions — three topics, one expression.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: cancelling terms instead of factors — in x squared minus twenty-five over x minus five, the x squared and the x may not be struck out separately; only whole brackets cancel. Exhibit two: the lost solution from dividing by x. Exhibit three: the unflipped inequality sign. Exhibit four: x to the fourth plus x squared merged into x to the sixth. Each exhibit costs candidates marks every single year — except the ones who rehearsed them here.

The final questions of this part are with you now — laws applied, chains followed, and all four exhibits sidestepped.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same revision now runs as a sorting desk — four counters, one procedure per counter, and the labels that tell you instantly where any question belongs.

## Subtopic: The Sorting Desk — Four Counters

Picture your algebra as a busy sorting desk with four counters, and every algebra question as a parcel that names its own counter.

Counter one: factorising — the take-apart service. The job: rewrite an expression as things multiplied. Taped to this counter is a fixed order of service: common factor first, always; then the difference-of-squares check; then the trinomial hunt for two numbers; then grouping or the cube patterns. The order exists because each step clears the road for the next — pulling the five out of five x squared minus forty-five reveals the difference of squares x squared minus nine waiting underneath.

Counter two: equations — one procedure per species. Plain x: gather and divide. X squared: everything to zero, factorise, keep both answers. X upstairs in an exponent: same base on both sides.

Counter three: inequalities — the linear procedure with one red warning sticker on the till: dividing or multiplying by a negative flips the sign.

Counter four: exponents — five laws, valid on products, quotients and powers, never on sums.

What the year-end paper really examines is not any single counter — it is routing the parcel fast. So read the label before working: the word factorise routes to counter one; solve with an equals sign routes to counter two; an inequality sign routes to counter three; powers everywhere route to counter four. Say the counter's name out loud, then begin its procedure.

## Subtopic: Rewinding the Tape to Solve

Every equation is a recording of things done to x: multiply, subtract, square. Solving is playing the tape backwards — undoing the most recent operation first, the way you unlock a door before opening it, never after.

The linear tape: six x minus five equals two x plus nineteen. X appears on both sides, so first gather the family: subtract two x from both sides, four x minus five equals nineteen. Now rewind the minus five: four x equals twenty-four. Rewind the times four: x equals six. Each move undoes one operation, and each move lands on BOTH sides — an equation is a balanced scale, and whatever one pan receives, the other must too.

The quadratic tape needs a different opening move, because x squared and x refuse to gather into one pile. Instead: sweep everything to one side, park zero on the other, and factorise. X squared minus three x minus ten equals zero becomes x minus five, times x plus two, equals zero. Now the beautiful step — the zero-product idea: two things multiply to zero only when at least one of them IS zero. No other number carries that power — numbers multiply to twenty in endless ways, but to zero only through a zero. So x minus five is zero, or x plus two is zero: x equals five or minus two. Both answers, always — a quadratic expects a pair.

The exponential tape: five to the x equals one hundred and twenty-five. Rewrite the right side as five cubed and the equation reads: five to the x equals five to the three. Same base, same result — the exponents must agree: x equals three. If a coefficient blocks the view, as in three times five to the x equals three hundred and seventy-five, clear it by division first, then match the bases.

Here are this section's questions — rewind in reverse order, keep the scale balanced, and let zero work its magic.

## Subtopic: Trap Patrol and the Free Proof

Revision is mostly trap patrol, because at year-end the mathematics is familiar but the traps arrive freshly painted. Here are the four that harvest the most marks, each with its antidote.

Trap one: cancelling into a sum. In x squared minus twenty-five over x minus five, the urge is to strike the x's and the numbers separately. Forbidden — cancelling is division, and only a factor of the WHOLE top and the WHOLE bottom may be divided out. Antidote: factorise first. The top becomes x minus five times x plus five, and now the entire bracket x minus five cancels legally, leaving x plus five with its restriction.

Trap two: dividing an equation by x. X squared equals seven x, divided by x, gives x equals seven — and silently erases the second answer, x equals nought. Antidote: never divide by the unknown you are hunting; sweep to one side and factorise: x times x minus seven equals zero, both answers intact.

Trap three: the unflipped sign. Minus three x greater than fifteen becomes x greater than minus five under careless division — the wrong side of the mirror. Antidote: circle the inequality sign the instant you divide by any negative, and flip it: x less than minus five. Then audit with one value: x equals minus six gives minus three times minus six, eighteen, greater than fifteen — true, and the flip stands confirmed.

Trap four: inventing exponent laws for sums. X to the fourth plus x squared stays exactly as written; only like terms add.

And the final habit, the one that turns near-marks into full marks: substitute your answer back. X equals six in six x minus five: thirty-six minus five, thirty-one; and in two x plus nineteen: twelve plus nineteen, thirty-one. Balanced — proven correct at your own desk before any marker sees it. Factorised something? Expand it back. Solved an inequality? Test a number from your interval. Every counter at the sorting desk offers its own proof, and the proof costs nothing.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — four traps, four antidotes, and every answer proven before it leaves your desk.
