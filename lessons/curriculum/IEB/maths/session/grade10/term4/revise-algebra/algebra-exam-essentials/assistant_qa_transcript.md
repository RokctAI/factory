**Thandi:** Revision always stirs up the same worries, so let's take them one by one. First: with so much algebra in the year, how should someone actually structure their studying for this section?

**Tutor:** Structure it around decisions, not topics — because that is how the marks are structured. The paper never asks you to recite the difference of squares; it puts an expression in front of you and watches which tool you reach for. So the four decisions of this lesson are the study plan itself. Session one: factorising order — drill the sequence common factor, squares, trinomial, grouping until it runs without thought, using ten mixed expressions. Session two: equation species — take a page of mixed equations and, before solving anything, only CLASSIFY them: plain, squared, or upstairs. Classification is half the marks and it trains fastest when practised alone. Session three: inequalities, where one rehearsed flip covers most of the danger. Session four: exponent laws with the sum-trap in view. Twenty minutes per session beats three hours of rereading notes, because the deciding muscle only grows through choosing. And end every session by expanding or substituting your answers back — the free proof — so checking becomes reflex before the real day arrives.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the trinomial hunt. Finding the two numbers for something like x squared plus three x minus twenty-eight feels like guesswork to a lot of people. Is there a method, or just luck?

**Tutor:** It is a fully deterministic search, and the signs shrink it before you start. You need two numbers that multiply to minus twenty-eight and add to plus three. Read the product's sign first: negative product means the two numbers carry DIFFERENT signs. Read the sum's sign second: positive sum means the larger number is the positive one. Now list the factor pairs of twenty-eight: one and twenty-eight, two and fourteen, four and seven. You are hunting a pair whose difference is three, since the signs oppose — and four and seven differ by exactly three. Larger one positive: plus seven and minus four, giving x plus seven times x minus four. Three reads of a sign and one short list — no guessing anywhere. Had the product been positive, the numbers would share a sign and you would hunt a SUM instead of a difference. And when the leading coefficient is not one, as in three x squared plus ten x minus eight, the same discipline applies to the outer product, with the expand-back check as the final referee. Method, not luck — every time.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the one that costs everyone: why does dividing an equation by x lose an answer, when dividing by two is perfectly fine?

**Tutor:** Because two is guaranteed to be a number you may divide by, and x is not — x is the suspect you are still investigating. Watch the crime happen in x squared equals seven x. Dividing both sides by x looks harmless and delivers x equals seven, which is indeed a solution. But the division step assumed x is not zero — division by zero is meaningless — so the working quietly declared zero to be impossible without ever checking. And zero IS a solution: nought squared equals seven times nought, both sides zero, perfectly balanced. The equation had two answers; the division deported one of them. Compare dividing by two: two is never zero, so no assumption sneaks in and no answer dies. The safe procedure treats zero with respect: sweep everything to one side, factorise x times x minus seven equals zero, and let the zero-product idea list every culprit — x is nought, or x is seven. As a rule of thumb for the exam room: the moment your pen wants to divide by anything containing the unknown, stop, move everything left instead, and factorise. The habit costs one extra line and saves a mark almost every time it fires.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: under time pressure, checking answers feels like a luxury. Is the substitute-back habit really worth the minutes?

**Tutor:** It is the cheapest insurance on the paper, and the arithmetic says so. Substituting x equals six into both sides of six x minus five equals two x plus nineteen takes perhaps fifteen seconds: thirty-one on the left, thirty-one on the right, balanced, certain. Against that, consider the cost of an unchecked slip: the answer mark dies, and if later parts build on the value, they can bleed too. Fifteen seconds against several marks is not a luxury; it is the best-paying trade available in the room. The key is knowing the fast check for each counter, because each has one. Equations: substitute into the ORIGINAL equation, not your own second line, which may already carry the error. Factorising: expand the brackets back in your head — first terms, outer, inner, last — and match the middle term especially. Inequalities: test one number from inside your interval, plus, when the flip was involved, one from outside to feel the boundary. Exponentials: power the base back up. And the discipline that makes it affordable: check immediately while the question is loaded in your head, not in a panicked sweep at the end. Fifteen seconds each, spent well, is how careful candidates finish with the marks they actually earned.
