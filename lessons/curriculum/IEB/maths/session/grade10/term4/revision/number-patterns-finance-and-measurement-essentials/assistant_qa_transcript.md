**Thandi:** Three topics in one sweep means three sets of worries, so let's take the big ones. First: patterns. The formula-building feels fine in class, but in tests people blank on where the plus-two comes from. What's the reliable rebuild?

**Tutor:** Rebuild it from the lamppost picture, because pictures survive stress better than recipes. The coefficient of n is never in doubt — it is the spacing, read straight off the gaps: four, in our six, ten, fourteen. The constant that follows is the depot: the spot one gap BEFORE the first lamppost. Walk backwards from six by one spacing of four and you stand at two — so the address of lamppost n is four n plus two. Why one gap before? Because the term four n has already taken one full stride by the time n equals one: four times one is four, not zero. The formula needs a starting point that makes the first stride land exactly on six, and two plus four does precisely that. If the picture ever deserts you, the algebra version is first term minus difference: six minus four, two. And the rescue that works even in a blank panic: write four n plus c, substitute n equals one, set it equal to the first term, and solve for c — one line, no memory required. Three routes to the same constant; carry whichever one your nerves prefer, but always confirm with the five-second test on term two.

**Thandi:** Finance next. Students mix the two formulas constantly — simple where compound belongs and the reverse. Is there a foolproof selector?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is a vocabulary test before it is a mathematics test. Scan the question for its signal words. On the original amount, hire purchase, flat rate, straight-line growth — those name SIMPLE interest, the flat fee: A equals P times one plus i n, the same rand amount of interest every year forever. Compounded, inflation, interest on the accumulated amount, growing by a percentage each year — those name COMPOUND: A equals P times one plus i to the power n, this year's interest computed on last year's total. When the words are ambiguous, ask the structural question: does the interest itself earn interest next year? If yes, snowball; if the interest is set aside and only the deposit works, flat fee. Then respect the two formula-level traps. In the simple formula the i n multiplies INSIDE the bracket — seven percent for four years is one plus nought comma two eight, not one comma zero seven times four. In the compound formula the power applies to the whole bracket — one comma zero seven to the fourth, never one plus nought comma zero seven times itself loosely. And hire purchase adds its own step order: deposit off first, simple interest on the balance for the FULL term, then divide by the months. Words select the machine; the machine then runs itself.

**Thandi:** On measurement: the catalogue has five solids and ten formulas, which sounds like a memory burden. How much is truly memorised versus rebuilt?

**Tutor:** Only the sphere is honestly memorised; everything else is rebuilt from two ideas. Idea one, for volume: stack the base. A prism stacks its rectangle, length times breadth, up its height. A cylinder stacks its circle, pi r squared, up its height. That single idea IS both volume formulas — no separate learning. Cones and pyramids are the pointed versions, and pointed means one third of the full stack: the pour test from earlier in the term showed a cone filling its cylinder in exactly three pours. So four of the five volumes come from stack-the-base, with or without the one-third discount. Idea two, for surface: unfold and add. The prism unfolds into three pairs of rectangles; the cylinder into two lids and a label whose width is the circumference; the pyramid into a base plus leaning triangles — and leaning is the cue that the slant height takes over, manufactured by Pythagoras from the perpendicular height and half the base or the radius. That leaves the sphere: four thirds pi r cubed to fill, four pi r squared to wrap, learned outright, with the powers of r as the memory aid — cubed fills, squared wraps. Two ideas, one exception, ten formulas recovered on demand. That is a far lighter bag to carry into an examination room.

**Thandi:** Last one: the scale factor. The law sounds simple — k, k squared, k cubed — but the partial-scaling questions still catch everyone. What's the discipline?

**Tutor:** The discipline is to stop applying the law to the SOLID and start applying it to the FORMULA, letter by letter. The full law — lengths k, areas k squared, volumes k cubed — holds only when EVERY length scales together, because volume's three dimensions each contribute a factor. The moment the question scales one dimension only, retire the slogan and read the formula instead. Cylinder volume: pi r squared h. Double only the radius: r appears twice — squared — so two factors of two arrive, and the volume quadruples. Double only the height: h appears once, one factor, volume doubles. Double both: three factors, times eight, and you are back at the full law as a consistency check. The procedure is mechanical: write the formula, underline each letter the question scaled, count the underlines including repeats from powers, and raise the scale number to that count. It also runs in reverse: told a volume grew sixty-four times under a full enlargement, take the cube root — k is four — then answer the area question with k squared, sixteen. The one-sentence summary to keep: the law counts dimensions, and the formula shows you exactly how many of them moved.
