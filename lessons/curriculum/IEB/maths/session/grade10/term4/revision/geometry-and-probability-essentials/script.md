# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the revision sweep for the geometry and data half of the year — quadrilaterals and the midpoint theorem, analytical geometry, probability, and statistics. Four topics, four compact toolkits: a family tree of shapes with their defining properties, three coordinate formulas, one addition rule that refuses to double-count, and a five-number portrait of any data set. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day edition — compressed, worked through fresh examples, and aimed at the traps that quietly collect marks at year-end.

## Subtopic: The Shape Family and the Midpoint Shortcut

The quadrilaterals form a family tree, and each generation ADDS a property while keeping everything inherited. The parallelogram founds the line: both pairs of opposite sides parallel and equal, both pairs of opposite angles equal, and diagonals that bisect each other. The rectangle inherits the lot and adds right angles — which forces its diagonals equal. The rhombus inherits the lot and adds four equal sides — which forces its diagonals perpendicular and makes them bisect the corner angles. The square crowns the tree, descending from both: it is simultaneously a rectangle and a rhombus. On side branches sit the trapezium, owning just one pair of parallel sides, and the kite, with two pairs of adjacent equal sides and diagonals crossing at right angles.

The examinable skill is selecting the MINIMUM test. To prove a parallelogram: one pair of opposite sides both equal AND parallel suffices — or diagonals bisecting each other. To promote a parallelogram to a rectangle: a single right angle, or equal diagonals. To promote to a rhombus: one pair of adjacent sides equal. Prove the cheapest ticket; the family tree hands over everything else at no charge.

Then the midpoint theorem, the year's most elegant shortcut: the segment joining the midpoints of two sides of a triangle is parallel to the third side and exactly HALF its length. Third side sixteen — midpoint segment eight, and parallel, no measurement required. The converse holds too: a line through one midpoint drawn parallel to another side must pass through the third side's midpoint. When a triangle question says midpoint twice, it has shown its hand — the theorem or its converse is the intended key.

This section's questions are with you now — cheapest test first, and let the family tree pay out the remainder.

## Subtopic: Two Points, Three Formulas

Three formulas run the whole of Grade 10 analytical geometry, and each answers one question about two points. Take A at one semicolon three, and B at six semicolon fifteen.

How far apart? Distance: the square root of, the difference in x squared plus the difference in y squared. Differences: five and twelve. Root of twenty-five plus one hundred and forty-four — root one hundred and sixty-nine — thirteen. This is Pythagoras in coordinate dress: the differences are the legs, the distance is the hypotenuse.

Where is the middle? Midpoint: average the x's, average the y's. Half of one plus six is three comma five; half of three plus fifteen is nine. Midpoint: three comma five semicolon nine. Averages, nothing fancier.

How steep is the join? Gradient: change in y over change in x — twelve over five. Uphill left to right when positive, downhill when negative, zero when horizontal — and undefined when vertical, because the run collapses to nought.

Now the two relationships every paper visits. Parallel lines: equal gradients — twelve fifths pairs with twelve fifths, no exceptions. Perpendicular lines: gradients multiplying to minus one — the partner of twelve fifths is minus five twelfths. Flip and negate: that is the perpendicular machine.

And the integrated classic: prove a quadrilateral on the grid is a parallelogram. The fastest route runs through the diagonals. Compute the midpoint of each diagonal; if the two midpoints coincide, the diagonals bisect each other, and the family tree from the previous section certifies the parallelogram. One formula used twice, plus one theorem — that is how the topics chain together.

Take this section's questions now — three formulas, two relationships, and Pythagoras underneath it all.

## Subtopic: Counting Chance Without Counting Twice

Probability opens with one fraction: favourable outcomes over total outcomes, when all outcomes are equally likely. A bag holds twenty marbles, five of them red: the probability of drawing red is five twentieths — a quarter, nought comma two five. That is THEORETICAL probability, computed before anyone draws. RELATIVE FREQUENCY is what actually happened: draw two hundred times with replacement, count the reds, divide. The two disagree in small experiments and edge together as trials pile up — that drift towards the theory is the entire point of comparing them.

Now the double-counting problem, the heart of the topic. In a class of thirty-six, twenty-two play hockey, fifteen play tennis, nine play both. How many play hockey or tennis? Not twenty-two plus fifteen — that counts the nine double-sport players twice. Correct count: twenty-two plus fifteen minus nine — twenty-eight. And so eight play neither. The Venn diagram shows it at a glance: fill the overlap FIRST with nine, then hockey-only is thirteen, tennis-only is six, and the outside region holds the remaining eight.

As a law: P of A or B equals P of A, plus P of B, minus P of A and B. Subtract the overlap once, because the addition counted it twice. Two special cases complete the toolkit. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together — the overlap is zero and the rule collapses to plain addition. Complementary events split the world in two: P of not A equals one minus P of A. If the probability of a delayed bus is nought comma two, the probability it runs on time is nought comma eight — and complementary is the stronger condition, because the two events must also cover everything between them.

This section's questions are ready — overlap first in every Venn, and subtracted exactly once in every rule.

## Subtopic: Five Numbers That Draw a Data Set

A data set answers two questions: where is its centre, and how widely is it spread? Take the ordered set: four; six; nine; eleven; fourteen; eighteen; twenty-two — seven values.

Centre, three ways. The mean: total over count — eighty-four over seven — twelve. The median: the middle value of the ordered list — the fourth of seven — eleven. The mode: the most frequent value — this set has none, and no mode is a legitimate answer. The mean consults every value, which is both its strength and its weakness: one wild value drags it. The median cares only about position, so outliers cannot move it far.

Spread, two levels. The range: maximum minus minimum — eighteen. Crude, because it listens only to the two extremes. Finer: the quartiles. The lower quartile is the median of the values below the median — of four, six, nine — six. The upper quartile: the median of fourteen, eighteen, twenty-two — eighteen. The interquartile range: eighteen minus six — twelve. The middle half of the data occupies a corridor twelve wide, and no outlier can edit that sentence.

The five-number summary parades them — minimum four, lower quartile six, median eleven, upper quartile eighteen, maximum twenty-two — and the box-and-whisker plot draws the parade: a box from six to eighteen with a line at eleven, whiskers stretching to four and twenty-two. Read the lean from the picture: the median sits left of the box's centre and the right whisker runs long, so the data leans right — and the mean at twelve floating above the median eleven repeats the message in numbers.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: citing a test that proves nothing — equal diagonals alone certify no parallelogram. Exhibit two: a gradient inverted as run over rise. Exhibit three: the overlap counted twice — thirty-seven players in a class of thirty-six. Exhibit four: quartiles pulled from an unordered list. Every exhibit costs real candidates real marks each year — except those who rehearsed them here.

The final questions of this part are with you now — order the data, station the five numbers, and let the box narrate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same revision now told as three pictures — a family tree, a counting machine that never counts twice, and a data set drawn as a box — with the habits that make each picture automatic.

## Subtopic: Inheritance — Properties Down, Proofs Up

Think of the quadrilaterals as a family where every child keeps all the family traits and contributes one of their own. The parallelogram opens the line: opposite sides parallel and equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals halving each other. The rectangle is the child who added right angles. The rhombus is the child who added equal sides. The square is the grandchild of both — whatever is true of rectangles and whatever is true of rhombuses is true of the square, automatically.

Inheritance is the exam cheat code, and it works in both directions. Downwards, for properties: asked to list the square's properties, recite the parallelogram's list, then the rectangle's addition, then the rhombus's — no separate memorising. Upwards, for proofs: pay only the cheapest entry ticket. Diagonals bisecting each other buys parallelogram. One right angle upgrades it to rectangle. A pair of adjacent equal sides upgrades it to rhombus. Never prove more than the ticket price — every extra property proven is time spent buying what inheritance already gave you.

And the midpoint theorem is the family's triangle shortcut: join two midpoints and the segment runs parallel to the third side at exactly half its length. Sixteen becomes eight, guaranteed, no ruler. When a question plants the word midpoint twice, it is showing its cards — the theorem or its converse opens the door.

Your questions for this section are ready — read the tree downwards for properties, upwards for proofs.

## Subtopic: Fair Fractions and the Overlap Fix

All of probability is honest counting, divided fairly. The probability of an event is the count of ways it happens over the count of everything possible — five red marbles among twenty, a quarter. Before the experiment, that fraction is theory. After the experiment, the tally gives relative frequency — and the longer the experiment runs, the closer practice leans towards theory. Twenty draws can look chaotic; two hundred settle into shape.

The one place honest counting fails is the overlap. Count the hockey players, count the tennis players, add — and every double-sport player has been counted twice, once on each list. The repair is one subtraction: add the two counts, subtract the overlap once. Twenty-two plus fifteen minus nine: twenty-eight, in a class of thirty-six, leaving eight outside both circles. The Venn diagram is that arithmetic drawn as circles, and its golden rule reads: fill the overlap FIRST, work outwards, and let the outside region catch whoever remains.

Two special layouts finish the picture. Circles that cannot overlap — events that never happen together — are mutually exclusive, and their probabilities add cleanly. Circles that split the whole rectangle into inside and outside — an event and its opposite — are complementary, and the outside is one minus the inside: a delayed bus at nought comma two means on time at nought comma eight. Complementary is the stronger claim: no overlap AND nothing left uncovered.

Here are this section's questions — count, subtract the overlap once, and let the outside region catch the rest.

## Subtopic: Reading the Box and the Last Patrol

Every data set tells a story, and the five-number summary is its plot: where it begins, where the middle half opens, the midpoint, where the middle half closes, where it ends. The box plot draws that plot on one line — box for the middle half, whiskers for the tails — so two data sets compare at a glance: whose box sits higher, whose stretches wider, whose whisker reaches suspiciously far.

Choosing the right average is part of the storytelling. The mean listens to every value — democratic, but one enormous salary in the room drags the average into fantasy. The median stands at the middle position and ignores how extreme the extremes are — the honest centre for skewed data. When the mean drifts above the median, the story leans right; below, it leans left. State which average you chose and why: that sentence is frequently a mark on its own.

The order of operations for any statistics question: ORDER the data first — a quartile computed on an unordered list is wrong before it begins. Then locate the median, then the quartile of each half, then draw. The interquartile range — upper quartile minus lower — measures the corridor housing the middle half, untouchable by outliers at either end.

The last patrol — four traps, four antidotes. Proving too much in a quadrilateral question: pay the ticket price and let inheritance deliver the rest. Rise and run inverted: gradient is change in y OVER change in x, without exception. The overlap added twice: subtract it once, and audit the total against the class size — thirty-seven out of thirty-six is a question marking itself wrong. Quartiles from an unordered list: order first, always. And the closing habit that turns near-marks into marks: check totals — Venn regions must sum to the class, the five numbers must march in increasing order, and a midpoint must land between its endpoints. Every picture in this lesson audits itself, free of charge.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — order the data, count without counting twice, and make every total confess.
