# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the revision sweep for three topics the other revision sessions leave untouched — number patterns, finance, and measurement. Each runs on a tiny engine: patterns on one constant difference, finance on two growth formulas, measurement on a short catalogue of solids and one scale-factor law. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day edition — compressed, worked through fresh examples, and wired to the traps that quietly claim marks every year.

## Subtopic: The Constant Difference and Term n

A linear number pattern identifies itself by one signature: a constant difference between consecutive terms. Take six; ten; fourteen; eighteen. The gaps are four, four, four — constant, so the pattern is linear, and that constant difference is the sequence's entire personality.

The general term follows in two moves. The coefficient of n is the constant difference itself, so the formula opens as four n plus something. To find the something, test the first term: when n is one, four times one is four, and the pattern says six — so add two. General term: T n equals four n plus two. Check it against a term the construction never used: n equals four gives sixteen plus two, eighteen. Correct — and that check is not politeness; it is the small proof that rescues the whole question.

Now the two exam directions. Direction one, forwards: find term forty. Substitute: four times forty plus two — one hundred and sixty-two. No listing, no finger counting; the formula is a teleport. Direction two, backwards: is four hundred and two a term of this pattern? Set four n plus two equal to four hundred and two: four n equals four hundred, n equals one hundred. A natural number — yes, it is term one hundred. Ask the same about one hundred and thirty-six: four n plus two equals one hundred and thirty-six gives four n equals one hundred and thirty-four, and n is thirty-three comma five — not a natural number, so one hundred and thirty-six is NOT in the pattern. The position n must land on a natural number; a fraction is the pattern's polite refusal.

One more for fluency: nine; sixteen; twenty-three. Difference seven, so T n equals seven n plus two, and term twenty is one hundred and forty-two.

This section's questions are with you now — build the formula from the difference, and test it before trusting it.

## Subtopic: Money Machines — Simple, Compound and Currency

Two formulas run all of Grade 10 finance. Simple growth: A equals P, times, one plus i n — interest computed on the original amount only, the same flat instalment of interest every year. Compound growth: A equals P, times, one plus i, all to the power n — interest computed on the growing total, so the interest itself starts earning.

Watch them race. Three thousand rand at seven percent for four years. Simple: three thousand times, one plus nought comma two eight — three thousand eight hundred and forty rand. Compound: three thousand times one comma zero seven to the fourth — three thousand nine hundred and thirty-two rand and thirty-nine cents. Same deposit, same rate, same years — the snowball outruns the flat instalments, and the gap widens with every extra year.

Hire purchase is simple interest in shop clothing. A sound system costs eight thousand rand. Deposit: ten percent, eight hundred rand. The balance, seven thousand two hundred, is charged SIMPLE interest at ten percent for the full two years: seven thousand two hundred times, one plus nought comma two — eight thousand six hundred and forty rand. Monthly payment: divide by twenty-four months — three hundred and sixty rand per month. The trap: hire purchase is always simple interest, always on the balance after the deposit, and the interest runs across the whole term even while the debt is shrinking.

Inflation is the compound formula in costume: prices grow at the inflation rate. An item at two hundred and fifty rand under five percent inflation costs, in two years, two hundred and fifty times one comma zero five squared — two hundred and seventy-five rand and sixty-three cents. Same machine, different story.

Exchange rates: the rate is the price of one unit of the other currency. If one pound costs twenty-three rand forty, then one hundred and fifty pounds cost three thousand five hundred and ten rand. When the rand weakens, that price per pound climbs and imports hurt; when the rand strengthens, the price falls. Read the rate as a price tag and the direction sorts itself out.

Take this section's questions now — pick the formula by the story, and keep the snowball and the flat instalments apart.

## Subtopic: The Solid Catalogue — Fetch the Right Pair

Measurement is a catalogue exercise: identify the solid, fetch its two formulas, substitute cleanly. The catalogue, with one worked example each.

The rectangular prism: volume is length times breadth times height — six by two by five gives sixty cubic units. Surface area: two times, the sum of the three different faces — two times, twelve plus thirty plus ten — one hundred and four square units.

The cylinder: volume is pi r squared h — a circle of area pi r squared, stacked h high. Radius two, height seven: twenty-eight pi, about eighty-seven comma nine six cubic units. Closed surface area: two pi r squared for the lids plus two pi r h for the label — together two pi r, times, r plus h: thirty-six pi, about one hundred and thirteen comma one zero.

The sphere: volume four thirds pi r cubed. Radius three: four thirds of pi times twenty-seven — thirty-six pi, about one hundred and thirteen comma one zero. Surface area: four pi r squared.

The cone: one third of its matching cylinder — volume one third pi r squared h. Radius six, height eight: ninety-six pi, about three hundred and one comma five nine. Its surface demands the SLANT height: by Pythagoras, the slant is the square root of thirty-six plus sixty-four — ten. Total surface: pi r, times, r plus slant — pi times six times sixteen, ninety-six pi — the same digits as the volume, in square units this time.

The pyramid: also one third — volume one third, base area, times perpendicular height. Square base of side eight, height three: one third of sixty-four times three — sixty-four. Surface area: the base plus the triangular faces, and the triangles use the slant height five — from half the base edge four and the height three: sixty-four plus four triangles of a half times eight times five — sixty-four plus eighty, one hundred and forty-four.

Two catalogue rules that guard marks. Perpendicular height in the volume, slant height on the surface — mixing them is the classic cone-and-pyramid error. And the one third belongs to cones and pyramids only; prisms and cylinders run at full strength.

This section's questions are ready — name the solid, fetch its pair, and keep the two heights apart.

## Subtopic: Powers of k and Questions That Chain

Multiply every length of a solid by k, and the three kinds of measurement respond in ranks: lengths multiply by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed. One dimension per power — that is the whole law.

Feel its force with k equals four. A solid of volume twenty becomes twenty times sixty-four — one thousand two hundred and eighty. Not four times bigger: sixty-four times. And the reverse trap: doubling every side of a box does not double the volume; it multiplies it by eight.

Partial scaling is the setter's favourite twist. Double ONLY the radius of a cylinder: volume is pi r squared h, and the radius appears squared, so the volume multiplies by four — not eight, because the height never moved. Read the law letter by letter: each scaled length contributes its own factor, once for every appearance in the formula.

Now the integrated question, where the paper chains topics. A cylindrical water tank, radius two metres, height seven metres, holds twenty-eight pi — about eighty-seven comma nine six cubic metres. Its replacement price climbs with inflation; its imported pump is priced through an exchange rate; a row of such tanks builds a number pattern in the total capacity. One question, three topics — and each topic keeps its own engine: the difference for patterns, the two growth formulas for money, the catalogue for solids.

The revision error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: reading a non-natural n as a yes — a fractional position means NOT in the pattern. Exhibit two: compound interest computed as simple, the snowball flattened into instalments. Exhibit three: slant height inside a volume formula. Exhibit four: doubling lengths and doubling the volume — forgetting that volume answers with the cube. Every exhibit costs marks each year — except to the candidates who rehearsed them.

The final questions of this part are with you now — powers matched to dimensions, and all four exhibits refused.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same revision now runs as three small machines — a row of lampposts, two ways for money to grow, and a stacking yard — with the levers that drive each one.

## Subtopic: Lampposts on a Straight Road

Picture a linear pattern as lampposts along a dead-straight road, planted at equal spacing. Six; ten; fourteen; eighteen — lampposts every four metres. The equal spacing IS the pattern; everything else is bookkeeping.

The general term is just the road's addressing system: to reach lamppost n, start at the depot and walk n gaps of four. Where is the depot? One gap before the first lamppost: six minus four is two. So the position of lamppost n is four n plus two — the depot plus n gaps. That is why the formula test always works: lamppost one should stand at four plus two, six. It does.

Forwards questions drive down the road: lamppost forty stands at one hundred and sixty-two, no walking required. Backwards questions ask which lamppost stands at a given position: set the formula equal to the position and solve for n. Position four hundred and two: n is one hundred — lamppost one hundred exists, so yes. Position one hundred and thirty-six: n arrives as thirty-three comma five — and no road has a lamppost thirty-three and a half. A fractional lamppost number means the position falls between posts: not in the pattern.

Every linear pattern question is one of those three: find the spacing, drive forwards, or check whether an address lands on a post. Name your question before computing.

Your questions for this section are ready — find the spacing, find the depot, then drive the formula.

## Subtopic: The Flat Fee and the Snowball

Simple and compound growth are two ways for money to climb. Simple interest is a flat fee repeated: every year adds the identical amount, because the interest is always charged on the original deposit. Compound interest is a snowball on a slope: each year's interest joins the ball, and next year's interest grows on the bigger ball.

Three thousand rand, seven percent, four years. The flat fee adds two hundred and ten rand each year — four fees, eight hundred and forty rand, finishing at three thousand eight hundred and forty. The snowball finishes at three thousand nine hundred and thirty-two rand and thirty-nine cents — ahead by nearly a hundred rand, and stretching its lead every further year. Same rate, different machines.

Hire purchase is the flat fee with a shop sign. Deposit first — ten percent of the eight thousand rand sound system is eight hundred. The remaining seven thousand two hundred pays the SIMPLE flat fee at ten percent for the whole two years, reaching eight thousand six hundred and forty, and the monthly payment shares that total across twenty-four months: three hundred and sixty rand. The formula never changes; only the story around it does.

Inflation is the snowball rolling against you: prices ride the compound formula uphill, so two hundred and fifty rand of shopping becomes two hundred and seventy-five rand and sixty-three cents after two years at five percent. And an exchange rate is nothing but a price tag on one unit of foreign money: one pound at twenty-three rand forty means one hundred and fifty pounds cost three thousand five hundred and ten rand — price times quantity, like buying oranges.

The reading skill: the words interest on the original amount, hire purchase, or straight growth name the flat fee; the words compounded, inflation, or interest on the new total name the snowball. Choose the machine before touching a number.

Here are this section's questions — flat fee or snowball first, arithmetic second.

## Subtopic: Stack, Wrap and the Closing Patrol

Every volume in the catalogue is one idea: area of the base, times how high you stack it. A prism stacks a rectangle; a cylinder stacks a circle — pi r squared, stacked h high. Cones and pyramids are the pointed cousins, and pointed costs two thirds: each holds exactly one third of the box or tin it fits inside. The sphere stands apart with four thirds pi r cubed.

Surface area asks a different question: not what the solid holds, but what it wears. Unfold the solid and add the faces — two lids and a label for the tin, a base plus triangles for the pyramid. The triangles lean, so they use the SLANT height, while volume always takes the vertical, perpendicular height. Two heights, two duties: the inside height fills, the leaning height wraps.

Then the scale factor, the one law that multiplies everything: lengths by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed — one factor of k per dimension. A box doubled in every direction holds eight times as much; a poster enlarged three times covers nine times the paper. And when only ONE length changes, count that letter's appearances in the formula: double a cylinder's radius and the volume quadruples, because r appears squared while h stayed home.

The closing patrol — four traps, four antidotes. A fractional position n: the number is not in the pattern; say so and move on. Flat-fee arithmetic in a snowball question: circle the word compounded before starting. Slant height in a volume: whisper inside height fills, leaning height wraps. A scale factor applied once to a volume: match the power to the dimension — square for area, cube for volume. And the final habit that converts near-marks into marks: test the general term on a known term, recompute one interest line, and sense-check every volume against the box it fits inside. Every answer carries its own free proof.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — stack the base, wrap the faces, and give every power its dimension.
