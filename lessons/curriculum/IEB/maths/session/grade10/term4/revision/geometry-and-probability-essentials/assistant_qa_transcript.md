**Thandi:** Four topics in one revision session invites four sets of questions, so let's move fast. First: the quadrilateral proofs. Students prove five properties when one would do. Why is proving MORE actually a problem?

**Tutor:** Because in an examination, time is the currency and over-proving is paying five times for a one-rand ticket. The logic of the family tree is that each certification unlocks everything inherited: prove the diagonals bisect each other and the shape IS a parallelogram — opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, all of it now guaranteed by the theorem, not by your labour. Prove those extra properties by hand and you earn no extra marks, but you spend minutes the last question needed. There is also a subtler danger: candidates who over-prove often UNDER-prove the one thing that matters. They show four properties of a rectangle but never the entry ticket — the right angle or the equal diagonals attached to an established parallelogram — and the chain of logic has a missing link where the mark lived. So the discipline runs: name the target shape; ask what its minimum test is; prove exactly that; cite inheritance for the rest. One more caution from the museum: the test must be a genuine certificate. Equal diagonals ALONE prove nothing — an isosceles trapezium has them too. The ticket must be one of the recognised minimum tests, quoted precisely.

**Thandi:** On the coordinate work: three formulas, and yet marks leak everywhere. Where exactly do the leaks spring?

**Tutor:** Three leaks account for nearly everything, and each has a one-line seal. Leak one: sign errors inside the differences. The distance formula squares the differences, so a slipped sign hides there — but in the gradient it does not: change in y over change in x must take BOTH differences in the same direction, B minus A on top and B minus A below. Mix directions and the gradient flips sign silently. Seal: write the subtraction order once, then reuse it mechanically for both differences. Leak two: the inverted gradient — run over rise instead of rise over run. Twelve over five became five over twelfths in someone's rush, and every parallel or perpendicular deduction afterwards inherits the damage. Seal: say the words as you write — change in y, OVER, change in x. Leak three: the perpendicular machine applied halfway — flipping without negating, or negating without flipping. The partner of twelve fifths is minus five twelfths; check by multiplying, twelve fifths times minus five twelfths is minus one, and if that product is not minus one, the machine misfired. Notice each seal is a check you run yourself in seconds: fixed subtraction order, spoken formula, product equals minus one. Analytical geometry is the most self-checkable topic on the paper — use that.

**Thandi:** Probability next: the addition rule works until the question says 'mutually exclusive' or 'complementary', and then everyone freezes. How should those words be read?

**Tutor:** Read them as statements about the PICTURE, and the freeze thaws. The general law is one sentence: P of A or B equals P of A plus P of B minus the overlap. It always holds — memorise nothing else. Mutually exclusive is the special picture where the circles cannot touch: no member of one event can belong to the other, like drawing a red marble and drawing a green marble on the same single draw. Overlap zero, so the law COLLAPSES to plain addition — nothing new, just a term vanishing. Complementary is the stronger picture: the two regions not only avoid overlapping, they tile the entire rectangle — event and opposite, delayed and on time. That strength buys the shortcut P of not A equals one minus P of A, which is often the fastest route to an answer: the probability of at least one of something is frequently one minus the probability of none. So when the words appear, translate: mutually exclusive, drop the overlap term; complementary, drop the overlap AND use one-minus. And when no such word appears, assume overlap exists and hunt for it — the question that gives you both counts and the both-count is begging for the subtraction. The rule never changes; only the picture does.

**Thandi:** Last one: the statistics. Mean versus median — students can compute both but stall when asked which one to USE. What decides it?

**Tutor:** The shape of the data decides, and the question usually whispers the shape at you. The mean is the democratic centre: every value votes, which makes it ideal for tight, symmetric data — test scores clustered in the sixties, daily temperatures across a week. But democracy has a flaw: one extreme voter shouts louder than everyone. Put a single salary of a hundred thousand into a room of five-thousand-rand earners and the mean reports a prosperous room that does not exist. The median is the positional centre: line everyone up and take the middle person. Extremes can move to the horizon and the middle person does not shift — which is why house prices, incomes and any long-tailed data report medians in serious publications. So the decision procedure: look for outliers or a long whisker on the box plot. Clean and symmetric — mean, and say it uses all the information. Skewed or outlier-ridden — median, and say it resists the extremes. Then use the pair as a diagnostic, the way our worked set did: mean twelve above median eleven means a right lean; mean below median, a left lean; mean equal to median, symmetry. And write the reason in a sentence — the choice justified is where the mark sits, not the arithmetic.
