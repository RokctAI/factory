# Part 1 — Expert

This session tackles the question setters prize because it rewards understanding over arithmetic: what happens to volume and surface area when a dimension is multiplied by a constant factor k? Two tasks carry the hour. First, a box five by two by three has its breadth doubled, then all three dimensions doubled — predict both new volumes and surface areas without rebuilding anything from scratch. Second, reverse gear: a container's volume grew sixty-four times — what factor stretched its dimensions? By the end, k, k squared and k cubed will each know exactly where they belong.

## Subtopic: One Dimension Stretched — the Volume Tracks It

Begin with the box: five by two by three. Volume: five times two times three, thirty cubic units. Surface area from the net: two times, bracket, ten plus fifteen plus six, close bracket — sixty-two square units.

Now multiply the breadth alone by k equals two. The box becomes five by four by three. New volume: five times four times three, sixty — precisely double the original thirty. That is no coincidence. Volume is a product of three dimensions, and the breadth sits in that product exactly once. Double one factor of a product and the entire product doubles. In symbols: length times two b times height equals two times the original volume. The rule: multiply any single dimension by k and the volume multiplies by k — as long as that dimension appears once in the formula.

Surface area declines to behave. The new box, five by four by three, has surface area two times, bracket, twenty plus fifteen plus twelve, close bracket — ninety-four. The original was sixty-two, and ninety-four is nowhere near double. The reason lives in the formula: the breadth features in some terms and not in others. The length-by-height faces, five by three, never felt the stretch, while the other four faces doubled. Part of the total scales, part stays frozen, so the whole scales by no tidy factor. The practical consequence: for a single-dimension change, predict volume with the k rule, but recompute surface area honestly from the new measurements.

This section's questions are with you now — the stretched box verified both ways, and the reason surface area broke ranks explained in full.

## Subtopic: Why the Radius Pays Double

Enter the cylinder, and the twist that separates the top candidates from the pack. Take the paint tin from the previous session: radius four, height fifteen, volume pi r squared h — two hundred and forty pi.

Double the height and leave the radius alone: volume becomes pi times sixteen times thirty, four hundred and eighty pi. Exactly twice the original. Height appears once in the formula, so k on the height puts k on the volume — consistent with the box.

Now double the radius instead, keeping the height: the new volume is pi times eight squared times fifteen — pi times sixty-four times fifteen, nine hundred and sixty pi. That is FOUR times two hundred and forty pi, not two. The formula explains everything: the radius is squared, so it enters the product twice. Multiply the radius by k and the volume multiplies by k squared. Triple the radius and the volume grows nine times over: pi times twelve squared times fifteen is two thousand one hundred and sixty pi, and that is nine times the original.

The governing principle, worth a line in your summary notes: k strikes the volume once for every appearance of the scaled dimension in the formula. Height in pi r squared h: one appearance, so k. Radius: two appearances, so k squared. It is why the large pizza, only a few centimetres wider than the regular, holds so much more food — width is charged twice.

Questions on this idea are usually conceptual: by what factor does a cylinder's volume grow if the radius is multiplied by three? No calculator required — nine, because the radius pays double. State the reason and collect the marks.

Work this section's questions now: height against radius, k against k squared, and the principle quoted with its justification.

## Subtopic: Enlarging Everything — the k Squared and k Cubed Laws

The headline case: every dimension multiplied by the same k. This is enlargement proper — the solid keeps its shape and changes only its size, like an architect's model beside the finished building.

Return to the box, five by two by three, all dimensions doubled: ten by four by six. New volume: ten times four times six, two hundred and forty. Original: thirty. Ratio: eight — which is two cubed. Each of the three dimensions delivered one factor of two, and two times two times two is eight. Multiply every dimension by k and the volume multiplies by k cubed — always, for absolutely any solid: box, cylinder, cone, or an irregular sculpture. Volume lives in three dimensions, so it gathers three copies of k.

This time surface area IS predictable — because now every face scaled identically. New surface area: two times, bracket, forty plus sixty plus twenty-four, close bracket — two hundred and forty-eight. Original: sixty-two. Ratio: four, which is k squared. Every face is a flat two-dimensional patch collecting two copies of k. Multiply every dimension by k and surface area multiplies by k squared — again, for any solid whatsoever.

The full pattern deserves its complete sentence: lengths scale by k, areas by k squared, volumes by k cubed. One, two, three copies of k for one, two, three dimensions. That single line explains why an ant hoists many times its body weight while an elephant cannot, and why a small pot boils dry faster than a large one: as k grows, volume sprints ahead of area — k cubed outruns k squared.

Percentages obey the same law. Enlarge every dimension by twenty percent: k is one comma two. Volume scales by one comma two cubed — one comma seven two eight — a seventy-two comma eight percent increase, not sixty. Surface area scales by one comma two squared — one comma four four — up forty-four percent.

## Subtopic: Backwards From the Multiplier to k

Question setters love running this machine in reverse: they announce the change in volume or area and demand k.

Task: a container is enlarged so its volume becomes sixty-four times bigger. What happened to the dimensions? The relationship is k cubed equals sixty-four, so k is the cube root of sixty-four — four. Every length quadrupled. And a follow-up question answers itself: the surface area multiplied by k squared, sixteen.

Another: a spherical balloon's surface area becomes nine times bigger. What happened to the radius, and to the volume? Area law: k squared equals nine, so k equals three — the radius tripled. Volume law: k cubed equals twenty-seven — the volume is twenty-seven times larger. Watch the flow: from the given quantity, extract k first; from k, predict all the rest. Never leap straight from an area change to a volume change without landing on k in between.

The choice of root matters. Volume information: cube root to reach k. Area information: square root. A plain length change: no root at all. And keep a sense-check running: k should be a reasonable scale — if the question says enlarged and your k is nought comma five, you have computed a shrinking; go back to the division.

The error museum for this topic, three exhibits. Exhibit one: doubling all dimensions and declaring the volume doubled — it multiplies by eight; the ant and the elephant forbid the shortcut. Exhibit two: applying k cubed to a single-dimension stretch — one moving dimension puts only one k on the volume. Exhibit three: adding percentages — twenty percent on each dimension is not sixty percent on volume; it is one comma two cubed, seventy-two comma eight percent, because scale factors multiply and never add.

The complete method: count the appearances of the scaled dimension — once, twice, or all three; raise k to that power; run the root backwards when the change is what you are given; and recompute surface area honestly whenever only one dimension moved.

This section's questions are with you now — reverse gear, both roots, and every museum exhibit named on sight.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole lesson again, now through two objects from the kitchen: a matchbox and a juice carton. Same laws, and no formulas until the final minute.

## Subtopic: Eight Matchboxes Build the Double Matchbox

Picture a matchbox: five long, two wide, three high, centimetres if you like. Its volume, thirty, counts the little unit cubes that fit inside — five in a row, two rows, three layers.

Make the box twice as wide and change nothing else. You have simply parked a second matchbox beside the first: two rows where there was one. Twice the cubes, twice the volume — sixty. Stretching ONE direction by two doubles the count, because the other two directions never moved. That is the whole single-dimension rule: the volume follows the one direction you stretched.

Now make the matchbox twice as big in EVERY direction. Here is the picture that installs the rule permanently: to assemble that double-size matchbox out of ordinary matchboxes, you need two along the length, two across the width, and two up the height. Two times two times two — eight matchboxes. Not two, not four: eight. Double everything and volume multiplies by eight, which is two cubed. Triple everything and you would need three by three by three — twenty-seven boxes. The multiplier is always k times k times k, one k for each direction of space.

The favourite trap declares: the dimensions doubled, so the volume doubled. You now hold the picture that destroys the trap — nobody has ever built a double-size matchbox from two matchboxes. It takes eight.

Your questions for this section are ready — count matchboxes, not formulas.

## Subtopic: Wrapping Grows Slower Than Juice

Same carton, two different questions. How much juice fills the inside? That is volume. How much paper wraps the outside? That is surface area. Scaling treats the two differently, and the difference sets real prices in the real world.

Double the carton in every direction. The juice inside multiplies by eight — two cubed, as the matchbox count proved. But the wrapping outside multiplies by only four — two squared. Why? Every face of the carton is a flat patch with just two directions of its own, length and width. Each doubles, so each face grows four times: two times two. A flat face has no third direction to collect, so it gathers only two copies of k.

Hence the slogan: wrapping scales by k squared, juice scales by k cubed. Areas are two-dimensional, volumes are three-dimensional, and every dimension claims exactly one copy of k.

Watch the gap widen. Double size: juice times eight, wrapping times four. Triple size: juice times twenty-seven, wrapping times nine. The greater the enlargement, the further volume pulls ahead of area. That is why a giant storage tank holds enormously more than it costs extra to coat, and why a tiny insect — nearly all surface, hardly any volume — steps away unhurt from a fall that would injure anything larger.

And the twenty percent question in this language: growing every side by twenty percent makes k one comma two. Wrapping: one comma two squared, one comma four four — forty-four percent more paper. Juice: one comma two cubed, one comma seven two eight — seventy-two comma eight percent more juice. Percentages ride the powers of the scale factor; they never simply add.

Here are this section's questions — before each one, decide: is this a wrapping question or a juice question?

## Subtopic: Reverse Questions Without Panic

Questions often hand you the ending and request the beginning: the volume became one hundred and twenty-five times bigger — what happened to the sides? Reverse questions look harder, but they are the same matchbox wall walked in the opposite direction.

The routine is three steps. Step one: name the quantity that changed. Volume changed? Its multiplier is k cubed. Area changed? k squared. A plain length? Just k. Step two: undo the power. Volume times one hundred and twenty-five: k cubed is one hundred and twenty-five, so k is the cube root — five; every side is five times longer. Area times sixteen: k squared is sixteen, so k is four. Step three: deploy the k you found on whatever else is asked. Sides times five means area times five squared, twenty-five; means any length, like a diagonal, times five.

Run the full chain once. A water tank is scaled up and now holds one hundred and twenty-five times more water. How much more coating does the outside need? Volume information first: k cubed is one hundred and twenty-five, so k is five. Then the coating: k squared is twenty-five. Twenty-five times the coating — and notice there is no road from one hundred and twenty-five to twenty-five that does not pass through k. The k in the middle is the bridge, and every reverse question crosses it.

Two sense checks to close. A k smaller than one announces shrinking — acceptable if the question said reduced, an alarm if it said enlarged. And the multipliers must sit in the correct order: for any enlargement with k above one, the volume multiplier is always the bigger number — if your wrapping figure beat your juice figure, the powers were swapped somewhere.

The entire lesson is one sentence: one k per dimension — once for lengths, twice for areas, three times for volumes, with roots to travel backwards. The final questions are with you now; cross the bridge at k every single time.
