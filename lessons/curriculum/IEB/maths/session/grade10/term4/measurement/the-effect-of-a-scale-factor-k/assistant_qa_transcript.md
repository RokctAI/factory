**Thandi:** Let's dig into the questions this topic keeps generating. First: the doubling trap. Doubling every side of a box feels like it should double the volume. Why is our intuition so wrong here?

**Tutor:** Because everyday intuition is trained on lengths, and volume is not a length — it is three lengths multiplied together. When you double every side, each of the three directions hands the product its own factor of two, and the factors multiply: two times two times two, eight. The matchbox construction makes it undeniable: lay two boxes along the length, two across the width, two up the height, and count what you used — eight boxes to build one double-size box. Nobody has ever done it with two. Intuition fails because we picture the enlargement edge by edge and forget the compounding. The same failure runs the other way at the shops: a bottle that looks only slightly bigger holds substantially more, because every one of its dimensions grew a little and the growths multiplied. So the defence is never to trust the feeling — count the appearances of k. Three dimensions moved, three copies of k, k cubed. If the feeling and the rule disagree, the rule wins, every time.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the cylinder. Students accept that doubling the height doubles the volume, but the radius quadrupling it feels unfair. What makes the radius so special?

**Tutor:** The radius is not special — the formula just uses it twice, and honesty demands we charge it twice. Look at pi r squared h and read it as a product of appearances: one r, another r, one h. Double the height and you have doubled one factor: the volume doubles, four hundred and eighty pi from our two hundred and forty. Double the radius and you have doubled two factors simultaneously: two times two, and the volume quadruples to nine hundred and sixty pi. There is a physical picture underneath the algebra: widening a cylinder grows the circular floor in two directions at once — across and around — while raising it grows the stack in only one. That is the pizza economics hiding in plain sight: the large pizza a few centimetres wider than the regular is dramatically more food, because width pays twice on a flat disc. The transferable rule for any formula: before predicting a scaling effect, count how many times the changed letter appears — squared means twice, so k squared; cubed would mean three times. The count is the whole answer.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the percentage trap: every side grows by twenty percent, and half the class writes sixty percent more volume. Where does the extra twelve comma eight come from?

**Tutor:** From compounding — the same force that makes interest grow faster than expected. Twenty percent growth means every length is multiplied by one comma two, and scale factors do not add; they multiply through the powers. Volume collects three copies: one comma two times one comma two times one comma two, which is one comma seven two eight — a seventy-two comma eight percent increase. The sixty percent answer comes from adding twenty three times, and adding is simply the wrong operation for scaling. Walk the multiplication and watch the surplus appear: one comma two squared is one comma four four, already forty-four percent rather than forty; the third factor lifts it to one comma seven two eight. That extra twelve comma eight percent is growth acting on growth — the second stretch acts on an already-stretched object, the third on that. The safe procedure is mechanical: convert the percentage to k first, one plus twenty over one hundred; raise k to the power the quantity demands, squared for area, cubed for volume; convert back to a percentage at the end, and only at the end. Any answer obtained by adding percentages across dimensions is wrong before you even check it.

**Thandi:** Last one: reverse questions. Volume became sixty-four times bigger, and students immediately want the area answer — some just halve the sixty-four. What is the reliable route?

**Tutor:** The reliable route always passes through k, and the halving instinct shows what happens when it does not. Sixty-four is a volume multiplier, so it equals k cubed — information about k, wrapped in a cube. Unwrap it first: k is the cube root of sixty-four, which is four; every length quadrupled. Only now predict the area: k squared, sixteen. Compare the wrong road: halving sixty-four gives thirty-two, and there is no power of four anywhere near thirty-two — the error is not small, it is structural. Think of k as the border post between the three quantities: length information, area information and volume information are three different countries, and every journey between them clears customs at k. Given volume, cube root down to k; given area, square root down to k; then raise k to whatever power the destination requires. Two sense checks seal the working: an enlarged object must give k above one, and the volume multiplier must always beat the area multiplier when k exceeds one — sixty-four against sixteen passes both. If either check fails, the powers got swapped, and the checks catch it before the marker does.
