# Part 1 — Expert

This session closes out the Grade 10 measurement toolkit: spheres, right pyramids, right cones, and the combined solids that questions assemble from them. Two anchor tasks run through the hour. First, a cone of radius five centimetres and perpendicular height twelve centimetres — its volume and total surface area, exact and rounded. Second, a spinning-top shape: a cone of radius three and height four with a hemisphere of radius three sealed onto its base — total volume, and the outside surface area. The entire topic stands on one new number, one third, and one trusted companion, Pythagoras.

## Subtopic: One Third of the Box — Volumes of Pyramids and Cones

A right pyramid is a solid with a flat base — square, at this grade — rising to a single point, the apex, directly above the centre of the base. A right cone tells the same story on a circular base. Neither is a stack of identical layers, so base times height overshoots: the layers shrink on the way up. The correction is exact and elegant: a pyramid or cone occupies precisely one third of the prism or cylinder sharing its base and height. Volume equals one third times base area times perpendicular height.

That one third is not folklore — it is a laboratory fact. Take a hollow cone and a hollow cylinder of matching base and height, fill the cone with water, and pour. Three pours fill the cylinder. Always exactly three.

Run the square pyramid: base edge twelve centimetres, perpendicular height eight. Base area: one hundred and forty-four. Volume: one third times one hundred and forty-four times eight — three hundred and eighty-four cubic centimetres.

Now the cone: radius five, perpendicular height twelve. Base area: pi times twenty-five. Volume: one third times twenty-five pi times twelve — one hundred pi cubic centimetres, which rounds to 314,16.

One piece of vocabulary discipline before the questions: the perpendicular height is the vertical drop from the apex straight to the centre of the base. It is not the sloping distance down the side. Volume formulas demand the perpendicular height, every time. The sloping distance has its own name and its own duties, and it takes over the next section.

This section's questions are with you now — the one-third fact, both volumes, and the correct height fed to the formula.

## Subtopic: Manufacturing the Slant Height with Pythagoras

Surface area of a pyramid or cone is a story about the sloping exterior, and that exterior is measured by the slant height, usually written as a small s. For a cone, s runs from the apex down the sloping surface to the rim of the circle. For a square pyramid, s is the height of one triangular face — apex to the midpoint of a base edge.

Here is the move that marks out a prepared candidate: the slant height is almost never handed over. Questions supply the perpendicular height and expect you to manufacture s using Pythagoras. Inside every cone hides a right-angled triangle: perpendicular height standing upright, radius lying flat, slant height as the hypotenuse. So s squared equals h squared plus r squared. Our cone: twelve squared plus five squared — one hundred and forty-four plus twenty-five, one hundred and sixty-nine. Slant height: thirteen. For the square pyramid the horizontal leg is half the base edge, centre of the base to the midpoint of a side: s squared equals eight squared plus six squared — sixty-four plus thirty-six, one hundred. Slant height: ten.

With s manufactured, the surface formulas build themselves from flat pieces. The cone unrolls into its circular base, pi r squared, plus a curved sector of area pi times r times s. Total: pi r squared plus pi r s. Our cone: twenty-five pi plus pi times five times thirteen — twenty-five pi plus sixty-five pi, ninety pi square centimetres, 282,74 when rounded.

The pyramid: a square base of one hundred and forty-four, plus four triangular faces of half times base edge times slant height — half times twelve times ten, sixty each — two hundred and forty for the four. Total: one hundred and forty-four plus two hundred and forty, three hundred and eighty-four square centimetres. A pleasing coincidence in this particular pyramid: volume three hundred and eighty-four and surface three hundred and eighty-four — identical digits, different units, cubic for filling and square for wrapping.

Take this section's questions now — Pythagoras first, formulas second, and the perpendicular and slant heights never swapped again.

## Subtopic: Sphere Formulas — the Power of r Remembers

The sphere is the one solid in the syllabus that demands genuine memorisation, because it has no flat net and no base to reason from. Two formulas, both governed by the radius alone. Volume: four thirds pi r cubed. Surface area: four pi r squared.

An anchor that never rusts: volume is three-dimensional and its formula cubes the radius; surface is two-dimensional and its formula squares it. Blank in the moment, and the power of r will tell you which formula you are holding — cubed fills, squared wraps.

Worked example, radius three. Volume: four thirds times pi times three cubed. Three cubed is twenty-seven; four thirds of twenty-seven is thirty-six. Volume: thirty-six pi cubic centimetres — 113,10 rounded. Surface: four pi times nine — thirty-six pi square centimetres, also 113,10 rounded. Radius three is the one sphere whose volume and surface share their digits — a coincidence worth noticing, not a rule.

The favourite variation is the hemisphere, half a sphere, and it hides a trap worth shouting about. Volume halves cleanly: half of thirty-six pi is eighteen pi. Surface does NOT halve. Half the curved skin is eighteen pi — but slicing a sphere exposes a brand-new flat face, a circle of pi r squared, nine pi. A closed hemisphere therefore carries eighteen pi plus nine pi, twenty-seven pi. Whether the flat circle joins the bill depends entirely on the story: a solid half-melon, yes; a dome resting on a wall, no — its rim is attached, not exposed. Read the object, then decide.

This section's questions are ready — both formulas anchored to the power of r, the radius-three numbers, and the hemisphere's new face treated with respect.

## Subtopic: Glued Solids and the Buried-Face Audit

Now the finale questions love: solids glued from two shapes. A spinning top of cone and hemisphere, a silo of cylinder and cone, a capsule of cylinder and half-spheres. Two principles command every combination.

Principle one, for volume: glued solids simply add. The spinning top: cone of radius three and height four — one third times nine pi times four, twelve pi — plus hemisphere of radius three, eighteen pi. Total: thirty pi cubic centimetres — 94,25 rounded. Volume never notices the join.

Principle two, for surface area: count only the faces that survive on the OUTSIDE. At the join, one face from each solid vanishes into the glue — the cone's base circle and the hemisphere's flat circle are buried against each other. What remains exposed: the cone's curved surface, pi r s with slant height five from the three-four-five triangle, fifteen pi, plus the hemisphere's curved skin, eighteen pi. Total: thirty-three pi square centimetres — 103,67 rounded. Adding the two complete surface areas blindly would have smuggled in eighteen pi of buried circles, and poisoned the answer.

So the audit for any combined solid: sketch or imagine the join; list every face of each component; strike out the faces that touch; add the survivors. For volume, skip the audit entirely and add everything.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: a slant height inside a volume formula, or a perpendicular height inside pi r s — each formula owns its proper height. Exhibit two: buried faces counted in a glued solid. Exhibit three: the hemisphere's exposed circle forgotten — or billed when the story glued it to something. Exhibit four: the one third missing from a cone or pyramid volume — an answer three times too large, visible to a marker at a glance.

The complete method: name each simple solid inside the combined one; volumes add; surfaces add after the buried-face audit; Pythagoras manufactures any missing slant height; exact in pi, round last, unit checked.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the full spinning-top calculation, the buried faces struck out, and every museum exhibit recognised.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same solids again, now from the ground up — a party hat, a marquee and a netball, with numbers so friendly they nearly compute themselves.

## Subtopic: A Party Hat, a Marquee and a Netball

Three new shapes joined the family, so shake hands with them as objects first. The cone: a party hat, a funnel, the sharp end of a pencil. The square pyramid: a marquee tent with a square floor gathered up to a point. The sphere: a netball.

For the hat and the marquee, one fact runs the entire volume show: a pointy solid holds exactly one third of the box or can it stands inside. Picture the cone snug inside a cylinder sharing its floor and its height. Fill the cone with water and tip it in: three fills complete the cylinder — not about three, exactly three. So the volume recipe is the familiar one carrying a discount: base area times height, divided by three.

Try it gently. The marquee: square floor twelve by twelve, one hundred and forty-four; height eight; one hundred and forty-four times eight is one thousand one hundred and fifty-two; a third of that is three hundred and eighty-four. The hat: floor a circle of radius five, twenty-five pi; height twelve; twenty-five pi times twelve is three hundred pi; a third is one hundred pi.

The netball plays its own game — no flat floor, no net to unfold — so its two formulas are learned outright, with one trick to keep them apart: filling is three-dimensional, so the volume formula cubes the radius, four thirds pi r cubed; wrapping is two-dimensional, so the skin formula squares it, four pi r squared. Cubed fills, squared wraps — say it twice and it never leaves.

Your questions for this section are ready — three objects, one discount, and the powers of r doing the memory work.

## Subtopic: Raising the Grain Silo

Time to build something real: a grain silo — cylinder walls wearing a cone roof. Walls: radius two metres, three metres tall. Roof: a cone of the same radius, one comma five metres of perpendicular height from ceiling to peak.

Question one: how much grain fits inside? Glued solids add their volumes, join or no join. The walls are a cylinder: floor area pi times four, times height three — twelve pi. The roof is a cone: a third of pi times four times one comma five — two pi. Together: fourteen pi cubic metres, about 43,98. Done — volume questions on combined solids are pure addition.

Question two: how much outside needs weatherproofing — paint for the wall, sheeting for the roof? Now the join demands its audit. Walk around the silo and list only what a hand outside could touch. The wall's curved surface: circumference times height, two pi times two times three — twelve pi, about 37,70 square metres of paint. The roof's slope needs the slant height, peak straight down the sheeting to the eaves. Pythagoras inside the roof: height one comma five, radius two — the hypotenuse is two comma five, a three-four-five triangle in disguise. Sheeting area: pi times radius times slant, pi times two times two comma five — five pi, about 15,71 square metres.

Now tally everything we did NOT bill. The cone's base circle? Buried — it is the ceiling, resting on the walls. The cylinder's top circle? The same buried ceiling, seen from below. The floor? Concrete against the ground, and the question asked for paint and sheeting — the story decides. Combined-solid surface area is a walk around the outside, touching every face and asking: exposed, or buried?

Here are this section's questions — raise the silo again piece by piece, and let the story rule on every face.

## Subtopic: Four Traps — Heights, Hemispheres and Hidden Circles

The shapes are learned; what remains is sidestepping the four traps that harvest marks every summer.

Trap one: two different heights live inside every cone and pyramid. The perpendicular height stands vertically, apex to centre of base — it serves VOLUME. The slant height lies along the outer slope — it serves SURFACE AREA. They share one right-angled triangle with the radius, or half the base edge, as the third side, so Pythagoras converts either into the other on demand. Before any formula, touch the height in your mind's diagram and ask: standing inside, or lying on the slope?

Trap two: the hemisphere's extra face. Halving a sphere halves the volume, cleanly. But the cut manufactures a new flat circle, so a closed hemisphere's surface is half the skin PLUS pi r squared. Then consult the story — a dome keeps its rim attached to the building, so its circle stays off the bill.

Trap three: buried faces in glued solids. Where two solids meet, a face from each disappears. Volume ignores the join entirely; surface area must strike both buried faces before adding.

Trap four: the missing one third. A cone or pyramid volume without its one third is a cylinder or prism answer — three times too generous. If your pointy solid holds as much as the can it stands in, the discount fell off somewhere.

Run the closing checklist over the numbers you now own: cone one hundred pi and ninety pi, pyramid three hundred and eighty-four twice over, sphere thirty-six pi twice over, spinning top thirty pi and thirty-three pi, silo fourteen pi of grain with twelve pi of paint and five pi of sheeting. Reproduce those and the toolkit is finished — every solid in the Grade 10 syllabus now reports to you.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — four traps, five solids, and a checklist that misses nothing.
