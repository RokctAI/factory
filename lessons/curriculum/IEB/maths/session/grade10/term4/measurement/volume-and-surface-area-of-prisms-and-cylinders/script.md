# Part 1 — Expert

This session restores the measurement toolkit that end-of-year questions assume is ready: volume and surface area of right prisms and cylinders. Two worked solids anchor everything. First, a closed rectangular tea box measuring nine centimetres by four centimetres by ten centimetres — its volume, then its total surface area. Second, a closed cylindrical paint tin of radius four centimetres and height fifteen centimetres — the same two quantities, held exact in terms of pi and only rounded to two decimal places at the very end. One law governs both solids, and by the close of the session it will be yours permanently.

## Subtopic: Base Times Height — the Single Volume Law

A right prism is a solid whose flat base is repeated, unchanged, along its whole length: a rectangular box, a triangular chocolate bar, an octagonal pencil. A cylinder is the identical idea built on a circular base. Because the cross-section never varies, one volume law serves them all: volume equals the area of the base multiplied by the perpendicular height.

The reason is worth owning, not just the rule. Imagine slicing the solid into identical thin layers, like a deck of cards cut to the shape of the base. Every layer supplies the same floor area, and the height simply counts the layers. Floor area times number of layers — that is the entire content of the formula.

Apply it to the tea box. The base is a rectangle, nine by four, so the base area is thirty-six square centimetres. The height is ten. Volume: thirty-six times ten — three hundred and sixty cubic centimetres.

Now the paint tin. Its base is a circle of radius four, so the base area is pi times radius squared: sixteen pi. Multiply by the height of fifteen: two hundred and forty pi cubic centimetres exactly. Only now does the calculator appear: 753,98 cubic centimetres to two decimal places. Note the discipline in the order — exact value first, decimal last. Rounding pi early buries a small error that swells through every following line.

A caution before moving on: the height in the law is the perpendicular distance between the two identical faces — the direction in which the base was copied. A prism lying on its side keeps its repeated face; the formula's height may then run sideways across the page. Locate the twin faces first, and the height is the gap between them.

This section's questions are ready for you now — the layer argument in your own words, the box volume, and the tin volume kept exact until the last line.

## Subtopic: Nets and Surface Area — Adding Every Face

Surface area asks a different question altogether: not how much a solid holds, but how much material its outside needs. The dependable method is to unfold the solid into its net and total the faces one at a time.

The tea box opens into six rectangles arranged in three matching pairs. Top and bottom: nine by four, thirty-six each. Front and back: nine by ten, ninety each. The two ends: four by ten, forty each. Total surface area: two times the sum of thirty-six, ninety and forty. The bracket is one hundred and sixty-six; doubled, three hundred and thirty-two square centimetres. The formula says exactly the same: two times, bracket, length times breadth plus length times height plus breadth times height, close bracket.

The tin unfolds into three pieces, and the middle piece is the revelation. Top and bottom are circles: pi r squared each, sixteen pi apiece, thirty-two pi together. The curved wall, cut down its seam and laid flat, is a rectangle. Its height is the tin's height, fifteen. Its width is the distance once around the rim — the circumference, two pi r, which is eight pi. The curved surface is therefore two pi r times h: eight pi times fifteen, one hundred and twenty pi. Grand total: thirty-two pi plus one hundred and twenty pi — one hundred and fifty-two pi square centimetres exactly, which rounds to 477,52.

Keep the physical picture that makes the wall a rectangle: the paper wrapper on a real tin slides off flat, and its width wraps precisely once around the rim. That one image rebuilds two pi r h on demand, no memorising required.

And watch the units. Volume arrived in cubic centimetres; surface area arrives in square centimetres. Filling and covering are different jobs with different units, and an answer wearing the wrong unit is wrong even when its digits are perfect.

Take on this section's questions now: the box's three pairs matched correctly, the unrolled wall explained, and both totals rebuilt from scratch.

## Subtopic: Working a Triangular Prism End to End

Now a full exam-style solid whose base is not a rectangle. A triangular prism: the base is a right-angled triangle with shorter sides five and twelve centimetres and hypotenuse thirteen, and the prism is twenty centimetres long.

Volume first, the one law as always. Base area: half times base times perpendicular height of the triangle — half of five times twelve, thirty square centimetres. The prism repeats that triangle along its twenty-centimetre length. Volume: thirty times twenty, six hundred cubic centimetres.

Surface area from the net. The prism unfolds into two triangles and three rectangles. The triangles: thirty each, sixty together. Every rectangle has the prism's length, twenty, as one side and an edge of the triangle as the other: five by twenty is one hundred, twelve by twenty is two hundred and forty, thirteen by twenty is two hundred and sixty. And here is the professional shortcut: those three rectangles amount to the triangle's perimeter times the length — five plus twelve plus thirteen is thirty, times twenty is six hundred. Total surface area: sixty plus six hundred, six hundred and sixty square centimetres.

Two traps inhabit this solid. Trap one: dropping the half from the triangle's area — five times twelve is the surrounding rectangle, and the triangle is only half of it. Trap two: pressing the hypotenuse into service as the height. Perpendicular height pairs with the base it is perpendicular to; in a right-angled triangle the two legs form that pair, and the hypotenuse belongs to neither role.

The questions for this section are with you now — the half defended, the perimeter shortcut deployed, and both final numbers verified.

## Subtopic: Housekeeping — Units, Litres and Open Solids

More measurement marks disappear to housekeeping than to formulas, so housekeeping gets its own section.

First, capacity. One thousand cubic centimetres make one litre. The tea box's three hundred and sixty cubic centimetres is 0,36 litres — divide by one thousand. The tin's 753,98 cubic centimetres is about 0,75 litres. When a question asks how many litres a container holds, the cubic-centimetre volume is only the halfway point.

Second, mixed units. If a base is measured in centimetres and the height in metres, convert before any multiplication, never afterwards. A height of zero comma nine metres is ninety centimetres; feed the formula centimetres throughout and the volume emerges honestly in cubic centimetres.

Third, open versus closed. A tin without its lid loses one circle: surface area becomes one circle plus the wall — sixteen pi plus one hundred and twenty pi, one hundred and thirty-six pi. A pipe open at both ends is the wall alone. The story in the question chooses the faces: a tank painted on the outside, a box with no top, a gutter — read the story, then count only the faces it keeps.

Now the error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: a diameter squared as if it were a radius — a tin described by its diameter of eight centimetres has radius four, and squaring eight instead of four multiplies the base area by four. Exhibit two: rounding pi in the middle of the working — carry the symbol pi to the final line. Exhibit three: cubic units on a wrapping answer or square units on a filling answer. Exhibit four: the vanished half on any triangular base.

The complete procedure, in order: name the solid and its base; find the base area; multiply by height for volume; unfold the net for surface area; let the story select the faces; close with the correct unit, exact first, rounded second.

This section's questions are in front of you now — litre conversions, an open tin, and each museum exhibit identified on sight.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same answers return now — three hundred and sixty and three hundred and thirty-two, two hundred and forty pi and one hundred and fifty-two pi — but this time they come from a stack of coins and a sheet of gift wrap.

## Subtopic: A Stack of Identical Coins

Picture a pile of identical coins. One coin covers a certain area of the table; the pile's height counts how many coins are stacked. The space the pile occupies is one coin's area times the number of coins. Nobody ever memorised a formula for that — it is bare common sense.

Every right prism and every cylinder is a coin stack. The tea box is a stack of identical nine-by-four rectangles, ten layers deep: thirty-six times ten, three hundred and sixty cubic centimetres. The paint tin is a stack of identical circles, fifteen layers deep. One circular layer covers pi times four squared, sixteen pi. Fifteen layers: two hundred and forty pi, which the calculator reads as 753,98 cubic centimetres.

That is the whole theory of volume for this topic: find the layer, count the layers. When an unfamiliar prism turns up — triangular, L-shaped, built on a trapezium — do not go hunting for an exotic formula. Ask exactly two questions. What shape is one layer, and what is its area? How many layers tall is the stack? Multiply, and the volume surrenders.

One habit guards the method: identify the repeated face before anything else. A prism resting on its side still has its twin faces; the stack may simply run left to right. The layer is the face that appears twice, and the height is the distance separating the twins.

Your questions for this section are ready — for every solid, state what the layer is before your calculator gets a say.

## Subtopic: Gift-Wrap Versus Fill

Volume fills a solid; surface area dresses it. Two different jobs, and the tool for dressing is the net — the solid opened out flat, like a cardboard carton flattened for recycling.

Flatten the tea box and count its panels: top and bottom, thirty-six each; front and back, ninety each; the two ends, forty each. Six panels total three hundred and thirty-two square centimetres of cardboard. Should a question present a box with no lid, one thirty-six vanishes — the net always displays exactly which panels the story kept.

The tin is the elegant case. Slide the wrapper off a real tin: it comes away as a flat rectangle. Its height matches the tin, fifteen. Its width is the journey once around the rim — two pi times the radius, eight pi. The wrapper's area is eight pi times fifteen, one hundred and twenty pi. Add the two circular ends at sixteen pi each and the full dressing is one hundred and fifty-two pi square centimetres — 477,52 after rounding.

Filling counts little cubes, so its unit is cubic centimetres. Dressing counts flat squares, so its unit is square centimetres. At the end of any measurement question, glance at your unit and ask: did I fill, or did I wrap? A unit that contradicts the job is an alarm bell ringing about something upstream.

Here are this section's questions — count panels on the net, slide the wrapper off in your imagination, and let the unit certify the job.

## Subtopic: The Checklist That Solves Every Solid

The whole lesson compresses into five steps that run on any prism or cylinder a paper can offer.

Step one: identify the base — the face that repeats — and name its shape aloud. Step two: compute the base area. Rectangle: length times breadth. Triangle: half times base times perpendicular height, and the half is non-negotiable. Circle: pi r squared, after checking whether the question supplied a radius or a diameter. Step three: volume equals base area times height, the height being the distance between the twin faces. Step four: for surface area, unfold the net and add every panel the story permits — closed solids keep everything, open tanks and pipes surrender their lids. A cylinder's wall is a rectangle: circumference times height, two pi r h. Step five: finish cleanly — exact answer with pi where it belongs, then the rounded decimal, then the proper unit, cubic for filling and square for wrapping, dividing cubic centimetres by one thousand whenever the question speaks in litres.

Rehearse the checklist against the six numbers now living in your head: the box at three hundred and sixty and three hundred and thirty-two, the tin at two hundred and forty pi and one hundred and fifty-two pi, the triangular prism at six hundred and six hundred and sixty. When your run of the checklist regenerates all six, the method is installed for good.

The sessions ahead stretch these very solids by a scale factor k and then round them into spheres, pyramids and cones — and every one of those sessions stands on base times height and the unfolded net.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: run the five steps in order, and let the checklist carry the thinking.
