# Part 1 — Expert

The parabola is the first curved graph you are asked to produce from an equation alone, and at Grade 10 level it arrives in one tidy form: y equals a x squared plus q. Two parameters, each with one job, and a short list of features every sketch must display. This session builds the mother function from a table, isolates the work done by a and by q, then runs a complete sketch and finishes by reversing the process — recovering an equation from a drawn graph.

## Subtopic: The Mother Function y Equals x Squared

Begin at the beginning: a equal to one, q equal to zero. y equals x squared.

Build the table. Negative three gives nine. Negative two gives four. Negative one gives one. Zero gives zero. One gives one. Two gives four. Three gives nine. Study the pairing: negative two and positive two both land on four, because squaring wipes out the sign. That one arithmetic fact is the source of everything symmetrical about this graph.

Describe the curve so someone could draw it blind. It lies entirely on or above the x-axis, resting its single lowest point on the origin, zero comma zero. From there it climbs on both sides — lazily at first, then harder and harder: one up at one across, four up at two across, nine up at three across. The left half is the exact mirror of the right half, folded over the y-axis. And it is a CURVE, never a V: at the bottom it flattens, rounds, and turns. No sharp corner.

Attach the official vocabulary. The TURNING POINT is zero comma zero — the place where falling becomes rising — and for this graph it is a MINIMUM. The AXIS OF SYMMETRY is the vertical line x equals zero, the y-axis itself. Both intercepts happen to sit at the origin here.

Then the pair that carries dedicated marks and endless confusion: domain and range. The DOMAIN is what you may feed in — and every real number squares without complaint, so the domain is all real x. The RANGE is what comes out — and a square is never negative, so y is greater than or equal to zero. The range must be written in terms of y, always. Writing "x greater than or equal to zero" as a range is the most common single error in this topic, and it scores nothing.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the table, the shape described in words, and the difference between what goes in and what comes out.

## Subtopic: The Effect of a — Stretch, Squash and Flip

Hold q at zero and let a move. Watch what changes — and what refuses to.

The SIGN of a sets the direction. Positive a: the parabola opens upward — the smile — and the turning point is a MINIMUM. Negative a: it opens downward — the frown — and the turning point is a MAXIMUM. One sign controls the whole attitude of the graph, so it is the first thing to read, every time.

The SIZE of a sets the width. Compare y equals three x squared with y equals x squared: at x equals one, three instead of one; at x equals two, twelve instead of four. Every height has been tripled, so the curve gains height faster and looks NARROWER. Now y equals one quarter x squared: at x equals two it reaches only one instead of four, climbing slowly, spreading out — WIDER. The rule: size bigger than one stretches the graph vertically and narrows it; size between zero and one squashes it and widens it.

A negative a does both of its jobs at once. y equals negative three x squared opens downward AND sits narrow: at x equals one, y is negative three; at x equals two, negative twelve. It is y equals three x squared reflected in the x-axis.

Just as important is what a leaves alone. With q at zero, the turning point stays welded to the origin and the axis of symmetry stays at x equals zero, no matter what a does — the curve pivots and flips about that anchored point, but never slides sideways or upward. The range simply follows a's sign: y greater than or equal to zero for positive a, y less than or equal to zero for negative a.

Stop for this section's questions now — sign read first, size translated into narrow or wide, and the range matched to the direction.

## Subtopic: The Effect of q — Shift, Range and Intercepts

The parameter q has exactly one power: it slides the entire graph VERTICALLY by q units. Up for positive q, down for negative. Width untouched, direction untouched, axis of symmetry untouched.

Three consequences follow, and you should be able to recite them cold. One: the turning point moves from the origin to zero comma q. Two: substituting x equals zero returns y equals q, so the y-intercept IS q — the value of q can be read straight off a drawn graph as the height where it cuts the y-axis. Three: the axis of symmetry remains x equals zero, because up-and-down movement cannot tilt a vertical line.

The range rides along with the shift. Positive a: y greater than or equal to q. Negative a: y less than or equal to q. In y, with the inequality pointing the way the graph opens.

Now the x-intercepts, where q quietly controls how many exist. Set y to zero and solve. For y equals x squared minus sixteen: x squared equals sixteen, so x equals positive or negative four — two crossings, at negative four comma zero and four comma zero. The plus-or-minus is not decoration; writing only x equals four discards half the answer and half the marks.

Compare y equals x squared plus five. Set y to zero: x squared equals negative five — and no real number squares to a negative. NO x-intercepts. The picture says the same thing without algebra: an upward-opening curve whose lowest point floats five units above the axis can never come down to touch it. Carry the general test: a and q with the SAME sign — no crossings. OPPOSITE signs — two crossings. q equal to zero — one touch, at the origin.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — q as the shift and the y-intercept, the range rewritten around q, and the sign test for counting x-intercepts.

## Subtopic: Sketching y Equals 2x² Minus 8, and Reading an Equation Off a Graph

Sketch y equals two x squared minus eight, with every feature shown.

Step one — shape. a is two, positive: upward opening, minimum turning point. Step two — turning point and axis. q is negative eight: turning point at zero comma negative eight, axis of symmetry x equals zero. Step three — y-intercept. x equals zero gives y equals negative eight — the turning point itself, as it must be in this form. Step four — x-intercepts. Zero equals two x squared minus eight, so two x squared equals eight, x squared equals four, x equals positive or negative two: the points negative two comma zero and two comma zero. Step five — domain all real x; range y greater than or equal to negative eight.

Describe the finished drawing. A smooth upward valley whose floor sits on the y-axis eight units below the origin. It rises to cut the x-axis at negative two and at two, then keeps climbing steeply. For accuracy, one extra point: at x equals three, y is two times nine minus eight — ten. So the curve passes through three comma ten, and, by the mirror, through negative three comma ten.

Now the reverse question, which appears just as often. A parabola of the form y equals a x squared plus q has its turning point at zero comma negative five and passes through two comma seven. The turning point hands you q instantly: q is negative five, so y equals a x squared minus five. Feed in the other point: seven equals four a minus five, so four a equals twelve and a equals three. The equation is y equals three x squared minus five.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: a range written in x instead of y. Two: the plus-or-minus dropped from x squared equals four. Three: q treated as a sideways shift — in this form the graph never moves sideways. Four: a equal to one quarter described as making the graph "smaller" instead of WIDER — the graph is squashed vertically, so it spreads horizontally. Five: inventing x-intercepts when a and q share a sign; if the algebra says x squared equals a negative number, the honest and correct answer is that no crossings exist.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the five-step sketch in order, and the reverse problem opened by taking q from the turning point before substituting anything.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same curve from a netball shot and a bowl on the kitchen counter — two pictures that carry every rule of the topic between them.

## Subtopic: The Shape of a Thrown Ball

Watch a netball shot arc toward the hoop. Up, slowing, a single weightless moment at the top, then down — and the way down copies the way up exactly, in reverse. That shape is a parabola. A stream of water from a garden sprayer draws one in the air; a stone lobbed into a dam draws one on its way in.

The heart of the shape is the turn. There is one moment — exactly one — where climbing becomes falling, and that is the turning point. The rest of the curve organises itself around it: left side and right side are identical twins, folded across the vertical line through the turn, like a page folded down its middle.

Now build the same shape from numbers. The recipe: take a number, square it. Zero gives zero. One gives one, two gives four, three gives nine. Go negative and nothing new happens: negative one gives one, negative two gives four, negative three gives nine. Both sides produce the same heights — that is the mirror, explained in one line. Plot the points and a smooth valley appears, floor at the origin, walls climbing gently at first and then fiercely: nine units of height within three units of width.

Two words to keep in separate pockets, because both get asked. What you are ALLOWED to put in is the domain — and any number squares, so everything is allowed. What COMES OUT is the range — and a square is never negative, so no height below zero ever appears. And the range is a statement about heights, so it is written with y, never with x.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the ball's flight are coming to you right now. Keep what-goes-in and what-comes-out firmly apart.

## Subtopic: Turning the Bowl Over and Lifting It

Put a mixing bowl on the kitchen counter. That is the basic curve: open side up, one lowest point at the bottom. Everything the equation's two numbers do, they do to that bowl.

The first number is the one multiplying the squared part. Its SIGN flips the bowl. Positive: right way up, ready for batter, with a lowest point. Negative: flipped over like a pot lid dropped over leftovers, now with a highest point instead. So the first question is always: mixing bowl, or pot lid?

Its SIZE moulds the bowl. Bigger than one and the walls climb steeply — a tall, narrow shape, like a glass vase; tripling the number triples every height, so four up becomes twelve up. A fraction like a quarter and the walls rise slowly — a flat, wide shape, like a serving platter. Big number, narrow. Small fraction, wide.

The second number, standing alone at the end, does something entirely different: it carries the whole bowl straight up or straight down. Add five and it sits five units up on the windowsill. Subtract eight and it sinks eight units down into a hole in the sand. Width unchanged, direction unchanged — only the height moves. And this number gives you two answers for free: it is the height where the curve crosses the up-and-down axis, and it is the height of the turning point. Same number, both jobs.

Combine the two numbers and you can predict crossings without algebra. An upright bowl lifted above the line can never touch the line — a mixing bowl on the windowsill does not reach the floor. No crossings. Sink that same bowl into a hole and its rim breaks the surface on both sides — two crossings. Same bowl, different height, opposite answer.

Your questions for this part are up now. For each one, decide first: right way up or flipped, and then how high or how deep it sits.

## Subtopic: Four Moves to a Sketch — and the Traps

Sketching looks like drawing. It is actually four short calculations and then a smooth line through the results.

Move one: which way up? Read the sign of the number in front of the squared part. Positive smiles, negative frowns. Write it down before touching the calculator.

Move two: where is the turn? The number standing alone at the end is the height of the turning point, and it always sits on the up-and-down axis. For two x squared take away eight, the floor of the valley is at eight below zero.

Move three: where are the crossings on the flat axis? Set the height to zero. Two x squared minus eight equals zero, so x squared equals four — and here is the moment that decides your marks: x is two OR negative two, because both square to four. Two crossings, one on each side, perfectly mirrored. Dropping the negative answer is the most expensive habit in the topic — it loses its own mark and breaks the symmetry of your drawing.

Move four: one extra point, doubled by the mirror. Feed in three: two times nine is eighteen, minus eight is ten. The curve passes through ten-up at three-across — and through the same height at negative three, free of charge.

Now draw: a smooth valley, floor at eight below zero on the vertical axis, rising through the crossings at two-left and two-right, reaching ten-up by three-across on both sides. Rounded at the bottom, always — never a sharp V.

Three traps, named so they lose their teeth. First: the range describes heights, so it is written with y — a range in x is worth nothing. Second: the lone number at the end never slides the curve sideways; in this form the parabola only ever moves up or down. Third: if the algebra hands you "x squared equals negative five", do not bend the signs to force an answer. Nothing real squares to a negative; the graph simply has no crossings, and the bowl-on-the-windowsill picture confirms it. Write that sentence and collect the mark.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: shape, turn, crossings, extra point — four moves, in that order, every single time. Make the routine automatic and every parabola question becomes a few quick lines and one clean curve.
