# Part 1 — Expert

Two graphs complete the Grade 10 function family, and both carry something the parabola never had: an asymptote — a line the curve approaches forever without ever touching. This session builds the hyperbola, y equals a over x plus q, and the exponential graph, y equals a times b to the power x, plus q. For each one: the basic shape from a table, the work the two parameters do, the asymptotes, the restrictions, and a complete sketch carrying every feature a marker checks for.

## Subtopic: The Hyperbola — Branches, Asymptotes and Restrictions

Start with y equals eight over x — "y equals eight divided by x".

Table the positive side first. When x is one, y is eight. When x is two, y is four. When x is four, y is two. When x is eight, y is one. When x is sixteen, y is one half. As x grows, y sinks toward zero without ever getting there — no value of x makes eight over x equal to nothing. Now shrink x instead: at one half, y is sixteen; at one tenth, y is eighty. As x closes in on zero, y explodes upward.

Now the negative side, and the whole pattern repeats with its sign flipped. Negative one gives negative eight. Negative two gives negative four. Negative eight gives negative one. Negative sixteen gives negative one half.

Describe the picture so a hand can draw it. Two separate curves, called BRANCHES. One lives in the first quadrant: high against the y-axis on the left, sweeping down and levelling off toward the x-axis as it travels right, touching neither. The other branch is its point-reflection through the origin, down in the third quadrant: hugging the x-axis far to the left, then diving down alongside the y-axis. The branches never meet, and neither one ever crosses an axis.

Name the features properly. The ASYMPTOTES are the two untouchable lines: vertical, x equals zero; horizontal, y equals zero. The vertical one is not a stylistic choice — it is a restriction. Substituting x equals zero would mean dividing by zero, which is undefined. So the DOMAIN is all real x except zero. And since eight over x never equals zero, the RANGE is all real y except zero.

Two facts the syllabus singles out. The hyperbola has two axes of symmetry — the lines y equals x and y equals negative x. And it has NO y-intercept, ever, because a y-intercept demands x equals zero, which the domain forbids. Calculating one is not a small slip; it is a broken restriction.

The sign of a chooses the quadrants. Positive a: branches in the first and third, as above. Negative a — say y equals negative eight over x — flips them into the SECOND and FOURTH: x equals one now gives negative eight, and x equals negative one gives positive eight.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the two branches, both asymptotes, the domain restriction, and why a y-intercept can never exist.

## Subtopic: The Effect of q on the Hyperbola

The parameter q does to the hyperbola what it did to the parabola: it lifts or drops the entire graph by q units and changes nothing else about the shape.

The consequence is larger here, though, because the graph takes its horizontal asymptote along for the ride. For y equals eight over x plus q, the horizontal asymptote is the line y equals q — NOT y equals zero. The vertical asymptote stays put at x equals zero, since the shift is vertical and the division by x is unchanged.

Work y equals eight over x plus two in full.

Asymptotes: vertical x equals zero; horizontal y equals two. Domain: all real x except zero. Range: all real y except two — the curve presses toward the line y equals two from both sides but never lands on it.

The x-intercept, which the shift has created. Set y to zero: zero equals eight over x plus two, so eight over x equals negative two, so eight equals negative two x, so x equals negative four. The point negative four comma zero — notice it sits on the LEFT branch this time, because the whole graph was lifted and it is the lower branch that now cuts the axis. The original y equals eight over x had no x-intercept at all; the shift manufactured one.

The y-intercept: none. Always none.

Two guide points for the sketch. At x equals one, y is eight plus two — ten. At x equals negative two, y is negative four plus two — negative two.

The sketch in words. Dash a horizontal line two units above the x-axis. The right-hand branch rides high in the first quadrant, through one comma ten, then flattens rightward, settling just above the line y equals two. The left-hand branch sits below that dashed line: far left it creeps just under y equals two, crosses the x-axis at negative four comma zero, passes through negative two comma negative two, and then plunges as x nears zero from the left.

The classic question runs backwards: read the equation off the graph. If a hyperbola of the form y equals a over x plus q has horizontal asymptote y equals two and passes through one comma ten, then q is two on sight, and substituting the point gives ten equals a over one, plus two, so a equals eight. The equation is y equals eight over x plus two.

Stop for this section's questions now — the horizontal asymptote as y equals q, the range that follows from it, and the x-intercept the shift created.

## Subtopic: The Exponential Graph — Growth, Decay and Its Asymptote

The exponential function is y equals b to the power x, with b positive and not equal to one. Look where the variable sits: in the EXPONENT. That placement is the whole difference between this graph and y equals x squared, where the variable sits in the base.

Take y equals three to the power x. The table: x equals negative two gives one ninth. Negative one gives one third. Zero gives one. One gives three. Two gives nine. Three gives twenty-seven.

Describe the curve. Far to the left it lies barely above the x-axis — visually flat, but strictly above, never on it. Travelling right it lifts through zero comma one, then three, nine, twenty-seven, and takes off almost vertically. No turning point, no symmetry, no ceiling. The horizontal asymptote is y equals zero, approached on the left side only.

The features. Domain: all real x — three may be raised to any power whatsoever. Range: y strictly greater than zero — three to any power is positive and never zero, and the word strictly is doing real work: writing greater-than-or-equal-to zero is wrong, because zero is never reached. Y-intercept: substitute x equals zero and any base gives b to the power zero, which is one. Every basic exponential curve passes through zero comma one. X-intercept: none, because the curve never meets the axis.

Now decay. Take y equals one third to the power x. At x equals negative two, y is nine; negative one gives three; zero gives one; one gives one third; two gives one ninth. This curve falls from left to right: high on the left, down through zero comma one, then flattening toward the x-axis on the right. Same asymptote, same range, same y-intercept — the growth curve reflected in the y-axis.

The rule to state: b greater than one means the graph rises — GROWTH. b between zero and one means it falls — DECAY. And the two exclusions have reasons: b equal to one gives one to every power, a flat horizontal line, not an exponential curve at all; and a negative b breaks down completely, since a negative number raised to a half has no real value.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — growth against decay, the asymptote at y equals zero, the strict inequality in the range, and the guaranteed y-intercept of one.

## Subtopic: The Effect of a and q, and a Full Sketch

The complete Grade 10 form is y equals a times b to the power x, plus q.

q slides the whole graph vertically and drags the horizontal asymptote with it: the asymptote becomes y equals q. a stretches the graph vertically, and when a is negative it flips the curve over its own asymptote, turning a rising curve into a falling one.

Sketch y equals three to the power x, minus nine.

Asymptote: y equals negative nine. Y-intercept: x equals zero gives three to the zero, one, minus nine — negative eight, the point zero comma negative eight. X-intercept: set y to zero — three to the power x equals nine, and nine is three squared, so x equals two: the point two comma zero. Domain: all real x. Range: y strictly greater than negative nine.

The sketch in words: a dashed horizontal line nine units below the x-axis. The curve enters from the far left just above that line, climbs gently through zero comma negative eight on the y-axis, cuts the x-axis at two comma zero, and then soars — at x equals three it is twenty-seven minus nine, eighteen.

Now a negative a. Sketch y equals negative three to the power x, plus three — negative one times three to the power x, plus three.

Asymptote: y equals three. Y-intercept: negative one plus three — two, the point zero comma two. X-intercept: set y to zero — three to the power x equals three, so x equals one: the point one comma zero. Range: y strictly less than three, because three always has something positive subtracted from it. The curve drifts in from the far left just below the line y equals three, slips down through zero comma two, crosses at one comma zero, and then dives steeply.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: writing the asymptote as y equals zero when q is not zero — the asymptote travels with the graph. Two: hunting for a y-intercept on a hyperbola, which has none to find. Three: writing the exponential range with greater-than-or-equal instead of strictly greater-than. Four: mixing up y equals three to the power x with y equals x squared — variable upstairs never turns; variable downstairs turns and comes back. Five: sketching a curve that touches or crosses its own asymptote — the one thing an asymptote, by its very meaning, never allows.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the asymptote read from q, both intercepts computed by substitution, and the range written with its strict inequality.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two curves out in the world — a shared hire fee, and a meme running loose through a school. Both behave exactly like these graphs, because these graphs are how sharing and doubling have always behaved.

## Subtopic: Sharing a Fixed Pot

An indoor five-a-side pitch costs two hundred and forty rand for the hour — fixed, no matter how many players pitch up. What does each player pay?

Two players: one hundred and twenty rand each. Three: eighty. Four: sixty. Six: forty. Eight: thirty. Twelve: twenty. Watch the pattern — more players, smaller share, and the saving shrinks as you go. From two players to three, everyone saves forty rand. From eleven to twelve, the saving is barely two rand.

Push the story to its edges and two impossibilities appear. Pack in more and more players and the share keeps dropping — but it never becomes zero. Someone always pays something, however tiny. Now go the other way: one player pays the full two hundred and forty. And zero players? You cannot divide a fee among nobody. That situation does not exist at all.

Those two impossibilities are precisely what the graph draws. Players along the bottom, rand-per-player up the side: the curve drops steeply, then flattens, sliding closer and closer to the bottom line without ever resting on it, and rearing up taller and taller near the left edge without ever touching the side line. The two untouchable lines have a name — asymptotes. One of them says "the share never reaches zero". The other says "you cannot share among nobody".

And because zero can never go in, the curve never meets the up-and-down axis at all. A hyperbola has no y-intercept. Not a hidden one — none.

One more piece completes the picture: an identical copy of the curve sits diagonally opposite, in the corner where both numbers are negative. Two branches, one graph.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the shared pitch are coming to you right now. Keep the two impossible things in front of you.

## Subtopic: Doubling Every Time

New story. You make a meme in the first period and send it to two friends. Next period, each of them forwards it to two more people. And on it goes, period after period.

Count who has it: one, two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two. By the tenth round the count passes a thousand. The numbers crawl at first and then detonate. That is growth by MULTIPLYING rather than adding, and its graph is unmistakable: flat and sleepy along the bottom for ages, then climbing almost straight up.

Now run the film backwards, because that is where the second key feature hides. One round before one person had it, half a person did — then a quarter, an eighth, a sixteenth. The values shrink and shrink but never reach zero: you can halve forever and something always remains. So on the left the curve tiptoes just above the bottom line without ever landing. That bottom line is the asymptote, and it is why the range must be written strictly ABOVE zero — never "zero or more". Zero is the one value the curve never achieves.

One free point belongs to every curve of this family. After zero rounds, exactly one person has the meme — and anything raised to the power zero is one. So the curve crosses the up-and-down axis at one, always, whatever the multiplying number happens to be.

And the same shape runs downhill too. Instead of doubling, halve: a second-hand phone losing half its resale value every year, or a cooldown timer cutting the remaining time by the same fraction each step. That curve starts high on the left and glides down toward the bottom line — still never touching it. Multiply by more than one and the curve climbs. Multiply by a fraction and it falls.

Your questions for this part are up now. Ask whether the story multiplies up or multiplies down, and never forget the line the curve refuses to land on.

## Subtopic: Asymptote First — the Routine and the Traps

Both graphs are sketched with the same four moves, in the same order: asymptote, intercepts, extra points, curve.

Move one: locate the untouchable line. The number standing alone at the end of the equation tells you its height. Minus nine at the end: the line runs nine below zero. Plus three: three above. Draw it dashed before anything else, because the whole curve is arranged around it. The sharing curve carries a second untouchable line — the vertical one where the division by zero would happen.

Move two: the crossing on the up-and-down axis. For the doubling curve, feed in zero rounds: one, plus whatever the equation adds at the end. Three to the power x minus nine at x equals zero gives one minus nine — eight below zero. For the sharing curve, skip this move completely: it never crosses.

Move three: the crossing on the flat axis. Set the height to zero and solve. Three to the power x minus nine equals zero means three to the power x equals nine, and nine is three times three, so x is two. Eight over x plus two equals zero means eight over x is negative two, so x is negative four.

Move four: one or two extra points, then draw — and the curve must lean into each dashed line without ever landing on it. That leaning-without-touching is the first thing a marker's eye goes to.

Three traps, named and defused. First: the untouchable line is NOT always the bottom axis. It moves whenever a number is added or subtracted at the end, and writing "asymptote at zero" for a shifted graph is the single most common lost mark in the topic. Second: the doubling curve's heights are strictly above the line — never "on or above". The curve closes the gap endlessly and still never lands. One symbol, one mark. Third: keep x squared and three to the power x apart. In x squared the variable is downstairs being squared, and the graph turns around and comes back. In three to the power x the variable is upstairs in the exponent, and the graph never turns — it only climbs or only falls. Ask "ground floor or upstairs?" of every equation and the wrong shape never gets drawn.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: dashed lines first, then the crossings, then the curve that leans in and never lands. Run the routine three times and both graphs become marks you can count on.
