**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the evergreen: domain versus range. Everyone says they understand it, and then the marks disappear anyway. What is going on?

**Tutor:** The understanding is usually fine — the letter is what goes wrong. Domain is the collection of values you are ALLOWED to feed in: the inputs, the left-right positions along the graph. Range is what COMES OUT: the outputs, the heights the curve actually reaches. For y equals x squared, anything can be squared, so the domain is all real x. But no square is ever negative, so the outputs start at zero and rise forever: range y greater than or equal to zero. Here is the marking reality, though. Write that same correct idea as "x greater than or equal to zero" and the marker reads a claim about widths where a claim about heights was required — zero, even though you understood perfectly. So install the habit: before writing any range, ask "how high and how low does this curve actually go?" — the word high pulls the letter y onto the page. And use the direction check as a free alarm: a downward-opening parabola must have a range running downward from its turning point, with less-than-or-equal. If your inequality points against your sketch, you have caught the error while it is still free to fix.

***

**Thandi:** Next: someone described the number in front of x squared as making the graph "bigger". Why do the marks care about the word we choose?

**Tutor:** Because "bigger" describes nothing measurable, while the number is doing something very specific: it multiplies every output height. Compare y equals x squared with y equals three x squared. At x equals one, the height jumps from one to three. At x equals two, from four to twelve. Every point is dragged three times further from the horizontal axis, so the curve reaches any given height much sooner — and a curve that gains height in a shorter sideways distance looks NARROW. The accepted words are narrower, or stretched vertically. Now run it downhill with y equals one quarter x squared: at x equals two, the height falls from four to one; the climb is lazy, the sideways journey to reach any height is long, and the curve looks WIDE. Learners who say "smaller" mislead themselves twice, because the graph is not small — it runs forever in both directions — it is flattened and spread. Comparison questions ask for exactly this vocabulary, and they pay for the words narrower and wider. Hang the picture on the vase and the platter: tall and narrow, low and wide. The language stops slipping once the crockery is in your head.

***

**Thandi:** A confession from the practice set: someone wrote that y equals x squared plus five crosses the x-axis at root five and negative root five. What went wrong?

**Tutor:** A method got skipped and a sign got ignored — worth untangling both. Finding x-intercepts always begins the same way: set y to zero and solve, with no shortcuts. Zero equals x squared plus five gives x squared equals NEGATIVE five. And no real number squares to a negative — positive times positive is positive, negative times negative is also positive — so there is no real solution, and the honest conclusion is that this parabola has no x-intercepts at all. The learner who wrote plus and minus root five solved x squared equals five, a different equation, by quietly dropping the minus. Now let the picture confirm the algebra, because it always will: the coefficient in front is positive, so the bowl sits the right way up, and the plus five lifts its lowest point five units above the axis. A bowl on the windowsill never touches the floor. That agreement gives you a sight-test to use before any algebra: front number and end number with the SAME sign — no crossings; OPPOSITE signs — two crossings. Predict with the rule, confirm with the algebra, and when a negative square appears, write "no real solutions, so no x-intercepts" and collect the mark for honesty.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's cheeky: every phone can graph these instantly. Why are we still sketching by hand?

**Tutor:** Because the exam is not testing the drawing — it is testing the reading, and reading is the skill the phone cannot lend you. The target ability is to glance at an equation and know, before any picture exists, that this curve opens downward, turns at a height of three, stands narrow, and never reaches the horizontal axis. That is what lets you interpret an unfamiliar graph, sanity-check an answer, and later read inequalities straight off a pair of curves. A screen answers one question; parameter-reading answers the whole species of questions. There is also a blunt mark-allocation reason. Sketching questions pay almost everything for the LABELLED features — intercepts, turning point, shape — and almost nothing for beauty. A rough curve with correct labels collects nearly full marks; a gorgeous unlabelled sweep collects nearly none. So the five-step routine is not busywork, it is the mark scheme in disguise. And it compounds: next year the parabola learns to slide sideways, and in your final year the same reading skill becomes curve sketching with calculus. The equation-reading you build now is the foundation everything after this stands on.
