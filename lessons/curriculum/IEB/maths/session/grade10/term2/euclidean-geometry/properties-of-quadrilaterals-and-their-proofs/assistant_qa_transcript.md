**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: properties and conditions feel like the same list written twice. What is actually different about them?

**Tutor:** The facts overlap, but the direction of travel is opposite, and the marks live in knowing which way you are moving. A property answers this question: given that the figure IS a parallelogram, what do I now get for free? Opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other, neighbouring angles totalling one hundred and eighty. A condition answers the reverse: I do not yet know what this figure is — what would be enough to give it the name? Both pairs of opposite sides parallel, both pairs equal, both pairs of opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other, or one pair of sides both equal and parallel. Notice that the diagonal fact sits on both lists — that is exactly why the two get blurred. The discipline is about sequence. When a question opens with "PQRS is a parallelogram, prove that…", the properties are available from your first line. When it opens with "prove that PQRS is a parallelogram", not one property of PQRS may be touched, because that assumes the conclusion; you must build toward a condition using only the given facts. Before every line you write, ask: has this figure earned its name yet?

***

**Thandi:** Next one: with five conditions on the menu, how do we pick the right one to aim for without wasting half the exam?

**Tutor:** Let the givens choose. Match the kind of information you were handed to the condition that consumes that kind of information. Handed side lengths? Aim at both pairs of opposite sides equal — or, if a parallel pair is also mentioned, at the fifth condition, one pair equal and parallel, which is usually the shortest road. Handed angles? Aim at both pairs of opposite angles equal. Handed diagonals — drawn in the figure, or a question that talks about points sitting on them or a shared crossing point? Aim at the diagonals bisecting each other; that is nearly always the intended route. Our rider is the perfect illustration: PGRH has P and R as one pair of opposite vertices, G and H as the other, and all four connect through the single point T. The diagonals are handed to you on a plate, so the diagonal condition is the obvious target. Two practical habits. Write the target down before the proof — "aim: diagonals of PGRH bisect each other" — so you cannot drift. And if the facts stop cooperating halfway, do not force the door; step back and ask which of the other four conditions your givens actually feed.

***

**Thandi:** Someone wants to know about the diagram. If the figure obviously looks like a rhombus, why can't we just write rhombus and move on?

**Tutor:** Because a proof must convince a reader who never saw your diagram, and because diagrams are often drawn out of scale deliberately. Two genuinely different shapes can be near-identical to the eye: a rhombus with angles of eighty-seven and ninety-three degrees looks like a square from your desk, yet it is not one, and every right-angle-dependent fact fails for it. Markers know this, so any statement whose only support is appearance earns nothing. What are you allowed to use? Three sources: the information stated in the question, the markings printed on the figure — tick marks for equal lengths, the little square for a right angle — and the theorems and definitions you can name. Everything else must be derived. A good honesty test is to think of the reason before the statement: if the best reason available is "it looks like it", the statement is not yet yours to write. The reverse discipline matters too — when the figure looks wrong but the given facts say otherwise, follow the facts. Some diagrams are distorted precisely to expose learners who eyeball instead of reason.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's the sore one: people write a full page of true geometry and get almost no marks for it. What is going wrong?

**Tutor:** Two things, almost always: writing forwards, and dropping reasons. Writing forwards means listing every deduction you can make and hoping the conclusion surfaces on its own. It produces long, true, aimless answers — and markers award the steps that build the required argument, not every true sentence on the page. Writing backwards means naming the finishing rule first, asking what that rule needs, and then supplying exactly those facts. In the PGRH rider the finishing rule needs precisely two equalities: GT equals TH, and PT equals TR. Every line you write should feed one of those two, and the whole proof lands in about five lines. The second fault is the missing reason column. Every statement needs a named justification — "diags of parm bisect each other", "given", "alternate angles, WX parallel to ZY", "common side". Those phrases score marks by themselves, they never change, and you can learn the standard wording in an evening — the cheapest marks geometry offers. Two columns, statement left, reason right, and no left-hand entry ever sits alone. Short, targeted, fully receipted: that is what full marks looks like.
