Hey, it's Thandi — if the word "rider" makes your shoulders tense up, breathe. This theorem is honestly the friendliest one in the whole geometry section, because it only ever asks you to notice one thing and then write about four lines.

Here is the entire topic, folded small. Two midpoints, on two different sides of a triangle? Then the line joining them runs parallel to the third side and measures exactly half of it. One sentence, two gifts. And the reverse: one midpoint, plus a line from it running parallel to another side, lands you dead on the middle of the remaining side. Two situations. That is genuinely all there is to memorise.

The mistake I would bet my lunch on is the halving one. Under pressure, people double when they should halve. So carry the carport with you: the brace between the two halfway marks is always the SHORT piece. Brace four metres, beam eight. Whisper "the join is the small one" before any number leaves your pen.

Then the habit that quietly collects marks: every time you use the theorem, write BOTH conclusions immediately — the parallel and the half — even when the question seems to want only one. The other one is nearly always needed two lines later, and having it ready on the page costs nothing. And glue a reason to every line. "Midpoint theorem" when you were handed two midpoints. "Line through midpoint parallel to second side" when you were handed one midpoint and a parallel. Short phrases, real marks.

Last warning: never promote a point to midpoint just because it sits in the middle of the picture. If the question did not say it and the ticks are not there, it is not one.

Tonight, do three riders — just three — and say "two midpoints, two sides, third side" before each. Watch how fast the pattern starts waving at you. Then go tackle the practice questions — you've got this!
