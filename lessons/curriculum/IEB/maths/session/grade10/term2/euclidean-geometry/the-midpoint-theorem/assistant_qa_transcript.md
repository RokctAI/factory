**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First up, the honest one: how do we know when a question actually wants the midpoint theorem? Under time pressure every triangle looks like every other triangle.

**Tutor:** Stop scanning triangles and start scanning for the theorem's two costumes — it only ever wears these two. Costume one: the question names two midpoints, or the diagram carries matching tick marks halving two sides, and those are two DIFFERENT sides of the same triangle. See that, and you immediately own two facts about the joining segment: it is parallel to the third side, and it is half that side's length. Costume two: only one midpoint appears, but the question adds that a line through it runs parallel to another side. That is the converse, and it delivers a second midpoint on the remaining side for free. The discipline that converts recognition into marks is this: the moment costume one appears, write BOTH conclusions down, even if the question looks like it wants only one. Riders nearly always call for the second conclusion a line or two later, and having it on the page already costs nothing. And if neither costume is present — a lone midpoint with nothing parallel — the theorem does not apply, and forcing it wastes minutes and earns zero.

***

**Thandi:** Next one, asked by three different people: why does the proof invent a new point? Extending the line and conjuring K out of nowhere feels like cheating.

**Tutor:** It feels like cheating because it is powerful, but it is completely legal — a construction never assumes anything, it only draws. Assuming a property without proof is forbidden; adding a line and then proving what that line does is the oldest legitimate move in geometry. Why this particular construction? Look at what we are trying to prove: facts about a lone segment floating inside a triangle. The toolkit at this level has nothing that speaks directly about such segments — but it has excellent machinery for parallelograms. So the proof manufactures one. Extending the join beyond the second midpoint by its own length, and connecting to the far vertex, creates a second triangle that must be congruent to the first — side, angle, side: the midpoint gives one pair of equal sides, the construction gives the second, and the vertically opposite angles sit between them. The congruence yields a pair of segments both equal and parallel, and one such pair is exactly the condition that certifies a parallelogram. From there both conclusions arrive together: a parallelogram's opposite sides are equal and parallel, and the original join is half the constructed side by design. The invented point is not a trick — it is a bridge from a problem without tools to a problem with plenty.

**Thandi:** A confession from the practice round: someone wrote that the joining line was double the third side instead of half. How do we make sure that never happens again?

**Tutor:** With a picture, not a rule — rules evaporate under pressure, pictures do not. Keep the carport in your head: two rafters meeting at the ridge, the long base beam along the bottom, and the short brace nailed between the halfway marks. Now ask which piece needs more timber. The beam, obviously — the brace sits higher up, where the triangle has already narrowed. The joining segment is always the shorter piece; the half always belongs to the join. Say "the join is the small one" before writing any number. Then rehearse the arithmetic both ways so neither direction ambushes you: join fifteen means third side thirty; third side fifteen means join seven comma five. Finally, use the sketch as an alarm rather than as evidence — if your computed value makes the short segment longer than the side it faces, you have inverted the relationship, and you have caught it before the marker did. That two-second glance regularly saves three marks.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's the clever kid's question. If the inner triangle's sides are all half as long, why is its area a quarter rather than a half? Half should give half, surely?

**Tutor:** That instinct deserves a proper dismantling, because it returns in the measurement work later this year. Length and area are different species of quantity, and a scale factor touches them differently. Picture tiling a stoep. Halve only the length of the stoep and you halve the tile count. But halve the length AND the width and the count drops to a quarter — you have halved twice, once in each direction. Area lives in two dimensions, so the scale factor applies twice: a half times a half is a quarter. The medial triangle makes this visible without any formula at all. Join the three midpoints and the original triangle shatters into four small triangles, each congruent to the others. Four identical pieces, and the inner triangle is one of them — a quarter, counted on your fingers. Carry the general principle forward: scale the sides by a factor, and the area scales by that factor squared. It is the very same idea that will later tell you why doubling every edge of a box multiplies its volume by eight, not by two — three dimensions, so the factor applies three times.
