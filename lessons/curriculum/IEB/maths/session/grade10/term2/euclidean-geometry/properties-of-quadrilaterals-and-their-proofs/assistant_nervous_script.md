Hey, it's Thandi — if proofs make your mind go blank, you are in good company. Algebra always tells you what to do next; a proof just stares back at you. But there IS a fixed starting point, and it never changes.

Start at the END. Read what you must prove, and name the rule that would finish the job. Proving a parallelogram? One of five doors gets you in — opposite sides parallel, opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals bisecting each other, or one pair of sides that is both equal and parallel. Choose the door the question has already given you keys for, and suddenly you are not lost — you are collecting.

Keep two boxes in your head, clearly labelled. Properties: what a parallelogram HAS. Conditions: what is ENOUGH to call a shape one. You may only open the properties box after the shape has earned its name. Open it early and you have assumed the very thing you were asked to prove — and markers catch that instantly.

Then the mark-saving habit: a reason beside every single line, even the obvious ones. Alternate angles, WX parallel to ZY. Diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other. Common side. Given. These phrases are learnable once and reusable forever — genuinely the cheapest marks in geometry.

And the warning that catches nearly everyone: do not believe the picture. Diagrams are rough, and sometimes deliberately misleading. A side that looks equal is not equal until something proves it. A corner that looks square is not square until something proves it.

So tonight, take our rider and rewrite it from memory: target first, then the receipts, then the conclusion. Do it twice and the pattern becomes yours. Then head straight into the practice questions — you're readier than you think!
