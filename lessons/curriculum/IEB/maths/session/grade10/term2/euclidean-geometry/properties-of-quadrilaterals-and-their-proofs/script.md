# Part 1 — Expert

Euclidean geometry is where mathematics rewards pure reasoning: marks are earned line by line for arguments, not answers. The special quadrilaterals are the first serious testing ground for that skill. This session builds three layers: the quadrilateral family and exactly what each member owns; the proofs behind the parallelogram's properties; and the conditions that allow you to prove a figure IS a parallelogram, rectangle or rhombus. A fully worked rider closes the session.

## Subtopic: The Family of Quadrilaterals

A quadrilateral is any closed figure with four sides, and its interior angles always total three hundred and sixty degrees. Each special quadrilateral is defined by what it adds to that baseline.

The TRAPEZIUM: exactly one pair of opposite sides parallel. That is the entire definition — nothing more is promised.

The PARALLELOGRAM: both pairs of opposite sides parallel. Every other fact it owns is a consequence of that single requirement.

The RECTANGLE: a parallelogram carrying one interior angle of ninety degrees. Because consecutive angles of a parallelogram are supplementary, one right angle forces the other three.

The RHOMBUS: a parallelogram with two adjacent sides equal. Since opposite sides are already equal, all four sides end up equal.

The SQUARE: a parallelogram that is simultaneously a rectangle and a rhombus — four right angles and four equal sides.

The KITE: two pairs of ADJACENT sides equal. No parallel sides are required, so the kite sits outside the parallelogram family and keeps its own rulebook.

Now the hierarchy, because it is tested directly. Every rectangle is a parallelogram. Every rhombus is a parallelogram. Every square is both a rectangle and a rhombus, hence also a parallelogram. But the flow is strictly one-directional: a rectangle need not be a rhombus, and an ordinary parallelogram is neither. The payoff of the hierarchy is inheritance — anything proved for parallelograms is instantly true for rectangles, rhombuses and squares, with no extra work.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They check the definitions word for word, and which shape inherits from which.

## Subtopic: The Parallelogram's Properties, and Where They Come From

A parallelogram owns four properties, each one a legitimate reason to quote in a proof.

One: both pairs of opposite sides are equal. Two: both pairs of opposite angles are equal. Three: the diagonals bisect each other. Four: consecutive angles are supplementary — they total one hundred and eighty degrees, being co-interior angles between parallel lines.

None of these is taken on trust. Each is proved from the definition, and the first proof is the template for all the reasoning in this grade.

Prove that the opposite sides of parallelogram WXYZ are equal. Draw the diagonal from W to Y, cutting the figure into triangle WXY and triangle YZW. Now build the chain, every statement wearing its reason.

Angle XWY equals angle ZYW — alternate angles, because WX is parallel to ZY. Angle XYW equals angle ZWY — alternate angles, because WZ is parallel to XY. Side WY equals side YW — it is common to both triangles. Two angles and a corresponding side match, so triangle WXY is congruent to triangle YZW by angle-angle-side. Congruent triangles have every corresponding part equal, so WX equals YZ and XY equals ZW. Opposite sides equal — and the equality of the opposite angles at X and at Z drops out of the same congruence at no extra cost.

Watch what the diagonal accomplished: it turned a claim about a four-sided figure into a claim about two triangles, where congruence is available as a tool. Draw the diagonal — that is the standard opening move for quadrilateral proofs.

These properties also arrive dressed in algebra. In parallelogram KLMN, angle K is four x minus twelve degrees and angle L is two x plus thirty degrees. Find all four angles. K and L are consecutive, so they are supplementary: four x minus twelve, plus two x plus thirty, equals one hundred and eighty. That is six x plus eighteen equals one hundred and eighty, so six x is one hundred and sixty-two, and x is twenty-seven. Angle K is four times twenty-seven minus twelve — ninety-six degrees. Angle L is two times twenty-seven plus thirty — eighty-four degrees. Opposite angles copy their partners, so angle M is ninety-six and angle N is eighty-four. Check the closure: ninety-six plus eighty-four plus ninety-six plus eighty-four is three hundred and sixty. Correct.

Stop now for this section's questions — the four properties stated precisely, and the congruence chain that delivers the first of them.

## Subtopic: Conditions — Proving a Figure IS a Parallelogram

A property describes what a parallelogram HAS. A condition states what is ENOUGH to conclude that a figure is one. Mixing up those two directions is the deepest error in the topic, so build the wall now: no property of a parallelogram may be used until the figure has been proved to be a parallelogram.

Five conditions exist, and any single one suffices. One: both pairs of opposite sides parallel — the definition itself. Two: both pairs of opposite sides equal. Three: both pairs of opposite angles equal. Four: the diagonals bisect each other. Five: ONE pair of opposite sides that is simultaneously equal AND parallel. The fifth is the workhorse, because it needs only two facts about one pair of sides — but it needs both facts. Equal alone proves nothing; parallel alone proves nothing.

For the rectangle, two conditions. A parallelogram with one angle of ninety degrees is a rectangle. And a parallelogram whose diagonals are EQUAL is a rectangle — memorise that one, because coordinate questions lean on it constantly.

For the rhombus, three conditions. A parallelogram with two adjacent sides equal. A parallelogram whose diagonals are PERPENDICULAR. And a parallelogram whose diagonals bisect the interior angles they pass through.

For the square, combine the sets: a parallelogram whose diagonals are equal and intersect at right angles is a square.

The kite keeps its own list, so learn it separately: two pairs of adjacent sides equal; one pair of opposite angles equal — the angles between the unequal sides; and the long diagonal acts as the axis of symmetry, cutting the short diagonal in half at right angles and bisecting the two angles it runs through.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — conditions rather than properties, and the difference between what a shape owns and what earns it its name.

## Subtopic: The Reasoning Chain — Writing a Proof That Scores

A geometry proof is marked statement by statement, and a statement without a reason beside it earns nothing. Set your work out in two columns — statements on the left, reasons on the right — and use the standard shorthand markers recognise: "opp sides of parm", "alt angles, WX parallel to ZY", "diags of parm bisect each other", "angles opp equal sides", "sum of angles of a triangle".

Now the rider. PQRS is a parallelogram whose diagonals intersect at T. Points G and H lie on the diagonal QS such that QG equals SH. Prove that PGRH is a parallelogram.

Read the target before writing anything: PGRH has vertices P, G, R and H, so its diagonals are PR and GH — and both of those pass through T. With two diagonals through one point on offer, aim at the condition "the diagonals bisect each other".

The chain. Statement: QT equals TS — reason: the diagonals of parallelogram PQRS bisect each other. Statement: PT equals TR — same reason. Statement: QG equals SH — reason: given. Statement: GT equals QT minus QG, and TH equals TS minus SH — reason: the points lie in that order along the diagonal. Therefore GT equals TH, because equal lengths were subtracted from equal lengths. Now both diagonals of PGRH are bisected at T — GT equals TH, and PT equals TR. Conclusion: PGRH is a parallelogram, because its diagonals bisect each other.

Study the architecture of that argument: name the target, select the condition you will aim at, then gather only the facts that condition demands. Learners who write forwards from everything they know produce a full page and collect a small fraction of the marks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: assuming the conclusion — quoting a parallelogram property about the very figure whose status is in question. Two: reading facts off the sketch because they look true; a diagram guides, it never testifies. Three: a statement standing without a reason, worth nothing however correct. Four: claiming congruence from angle-side-side — the equal parts must correspond, and angle-side-side is not a congruence condition.

The final questions of this part are with you now — target named, condition chosen, and a reason riding beside every statement.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same shapes, same proof — but built now from a family tree, a pair of scissors and a till slip, so the logic sits on things you already trust.

## Subtopic: The Family Where Every Child Inherits

Picture the quadrilaterals as one family, where every generation keeps everything handed down and adds exactly one thing of its own.

The grandparent is plain "four sides" — no promises. The first child is the parallelogram, whose single new rule is that both pairs of opposite sides run parallel — like the two long rails of a ladder and the rungs holding them apart. Everything else the parallelogram owns flows from that one rule for free.

The parallelogram then has two children. The rectangle adds square corners. The rhombus adds matching side lengths — all four identical. And the square, the youngest of the family, inherits from BOTH parents at once: square corners and four equal sides.

Inheritance is the working part of the picture. A rectangle is still a parallelogram, so every parallelogram fact applies to it automatically — opposite sides equal, diagonals cutting each other in half, the lot. Likewise the rhombus, likewise the square. Think of a sports team: every player wears the team jersey; the captain wears the jersey AND the armband. Spotting an armband tells you the person is on the team. Spotting only the jersey tells you nothing about the captaincy.

That is the exam trap compressed into one line: inheritance flows downward only. Every square is a rectangle; most rectangles are not squares.

And the kite? The cousin from another town — no parallel sides anywhere, just two pairs of neighbouring sides that match, exactly like a paper kite on a windy afternoon. Its rules are its own, so file it separately.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the family are coming to you right now. For each shape, name the single thing it adds to its parent.

## Subtopic: Cut It Corner to Corner

Why must opposite sides of a parallelogram be equal? Not because the drawing says so — because of something you can do with scissors.

Cut a paper parallelogram from one corner straight through to the opposite corner. You are left holding two triangles. Rotate one and place it on top of the other: they fit exactly, edge on edge, corner on corner. One triangle, printed twice. And if the two triangles are genuinely identical, the side that formed the top edge of the shape must be the same length as the side that formed the bottom edge. Opposite sides equal — revealed, not assumed.

Why do the halves match? Parallel lines. Imagine a straight footpath crossing two parallel fences on a farm. The angle the path makes at the first fence is exactly the angle it makes at the second — the two crossings are carbon copies. Inside the parallelogram, the diagonal is that footpath and the parallel sides are the fences, so the angles pair up at the two ends. Add that both triangles share the diagonal itself — one path, one length, used by both — and the triangles are forced to be identical.

That single cut hands you the parallelogram's whole toolkit: opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal, diagonals cutting each other exactly in half. Plus one more, straight from the two fences: any two neighbouring corners add up to one hundred and eighty degrees.

Put that last one to work. In a parallelogram, one corner measures four x minus twelve degrees and its neighbour measures two x plus thirty degrees. Neighbours total one hundred and eighty, so six x plus eighteen equals one hundred and eighty, six x is one hundred and sixty-two, and x is twenty-seven. The corners are ninety-six degrees and eighty-four degrees — and each opposite partner copies its twin, so the four corners run ninety-six, eighty-four, ninety-six, eighty-four. Total: three hundred and sixty. The shape closes perfectly, and you never touched a protractor.

Your questions for this part are up now. Make the corner-to-corner cut in your head first, and let the two matching triangles hand you the answer.

## Subtopic: A Proof Is a Chain of Receipts

The last piece is about writing, not shapes.

Suppose the school shop accuses you of not paying for your calculator. Saying "but I did pay" persuades nobody. Producing the till slip ends the argument instantly. A geometry proof works the same way: every line you write is a claim, and every claim must carry its receipt — the reason, written right beside it. A true statement with no reason earns zero, and no marker will fill the reason in on your behalf.

Receipts also come in a strict order. You cannot present the receipt for something you have not yet shown you own. That is exactly why you may never use a parallelogram's properties on a figure until you have first proved the figure IS a parallelogram. Name first, privileges after.

Watch it work on a real question. In parallelogram PQRS the diagonals cross at T. Two points, G and H, sit on the diagonal QS, with G as far from Q as H is from S. Show that PGRH is a parallelogram.

First decide the target. PGRH has its own two diagonals — the line PR, and the line from G to H — and both pass through T. So aim at the rule: if a quadrilateral's diagonals cut each other in half, it is a parallelogram.

Now collect receipts. In the original parallelogram the diagonals cut each other in half, so Q to T matches T to S, and P to T matches T to R. We are told G sits as far from Q as H sits from S. Trim those equal offcuts from the two equal halves, and the leftovers must match: G to T equals T to H. Both diagonals of PGRH are therefore halved at T — and by the rule we aimed at, PGRH is a parallelogram. Finished, with a receipt stapled to every step.

Three habits carry you through every rider of this kind. Announce your target before you begin. Never trust the drawing — a corner that looks square is not square until something proves it. And write a reason next to every line, including the obvious ones. Hold those three habits and geometry stops being the section you dread and becomes the section where the marks queue up for you.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: name your target, gather only the receipts that target needs, and let no claim stand without its slip. This section is yours to own.
