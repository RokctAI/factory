# Part 1 — Expert

The quadrilateral work gave us properties, conditions, and the discipline of the two-column proof. This session invests all of that in the hardest-working theorem of the grade — the midpoint theorem — then reverses it into its converse, and finishes on the riders where the two versions chain together. One hypothesis, two conclusions, and a proof that travels straight through a parallelogram.

## Subtopic: What the Midpoint Theorem Claims

State it exactly. In a triangle, take the midpoint of one side and the midpoint of a second side, and join them. The joining segment is parallel to the third side, and its length is half the length of that third side.

Before using it, read the architecture of the claim. One hypothesis — two midpoints, sitting on two DIFFERENT sides of the same triangle — purchases two independent conclusions. A conclusion about DIRECTION: the join runs parallel to the third side. And a conclusion about LENGTH: the join is half the third side, which you can equally read as the third side being double the join. In a rider you may quote either conclusion or both, all under the single reason "midpoint theorem".

The conditions are where marks leak away. Both points must genuinely be midpoints, and they must lie on two different sides. A midpoint on one side and an arbitrary point on another buys you nothing. And the side you conclude about is always the third one — the side neither midpoint touches.

Numbers immediately. In triangle XYZ, XY is eighteen centimetres, YZ is thirty centimetres, and XZ is twenty-two centimetres. M is the midpoint of XY and N is the midpoint of XZ. Then MN is parallel to YZ, and MN is half of thirty — fifteen centimetres. XM is half of eighteen, nine centimetres; XN is half of twenty-two, eleven centimetres. The perimeter of triangle XMN is nine plus fifteen plus eleven — thirty-five centimetres — against the big triangle's seventy. Exactly half, because each side of the small triangle is half of a matching side of the large one.

Push the idea one step and you meet the medial triangle: join all three midpoints of a triangle and the figure splits into four congruent smaller triangles, each with sides half the original's and each covering a quarter of the area. Riders are often built on that picture.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now. They test the statement word for word, both conclusions, and the two conditions that must hold before you may quote it.

## Subtopic: Proving It — Two Copies Make a Parallelogram

The proof itself can be asked, and it showcases the standard manoeuvre: convert a claim about a lone segment into a claim about a parallelogram.

Set up in triangle PQR, where M is the midpoint of PQ and N is the midpoint of PR. Construction: extend MN beyond N to a point K so that MN equals NK, and join K to R. You have deliberately planted a mirror copy of the segment on the far side of N.

Now the chain, statement and reason. In triangles PMN and RKN: PN equals NR, because N is the midpoint of PR. MN equals NK, by construction. Angle PNM equals angle RNK — vertically opposite angles. Two sides and the included angle match, so triangle PMN is congruent to triangle RKN, side-angle-side.

Harvest the congruence. Corresponding parts are equal, so PM equals RK, and angle PMN equals angle RKN. That second equality is the pivot: those two are alternate angles on the line MK, so PM is parallel to RK — and since Q lies on the line PM produced, MQ is parallel to KR.

Bring in the other midpoint. PM equals MQ, because M is the midpoint of PQ. But PM also equals RK, so MQ equals KR. Quadrilateral MQRK therefore has one pair of opposite sides, MQ and KR, that are both equal AND parallel — and that is exactly the condition that certifies a parallelogram.

Collect the reward. MQRK is a parallelogram, so MK is parallel to QR and MK equals QR. By construction MN is half of MK. Therefore MN is parallel to QR and MN equals half of QR. Proved.

Look at what carried the argument. The construction manufactured a congruent triangle; the congruence delivered one pair of sides equal and parallel; that pair delivered a parallelogram; and the parallelogram paid out both conclusions in one line. Nothing measured, nothing borrowed from the sketch.

Stop for this section's questions now — the construction step, the congruence condition used, and the precise reason that turns MQRK into a parallelogram.

## Subtopic: The Converse — One Midpoint and a Parallel Line

Every theorem can be turned around, and the midpoint theorem's converse appears in questions at least as often as the original. It trades a hypothesis for a conclusion.

The converse: a line drawn through the midpoint of one side of a triangle, parallel to a second side, bisects the third side.

In symbols: in triangle ABC, if P is the midpoint of AB, and PQ is drawn parallel to BC with Q on AC, then Q is the midpoint of AC — AQ equals QC.

Hold the two versions side by side, because quoting the wrong one forfeits the reason mark. The theorem receives two midpoints and concludes parallel-and-half. The converse receives one midpoint plus a parallel line and concludes a second midpoint. The reason to write for the converse is "line through midpoint parallel to second side" — markers expect that exact phrasing.

Work it with numbers. In triangle ABC, AB is sixteen centimetres and BC is twenty-six centimetres. P lies on AB with AP equal to eight centimetres, and PQ is drawn parallel to BC with Q on AC. Eight is half of sixteen, so P is the midpoint of AB. PQ is parallel to BC. By the converse, Q is the midpoint of AC — and with two midpoints now in hand, the original theorem switches on: PQ is half of BC, thirteen centimetres. Two theorems chained, the second unlocked only because the first delivered the missing midpoint.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the converse stated in full, the reason phrased the way markers want it, and what each version is given versus what each version concludes.

## Subtopic: Riders, Numbers and the Traps

A rider is marked line by line, so fix your target before writing, then collect only the facts the target needs.

The rider. In triangle KLM, X and Y are the midpoints of KL and KM, and Z is the midpoint of LM. Prove that XYZL is a parallelogram. Target first: a parallelogram, and the cheapest available condition is one pair of opposite sides both equal and parallel. Which pair? XY and LZ — because XY is exactly what the midpoint theorem describes, and LZ is half of LM.

The chain. X and Y are midpoints of KL and KM, so XY is parallel to LM and XY equals half of LM — midpoint theorem. Z is the midpoint of LM, so LZ equals half of LM — given. Therefore XY equals LZ, both being half of the same segment. And XY is parallel to LZ, because LZ lies along LM and XY is parallel to LM. One pair of opposite sides equal and parallel — XYZL is a parallelogram.

Four lines, one theorem, one condition — and notice that BOTH conclusions of the theorem were needed, which is why you write both down the moment you quote it.

Now the error museum, five exhibits. One: quoting the theorem when only ONE midpoint is given and nothing is parallel — the hypothesis fails, and everything built on it earns nothing. Two: doubling instead of halving. The joining segment is the SHORT one: if the join is fifteen, the third side is thirty; if the third side is fifteen, the join is seven comma five. Three: promoting a point to midpoint because the two halves look equal — a stated fact or a tick mark is evidence; an appearance is not. Four: assuming the second point is a midpoint in order to prove the parallel — that is the converse run in a circle. Five: a true statement with no reason beside it, worth zero.

One habit disarms all five: before using the theorem, recite "two midpoints, two different sides, third side", and confirm the question supplies every part.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the rider's target chosen before writing, both conclusions deployed, and each of the five exhibits recognised on sight.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same theorem from a building site — two sloping timbers, one cross-piece, and a length you can know before you ever pick up a tape measure.

## Subtopic: Halfway Up Two Rafters

Picture a carport going up next to a house. Two rafters lean up from opposite walls and meet at the ridge. Across the bottom, wall to wall, runs the base beam — eight metres of it.

The carpenter needs a cross-brace: one straight piece fixed from rafter to rafter to stop them sagging outwards. She measures halfway up the left rafter and marks it. Halfway up the right rafter, another mark. The brace goes between the marks.

Two things now happen, and they happen on every roof ever built this way. First, the brace sits perfectly level — exactly parallel to the base beam beneath it. Not more or less level. Exactly. Second — and this is the part that saves her a climb — the brace measures exactly four metres, half the base beam. She could have cut it at the saw bench before touching the ladder.

That is the entire theorem in work clothes: mark the middle of two sides of a triangle, join the marks, and the new piece runs parallel to the third side at exactly half its length.

Run other numbers until the pattern is automatic. Beam twelve metres: brace six. Beam nine: brace four and a half. On paper instead of timber, third side thirty centimetres: the join is fifteen. Always parallel, always half, no measuring needed. And read it in reverse, which is just as useful on site: brace measuring four metres means the beam below is eight. The join is always the short piece.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the carport are coming to you right now. For each one, decide first which piece is the long one and which is the half.

## Subtopic: Why Exactly Half

Half is suspiciously tidy, so let us see where it comes from — with nothing but imaginary scissors.

Cut the triangle along the brace. Two pieces: a small triangle from the top, and a four-sided piece below. Take the small triangle and give it a half-turn — spin it around the mark on the right-hand rafter so it swings down beside the lower piece.

Watch closely. The mark it spun around was the exact middle of that rafter, so the upper half of the rafter lands precisely on the lower half. No overlap, no gap. The small triangle clicks in next to the four-sided piece, and together they form one clean shape whose opposite sides run parallel in both pairs — a parallelogram.

Now count lengths. Along the top of that parallelogram lie the brace and its spun copy, end to end — two brace-lengths. Along the bottom lies the base beam — one beam-length. And a parallelogram's top and bottom are always equal. So two braces make one beam, which is exactly the statement that one brace is half a beam. The half was never luck. The parallel comes free from the same shape, because a parallelogram's top and bottom always run parallel.

One bonus picture: mark the middle of all three sides and join all three marks. The triangle falls into four small triangles, each one identical to the others — like a round vetkoek cut into four matching quarters. Each small triangle has sides half the original's and covers exactly a quarter of the area.

Your questions for this part are up now. See the spin and the parallelogram in your head first — the shape does the arguing for you.

## Subtopic: Spotting It Under Exam Pressure

Knowing the theorem is the easy part. Recognising that a question is asking for it — that is the skill.

Scan for the word "midpoint", or for matching tick marks showing a halved side, appearing on TWO different sides of one triangle. That is the theorem's signature. The moment you spot it, write down both gifts — the parallel and the half — even when the question seems to need only one. The other gift is usually required two lines later, and writing it early costs nothing.

Then the reversed costume, which catches people because it points the other way. A question may hand you only ONE midpoint, plus the fact that a line through it runs parallel to another side. That alone is enough: the line must land exactly on the middle of the remaining side. One midpoint and a parallel buys you a second midpoint, free of charge.

Four habits to drop, because each one costs real marks. Do not double when you should halve — the joining piece is always the short one, so whisper "the join is the small one" before writing any number. Do not invoke the theorem on one midpoint with nothing parallel; the hypothesis fails and the answer collapses. Do not appoint midpoints by eye — diagrams are drawn to be readable, not accurate. And never leave a line without its reason: "midpoint theorem" when two midpoints were given, "line through midpoint parallel to second side" when one midpoint and a parallel were given. Two short phrases, each worth its own mark.

Put everything together on one question. Triangle KLM, with X the middle of KL, Y the middle of KM, and Z the middle of LM. Show that XYZL is a parallelogram. Aim at the easiest condition: one pair of opposite sides equal and parallel. XY is half of LM and parallel to it — two midpoints, theorem applies. LZ is half of LM as well, because Z is its middle. Two halves of the same thing are equal, so XY equals LZ — and they are parallel, since LZ lies along LM itself. One pair, equal and parallel: parallelogram.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: two midpoints or one plus a parallel, the join always the short piece, and a reason beside every claim. Master these and midpoint riders turn from riddles into routine.
