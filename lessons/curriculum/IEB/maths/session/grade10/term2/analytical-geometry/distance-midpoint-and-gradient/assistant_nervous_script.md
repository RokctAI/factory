Hey, it's Thandi — I know three formulae in one lesson can feel like three separate mountains. Let me flatten them for you, because they are really one small hill.

Every one of them starts with the same move: compare the two points. How far apart are the x's? How far apart are the y's? For our points that was six across and eight up, and once you have that pair, each formula just does one thing with it. Distance squares them, adds, and square-roots — that gave us ten. Gradient divides the up by the across — eight over six, which is four over three. And midpoint is the odd one out: it does not use differences at all, it adds each pair of coordinates and halves. Say the rule once, out loud: middle adds, the other two subtract. That sentence alone prevents the most common mistake in the entire topic.

Minus signs are the second trap. Five take away negative one is six, not four. Write the brackets in every time — five minus, open bracket, negative one, close bracket — and let your handwriting do the remembering. In gradients, pick an order and keep it: if the second point's y sits on top, its x sits underneath. Swap halfway and your line falls when it should climb.

One more mark-saver: a flat line has gradient zero, but a vertical line's gradient is UNDEFINED. Not zero — there is nothing to divide by. Flat is zero, wall is undefined.

And never trust the picture. Our four points looked exactly like a rectangle, but the gradients multiplied to negative four over five, not negative one. The numbers decide, always.

Tonight, plot two points of your own and find all three things — distance, middle, steepness. Do that three times and the formulae become one routine you simply run. Now go try the practice questions — you're more ready than you feel!
