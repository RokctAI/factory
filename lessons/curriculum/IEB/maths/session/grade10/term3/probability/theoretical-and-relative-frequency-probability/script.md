# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches you to put a number on chance. Probability is the mathematics of "how likely", and it runs on two engines: theoretical probability, found by counting what COULD happen, and relative frequency, found by counting what actually DID happen. One fair eight-sector spinner and one bag of coloured marbles accompany us through the whole session, and by the end you will command the vocabulary, calculate confidently with both engines, and know precisely which engine each situation demands.

## Subtopic: The Language of Probability

The vocabulary comes first, because every probability question is written in it.

An EXPERIMENT is any action whose result is uncertain: spinning a spinner, tossing a coin, pulling a marble from a bag. An OUTCOME is one possible result of the experiment — the spinner landing on 5, for instance. The SAMPLE SPACE, written S, is the set of ALL possible outcomes. For one spin of a fair spinner with eight equal sectors numbered one to eight: S contains 1 through 8, and we write n of S equals 8 — the count of outcomes in the sample space. An EVENT, usually labelled E or A, is the set of outcomes you are interested in. The event "landing on a multiple of three" contains 3 and 6, so n of E equals 2.

Every probability lives on one fixed scale, running from 0 to 1. A probability of 0 means impossible: spinning a 9 on this spinner. A probability of 1 means certain: spinning a number below nine. Every uncertain event sits strictly between the ends, and the exact middle, 0,5, means as likely as not. You may express a probability as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage — a quarter, 0,25 and 25 percent all say the same thing — but a probability of 1,3, or a probability below zero, means nothing at all. If a calculation ever hands you a number outside the 0-to-1 scale, the scale itself is telling you to go back and find the mistake.

## Subtopic: Theoretical Probability

Engine one. When every outcome in the sample space is EQUALLY LIKELY — a fair spinner, an unbiased coin, a properly shaken bag — probability reduces to pure counting. The definition: P of E equals n of E over n of S — outcomes in the event, divided by outcomes in the sample space.

Run it on the spinner. The probability of landing on a multiple of three: the event holds 3 and 6, so n of E is 2, and the sample space holds eight sectors. P equals 2 over 8, which simplifies to a quarter, or 0,25. The probability of landing on a number greater than five: the event holds 6, 7 and 8, so P equals 3 over 8. The probability of an even number: 2, 4, 6 and 8 give 4 over 8 — exactly a half.

Now the bag, this topic's favourite prop. A bag holds 4 yellow marbles, 5 purple and 3 orange — twelve marbles in total, so n of S is 12. One marble is drawn blind. P of purple equals 5 over 12. P of yellow equals 4 over 12, which simplifies to a third. P of yellow or orange equals 7 over 12, because 4 yellows and 3 oranges make 7 favourable marbles.

Two habits protect these marks. First, count the sample space honestly: the equally-likely requirement means counting MARBLES, not colours. The bag holds three colours but twelve marbles, so the denominator is 12, never 3. Second, keep fractions exact unless told otherwise — 5 over 12 is exact; 0,42 is already an approximation — and simplify only when the arithmetic is genuinely done.

Stop for this section's questions now. Count the event, count the space, divide — and make certain that what you are counting really is equally likely.

## Subtopic: Relative Frequency

Engine two, for the world where equal likelihood cannot be assumed. Toss a drawing pin: it lands point-up or point-down, but nothing about its lopsided shape says those two results are equally likely. Theory has no numbers to offer here. The only way forward is to EXPERIMENT.

Toss the drawing pin 250 times. Suppose it lands point-up 90 times. The RELATIVE FREQUENCY of point-up is the number of occurrences divided by the number of trials: 90 over 250, which is 0,36. That value is the experimental probability — the estimate that the data offers.

Here is the essential behaviour: relative frequency SETTLES as the trials pile up. After 10 tosses the figure might be 0,6 — tiny samples lurch about. After 250 tosses, 0,36. After 2 500, perhaps 0,38. The estimate steadies as the evidence grows, and this settling is the foundation the whole topic stands on: more trials produce a more trustworthy estimate, and for a fair object the relative frequency drifts toward the theoretical value in the long run.

The same idea explains why fair objects "misbehave" over short runs. A fair coin tossed 60 times that shows 33 heads has a relative frequency of 0,55 — not 0,5. That is not evidence of a crooked coin; it is ordinary small-sample wobble. But turn it around: if 3 000 tosses produced heads 70 percent of the time, suspicion would be entirely justified — a large sample sitting far from theory is exactly how a weighted coin or a doctored spinner gets caught.

Choosing the engine: symmetric objects with equally likely outcomes — spinners, coins, dice, shaken bags — take theoretical probability. Irregular objects and real-world processes — drawing pins, bottle tops, weather, whether the bus is late — take relative frequency built from data. And when both engines can be applied to the same object, their answers should roughly agree; a large disagreement condemns the object, never the mathematics.

Pause now for this section's questions — occurrences over trials, plus judgement calls about sample size.

## Subtopic: Complementary Events

The closing tool, and the best shortcut this topic owns. The COMPLEMENT of an event A, written "not A", is the event that A fails to happen — it collects every outcome of the sample space that lies outside A. On the spinner: if A is "landing on 8", then not-A is "landing on 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 or 7".

Because A and not-A cover the entire sample space between them and share no outcomes, their probabilities must add to exactly 1: P of A plus P of not-A equals 1. Rearranged into the form you will actually use: P of not-A equals 1 minus P of A.

Put it to work. P of not landing on 8: 1 minus an eighth, which is seven eighths — no need to count seven outcomes one by one. A forecast puts the chance of rain tomorrow at 0,25: the chance of a dry day is 1 minus 0,25, which is 0,75. From the bag: P of not drawing an orange marble is 1 minus 3 twelfths, which is 9 twelfths, or three quarters — and the direct count agrees: 4 yellows plus 5 purples are 9 non-orange marbles out of 12. Both routes land together, and the shortcut earns its keep in problems where direct counting is genuinely painful.

For theory questions, quote the pair of properties behind the identity: an event and its complement are mutually exclusive — they can never both occur — and exhaustive — one of them is guaranteed to occur. That is exactly why the two probabilities total 1. This identity powers every "at least one" question waiting for you in Grade 11 and 12, so install the reflex now: the words "not" or "at least" should trigger a subtraction from 1 before any counting starts.

The whole part, compressed: name the sample space and count it; equally likely means divide the counts; irregular means run trials and divide occurrences; and the word "not" means 1 minus. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — vocabulary, both engines, and the complement shortcut.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same ideas over again now, built from a volume slider, a packet of fruit gums and a very patient afternoon of tossing a drawing pin. Nothing new is coming: the answers will land on the same quarters, thirds and 0,36. What changes is that likelihood will turn into something you can see and point at.

## Subtopic: A Scale From Impossible to Certain

Picture the volume slider on a phone. Dragged all the way left it is on MUTE — zero, nothing, silence. Dragged all the way right it is at FULL — one, everything, maximum. Every event in the world has its own slider position, and probability is nothing more mysterious than where the slider sits.

Slide some events. "The sun rises tomorrow morning" — slider hard right, at 1. "The spinner shows a 9" — slider hard left, at 0: there is no 9 to show. "A tossed coin lands heads" — dead centre, 0,5: as likely as not. "The spinner shows an even number" — also dead centre, because four of the eight sectors are even. "Rain in Durban in January" — well right of centre; "snow in Durban in January" — squashed hard against the left. Every gut feeling of "no chance", "maybe" and "definitely" is a rough slider position; probability simply swaps the feeling for a number.

The slider also polices your work. Nothing can sit left of mute — you cannot be less likely than impossible. Nothing can sit right of full — you cannot be more likely than certain. So when a calculation produces 1,3 or minus 0,2, that answer is not "unusual" — it is WRONG, and the slider has just said so. Keep the slider in your head permanently as an error alarm.

## Subtopic: Counting Your Way to a Probability

Now the fair-object engine, rebuilt with a packet of fruit gums. Twelve gums in the packet: 4 yellow, 5 purple, 3 orange. Shake the packet, shut your eyes, take one.

Only two questions matter. Question one: how many gums COULD your fingers close on? Twelve — with eyes shut and the packet shaken, every gum is an equal candidate. Question two: how many of those would make you smile, if purple is your favourite? Five. Smiles over candidates: 5 out of 12. That fraction IS the probability — 5 twelfths, a slider position just under halfway.

Every fair-object question is those two counts and one divide. Chance of an even number on the spinner: eight sectors could come up, four are even — 4 over 8, a half, slider dead centre. Chance of yellow or orange from the packet: twelve candidates, and 4 yellows plus 3 oranges make 7 happy ones — 7 over 12, just past the middle.

Now the trap the topic loves most: count THINGS, not TYPES. The packet holds three colours, and the lazy answer says "one colour out of three — a third". But the gums are the equal candidates, not the colours: purple holds five tickets in this draw, orange only three. The denominator is 12, always. Whenever a question hands you group sizes, the equal candidates are the individual objects, and the group sizes are simply a fast way of counting them.

Your questions for this section are up now. Two counts and a divide — and count gums, never colours.

## Subtopic: Try It a Hundred Times

Final idea: what to do when the object is lopsided and nothing about it is fair — and why a mountain of evidence beats a handful.

A drawing pin is nothing like a spinner. Toss it: point-up or point-down, but its shape is all on one side, so no clever counting can predict the split. When theory cannot count, experience has to count instead. So toss it, and toss it, and toss it, keeping a tally. After 250 tosses: 90 point-ups. Experience reports: point-up runs at 90 out of 250 — 0,36. That number, occurrences divided by attempts, is the relative frequency — probability measured rather than reasoned.

Feel the weight of sample size through a smaller story. Toss the pin just 5 times and see 3 point-ups: is point-up really 0,6? You would not put money on it — five tosses is a rumour, not evidence. Two hundred and fifty tosses is a report. Two and a half thousand is an audit. The wobble shrinks as the pile of tallies grows, and the value it settles on is the pin's true tendency showing itself. The same logic protects fair coins from false accusations — 33 heads in 60 tosses alarms nobody, short runs wobble — and convicts crooked ones: a coin delivering 70 percent heads across 3 000 tosses has been caught red-handed, because big samples do not drift that far from a half on their own.

So carry both engines and let the object choose. Symmetrical and fair — spinners, coins, dice, shaken packets: count and divide, no experiment required. Lopsided or unpredictable — drawing pins, bottle tops, weather, whether the bus arrives on time: gather data and divide occurrences by attempts. And whenever you CAN run both, the two answers should settle into the same neighbourhood — theory predicting, experience confirming.
