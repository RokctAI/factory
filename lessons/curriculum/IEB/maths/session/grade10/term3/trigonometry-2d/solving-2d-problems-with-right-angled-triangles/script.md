# Part 1 — Expert

This session turns the three trigonometric ratios you already own into a working problem-solving machine. Heights of masts, distances to cars below a cliff, angles of leaning ladders — every one of them is a right-angled triangle dressed up in a story. One communications mast travels through the whole session, a second triangle joins it near the end, and by the close you will convert any two-dimensional description into a labelled sketch and an exact, unit-carrying answer.

## Subtopic: Angles of Elevation and Depression

Two definitions carry this entire topic, and both begin at the same place: the horizontal.

The angle of elevation is the angle between the horizontal line through your eye and your line of sight when you look UP at something — the top of a mast, an aircraft, a drone. Eye level first, then the tilt upward; the tilt IS the angle of elevation.

The angle of depression is the angle between the horizontal line through your eye and your line of sight when you look DOWN — from a clifftop to a car on the beach road, from a rooftop to a gate. Horizontal first, then the tilt downward; the tilt is the angle of depression. The single most common error in this topic is measuring depression from the vertical face of the cliff instead of from the horizontal. Depression hangs BELOW the horizontal — always.

Now the fact that unlocks half the questions ever set: the angle of depression from the top, down to an object, EQUALS the angle of elevation from that object, up to the top. The reason is pure geometry: the two horizontals — one through the observer's eye, one along the ground — are parallel lines, and the line of sight is a transversal cutting both. Alternate angles between parallel lines are equal. So the moment a question hands you an angle of depression, you may mark the equal angle of elevation inside the triangle at ground level, where the known sides usually live. And write the reason down — alternate angles, parallel lines — because that geometry statement carries credit of its own.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. They test the two definitions and the alternate-angle link: measure from the horizontal, every single time.

## Subtopic: The Three-Step Method — Sketch, Label, Choose the Ratio

The case study begins. A surveyor stands 35 metres from the base of a communications mast and measures the angle of elevation to the top as 28 degrees. Find the height of the mast.

Step one: sketch. Draw the vertical mast, the horizontal ground, and the line of sight from the surveyor to the top. The right angle sits where the mast meets the ground — vertical against horizontal is always 90 degrees. Mark 28 degrees at the surveyor's position, between the ground and the line of sight.

Step two: label the sides FROM THE ANGLE'S POINT OF VIEW. The side opposite the 28 degrees is the mast — the height we want; call it h. The side adjacent, running along the ground from the surveyor to the base, is 35 metres. The hypotenuse is the line of sight, and this question never mentions it.

Step three: choose the ratio connecting what you HAVE to what you WANT. We have the adjacent, we want the opposite — tangent territory: tan of the angle equals opposite over adjacent. So tan 28 degrees equals h over 35.

Solve: h equals 35 times tan 28 degrees. Calculator in DEGREE mode — confirm the little D on the screen before touching anything else. The value of tan 28 degrees is 0,5317 to four decimals, and 35 times that is 18,61 near enough. The height is 18,6 metres, rounded to one decimal place. And state the unit — a bare 18,6 is an unfinished answer.

The discipline to memorise: sketch, label from the angle, choose the ratio by have-and-want. Three steps, every problem, no exceptions.

Stop for this section's questions now — they walk the same three steps on the same mast. Execute exactly as demonstrated.

## Subtopic: Finding an Angle and Finding a Distance

Same machine, two new gears: sometimes the angle is the unknown, and sometimes a ground distance is.

First, the unknown angle. A 6-metre ladder leans against a wall with its foot 1,8 metres from the base of the wall. What angle does the ladder make with the ground? Work from the angle at the foot: the ladder itself is the hypotenuse, 6; the ground distance is the adjacent, 1,8. Adjacent with hypotenuse — cosine. Cos of theta equals 1,8 over 6, which is 0,3. Now the reverse move: theta equals the inverse cosine of 0,3. On the calculator: shift, then cos, then 0,3 — giving 72,54 degrees, so 72,5 degrees to one decimal. The inverse function is the only road from a ratio back to an angle; no amount of dividing will take you there.

Second, the unknown distance, with a depression built in. From the top of a vertical cliff 45 metres high, the angle of depression to a car on the beach road is 32 degrees. How far is the car from the base of the cliff? Deploy the alternate-angle fact at once: the angle of elevation from the car up to the clifftop is also 32 degrees. In the triangle at the car's corner: opposite is the cliff, 45; adjacent is the distance we want, d. Tangent again: tan 32 degrees equals 45 over d. Rearrange — d equals 45 over tan 32 degrees. The value of tan 32 degrees is 0,6249; forty-five divided by that is 72,01 and a whisker. The car is 72,0 metres from the base.

Study the shape of that rearrangement: when the unknown sits in the BOTTOM of the ratio, you divide by the tan — you do not multiply. Write the equation first and rearrange second; guessing the operation is exactly where marks leak away.

Pause now for this section's questions. One asks for an angle, one for a distance — let the have-and-want reading pick the ratio, and let algebra do the rearranging.

## Subtopic: Two Triangles, One Height

The distinction-level question: two observations of the same object from two different positions.

From point P, the angle of elevation to the top of a water tower is 35 degrees. Walking 25 metres straight towards the tower brings you to point Q, where the angle of elevation is 55 degrees. Find the height of the tower.

The strategy in one sentence: both triangles SHARE the same height, so write the height twice — once from each triangle — and set the two expressions against each other.

Let d be the distance from Q, the nearer point, to the base. From Q: tan 55 degrees equals h over d, so h equals d times tan 55 degrees. From P, which stands d plus 25 from the base: tan 35 degrees equals h over the quantity d plus 25, so h equals the quantity d plus 25, times tan 35 degrees.

One height, two expressions — equate them: d times tan 55 degrees equals the quantity d plus 25, times tan 35 degrees. Expand the right side, gather the d terms on the left: d tan 55 minus d tan 35 equals 25 tan 35. Factor out d: d times the quantity tan 55 minus tan 35, equals 25 tan 35. Divide: d equals 25 tan 35 over the quantity tan 55 minus tan 35.

Numbers, full decimals carried to the end: tan 55 is 1,4281; tan 35 is 0,7002. Numerator: 25 times 0,7002 is 17,505. Denominator: 1,4281 minus 0,7002 is 0,7279. Divide: d is 24,05 metres. Then the height: h equals d times tan 55 — 24,05 times 1,4281 — which is 34,3 metres to one decimal.

Verify through the other triangle: 24,05 plus 25 is 49,05; times tan 35, which is 0,7002, gives 34,35 — the same 34,3. When both triangles report the same height, the answer is beyond argument.

The compressed method: assign d to the NEAREST point, write h from each triangle, equate, factor, divide, and verify through the triangle you did not use. The final questions of this part are with you now — same structure, and the shared height is always the bridge.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same problems once more, rebuilt slowly from the ground up — from where your eyes actually point. Nothing new arrives in this half: the mast will still be 18,6 metres, the car still 72 metres out. What changes is that you will feel WHY each step happens.

## Subtopic: Looking Up, Looking Down — Everything Starts Level

Stand in the middle of a sports field and stare straight ahead at the far goalposts — dead level, no tilt. That imaginary level line through your eyes is the starting line for every angle in this topic.

A drone hovers above the field. To watch it, your chin lifts and your eyes rotate UP from that level line. The amount of turn — that is the angle of elevation. Small tilt, drone far and low; big tilt, drone nearly overhead. Elevation is simply "how far up from level".

Now climb to the top row of the grandstand and look down at a friend standing on the field. Your eyes start level and rotate DOWN. That amount of turn is the angle of depression — "how far down from level". Not from the front wall of the stand, not from your shoes: from LEVEL.

And a beautiful secret ties the two together. Your level line at the top of the stand and the flat field below are parallel — level lines never meet. Your line of sight to your friend is one straight line crossing both. Geometry promises that the angle below your level line equals the angle your line of sight makes with the field at your friend's feet. In human terms: the angle you look DOWN at your friend equals the angle your friend looks UP at you. Perfect symmetry — two people making eye contact along one straight line. That single fact lets you slide any depression angle down into the ground triangle, where the measurements actually live.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the two angles are coming to you right now. Ask one thing each time: up from level, or down from level?

## Subtopic: One Triangle at a Time

The mast again, in slow motion. Thirty-five metres from the base, looking up at 28 degrees.

Build the picture from three lines only. Line one: the mast, standing straight up. Line two: the ground, flat, from your feet to the base. Line three: your line of sight, slanting from your eyes to the top. A triangle — and the corner where the mast meets the ground is a perfect square corner, because upright against flat is always 90 degrees.

Now stand INSIDE the 28-degree angle and look around the triangle. The side straight across from you — the one a thrown ball would hit — is the OPPOSITE: the mast. The side you are touching along the ground is the ADJACENT: 35 metres. The slanted one is the HYPOTENUSE — always across from the square corner, always the longest.

Which ratio? Say the memory phrase slowly — SOH CAH TOA. Sine is opposite over hypotenuse. Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse. Tangent is opposite over adjacent. We know the adjacent, we want the opposite — that pair appears only in TOA. Tangent. So tan 28 equals the mast over 35, and the mast equals 35 times tan 28 — about 18,6 metres. Roughly a four-storey building, which sounds right for a mast seen at a gentle tilt. Always ask whether the size sounds like the picture.

A zipline hands us one more ratio for balance. A 40-metre cable runs taut from the ground to a platform at an elevation of 25 degrees — how high is the platform? The cable is the slanted line — the hypotenuse, 40. The height is opposite the angle. Opposite with hypotenuse — that pair lives in SOH: sine. Height equals 40 times sin 25, which is 40 times 0,4226 — about 16,9 metres.

Your questions for this section are up now. For each one, do only this: find the square corner, name the three sides from the angle, and let the two sides in the story choose the ratio for you.

## Subtopic: Reading the Story Into a Sketch

Last skill, and it is the one that truly decides marks: turning words into the right picture. The question gives you a paragraph; you answer with a triangle.

The reading habits, one at a time. Habit one: find something vertical and something horizontal. A mast, cliff, wall or tower is your vertical line; the ground, the field or the sea is your horizontal; the right angle lives where they meet. You never hunt for the right angle — the story builds it for you.

Habit two: the angle sits with the OBSERVER. "From a point 35 metres away" — the angle goes at that point, never at the top of the mast. "From the top of the cliff, the angle of depression" — the angle goes at the top, hanging below a small level line you draw through the observer's eye. Sketch that level line every time depression appears; it is the anchor the angle hangs from.

Habit three: translate the words into sides before touching the calculator. "How far from the base" — the horizontal side. "How high" — the vertical side. "The ladder", "the cable", "the line of sight" — the hypotenuse. Once every phrase owns a side, the have-and-want pair selects sine, cosine or tangent all by itself.

And habit four: after the answer, read the picture back. A mast of 18,6 metres — sensible. A mast of 1 860 metres — the calculator was in the wrong mode or the ratio was upside down; no mast stands taller than a mountain. A car 72 metres out from a 45-metre cliff at a 32-degree tilt — a gentle angle, the car further out than the cliff is tall; the picture nods in agreement.

You now own the entire machine: level lines, the three-step method, the alternate-angle bridge, and the two-triangle trick waiting at the summit of the topic. The final questions of the lesson are arriving right now — sketch first, label from the angle, and let the triangle carry the weight. You have earned this.
