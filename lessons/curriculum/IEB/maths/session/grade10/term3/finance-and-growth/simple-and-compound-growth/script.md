# Part 1 — Expert

This session is about the two growth formulae that sit behind every savings account, every hire-purchase contract and every rising price you will ever meet: simple growth and compound growth. One investment travels with us for the whole lesson — R4 000 deposited at 7 percent per annum for 3 years — and by the end you will be able to produce both final amounts, account for the difference between them to the cent, and identify on sight which formula a scenario is asking for.

## Subtopic: The Cast of Symbols and the Two Kinds of Growth

Begin by writing down the two formulae, because everything else hangs off them. Simple growth: A equals P, times the quantity one plus i n. Compound growth: A equals P, times the quantity one plus i, all raised to the power n.

Now define every symbol with care, since the substitution marks live or die on these definitions. P is the principal — the amount you start with. A is the accumulated amount — what the money has become at the end. The letter n counts the number of periods, which for us means years. And i is the interest rate per period, written as a DECIMAL. Underline that word. Seven percent goes into the formula as 0,07 — seven hundredths — and never as 7. If you substitute 7 where 0,07 belongs, every rand in your answer is wrong by a factor of one hundred, and the damage runs to the last line of the question. So build the habit now: the instant you read a rate, divide it by 100, write the decimal next to it, and work only from the decimal.

The two formulae describe two genuinely different agreements about interest. Under simple growth, interest is calculated on the ORIGINAL amount only — the same calculation on the same base, repeated every year. That is why i multiplied by n sits together inside the bracket: one year's interest, scaled up by the number of years. Under compound growth, interest is calculated on the original amount PLUS every cent of interest already earned. Each year's interest is folded into the balance, and the next year's percentage is taken of that bigger balance — which is why the bracket carries a power: the growth factor is applied afresh, once for every year.

Be able to state the difference in a single sentence, because that sentence is routinely worth a mark: simple interest is calculated on the principal alone; compound interest is calculated on the principal together with all accumulated interest — interest earning interest.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test the symbols and that one-sentence distinction — answer with the definitions exactly as they were stated.

## Subtopic: Simple Growth — Executing A = P(1 + in)

Our travelling example, simple version first. R4 000 is invested at 7 percent per annum simple interest for 3 years. Find A.

Set out the solution the way marks are earned — one visible line per step. Line one, quote the formula: A equals P times one plus i n. Line two, list the data with the rate already converted: P is 4 000; i is 7 over 100, which is 0,07; n is 3. Line three, substitute so that every value can be seen: A equals 4 000 times the quantity one plus 0,07 times 3.

Now the arithmetic, in the correct order: work inside the bracket first, and inside the bracket, multiplication before addition. The product 0,07 times 3 is 0,21. Adding the one gives 1,21. So A equals 4 000 times 1,21.

Stop and read that 1,21 properly, because it is more than a number on the way to the answer: it says the investment has become 121 percent of itself — the full original 100 percent, plus 7 percent collected three separate times. The bracket is the growth factor, and learning to read it is learning the whole topic.

Multiply out: 4 000 times 1,21 is 4 840. The accumulated amount is R4 840 — and write the rand symbol, because units are part of a full answer; a bare 4 840 is an incomplete final line.

Check the answer by pulling it apart. The interest earned is 4 840 minus 4 000 — R840 across three years, which is R280 per year, and R280 is exactly 7 percent of R4 000. Every year paid the identical R280, computed on the untouched original principal. Equal annual payments on P are the signature of simple growth — straight-line growth, the same step every year. If your simple-interest answer will not break into equal annual pieces of the principal, your substitution went wrong somewhere. This check costs half a minute and catches almost everything.

Pause here for this section's questions. They ask you to run the same layout and to read the bracket as a growth factor — work exactly as demonstrated and let the equal yearly payments confirm you.

## Subtopic: Compound Growth — Executing A = P(1 + i)ⁿ

Identical data, compound version: R4 000 at 7 percent per annum, compounded annually, for 3 years.

Same disciplined layout. Formula: A equals P times the quantity one plus i, to the power n. Data: P is 4 000; i is 0,07; n is 3. Substitution: A equals 4 000 times 1,07 to the power 3.

Look carefully at where the 3 now lives: it has become an EXPONENT, not a multiplier. The most damaging structural error in this topic is writing 4 000 times one plus 0,07 times 3 underneath a compound heading — that is the simple formula in disguise, and once it appears, every mark after the formula line is gone. The power is the entire meaning: 1,07 cubed is 1,07 times 1,07 times 1,07 — the growth factor applied three separate times, each application working on a balance that has already grown.

Compute the factor step by step: 1,07 squared is 1,1449; multiply by 1,07 again and you get 1,225043. Then A equals 4 000 times 1,225043, which is 4 900,17 when rounded to cents. The accumulated amount is R4 900,17. On your calculator, go straight to the power key — 1,07, power, 3 — and carry the full string of decimals all the way to the final line. Rounding the factor early to 1,23 gives R4 920 and throws away an accuracy mark. Money is rounded to two decimals once, at the very end.

Now the comparison that this pair of answers exists to teach. Simple: R4 840. Compound: R4 900,17. The gap is R60,17 — and you must be able to say exactly where every cent of it was born. Year one is identical under both schemes: 7 percent of 4 000 is R280. In year two, however, compound pays 7 percent of 4 280 — that is R299,60 — while simple still pays its flat R280: the extra R19,60 is interest earned BY year one's interest. Year three widens the gap: 7 percent of 4 579,60 is R320,57, which runs R40,57 ahead of simple's R280. Add the surpluses: 19,60 plus 40,57 is the R60,17. Interest on interest, traced coin by coin rather than merely named — an answer that can point at the R19,60 and the R40,57 is an answer operating at the top of the topic.

The questions for this section are ready for you now — they dress this same comparison in new clothing. Take the same route every time: both formulae, both totals, and the gap explained by what the rate was charged on.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Formula and Working Backwards

The wording of a scenario selects the formula, and the conventions are fixed by the syllabus. Hire purchase — the fridge, the phone, the lounge suite bought "on terms" — is worked at SIMPLE interest; that is the standing convention for this topic. Inflation, savings and investments compounding annually, and population growth all take COMPOUND growth, because in each of them the quantity grows on top of its own previous growth. Train your ear: "simple interest" or "hire purchase" points to A equals P times one plus i n; "compound", "compounded annually", "inflation" or "population" points to the power formula.

Two applications, fully worked. Hire purchase: a laptop priced at R12 000 is bought over 3 years at 10 percent per annum simple interest. A equals 12 000 times one plus 0,10 times 3 — the bracket is 1,30 — so the total payable is R15 600. The monthly instalment spreads that over 36 months: 15 600 divided by 36 is R433,33. Inflation: a taxi ride costs R15 today; at 5 percent annual inflation, the fare in 4 years is 15 times 1,05 to the power 4. The factor 1,05 to the fourth is 1,215506 to six decimals, so the fare becomes R18,23. Different stories, same machinery.

Now learn to drive the formula in reverse, because questions frequently hand you the ending and ask for the beginning. What principal must be deposited today at 5 percent compound interest to grow to R8 000 in 3 years? The unknown is P. Quote the formula and substitute everything known: 8 000 equals P times 1,05 to the power 3. Evaluate the factor: 1,05 cubed is 1,157625. Divide both sides to isolate P: P equals 8 000 over 1,157625, which is R6 910,70. Then verify by driving forwards: 6 910,70 times 1,157625 lands on 8 000, to the cent. A reverse answer that has been checked forwards cannot be argued with.

Here is the whole method, compressed. One: classify the scenario — simple or compound — and quote the matching formula. Two: list P, i as a decimal, and n. Three: substitute visibly. Four: compute bracket first, exponent before multiplication, full decimals throughout. Five: state the answer in rands, two decimals, units included. Six: verify — break simple answers into equal yearly payments; rebuild compound answers year by year, or run reverse answers forwards. The deep takeaway to carry into Grade 11 and 12: the bracket is a growth factor, and compound growth feeds that factor its own output. Read the formulae as factors instead of recipes and every later variation — depreciation, changing rates, timelines — becomes the same two moves again.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now. Classify first, then execute: quote the formula the scenario demands, substitute in full view, and verify the way you have just been shown.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same two formulae now, rebuilt from the ground floor — slowly, with actual rands visible at every step. Nothing new is arriving: the totals will land precisely where the first part left them. What changes is that this time you will watch WHY they land there.

## Subtopic: Two Pictures — the Bicycle and the Hens

Put the formulas away for a few minutes. Two pictures first.

Picture one. You own a bicycle, and your neighbour rents it every month for a flat R280. Fixed arrangement. One month, R280. Three months, three times R280 — R840. The payment never budges, because it is calculated on the same thing every time: the use of one bicycle. Next year, still R280. That is the first kind of growth: the same fixed amount arriving again and again. Steady, predictable — you could tally ten years of it in your head.

Picture two. Your aunt keeps six hens. The hens lay eggs, and she could sell every egg — but she does something smarter: she lets some eggs HATCH. The chicks grow into hens, and now more hens are laying. Those extra eggs hatch too, and the flock grows faster than it did the year before. Her flock is not growing by the same amount each year — it grows by MORE each year, because the growth itself has started producing. The hens are raising hens.

Keep both pictures in your head. The bicycle rent is simple growth: the extra money never joins the working side of the deal; every payment is computed on the original arrangement. The hen coop is compound growth: every bit of growth joins the flock and gets to work. Same starting point, wildly different tenth years — and this whole lesson is just these two pictures wearing numbers.

Quick check before we move on — a few questions on the pictures are coming to you right now. No formulas needed yet: for each situation, simply decide whether the money is renting out a bicycle or hatching hens.

## Subtopic: The Same R4 000, Built Year by Year

Now the travelling example from the first part — R4 000 at 7 percent for 3 years — with no formulas at all. Just honest arithmetic, one year at a time.

The bicycle way first — simple interest. Seven percent of R4 000 is R280. That R280 is this deal's monthly-rent equivalent: it arrives every year, calculated on the original R4 000 every single time. Year one: R280. Year two: R280 again — the deal is blind to how big your balance has become. Year three: R280 once more. Total interest: three lots of R280, which is R840. Final amount: 4 000 plus 840 — R4 840. Write the three identical R280s in a row and stare at them: THAT is what the word simple means.

Now the hen-coop way — compound interest. Year one: 7 percent of 4 000 is R280, exactly as before. But now the move that changes everything: the R280 does not sit outside the deal — it JOINS the balance. You now have R4 280 working for you. Year two: 7 percent of 4 280 — not of 4 000 — which is R299,60. Spot the extra R19,60? That is interest earned by last year's R280. The interest hatched. New balance: R4 579,60. Year three: 7 percent of 4 579,60 is R320,57. Balance: 4 579,60 plus 320,57 — R4 900,17.

Set the two finals side by side: R4 840 the bicycle way, R4 900,17 the hen-coop way. The R60,17 difference is not a trick — you watched it arrive in pieces: R19,60 extra in year two, R40,57 extra in year three, both produced by interest that had joined the flock.

Only NOW do the formulas appear — because they are nothing but this arithmetic moving quickly. The bicycle way, three equal payments of i times P, compresses into A equals P times one plus i n. The hen-coop way, multiplying by 1,07 three times over, compresses into A equals P times one plus i to the power n. And if a test ever wipes both formulas from your memory, you have just seen the escape route: rebuild the deal year by year, exactly as we did — slower, but impossible to break. A formula is only arithmetic you already own, in a hurry. And notice what you just did without help: compound interest, by hand.

Your questions for this part are up now. Rebuild the years in each one just as we did — take the balance, find 7 percent of it, add it on — and watch the correct answer assemble itself in front of you.

## Subtopic: Which Deal Is in Front of You?

One skill remains: recognising which picture a question is showing you — because no question will ever tell you "use the hen-coop method".

Listen for the shape of the deal. Hire purchase — the stove, the phone, the couch "on terms" — is the BICYCLE deal: simple interest, by the standing convention of this topic. The shop calculates all the interest once, on the original price, then divides the total into flat monthly payments. A savings account "compounded annually" is the HEN-COOP deal — the bank genuinely adds your interest to your balance, and next year pays interest on the enlarged total. Inflation is a hen-coop too: each year's price increase lands on top of prices that had already risen — which is why the bus fare your grandfather paid sounds like a joke. Population growth: children grow up and have children of their own — the growth itself grows. Hen coop.

Run the reflex on three quick ones. "A loan at 10 percent per annum simple interest" — the label is printed on it: bicycle deal, bracket formula. "Savings growing at 5 percent compounded yearly" — hen coop, power formula. "The taxi fare rises by 5 percent every year" — each rise stands on the previous one: hen coop.

Carry one honest warning out of this lesson: the hen coop is glorious when the money is YOURS — savings, investments — and merciless when the money is OWED, because debts hatch interest in exactly the same way. Same mathematics, pointing in both directions.

You now own both pictures, both formulas, and — the part no test can confiscate — the year-by-year arithmetic underneath them. The last questions of the lesson are reaching you right now: listen for each deal's shape, match it to its picture, and finish strong. You have done the real work already.
