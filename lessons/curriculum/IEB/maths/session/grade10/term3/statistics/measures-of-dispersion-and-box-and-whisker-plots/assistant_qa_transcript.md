**Thandi:** Question round before we close — and today's pile is all about the folds. First up: with our eleven juice days, the median person held 13 and joined neither half. But what happens with an EVEN number of values? A few of us tried ten values at home and got stuck.

**Tutor:** The even case follows one clean adjustment. With ten ordered values there is no single middle person: the median is the mean of the fifth and sixth values — a number that may belong to nobody. Then the halves are beautifully simple: the lower half is exactly the first five values and the upper half the last five, because the median line falls BETWEEN people rather than on one. Q1 is the middle of that lower five, Q3 the middle of the upper five. So remember the pairing: odd count — the middle person exists and stands on the fence, joining neither half; even count — the fence falls between people, and everybody joins a half. Practise once with each and the confusion never returns.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the one I got asked three times: why do we even need the IQR when the range is so much quicker? One subtraction and you're done.

**Tutor:** Quickness is precisely the range's temptation and its trap. The range consults exactly two witnesses: the most extreme day on each end. If either witness is a freak — the day the fridge broke, the heat wave that sold thirty-three cups — the range reports the drama as if it were the whole story. The IQR interviews the middle half of all the days: it measures how wide ORDINARY is, from crease to crease, where no freak can reach. Our juice data says it plainly: range 30, IQR 16 — the extremes stretch almost twice as wide as the everyday days. Report the range when asked, but when a dataset carries wild values, add the IQR and say why: it is resistant to outliers. That one justification sentence is a mark waiting for you in nearly every spread question.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the subtle one from the break: two learners looked at the same box plot. One said the long right whisker means "lots of busy days". The other said it means "busy days vary a lot". Who's right?

**Tutor:** The second learner — and the difference between them is the single most-tested idea in this topic. Every section of a box plot, whisker or half-box, holds the SAME quarter of the data. Always. So the long right whisker from 24 to 33 does not hold more days than the stubby left whisker from 3 to 8 — both hold about a quarter of the eleven days. What the length shows is how widely that quarter is scattered: the quiet quarter's days are packed into five units, all alike; the busy quarter's days sprawl across nine units, from decent to record-breaking. Length is spread, never count. Carry the first learner's mistake with you as a warning — it is the exact wrong answer the multiple-choice options are built around.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's about the future: my cousin in Grade 11 keeps talking about outlier fences and something called standard deviation. Should we be worried that this lesson isn't the full story?

**Tutor:** Not worried — prepared, because everything ahead is built directly on today's folds. In Grade 11 the quartiles you found today get a promotion: they build formal fences, set one and a half IQRs beyond each quartile, and any value outside the fences is officially declared an outlier — no more squinting. Standard deviation arrives as a finer wandering-meter: where the IQR watches only the middle half, standard deviation asks every single value how far it strays from the mean and averages the answer. Different tool, same question — how far does the data wander? So nothing you learned today expires. The five-number summary, the box, the quarter-per-section rule: these remain in the toolkit all the way through your final year. Master the folds now, and next year's fences will feel like a friend extending a hand rather than a stranger.
