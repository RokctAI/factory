# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the arithmetic of "typical": the mean, the median and the mode — the three measures of central tendency that squeeze an entire dataset into one representative number. A dataset of reading minutes drives the first half of the session, a grouped frequency table takes over in the second, and by the end you will compute all three measures, defend your choice between them, and estimate a mean that cannot be computed exactly.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median and Mode for Ungrouped Data

The running dataset: nine learners recorded how many minutes each spent reading last night. In order: 10, 13, 16, 16, 19, 22, 24, 27 and 33.

The mean first — the share-out-equally number. Add every value and divide by how many values there are. The sum: 10 plus 13 is 23; plus 16 is 39; plus 16 is 55; plus 19 is 74; plus 22 is 96; plus 24 is 120; plus 27 is 147; plus 33 is 180. Nine values, so the mean is 180 over 9 — exactly 20 minutes. Interpretation: if all the reading were pooled and shared perfectly evenly, each learner would sit at 20 minutes.

The median next — the middle value of the ORDERED list. Order first, always: an unordered median is meaningless, and questions supply shuffled data on purpose to catch the hasty. Nine values put the middle at the fifth position — four below, four above. Count in: 10, 13, 16, 16, and the fifth is 19. The median is 19 minutes. For an EVEN number of values there is no single middle value, so you take the mean of the two middle ones — park that rule; it returns in a moment.

The mode last — the value appearing MOST often. Only 16 appears twice; every other value appears once. The mode is 16 minutes. A dataset can carry one mode, two modes, or no mode at all when nothing repeats.

Three answers from one dataset: mean 20, median 19, mode 16. Three different numbers, each with a legitimate claim to "the centre" — which is exactly why the next section exists.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. Order the data before anything else, and keep the three definitions strictly apart.

## Subtopic: Outliers and Choosing the Right Measure

A tenth learner's time now arrives late: 130 minutes — over two hours. Watch what this single extreme value, an OUTLIER, does to each measure.

The mean: the sum leaps from 180 to 310, and dividing by ten gives 31 minutes. Now read that number against the room: nine of the ten learners sit BELOW 31 — the "typical" value has become typical of nobody. The mean was dragged, because the mean feels every value; one giant hauls the share-out upward exactly the way one very tall learner lifts a class's average height.

The median: ten values now, so the median is the mean of the fifth and sixth values — 19 and 22 — which is 20,5 minutes. It scarcely moved. The median responds only to position in the ordered line, never to size; the outlier is merely one more body at the end of the queue. We say the median is RESISTANT to outliers.

The mode: still 16. The repetition pattern never changed.

The examination skill here is choosing and JUSTIFYING. Roughly symmetrical data with no extremes: the mean is best, because it uses every value. Outliers or strong skew present — house prices, incomes, that 130-minute reader: report the median, and say why — the median is unaffected by extreme values, while the mean is pulled toward them. The mode earns its place with category-style data — the most popular cooldrink flavour a tuck shop stocks — where adding and averaging are nonsense. One sentence of justification is one mark; memorise its shape: "the median, because the data contains an outlier which distorts the mean."

Stop for this section's questions now — they hand you datasets with and without extremes and ask you to choose a measure, and to defend the choice.

## Subtopic: Grouped Data and the Estimated Mean

Real datasets are often too large to list, so they arrive GROUPED. Fifty learners wrote a test, marked as a percentage, and the results come as a frequency table with five class intervals. From 40 up to but not including 50: six learners. From 50 to below 60: twelve. From 60 to below 70: thirteen. From 70 to below 80: fourteen. From 80 to below 90: five. Check the frequency total before anything else: 6 plus 12 plus 13 plus 14 plus 5 is 50 — a miscounted table poisons every calculation built on it.

Now face the honest problem: the table has destroyed the individual marks. Fourteen learners sit somewhere between 70 and 80, but nobody knows where. An exact mean is impossible — so we estimate, using one fair assumption: treat every learner in an interval as sitting at that interval's MIDPOINT. The midpoint of 40 to 50 is 45 — add the endpoints and halve. The midpoints run 45, 55, 65, 75, 85.

The estimated mean: multiply each midpoint by its frequency, add, divide by the total. Six times 45 is 270. Twelve times 55 is 660. Thirteen times 65 is 845. Fourteen times 75 is 1 050. Five times 85 is 425. The grand total: 270 plus 660 is 930; plus 845 is 1 775; plus 1 050 is 2 825; plus 425 is 3 250. Divide by 50: the estimated mean is 65 percent.

And say the word ESTIMATED in your final line. The true mean of the fifty actual marks is almost certainly not exactly 65 — the midpoint assumption is a fair guess, never a fact — and marking guidelines reward the candidate who shows they know the difference.

Pause now for this section's questions. Midpoints, products, total, divide — and audit that frequency column before trusting a single digit of it.

## Subtopic: Modal Interval, Median Interval and Reading the Table

Grouped data cannot deliver an exact mode or median either — it delivers intervals, and finding them costs no arithmetic at all, only disciplined reading.

The MODAL interval is the class with the highest frequency. Scan the frequency column: 6, 12, 13, 14, 5. The largest is 14, so the modal interval is 70 up to below 80. Careful: the ANSWER is the interval, naming the class where marks pile up thickest — writing the frequency 14 instead of the interval is a classically thrown-away mark.

The MEDIAN interval is the class containing the middle of the ordered data. Fifty values: the median sits between the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth values. Locate them with the running total called cumulative frequency. After the first class: 6. After the second: 6 plus 12 is 18. After the third: 18 plus 13 is 31. Stop — the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth values both live inside that third class, because the count stood at only 18 before it and reached 31 by its end. The median interval is 60 up to below 70.

Assemble the full report, because that is what a complete question demands: estimated mean 65 percent; modal interval 70 to below 80; median interval 60 to below 70. Notice the disagreement — the mean sits below the modal class because the tail of weaker marks in the forties and fifties drags it down. Being able to SAY that — the low scores pull the mean beneath the mode — is the analysis sentence that separates a top script from a mere computation script.

The compressed method for any grouped table: audit the frequency total; midpoints for the estimated mean; the largest frequency names the modal interval; cumulative frequency locates the median interval; one sentence comparing them. The final questions of this part are with you now — a different table, the identical five moves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three measures once more, rebuilt from scenes you already know — pooling airtime, tapping the middle shoulder in a queue, spotting the tuck shop's bestseller. Nothing new arrives: the answers will land on the same 20, 19 and 16. What changes is that each measure becomes a picture you cannot forget.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Say Typical

Picture one: the pool-and-split. Nine friends each read for a while last night — one managed 10 minutes, one managed 33. Pour ALL the minutes into one shared pool: 180 minutes. Now split the pool perfectly equally: 180 split among 9 is 20 minutes each. That is the MEAN — what everyone would have if the effort were communal. It is the only measure that touches every single value; that is its power, and in a moment you will watch it become its weakness.

Picture two: the queue. Ask the nine learners to form a queue, ordered by reading time, shortest at the front. Walk to the person standing dead centre — four ahead of them, four behind — and tap their shoulder. Ask their time: 19 minutes. That is the MEDIAN — the value of the middle position. Nothing was added; nothing was divided. You ordered, you pointed, you were done.

Picture three: the bestseller. The tuck shop does not ask for the average flavour of cooldrink — an average of orange and cola is not a drink anyone can buy. It asks which flavour sells MOST. Count repeats in our data and only 16 minutes occurs twice: 16 is the mode, the bestseller of the dataset. Whenever a question wants the most common thing rather than a balanced summary — the shoe size a shop should restock, the jersey size a team should order — that is mode thinking.

Three pictures: the pool, the queue, the bestseller. Every question in this topic is one of these three, wearing formal clothing.

## Subtopic: When One Number Lies

Back to our nine readers — and in walks a tenth, who read for 130 minutes. Watch each picture take the news.

The pool: the newcomer tips more than two hours in. The pool jumps from 180 to 310 minutes, and split among ten people that is 31 minutes each. But glance around the room: nine of the ten actually read LESS than 31 minutes. The split now describes almost nobody — one enormous contribution has distorted it. That is what an outlier does to a mean: the mean is fair to the TOTAL, not to the people.

The queue: the newcomer walks quietly to the very back, and every other person keeps their exact place. The middle of ten people falls between the fifth and sixth — between 19 and 22 — so the median is 20,5. Barely a flicker. The queue does not care HOW extreme the last person is: 130 minutes or 1 000 minutes, the middle of the line moves not at all.

That is the entire choosing rule, felt instead of memorised: extreme values wreck the pool but never the queue. So when you hear "salaries at a company where the owner pays herself two million", think: pool ruined, use the queue — the median. When the data is calm and balanced, the pool is excellent, precisely because it listens to everyone.

Your questions for this section are up now. For each scenario, first ask: is there a monster value in the room? Then choose pool or queue, and defend it in one sentence.

## Subtopic: Grouped Data Without Fear

Final scene: the marks of fifty learners arrive not as a list but as five crates — the forties, fifties, sixties, seventies and eighties. Six learners in the forties crate, twelve in the fifties, thirteen in the sixties, fourteen in the seventies, five in the eighties.

First feel WHY the exact mean is gone: crates hide details. You know fourteen learners scored in the seventies, but a 71 and a 79 look identical from outside the crate. So make the fairest guess available — assume everyone in a crate sits at its middle. Forties crate: call them all 45. Fifties: 55. And onward: 65, 75, 85. Some sat lower and some higher; the middle is the guess that lets those errors cancel.

Then the pool, exactly as before, crate by crate: six people at 45 contribute 270. Twelve at 55 contribute 660. Thirteen at 65 contribute 845. Fourteen at 75 contribute 1 050. Five at 85 contribute 425. Everything in the pool: 3 250 marks. Split among 50 learners: 65 percent. An ESTIMATED mean — honest about the guessing baked inside it.

The other two measures get even easier — you simply point at crates. The fullest crate, fourteen learners, is the seventies: that is the modal interval. For the median, line all fifty up in mark order and find the middle pair — the twenty-fifth and twenty-sixth learners. Count through the crates: six, then eighteen, then thirty-one. The middle pair stands inside the sixties crate. Median interval: the sixties.

Notice that you already knew every move — midpoint guess, pool, fullest crate, count to the middle. Grouped data is not new mathematics; it is the same three pictures viewed through a frequency table.
