**Thandi:** Class questions before we finish — and there's a good pile today. First up: if the median handles outliers so gracefully, why do we ever bother with the mean at all? A few of us are ready to abandon it completely.

**Tutor:** Do not abandon it — the mean carries information the median simply cannot hold. The median knows only the middle of the queue; it would report the same 19 whether the friends around it read for 18 and 22 minutes or for 1 and 60. The mean listens to every single value, which makes it the right tool whenever the total genuinely matters: total marks in a class, total rainfall over a season, total minutes of reading. When the data is calm and roughly balanced, the mean is both fair and richly informative, and that is why it remains the default. The median is the specialist you call in when an extreme value has poisoned the share-out. Two tools, two jobs — the skill is matching tool to data, not crowning one forever.

***

**Thandi:** Next one, very practical: in a test, how do we actually recognise an outlier? Is there a rule, or do we just squint at the numbers?

**Tutor:** At this level, trained squinting is genuinely the expected method — but train it properly. Order the data first, always, because outliers hide in shuffled lists. Then look at the gaps between neighbours: our reading times climbed in small steps of two or three minutes, and then leapt from 33 all the way to 130 — a gap ten times bigger than any other. That leap is the signature: an outlier is a value sitting far from the pack, not merely the biggest value. A 33 in our data is the largest but sits snugly at the end of the staircase; the 130 stands in a different postal code. In later grades you will meet a formal fence built from quartiles that draws the line precisely. For now: order, scan the gaps, and if one value lives alone across a chasm, name it an outlier and say what it does to the mean.

**Thandi:** Here's one that cost people marks in the practice set: the question asked for the modal interval and half of us wrote 14, the frequency. Why is that wrong when 14 is the number we hunted for?

**Tutor:** Because the 14 is the evidence, not the verdict. The question asks WHERE the marks pile up thickest, and the answer to a where-question must be a place: the interval 70 up to below 80. The frequency 14 is merely how you found that place — the height of the tallest stack, not its address. Here is the discipline that prevents the slip forever: after scanning the frequency column, move your finger LEFT to the interval column and copy what your finger lands on. The same discipline protects the median interval: cumulative counting tells you the middle pair lives in the third class, and your written answer is that class's interval — 60 to below 70 — never the number 31 from your running total. Frequencies locate; intervals answer.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's the thoughtful one: the estimated mean assumed everyone sits at the midpoint of their crate. Isn't that assumption just wrong? How can a wrong assumption give a trustworthy answer?

**Tutor:** A beautiful question, because it touches how real statistics works. The assumption is wrong for individuals and fair for the crowd. Within any crate, some learners sit below the midpoint and some above; when you replace them all with the midpoint, the underestimates and overestimates lean against each other and largely cancel. The estimate is not guaranteed to be exact — the true mean of the fifty real marks might be 64 or 66 — but it is unbiased: the guess has no built-in reason to lean high or low. That is why the honest word "estimated" belongs in your answer, and why the method is trusted everywhere from school tests to national surveys: when you cannot know each value, the fairest stand-in is the middle of where it must be. Precision lost, honesty kept — that trade is the very heart of working with grouped data.
