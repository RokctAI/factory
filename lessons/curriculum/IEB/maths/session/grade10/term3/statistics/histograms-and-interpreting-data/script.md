# Part 1 — Expert

This session carries a dataset the whole way — from a messy list of raw numbers to a finished statistical comment: organise, summarise, represent, interpret. The vehicle is the frequency table and its picture, the histogram, and the destination is the analysis sentence that collects the final marks. One dataset of forty homework times travels through the entire session, and by the end you will build the table, describe the histogram precisely, and comment on data the way an analyst does.

## Subtopic: From Raw Data to a Frequency Table

The raw data: forty learners each recorded, in minutes, how long they spent on homework yesterday evening. The numbers arrive in a heap — a 12, a 35, an 86, a 27, and so on, forty of them. In this state the data answers nothing; the first professional move is always to ORGANISE.

Continuous data — times, masses, lengths, anything measured rather than counted — is organised into CLASS INTERVALS. Choose intervals of equal width that cover every value. Here: from 0 up to but not including 20 minutes; from 20 to below 40; 40 to below 60; 60 to below 80; and 80 to below 100. Now state the boundary rule out loud, because boundaries are where the errors breed: each interval includes its lower boundary and excludes its upper one. A learner who worked exactly 40 minutes belongs to the 40-to-60 class, not the 20-to-40 class. Every value has exactly one home, and no value has two.

Tally the forty values into the classes and the table appears: 6 learners in the 0-to-20 class; 16 in the 20-to-40 class; 9 in the 40-to-60 class; 6 in the 60-to-80 class; and 3 in the 80-to-100 class. Then the non-negotiable audit: add the frequencies — 6 plus 16 is 22, plus 9 is 31, plus 6 is 37, plus 3 is 40. Forty learners, forty tallies. If that total misses, a value was dropped or counted twice, and every calculation downstream inherits the damage.

Pause here — questions on this section are coming to you now. Boundaries and totals: the unglamorous checks are the ones holding the whole building up.

## Subtopic: Building the Histogram

The histogram is the frequency table turned into a picture, and you must be able to construct one and describe it exactly in words.

The horizontal axis carries the measurement — homework time in minutes — marked continuously: 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, 100. The vertical axis carries FREQUENCY — the number of learners — running from 0 past the largest frequency, 16. Each class becomes a bar standing on its own interval: the first bar spans 0 to 20 on the horizontal axis and rises to height 6. The second spans 20 to 40, height 16 — the tallest. Then 40 to 60 at height 9, 60 to 80 at height 6, and 80 to 100 at height 3.

The defining feature: the bars TOUCH. No gaps anywhere. A histogram's bars share their walls because the data is continuous — 39,9 minutes flows into 40,0 with nothing in between, so the picture is one connected skyline rather than separate towers. This is exactly what distinguishes a histogram from a bar graph. A bar graph displays CATEGORIES — favourite subjects, sports codes, taxi routes — and its bars stand apart, because nothing exists between soccer and hockey. Gaps mean categories; touching bars mean a continuous measurement scale. That one-sentence distinction is a classic short theory question, so own it.

Label everything: both axis titles with units, and a heading for the display. An unlabelled histogram is a shape, not a statistical graphic.

Stop for this section's questions now — bars, boundaries, heights, and the touching-bars rule together with its reason.

## Subtopic: Reading the Shape — Mode, Middle and Skew

A completed histogram is read in three passes.

Pass one: the peak. The tallest bar stands on the 20-to-40 class — that is the MODAL CLASS, the most common homework bracket. Sixteen of the forty learners, four in every ten, worked between 20 and 40 minutes.

Pass two: the middle. Forty values place the median between the twentieth and twenty-first values. Run the cumulative count across the bars: 6 by the end of the first class, 22 by the second, 31 by the third. The twentieth and twenty-first learners both sit inside the second bar, so the median class is 20 to below 40. Now the estimated mean, using midpoints 10, 30, 50, 70 and 90: six times 10 is 60; sixteen times 30 is 480; nine times 50 is 450; six times 70 is 420; three times 90 is 270. The grand total is 1 680, and 1 680 over 40 gives an estimated mean of 42 minutes. Peak in the twenties-to-forties, median class the same, mean at 42 — a coherent centre.

Pass three: the shape. Ask where the tail lies. This skyline climbs quickly from the left, peaks on 20-to-40, then trails away rightward through heights 9, 6 and 3. A longer tail on the RIGHT is called skewed to the right, or positively skewed: most learners finish homework in moderate time, while a smaller scattered group works much longer. A longer left tail would be skewed to the left, negatively skewed; roughly mirror-image sides make the distribution symmetrical. One vocabulary trap to disarm permanently: the skew is named after the TAIL'S direction, never the peak's — a right-skewed histogram carries its peak on the LEFT.

Pause now for this section's questions — peak, cumulative middle, tail. Three passes, three facts.

## Subtopic: Writing the Analysis Comment

The closing skill, where the marks concentrate: the comment. A question ends with "comment on the data" or "compare the two groups", and the credit goes to structured sentences, never to vague impressions.

The template has three slots: CENTRE, SPREAD, SHAPE. Each sentence quotes a number or a feature from the display, then translates it back into the real situation.

Centre: "The modal class is 20 to below 40 minutes and the estimated mean is 42 minutes, so a typical learner spends somewhere around forty minutes on homework." Spread: "Times cover all five classes, from under 20 minutes to nearly 100 — a wide spread, so homework habits differ sharply across the group." Shape: "The distribution is skewed to the right: most learners finish in moderate time, while a small group works far longer than everyone else."

For a COMPARISON of two histograms, run the same three slots side by side. Suppose a second grade also peaks on the 20-to-40 bar, but shows nobody beyond 60 minutes. The comparison: their centre is similar — typical times match; their spread is narrower — no long tail of marathon sessions; so homework time in that grade is more uniform. Always compare like with like: centre against centre, spread against spread, and never a centre against a spread.

Two hygiene rules complete full marks. Rule one: stay in CONTEXT — "the data is skewed right" earns less than "most learners work a moderate time while a few work very long", because interpreting means returning to the world the numbers came from. Rule two: claim only what the display can show. This histogram cannot explain WHY some learners work two hours, and it reveals nobody's exact time — grouped displays trade detail for overview, and a top answer says exactly that when asked about limitations.

The compressed method: organise with clean boundaries and audit the total; draw touching bars on a labelled continuous axis; read peak, middle, tail; then comment in centre-spread-shape sentences anchored in context. The final questions of this part are with you now — new table, identical method.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same journey again, from heap of numbers to confident sentence — rebuilt this time with pigeonholes, mountain ranges and stories. Nothing new arrives: the tallest bar will still be the twenty-to-forties, and the mean will still be 42. What changes is that every step will feel like something you would have done anyway.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Stack Into Pigeonholes

Picture the forty homework times written on forty slips of paper, stacked in one messy heap on your desk. Ask the heap "how does this class handle homework?" and the heap says nothing. The fix is the one every school office already uses: pigeonholes.

Mount five pigeonholes on the wall, each labelled with a range: nought to under twenty minutes, twenty to under forty, forty to under sixty, sixty to under eighty, eighty to under a hundred. Take each slip and post it into the hole its number belongs to. A slip saying 12 — first hole. A slip saying 35 — second. A slip saying 86 — fifth.

Only one rule needs real care: the boundary slips. Where does exactly 40 go? Every pigeonhole keeps its bottom number and refuses its top one — so 40 posts into the forty-to-sixty hole, while 39,9 goes into the hole before. That way no slip can claim two homes and no slip is left homeless. It is the same logic as data bundles on your phone: the instant you reach the next threshold, you are in the next bracket.

When the stack is gone, count each hole: 6 slips, 16 slips, 9, 6, 3. Then the habit that saves marks for the rest of your life: count the counts. Six plus 16 plus 9 plus 6 plus 3 — forty. Forty slips posted, forty accounted for. Now the heap can finally talk: most of this class sits in the second hole. That is all a frequency table is — sorted post with labels.

Quick check before we carry on — a few questions on the pigeonholes are coming to you right now. Watch the boundaries; they are the only trap in the room.

## Subtopic: The Mountain Range Tells the Story

Now turn the pigeonholes into a picture you can read in one glance. Stand each hole's count up as a peak of rock: heights 6, then 16, then 9, then 6, then 3, rising side by side along the minutes line with no valley floor showing between them — because time is continuous; 39,9 minutes touches 40 with nothing in between. What you have drawn is a mountain range, and a mountain range can be read from a distance faster than any list of numbers.

Read the range the way a hiker would. Where is the summit — the highest peak? Over the twenty-to-forties: that is where the homework times crowd. Which way do the foothills run? To the right: after the summit, the ridges step down through 9 and 6 and 3, stretching out toward the two-hour workers. So the story so far: a big crowd at moderate times, and a long thinning ridge of learners who work far longer.

One misreading to eliminate on the spot, because it feels so natural: a WIDE bar never means more learners. Every bar here is twenty minutes wide by construction; only HEIGHT counts people. Height is frequency; width is just the pigeonhole's label stretched along the axis. Say it once and keep it: tall means many, wide means nothing.

And keep the mountain range apart from its cousin, the bar graph. Favourite subjects, taxi ranks, cricket versus hockey — separate categories, drawn as separated towers with gaps, because nothing lives between cricket and hockey. The histogram's peaks touch because the minutes line is one unbroken road. Gaps for categories, touching for measurements — one sentence, one theory mark, every time it is asked.

Your questions for this section are up now. Read the range: find the summit, follow the foothills, and trust height, never width.

## Subtopic: Saying What the Picture Means

Final skill, and the one the marks pile onto: turning the mountain range into sentences. The recipe is three questions, asked in order, answered in context.

Question one: where is the crowd? Point at the summit. "Most learners — sixteen of the forty — spend between twenty and forty minutes on homework." If you computed the estimated mean, attach it: "on average about 42 minutes." That is your CENTRE sentence.

Question two: how scattered are they? See how much of the road the range covers. "Times stretch from under twenty minutes to nearly a hundred — a wide spread; some finish before supper while others are still working at bedtime." That is your SPREAD sentence.

Question three: is the range lopsided? Follow the ridge. "The long ridge runs to the right: a handful of learners work far longer than everyone else — the distribution is skewed to the right." That is your SHAPE sentence. Name the skew after the tail's direction, never the summit's position.

Three sentences, and look at what each one accomplishes: it takes a feature of the picture and returns a fact about real learners. That is the entire meaning of "interpret" — the numbers came from the world, and your sentence must deliver them back. "Skewed right" on its own is homework; "a few learners work very long evenings while most finish quickly" is analysis.

And when two ranges stand side by side — this class against that one, this term against last — ask the same three questions of both and compare the answers in matched pairs: crowd against crowd, scatter against scatter, ridge against ridge. Never let a comparison sentence mix its categories.

You now own the full journey: heap to pigeonholes, pigeonholes to mountain range, range to sentences. The final questions of the lesson are arriving now — sort carefully, read the peaks, and let the three sentences walk you home. This topic belongs to you.
