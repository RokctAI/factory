# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice run across Microeconomics and Contemporary Economic Issues, built entirely from this session's own questions and mark values. The terrain is the graph-heavy half of the subject: cost and revenue curves, market structures, market failure, inflation, tourism and the environment. Every graph in this run is described in words, exactly as you must be able to describe one yourself, and every answer arrives with its marking logic showing, because the skill under training is not just knowing the content — it is converting content into marks.

## Subtopic: Quick-Fire Recall Across Micro and Contemporary Issues

Warm up with multiple-choice items at two marks each. Item one: when the firms in an oligopoly follow the price set by the dominant firm without any agreement, this is called... options: a cartel, price leadership, perfect competition, price taking. Price leadership — the tacit form of collusion, as against the overt collusion of a cartel. Item two: a market in which one large producer can supply the whole market more cheaply than several smaller ones could is a... monopolistic competitor, oligopoly, natural monopoly, free market. Natural monopoly — think of electricity transmission. Item three: a slowing in the RATE of inflation, with prices still rising but more gently, is... deflation, stagflation, disinflation, reflation. Disinflation — and notice how these items live or die on exact definition wording, which is why a precise glossary beats a vague understanding every time.

Matching items next. Enjoying a good without paying because no one can be excluded from it — free riding, the reason public goods need the state. Taxpayers pushed into higher tax brackets by purely inflationary pay rises — bracket creep. Travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and sustains local people — ecotourism.

Give-the-term, where no options rescue you. The profit just sufficient to keep a business in its current line of production — normal profit. Competition through advertising, branding, packaging and service rather than through price — non-price competition. The index that tracks the prices of the basket bought by households — the consumer price index, written out in full, because shortened forms earn nothing in careful marking.

Pause here — the first practice questions are with you now, in all three quick-fire shapes.

## Subtopic: Short Answers: Quick Marks on Market Concepts

The long questions in this practice set open with short answers worth four marks: two one-mark naming items and one two-mark application. Openers, not obstacles.

Practise the naming pairs. Name TWO barriers to entry that protect a monopolist — patents and licences; economies of scale and control of raw materials also score. Name TWO characteristics of an oligopoly — a few large firms and mutual interdependence; non-price competition and barriers to entry would also count. Name TWO examples of negative externalities — air pollution from factories and traffic congestion; noise and dumping also earn the marks. Any correct pair from the family, one mark each — never leave a naming item blank.

The two-mark application item wants one line of cause and consequence. Example: why does a maximum price set below equilibrium create a shortage? Because at the lower price, quantity demanded rises while quantity supplied falls, so buyers outnumber the goods available. Another: how does a levy on plastic bags change producer behaviour? It raises the cost of supplying the harmful product, so producers and shoppers switch toward alternatives — direction plus reason, two marks.

One habit to carry through every short answer: when the instruction says name or list, resist the urge to explain. The mark pays for the item, not the speech around it. Save the sentences for the paragraphs, where they are paid for.

Stop for the practice questions on this section now — naming pairs and two-mark applications, at pace.

## Subtopic: Data Response: The Monopolist's Graph, Described and Solved

The stimulus, spoken as a picture: a downward-sloping demand curve labelled average revenue, with the marginal revenue curve falling twice as steeply below it; a U-shaped average cost curve; and a marginal cost curve cutting through its lowest point. The equilibrium quantity of eighty units sits where marginal cost crosses marginal revenue. Directly above, the price on the demand curve reads two hundred rand. At the same quantity, the average cost curve reads one hundred and sixty rand.

One-mark starters. What is the selling price? Two hundred rand — read straight up from equilibrium to the demand curve. What quantity does the business produce? Eighty units.

Two-mark description: briefly describe the term monopoly — a market structure with a single seller of a product without close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry.

Four-mark reasoning: why does the monopolist's marginal revenue lie below its demand curve? Because to sell one additional unit, the price must fall on ALL units sold, so the extra revenue from the last unit is less than its price. Two developed statements, four marks.

Four-mark calculation, shown as number sentences: profit per unit equals price minus average cost — two hundred minus one hundred and sixty gives forty rand. Total economic profit equals forty rand times eighty units — three thousand two hundred rand. Setup line, subtraction, multiplication, labelled answer. And if the curves had crossed the other way, with average cost at two hundred and price at one hundred and sixty, the identical chain would deliver a three thousand two hundred rand economic loss — the procedure never changes, only the direction of the gap.

The practice questions for this section arrive now, anchored on the same described graph.

## Subtopic: Data Response: Inflation Figures and the Weighted Basket

The second data question supplies a table: the consumer price index standing at 130,0 last year and 139,1 this year, alongside a mini-basket with three categories — food weighted 50 percent with prices up 8 percent, transport weighted 30 percent with prices up 5 percent, and education weighted 20 percent with prices up 3 percent.

One-mark starters: which organisation publishes the consumer price index? Statistics South Africa. Which category carries the greatest weight? Food.

Two-mark description: briefly describe headline inflation — the rate of change of the all-items consumer price index, the number announced monthly.

Four-mark calculation one: the inflation rate. New index minus old, over old, times one hundred: 139,1 minus 130,0 gives 9,1; divided by 130,0 and multiplied by one hundred gives 7,0 percent. The classic trap is dividing by the new index — always the OLD index, because a rate measures growth from where prices started.

Four-mark calculation two: the weighted average increase from the mini-basket. Multiply each weight by its increase and add: 0,5 times 8 gives 4,0; 0,3 times 5 gives 1,5; 0,2 times 3 gives 0,6. The weighted increase is 6,1 percent. Notice the economics inside the arithmetic: food's heavy weight drags the average toward food's experience, which is why low-income households, whose real baskets are food-heavy, live a harsher inflation than the headline number reports.

The higher-order tail: if the table also showed fuel and electricity leading the increases, is this inflation demand-pull or cost-push? Cost-push — costs entering from the supply side — and the policy line follows: a higher repo rate will cool spending but cannot cheapen fuel.

Pause for this section's practice questions now — table and basket in front of you.

## Subtopic: Paragraph Answers: Graphs in Words and Judgements in Context

Paragraph answers in this practice set carry ten marks: five developed facts at two marks each, with a graph described in words able to stand in for one of them.

Explaining practice: with the aid of a described graph, explain why a perfectly competitive business earns only normal profit in the long run. Model answer: the individual business is a price taker, so its demand curve is a horizontal line at the market price. In the short run, price may sit above average cost, leaving a rectangle of economic profit. But entry is free — newcomers arrive chasing that profit, market supply rises, and the market price falls. The price line slides down until it just touches the lowest point of the average cost curve, where only normal profit remains. Losses run the film in reverse: exits shrink supply and lift the price back. Five linked statements, and the verbal graph counts among them.

Judging practice: evaluate whether the levy on sugary drinks is good policy. Argue both sides and land a judgement. In favour: it prices the health harm into the drink, so overconsumption shrinks; it raises revenue that can fund clinics; and it nudges manufacturers to reformulate with less sugar, which they have done. Against: it is regressive, taking a larger share from poor households; it can cut employment in the sugar value chain; and determined consumers simply switch to untaxed sweet foods. Judgement: the levy works where the harm is clear and alternatives exist, and its fairness improves when the revenue visibly funds health — a tax on harm is best judged by what it changes, not what it collects.

A second judging shape to rehearse: propose measures a municipality could take to grow township tourism, and justify each. Market the area's heritage routes so visitors know they exist; train local guides so spending stays in local hands; improve lighting, transport and signage so visitors feel safe and welcome; and support craft markets so each visitor's rand spreads across many households. Each proposal justified by its mechanism — that justification is where the second mark of every pair lives.

The practice questions on both paragraph shapes are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Essay, Built Live: Compare and Contrast Market Structures

This session's practice essay is worth thirty marks, on its own fixed plan: a definition opening worth 2, the main body worth 20, an evaluation worth 6, and a closing judgement worth 2. The instruction: compare and contrast perfect competition and monopoly, and use a described graph to explain the monopoly's long-run equilibrium position.

The opening, two marks: a market structure describes how a market is organised — the number of sellers, the nature of the product and the ease of entry. Clean, definitional, no borrowed question wording.

Main body, twenty — run the comparison as paired contrasts, two marks per developed pair. Number of firms: very many against one. Nature of product: identical against unique without close substitutes. Entry: free against blocked by scale, licences or patents. Price: the price-taking competitor against the price-making monopolist, though even the monopolist remains bound by demand. Demand curve: horizontal for the individual competitor against downward-sloping for the monopolist, with marginal revenue below it. Long-run profit: normal only, as entry competes gains away, against persistent economic profit behind barriers. Efficiency: the competitor pressed toward lowest-cost production against the monopolist producing less at a higher price. Examples: fresh produce on a market floor against the electricity grid. Eight developed contrasts and the body is full.

The graph paragraph, told in words: in the monopoly's long-run equilibrium, output sits where marginal cost equals marginal revenue; the price stands above it on the demand curve; and because barriers hold, price can remain above average cost, leaving the rectangle of economic profit between price and average cost across the equilibrium quantity.

Evaluation, six marks — your own weighing: monopoly is not automatically evil, because economies of scale can make one large producer cheaper than many small ones, which is why regulated natural monopolies exist; but unregulated monopoly transfers welfare from consumers and dulls innovation, which is why competition authorities police abuse of dominance and prosecute collusion. A balanced verdict with South African anchors earns the six.

Closing judgement, two marks: one fresh line — structure shapes conduct, so policy watches structure.

Practice questions on the comparison essay are with you now.

## Subtopic: A Second Essay Plan: Combating Inflation, Built to Its Marks

The alternative essay in this practice set comes from Contemporary Issues: discuss the measures used to combat inflation, and evaluate the success of inflation targeting in South Africa. Plan it to the same thirty-mark grid.

Opening, two: inflation is a sustained and significant increase in the general price level, measured by the consumer price index.

Main body, twenty, organised by policy family with every measure tied to the inflation it fights. Monetary policy first: the central bank raises the repo rate, making credit dearer across every lending rate and cooling consumption and investment — the direct answer to demand-pull pressure; selling securities withdraws liquidity; stricter reserve requirements and moral suasion tighten lending further. Fiscal policy second: restraint in government spending trims demand injections; taxation withdraws spending power; discipline over administered prices such as electricity tariffs and fuel levies attacks a cost-push engine directly. Other measures third: promoting competition so price-setters face rivals, lifting productivity so wages can grow without lifting unit costs, and moderating the wage-price spiral through negotiated settlements.

Evaluation, six — weigh targeting honestly. Successes: the three-to-six percent band has anchored expectations for a generation, kept South Africa far from the runaway-inflation cases, and made the central bank's reactions predictable, which itself steadies prices. Limits: the repo rate is a demand weapon, so imported oil shocks, drought food prices and administered tariffs resist it; the squeeze lands on borrowers and job-seekers first, raising the social cost of each disinflation. Verdict: targeting has delivered credibility more reliably than comfort, and it succeeds only when fiscal policy rows in the same direction.

Closing, two: the cure must match the cause — a final line the body has earned but not yet spoken.
