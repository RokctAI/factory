# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice run across Macroeconomics and Economic Pursuits. Every question in it was written for this session, carrying this session's own mark values, so that you can train the one habit that decides final-exam results more than any fact does: matching your effort to the marks on offer. Attempt each question before the answer arrives, because every answer comes with its marking logic attached — knowing how marks are earned is half the preparation.

## Subtopic: Quick-Fire Recall: Objective Questions Under the Clock

Our practice set opens with quick-fire objective work worth thirty marks in this session: multiple-choice items at two marks each, matching items at one, and give-the-term items at one.

Try three multiple-choice items now. Item one: money entering the circular flow through investment, government spending and exports is known as... options: leakages, injections, money flows, transfers. The answer is injections — two marks for one confident word, which is why disciplined glossary work is the cheapest training in Economics. Item two: the phase of the business cycle where activity has stopped falling and begins to climb again is the... trough, peak, recovery, depression. Recovery. Item three: the income earned for supplying natural resources as a factor of production is... interest, rent, profit, wages. Rent — the factor rewards are a matched set worth memorising as a family.

Matching items pair a description with its concept. The account of the balance of payments that records everyday trade in goods and services — the current account. The school of economists who blame business cycles on shocks arriving from outside the market system — the monetarists. The rate at which the central bank lends to commercial banks — the repo rate.

Give-the-term is the sharpest of the three shapes, because no options appear and abbreviations earn nothing. The index of export prices divided by the index of import prices, multiplied by one hundred — the terms of trade. An increase in the value of a currency under a floating exchange rate system — appreciation. The fraction of each extra rand of income that households choose to spend — the marginal propensity to consume, spelled out in full, because a shortened form earns no credit in careful marking.

Pause here — the first practice questions are with you now, in all three quick-fire shapes.

## Subtopic: Short Answers: Banking the Easy Marks First

Longer questions in this practice set open with short items worth four marks: two one-mark naming items and one two-mark application item. Treat them as marks to be banked in seconds, not puzzles to be admired.

Practise the naming pairs. Name TWO injections into the circular flow — investment and government spending; export earnings would also count. Name TWO instruments of fiscal policy — taxation and government spending, the only two levers that policy owns. Name TWO social indicators of development — life expectancy and access to clean water, with child mortality, school enrolment and housing all acceptable. One mark each, any correct pair from the family — never leave a naming item blank.

The two-mark application item asks what, why or how, and wants exactly one line of cause and consequence. Example: what effect will an appreciation of the rand have on South African imports? Imports become cheaper in rand terms, so import volumes rise — direction plus reason, two marks. Another: why does a sudden rise in household saving slow the economy in the short run? Because saving is a leakage — money withdrawn from the spending stream — so businesses sell less and cut production. One sentence, cause and consequence, done.

The rule that governs every short answer is proportion. A two-mark answer is one or two lines, never a paragraph; the mark value is the instruction about how much to write. Spend your sentences where the marks are, and nowhere else.

Stop for the practice questions on this section now — naming pairs and two-mark applications, at pace.

## Subtopic: Data Response: Reading a Business Cycle Diagram

Data questions in this practice set carry ten marks each and climb from easy to demanding. Picture the diagram this one supplies, described in words: a wave-shaped line rising and falling around a straight upward-sloping trend line, with point P at a crest, point Q on the falling section after it, point R at the bottom of the following dip, and the horizontal distance between two successive crests labelled L.

First questions, one mark each. Identify the phase at point P — the peak, sometimes called prosperity. Identify the phase at point Q — the recession, the downswing where activity contracts. These are read-off marks; the answers sit in the picture.

Middle questions, two marks. Briefly describe the term extrapolation — extending the established trend line beyond the present to predict where the economy is heading. Why would point R be the moment when stimulus policy is most expected? Because R is the trough, where activity has fallen as far as it will; stimulus applied there supports the recovery rather than feeding a boom.

Demanding questions, four marks. Explain what distance L measures and why forecasters value it. L is the length of the cycle — the time from one peak to the next. A known typical length lets forecasters estimate when the next turning point is due, and combined with moving averages, which smooth away short-lived noise, it separates a genuine change of phase from a temporary wobble. Two developed facts at two marks each — that is the four-mark rhythm across every data question in this run.

The practice questions for this section arrive now, built on the same diagram described in words.

## Subtopic: Data Response: A Balance of Payments Table

The second data question supplies a table from the current account, in millions of rands: goods exports 950; net gold exports 150; services receipts 300; income receipts 100; merchandise imports 1 250; payments for services 180; income payments 220; current transfers plus 30.

One-mark starters. Under which item would the profits of a South African bank's branch in London, sent home, be recorded? Income receipts. Which single item is largest on the credit side? Goods exports.

Two-mark description. Briefly describe the term trade balance — goods exports plus net gold exports minus merchandise imports, the goods-only snapshot inside the current account. Here: 950 plus 150 minus 1 250 gives minus 150 million — a trade deficit.

Now the four-mark calculation, written as number sentences. The balance on the current account equals all receipts minus all payments plus net transfers. Receipts: 950 plus 150 gives 1 100, plus 300 gives 1 400, plus 100 gives 1 500. Payments: 1 250 plus 180 plus 220 gives 1 650. Then 1 500 minus 1 650 gives minus 150, and adding transfers of plus 30 gives minus 120 million — a deficit on the current account. Marks land on the setup line, the totals, the subtraction, and the final answer labelled with its sign, its units and its name.

The follow-up is the higher-order tail: how could the authorities respond to this deficit? Develop two measures: raise interest rates so that foreign capital flows into the financial account and finances the gap; promote exports through incentives and trade missions so receipts grow; allow the exchange rate to depreciate so imports become dearer and exports more competitive; or manage import spending within the limits trade agreements allow. Two developed measures, four marks.

Pause for this section's practice questions now — the table stays in front of you.

## Subtopic: Paragraph Answers: Explaining and Arguing for Marks

Each long question in this practice set closes with two ten-mark paragraph answers: one that explains, one that argues. Ten marks means five developed facts at two marks each — or a graph described in words standing in for one of them.

Explaining practice: with the aid of a described graph, explain how the multiplier turns one injection into a larger rise in total income. Model answer, spoken as you would write it: the graph plots total spending against total income, with a forty-five degree line marking every point where spending equals income. The spending line cuts it at equilibrium. A new injection — say investment — lifts the spending line upward by the size of the injection. But the new equilibrium moves rightward by MORE than the injection, because the first round of new spending becomes income, most of which is spent again, becoming income again. If households spend three quarters of each extra rand, the multiplier is one divided by a quarter — four — so ten million rand of new investment raises income by forty million. Five linked statements, and the verbal graph counts among them.

Arguing practice: does a weaker rand help or harm South Africa? Build a case, not a list. In favour: exports become cheaper abroad, so mining, agriculture and manufacturing sell more; inbound tourism booms because the country becomes a bargain; local producers win customers back from imports. Against: fuel, medicine and machinery are imported, so costs rise across the economy; imported inflation erodes wages; businesses that rely on foreign inputs suffer squeezed margins. Then the judgement the marks are waiting for: the net effect depends on how export-ready the economy is and how import-dependent households are — a weaker rand rewards producers and taxes consumers. That final weighing sentence is what separates an argument from a list.

The practice questions on both paragraph shapes are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Essay, Built Live: Smoothing of Business Cycles

This session's practice essay is worth thirty marks, and its mark plan is fixed for this run: a definition opening worth 2, the main body worth 20, an evaluation worth 6, and a closing judgement worth 2. Build this one live: discuss how monetary and fiscal policy can be used to smooth out business cycles, and evaluate how well these demand-side policies work.

The opening, two marks — one clean definition, nothing borrowed from the question: business cycles are the repeating expansions and contractions of economic activity around a long-term trend. Define and move; announcing your plan earns nothing.

Main body, twenty — organise by policy, and tie every tool to smoothing, because a fact without its link earns one mark instead of two. Monetary policy in the downswing: the central bank cuts the repo rate, making credit cheaper, lifting consumption and investment; buying securities in the open market injects liquidity; moral suasion nudges banks to lend. In the upswing, every lever reverses to cool excess demand. Fiscal policy in the downswing: government lifts its spending and trims taxes, injecting demand that the multiplier enlarges; in the upswing it restrains spending and lets progressive taxation bite harder as incomes climb. Add the stabiliser point: progressive taxes and social grants adjust automatically with income, smoothing the cycle without a single new decision. And describe the graph in words for its marks: aggregate demand shifts right under stimulus and left under restraint, moving equilibrium output along aggregate supply.

Evaluation, six marks — the judgement the question ordered: policies act with lags, so stimulus can arrive after the phase it was aimed at has passed; pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up, so smoothing buys one goal with another; a repo rate cannot fix supply shocks like droughts or electricity failures; and borrowed stimulus becomes tomorrow's interest bill. Weighed whole: demand-side policy smooths moderately well and works best alongside supply-side reform.

Closing judgement, two marks: one fresh line — smoothing succeeds when the policy mix matches both the phase and the cause of the cycle — never a repeat of the body.

Practice questions on essay architecture are with you now.

## Subtopic: A Second Essay Plan and Final Exam Wisdom

The second essay in this practice set comes from Economic Pursuits. Practise planning it in skeleton form: discuss export promotion and import substitution as trade policies, and evaluate the mix South Africa has chosen. Opening: trade policy is the set of measures a government uses to shape what its economy sells to and buys from the world. Body headings, each expanded with two-mark facts: export promotion pushing local producers into world markets with incentives and marketing support, at the risk of subsidising firms that never learn to compete; import substitution sheltering home industries behind tariffs and quotas, at the risk of expensive, inefficient producers; the case for freer trade — specialisation, scale, choice, innovation; and South Africa's agreements linking the economy to its region and its trading partners. Evaluation: the honest scorecard — openness has widened markets and cut prices, but exposed industries have shed jobs, so the mix must shelter genuinely young industries briefly while opening the rest. Closing: trade policy is a dial, not a switch.

Now the exam wisdom that turns near-misses into full marks. One: obey command verbs — name wants a word, describe wants a sentence or two, discuss wants developed paragraphs, evaluate demands your own judgement. Two: the mark value is the recipe — two marks per developed fact almost everywhere, so count facts before you write. Three: in calculations, the setup line and the units are marks; write them even when the arithmetic feels easy. Four: in essays, never let the closing paragraph summarise — it is a separately marked fresh response. Five: when questions offer choices, choose by the data you read best, because data questions and paragraphs carry the weight of the marks.
