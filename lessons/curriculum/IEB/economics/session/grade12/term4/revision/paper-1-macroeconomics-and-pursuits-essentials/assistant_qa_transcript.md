**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the policy names. RDP, the growth strategies, the New Growth Path, the National Development Plan — people keep scrambling the order and what each one was for. Is there a clean way to hold them?

**Tutor:** Hold them as a story with one sentence per chapter, because a story resists scrambling where a list does not. Chapter one, straight after 1994: the Reconstruction and Development Programme — taps, houses, clinics; the country rebuilding basics. Chapter two: the discipline years — stabilising the budget and chasing growth so the rebuilding could be paid for. Chapter three: the bottleneck years — targeted pushes on infrastructure and skills, because growth had arrived but could not squeeze through the pipes. Chapter four: the New Growth Path — jobs moved to the centre of the target. Chapter five: the National Development Plan — the long map aiming at 2030. Notice the logic: rebuild, stabilise, unblock, employ, plan long. Each plan answers the problem the previous chapter exposed, so if you remember the problem, the plan names itself. In an exam answer, one sentence on what each plan aimed at, in order, is worth more than three paragraphs of muddle — and the ordering sentence itself often carries a mark.

***

**Mandy:** Next one: the multiplier. The formula is easy enough, but a few of us cannot explain WHY the number is bigger than one, and explanation questions keep asking exactly that.

**Tutor:** Then practise the second round, because the second round is the whole secret. The depot costs eight million; the builders receive eight million — that is round one, and if the story stopped there the multiplier would be exactly one. But the builders are also customers. They take their wages to the shops, and three quarters of the eight million becomes round two: six million of new income for shopkeepers. The shopkeepers spend three quarters of that — four and a half million, round three — and the rounds keep shrinking until they fade. Add every round and you get thirty-two million from the original eight: the multiplier of four. So the number exceeds one because income is re-spent — one person's spending is always another person's income, and the circular flow turns a single splash into rings. The leak decides the size: the more each round loses to saving, taxes and imports, the sooner the rings die, which is why the formula divides one by the leak. Say those two sentences in an exam — spending becomes income again, and the leak sets the limit — and the explanation marks are yours.

***

**Mandy:** Third: the balance of payments accounts. Current, capital transfer, financial — sorting real examples into them still goes wrong under pressure. Any test that works fast?

**Tutor:** Ask one question of every example: what crossed the border — stuff, a gift, or claims? If goods or services crossed, with payment flowing the other way, you are in the CURRENT account: the fruit shipment, the tourist's guest-house bill, the shipping fee, the dividend flowing home. If nothing was bought and nothing is owed — a pure gift, money migrating with a person — that is the small CAPITAL TRANSFER account. If what changed hands was ownership or claims — shares, bonds, a company bought, reserves moved — you are in the FINANCIAL account, and one further question separates its rooms: does the buyer gain lasting control? A foreign company buying a South African retailer gains control — direct investment. A foreigner buying shares on the exchange holds paper without control — portfolio investment. Stuff, gift, or claims; and for claims, control or no control. Two questions, asked in that order, sort every example the syllabus can throw at you — drill them until they take five seconds each.

**Mandy:** Last one: evaluation questions on the weak rand, on protection, on the plans — how do we stop our answers sounding like we are just sitting on the fence?

**Tutor:** A fence-sitter lists both sides and stops; an evaluator lists both sides and then DECIDES something conditional. The difference is one sentence with the word depends made specific. Weak rand: exporters and tourism win, importers and commuters lose — so the verdict depends on which side of the border your income is earned; for a mining nation with food-importing households, the gains and the pain land on different people, and policy must watch both. Protection: the cobbler keeps jobs, families pay more — so the verdict depends on whether the sheltered industry is genuinely young and genuinely learning; shelter with a time limit differs from shelter as a lifestyle. The plans: delivery beats design — so judge each plan by what reached households, not by what the document promised. Notice the pattern: name the winners, name the losers, then state the condition under which the policy earns its keep. That conditional verdict is not fence-sitting — it is the most precise position available, and it is exactly the kind of judgement evaluation marks exist to reward.
