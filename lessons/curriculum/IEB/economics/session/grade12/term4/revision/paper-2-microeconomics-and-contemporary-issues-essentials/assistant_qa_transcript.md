**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the three profit positions in perfect competition — profit, loss, normal. People can recite them, but the questions ask us to SHOW them on the graph. What is the reliable way?

**Tutor:** One comparison, made at one place. Go to the equilibrium output — where marginal cost crosses marginal revenue — and compare exactly two numbers at that quantity: the price, and average cost. Price above average cost: the gap times the quantity is your profit rectangle, drawn between the two lines across the equilibrium output. Price below: the same rectangle, now a loss. Price just touching the lowest point of the average cost curve: no rectangle at all — normal profit. Learners go wrong by comparing in the wrong place, reading average cost at some other quantity, or by comparing price with marginal cost, which decides the OUTPUT, not the profit. Keep the jobs separate: marginal figures choose how much to produce; average figures reveal what that production earns. And rehearse the sentence that explains the long run: the rectangle is temporary, because entry shaves profit rectangles and exit heals loss rectangles until price rests on lowest average cost. One comparison, one place, one closing sentence — that is the full answer, every time.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the kinked demand curve. Everyone can draw the kink, but explaining WHY the top half is elastic and the bottom half is not still trips us up.

**Tutor:** Explain it as two stories about rivals, because the kink is really a prediction of their behaviour. Story one — you raise your price alone: your rivals hold still and smile, because your customers are now walking to them. A small rise loses you many customers: that is elastic demand, the flatter section above the kink. Story two — you cut your price: your rivals cannot afford to smile, they match you within the hour, so nobody's customers move and you have simply cut your margin. A big cut wins little volume: inelastic demand, the steeper section below. Put the two stories together and both directions lose you revenue, which is why the rational oligopolist leaves price exactly where it is — the kink sits at the current price because the current price is the trap. Then add the consequence that earns the final mark: since price is frozen, competition flows sideways into advertising, branding and loyalty programmes. If you can tell those two stories in four sentences, you never need to memorise which half is which — the rivals' reactions rebuild the curve for you.

***

**Mandy:** Third: cost-benefit analysis feels vague next to the tidy graphs. When a question asks us to apply it — like the new taxi rank — what does a full answer actually contain?

**Tutor:** Four moves, in order. Move one: list the costs, private AND social — the construction bill, yes, but also the hawkers displaced from the pavement, the noise, the dust, the traffic during building. Move two: list the benefits the same way — fares and stall rentals, but also commuting hours saved, safer boarding, cleaner queues, the businesses that grow around a working rank. Move three: put the lists on one scale — money where prices exist, careful estimates where they do not, and future values discounted because a benefit in ten years weighs less than one today. Move four: the decision rule, stated as a sentence — proceed only if social benefits exceed social costs. The marker is watching for one thing above all: whether your lists include the UNPRICED items, because forcing pollution, time and health into the arithmetic is the entire reason the tool exists. A learner who writes eight priced items and no social ones has missed the point; a learner who weighs the hawkers against the commuters has understood it. End with the rule, and the answer is complete.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: inflation, tourism, sustainability — three separate topics, and revision time is short. Is there one thread that ties them so we revise smarter?

**Tutor:** There is, and the session walked it: all three are stories about prices telling the truth or failing to. Inflation is the price level losing its anchor — too much spending pulling it, or costs pushing it — and the whole policy debate is about restoring a truthful, stable measuring stick, which is what the target band provides. Tourism is a price story in the happy direction: a weaker rand makes the whole country better value, visitors arrive, and their spending spreads through hands the formal economy struggles to reach. Sustainability is the price story in the dangerous direction: the river, the air and the dump carry a price of zero, so they get overused — and every tool in the topic, from the bag levy to tradeable permits, is a way of making the price tell the truth at last. So revise them as one question asked three times: what happens when prices mislead, and how does policy correct them? Hold that thread and the three topics stop being separate lists — they become one idea wearing three outfits, and evaluation sentences write themselves from it.
