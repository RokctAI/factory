## Subtopic: Perfect Markets: Equilibrium, Profit and the Shutdown Point

Perfect competition is a benchmark rather than a photograph: many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous product, free entry and exit, perfect information, and no single seller with any grip on the price. The nearest South African picture is a produce auction floor, where identical crates of maize or oranges meet a hall full of buyers. Because no single farmer can move the price, each business is a PRICE TAKER, and its demand curve, told in words, is a horizontal line at the market price — for the individual seller, demand, price, average revenue and marginal revenue are all the same flat line.

Equilibrium sits where marginal cost equals marginal revenue while marginal cost is rising. Everything that follows comes from comparing price with average cost at that output. Price above average cost: ECONOMIC PROFIT, the rectangle between the two across the equilibrium quantity. Price below average cost: ECONOMIC LOSS. Price just touching the lowest point of the average cost curve: NORMAL PROFIT — earnings no better than the owner's resources could fetch elsewhere. And the long run drags every perfect competitor to exactly that point: profits attract entrants whose extra supply pushes the price down, losses drive exits whose vanished supply lifts it, until only normal profit remains.

The SHUTDOWN decision is a favourite in its own right. In the short run a loss-making business keeps producing as long as price covers average VARIABLE cost, because every unit sold still contributes something toward the fixed costs that must be paid regardless. Once price slips below average variable cost, each unit deepens the wound, and closing — losing only the fixed costs — is the smaller loss. In words: the shutdown point is where the price line touches the minimum of the average variable cost curve.

Close with COMPETITION POLICY: the Competition Act of 1998, enforced by the Competition Commission which investigates, the Competition Tribunal which adjudicates, and the Competition Appeal Court above them. Its scoreboard includes prosecuted collusion in construction and in staple foods — real anchors that evaluation answers are expected to name.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: characteristics, the flat demand line, the three profit positions, shutdown, and the competition authorities.

## Subtopic: Imperfect Markets: Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition

A MONOPOLY is a single seller of a product without close substitutes, behind high barriers — natural monopolies like the electricity grid, or artificial ones built on licences and patents. Because the monopolist IS the industry, its demand curve slopes downward and marginal revenue hangs BELOW it: selling one more unit means lowering the price on every unit. Equilibrium still sits where marginal cost equals marginal revenue, but the price is read UP to the demand curve above that output — so output is lower and price higher than perfect competition would deliver, and economic profit can persist into the long run because barriers keep rescuing competition out. Persist — not guaranteed: a monopolist facing weak demand can still make losses.

OLIGOPOLY: a few large, mutually watchful sellers — banking, fuel retail, mobile networks. INTERDEPENDENCE is the defining word: each firm's best move depends on what rivals will do in reply. That logic produces the KINKED DEMAND CURVE, told in words: above the current price demand is elastic, because a lone price-riser loses customers to rivals who stay put; below it demand is inelastic, because a price cut is matched at once and wins little volume. The kink makes prices sticky, so rivalry migrates into NON-PRICE competition — advertising, branding, loyalty programmes — or slides into COLLUSION: overt collusion in a cartel, tacit collusion through price leadership. Both are illegal under the Competition Act.

MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION blends the worlds: many sellers, easy entry, but DIFFERENTIATED products — salons, restaurants, spaza shops each with a loyal corner. Differentiation tilts each seller's demand curve gently downward and allows short-run economic profit, but easy entry copies every success until only normal profit survives.

For any compare-and-contrast answer, hold the four structures against the standard checklist: number of firms, nature of the product, entry, price control, information, examples, the shape of the demand curve, and long-run profit.

Stop for the questions on this section now — monopoly's two curves, the kinked demand story, the two kinds of collusion, and the four structures compared.

## Subtopic: Market Failures, State Intervention and Cost-Benefit Analysis

Markets fail when prices steer resources to the wrong places, and the syllabus names six causes to discuss: externalities, missing markets, imperfect competition, lack of information, immobility of factors, and unequal distribution of income and wealth.

EXTERNALITIES are the centrepiece, and both graphs must be tellable in words. Negative externality — a tannery discharging waste into a river: the market's supply curve counts only private cost, so it lies to the right of the true social-cost curve, and the market delivers too much output at too low a price; the efficient point sits where social cost crosses demand, with less produced and a higher price. Positive externality — education, vaccination: private demand understates social benefit, so the social-benefit curve lies to the right of private demand and the market delivers too little. PUBLIC GOODS are the missing market: non-rivalrous and non-excludable, like street lighting — nobody can be charged at the point of use, free riding collapses private supply, and the state must provide.

STATE INTERVENTION follows, each tool with its verbal graph. A MAXIMUM price set BELOW equilibrium keeps essentials affordable but opens a gap where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — a shortage, with queues and black markets in its shadow. A MINIMUM price set ABOVE equilibrium creates a surplus; the minimum wage is its labour-market form, defended for dignity and attacked for pricing some workers out of jobs. Taxes on harmful things — the sugary drinks levy, fuel levies — shift supply left and shrink overproduction; subsidies on beneficial things shift supply right; direct provision covers public goods; and redistribution runs through progressive taxation and grants.

COST-BENEFIT ANALYSIS is the state's decision tool for large projects: count ALL costs and benefits over the project's life, private and social alike, discount them to today, and proceed only when social benefits exceed social costs. Its point is to force the unpriced consequences — pollution, travel time, health — into the arithmetic, which is exactly why the syllabus attaches it to market failure.

The questions for this section arrive now: the six causes sorted, both externality graphs in words, the shortage and the surplus, and the logic of cost-benefit analysis.

## Subtopic: Contemporary Issues: Inflation, Tourism and Environmental Sustainability

Three topics complete this half of the subject, and each can carry a full essay.

INFLATION: a sustained and significant increase in the general price level, measured by the consumer price index for households and the producer price index at the factory gate. The rate is the percentage change in the index — new minus old, divided by the OLD index, times one hundred. Demand-pull inflation comes from spending outrunning capacity: credit booms, government expenditure, export surges. Cost-push arrives from the supply side: wage settlements above productivity, a weaker rand raising import costs, fuel and electricity increases, drought. The consequences roll through the economy: debtors gain at creditors' expense, fixed incomes and savings erode, taxpayers creep into higher brackets, and expectations feed the spiral. The standing response is inflation targeting: the central bank steers consumer inflation into a three-to-six percent band with the repo rate — powerful against demand-pull, blunt against cost-push, because interest rates cannot cheapen oil.

TOURISM: the movement of people away from their usual surroundings for leisure, business or other purposes, counted through arrivals and their spending. Its effects reach GDP directly, and employment even faster, because tourism is labour-intensive and open at entry level, absorbing job-seekers quickly. Benefits spread to households through jobs and craft markets, to businesses through demand, to the state through taxes, and to everyone through infrastructure that residents keep using after the visitors fly home. South Africa's offer leans on wildlife, heritage sites and indigenous knowledge — and the policy conversation covers marketing the country, spreading visitors beyond the famous icons, sensible taxation and infrastructure investment.

ENVIRONMENTAL SUSTAINABILITY: development that meets present needs without robbing future generations. The state of the environment spans pollution, erosion, deforestation, climate change, conservation and preservation. Markets fail here because environmental costs go unpriced — so governments assign property rights, charge for environmental use, levy green taxes like the plastic bag levy, pay subsidies for clean alternatives, issue marketable permits that cap and trade the right to pollute, and command directly where pricing fails. Internationally: the Rio summit, the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement and the Sustainable Development Goals — with the honest evaluation resting on the distance between signatures and delivery.

Pause for the final Expert questions now — the inflation formula and its two engines, tourism's effects and beneficiaries, and the market and state tools for sustainability.

## Subtopic: The Spaza and the Giant: Market Structures Made Plain

Walk the street. Outside the station, a line of fruit sellers offers the same bananas at the same price — near-perfect competition. No seller can charge a rand more, because the next table is a step away; the price is simply given. Each seller's demand, told as a picture, is a flat line: sell all you like at the going price, sell nothing above it. When banana margins fatten, new tables appear within days, and the fat melts back to normal. That melting is the long run doing its patient work.

Now the giant. The water utility is a monopoly: one network of pipes, no substitute, and nobody digs a second network to compete. The giant still chooses output the way everyone does — where the cost of one more unit meets the revenue from one more unit — but then charges what its demand curve will bear above that point: less delivered, more charged, and no entrant can fix it, which is why a regulator watches its tariffs.

Between the many and the one sit the FEW: the fuel stations and the banks, each watching the others the way taxi associations watch a rival's fares on a shared route. Nobody moves price alone — a rise loses customers, a cut gets matched — so they fight with cleaner forecourts, loyalty cards and adverts instead. That is oligopoly, and its sticky price is the kinked demand curve wearing overalls.

And the spazas themselves are the MANY with a difference — monopolistic competition. Each shop is slightly its own thing: this one stays open latest, that one stocks the good bread, the third lets regulars buy on trust. Each keeps a loyal corner and earns nicely for a season — until a new spaza opens two doors down and copies the trick.

Quick check — questions on the street's four markets are coming to you right now: who takes the price, who makes it, who watches rivals, and who differentiates.

## Subtopic: When the Market Drops the Ball

The market is a brilliant delivery system with a few famous blind spots, all visible from one corner of the street.

Blind spot one: costs dumped on others. The taxi-washing bay pours its soapy runoff into the stream where children play. The wash price covers soap and labour — never the sick child downstream. Because the harm is priced at zero, there is more washing, and more runoff, than the street would ever choose. That is a negative externality: the true cost curve sits above the one the market uses.

Blind spot two: benefits that leak to everyone. The community study centre helps the learners who pay its small fee — but every neighbour gains too: safer evenings, better results, stronger future earners. Families weigh only their own benefit, so left alone the street buys too little studying. Positive externality: the market undershoots exactly on the best things.

Blind spot three: things nobody can sell. The streetlight shines on payers and non-payers alike, so no shop will ever build one for profit. Public goods arrive through the municipality or not at all — free riding sees to that.

Then the fixes, each with its catch. Cap the price of paraffin below the market and the drums run dry by mid-morning — a maximum price makes a shortage. Set a minimum wage above the going rate and the workers who keep jobs live better while some never get hired — a floor makes a surplus, and in the labour market a surplus wears a human face. Tax the runoff, subsidise the study centre, build the light. And before the municipality builds the new taxi rank, it should count EVERYBODY'S costs and benefits — the hawkers displaced, the commuters saved an hour, the air, the noise — not only the items with price tags. That is cost-benefit analysis in one sentence.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three blind spots, the shortage and the surplus, and which fix answers which failure.

## Subtopic: Prices, Tourists and the Planet in One Sweep

Three last stories on the same street.

Inflation is the street's rising tide. Month-end, bonus season, easy credit — everyone flush, and the shops mark up because they can: demand-pull, prices pulled from the till side. Then the wholesaler's diesel jumps, the rand slips, the electricity bill lands — and the shops mark up to survive: cost-push, prices shoved in through the back door. The cure must match the cause: the repo rate cools the flush spenders beautifully, but it cannot make diesel cheaper — hold that one sentence and the evaluation marks are yours. The three-to-six percent target band exists so the street can plan a year ahead: rent, stock, school fees.

Tourism is the street's export that never leaves home. The foreign visitor eating at the grill, buying beadwork, sleeping in the back-room guest suite, is South Africa selling to the world without loading a single container. The rand spreads wide and fast — the cook, the crafter, the cleaner, the driver — because tourism hires many hands at the door. That is why the country markets itself, coaxes visitors beyond the famous parks, and builds the roads and water that tourists need and residents keep.

And sustainability is the street's own yard. The free dump grows because free things get overused — so the town makes the polluter pay: a bag levy, a dumping charge, a tradeable permit, a fine for the worst offenders. Countries play the same game at world scale — Rio, Kyoto, Paris, the development goals — and the honest evaluation is the street's own wisdom: promises are easier than delivery. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: matching the cure to the inflation cause, tracing the visitor's rand along the street, and pricing the planet so the future is not the one who pays. Take the street with you — every graph in this half of the subject is hanging on it somewhere.
