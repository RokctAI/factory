# Part 1 — Expert

Macroeconomics and Economic Pursuits together tell one story: how money moves through a nation, how the state steers that movement, and how we measure whether the steering works. Final exams rebuild that story every year from the same skeleton — circular flow, business cycles, the public sector, foreign exchange, trade policy, growth strategies and the indicators that grade them. This revision session walks the skeleton end to end, pinning down the definitions, calculations, curve stories and evaluation lines that keep returning. Treat every stop as a promise about what you can be asked.

## Subtopic: The Circular Flow, the Multiplier and Business Cycles

Begin with the OPEN four-sector circular-flow model. Four participants — households, businesses, the state and the foreign sector — joined by two kinds of flows. REAL flows carry factors of production and goods; MONEY flows carry income and spending the opposite way. Households sell labour on the factor market and earn wages; businesses sell products on the product market and earn revenue; the state takes taxes in and sends services and grants out; the foreign sector joins through exports and imports. Between them sit the financial institutions, turning savings into investment, and the foreign exchange market, converting rands whenever trade crosses a border.

The equalities you must produce on demand live here. In equilibrium, leakages equal injections: savings plus taxes plus imports on one side, investment plus government spending plus exports on the other. Total income equals consumption plus investment plus government spending plus exports minus imports. Then the three methods of measuring GDP — production, income and expenditure — with the national account adjustments: basic prices become market prices by adding taxes on products and subtracting subsidies, and GDP becomes GNP by adjusting for income flowing between residents and the rest of the world.

The MULTIPLIER is the favourite calculation of this whole territory. It equals one divided by one minus the marginal propensity to consume — the same thing as one divided by the marginal propensity to save. If households spend 0,75 of each extra rand, the multiplier is one divided by 0,25, which is four: an injection of five billion rand of new investment eventually lifts income by twenty billion. Told as a graph in words: total spending rises with income, a forty-five degree line marks every point where spending equals income, and a small upward shift of the spending line drags equilibrium income rightward by a multiple of the shift.

Business cycles close the section. A cycle runs through recovery, prosperity, recession and depression around a rising trend line. Monetarists blame EXOGENOUS shocks from outside the market system and trust markets to heal themselves; Keynesians find ENDOGENOUS causes inside the economy — swings of confidence and spending — and want the state to lean against them. Forecasting draws on leading, coincident and lagging indicators, the length and amplitude of the cycle, extrapolation of the trend, and moving averages that smooth the noise.

Pause here — the first questions are with you now: flows and equalities, the multiplier calculation, GDP methods, and the two rival stories of the cycle.

## Subtopic: The Public Sector, the Budget and Fiscal Policy

The public sector exists to provide public goods, protect resources and manage the economy, and its five macro objectives are a list to know cold: economic growth, full employment, price stability, exchange rate stability with external balance, and economic equity. Set against them stand the PROBLEMS of public provisioning: weak accountability, inefficiency, the difficulty of assessing what people need, the awkward pricing of free services, struggling state enterprises, and the standing debate about privatisation.

The BUDGET is the state's annual money plan, and two documents around it are often confused. The Medium Term Expenditure Framework is the rolling three-year spending plan; the Medium Term Budget Policy Statement is the October update that adjusts course mid-year. Know where revenue comes from — personal income tax, value-added tax and company tax carry most of the load — where spending goes, and why public debt is watched as a percentage of GDP rather than as a raw number. Personal income tax can arrive as a table question, so practise reading brackets, rebates and thresholds until the arithmetic is calm.

FISCAL POLICY steers through those two levers, taxation and government spending. It is goal-bound, demand-focused and cyclical. It reshapes income distribution through progressive rates, consumption through value-added tax and transfers, and incentives through marginal rates — which is where the LAFFER CURVE earns its place. In words: tax revenue on the vertical axis, the tax rate on the horizontal; revenue is zero at nought percent because nothing is collected, zero again at one hundred percent because effort loses all reward, and between the extremes it climbs to a peak at some optimal rate before falling. Beyond that peak, cutting the rate raises MORE revenue — the sentence the whole curve exists to deliver.

Close with PUBLIC SECTOR FAILURE: management weakness, apathy, poor motivation, bureaucracy, political interference, structural rigidity and the pull of special interest groups — the reasons good intentions misfire, with consequences for resource allocation, income distribution and social stability.

Stop for this section's questions now — objectives against problems, the two budget documents told apart, the Laffer story in words, and the causes of public failure.

## Subtopic: Foreign Exchange Markets and the Balance of Payments

Nations trade for demand reasons — population size, income levels, tastes — and for supply reasons — natural resources, climate, labour skills, technology and specialisation. South Africa sends minerals, fruit, wine and vehicles into the world and brings back oil, machinery and electronics.

The BALANCE OF PAYMENTS records the whole exchange in three accounts. The CURRENT account carries everyday trade: goods exports plus net gold exports plus services and income receipts, against merchandise imports, payments for services and income payments. The CAPITAL TRANSFER account is small and quiet. The FINANCIAL account carries investment: direct investment, portfolio investment and reserve assets. Sort examples fast: a fruit export season lands in the current account; a foreign company buying a South African retailer is direct investment; a foreigner buying shares on the exchange is portfolio investment. Correcting a deficit is a standing paragraph question: raise interest rates to draw capital in, promote exports, manage imports, borrow with care, or let the exchange rate adjust.

Now the market where the rand is priced. The DEMAND for foreign currency comes from importers, South Africans travelling out and capital leaving; the SUPPLY comes from exporters, tourists arriving and investment flowing in. As a graph in words: the rand price of one unit of foreign currency on the vertical axis, the quantity on the horizontal; demand slopes down, supply slopes up, the crossing sets the rate. More imports shift demand right and the foreign currency costs more rands — the rand has DEPRECIATED. An export surge shifts supply right and the rand APPRECIATES. Keep the vocabulary tidy: depreciation and appreciation belong to floating systems; devaluation and revaluation belong to fixed ones. South Africa's rand floats freely, with the central bank smoothing extremes rather than defending a level.

Finish with the TERMS OF TRADE: the index of export prices divided by the index of import prices, times one hundred. When the ratio rises, each ton of exports buys more imports — an improvement even if not a single extra ton is shipped.

The questions for this section arrive now: the three accounts sorted, deficit corrections, the rand's market told in words, and the terms of trade formula.

## Subtopic: Economic Pursuits: Trade Policy, Growth and Indicators

Economic Pursuits opens with the trade-policy debate. EXPORT PROMOTION pushes local producers into world markets with incentives, finance and marketing support; its danger is subsidising firms that never learn to stand alone. IMPORT SUBSTITUTION shelters home industries behind tariffs and quotas so they can replace imports; its danger is protected producers who stay expensive and slow. The arguments FOR protection: infant industries, jobs, self-sufficiency, defence against dumping. The arguments FOR free trade: specialisation, economies of scale, wider choice, faster innovation, better international relations. The settled position is a sensible mix, tilted toward openness through the world trading system, with South Africa linked into its customs union with its neighbours, its regional development community, and its wider trade partnerships.

Growth and development next — and define both, because the distinction is worth marks on its own. GROWTH is the increase in real GDP; DEVELOPMENT is the widening of living standards, capabilities and choices. Demand-side approaches stimulate spending through monetary and fiscal policy; supply-side approaches cut the cost of doing business, sharpen market efficiency and build skills. Then the policy timeline in order: the Reconstruction and Development Programme rebuilt basic services after 1994; its successor strategy chased stability and growth; later initiatives targeted bottlenecks and skills; the New Growth Path chased jobs; and the National Development Plan aims at 2030. Regional policy adds Spatial Development Initiatives, development corridors linking industry to ports across borders, Industrial Development Zones on the coast, and their successors the Special Economic Zones with their tax incentives. The North-South divide frames it globally: an industrialised North, a developing South, and unequal power in trade between them.

Last, the INDICATORS. Economic indicators: inflation through the consumer and producer price indices, the exchange rate and terms of trade for the foreign sector, employment and unemployment against the economically active population, labour productivity, the repo rate, and the money supply measures. Social indicators: life expectancy, child mortality, clean water and sanitation, school enrolment, electricity and housing. International comparison runs through the standardised statistics of the global financial institutions.

Pause for the final Expert questions now — trade policy argued both ways, growth against development, the plans in order, and indicators sorted into economic and social.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole territory again, but told the way you would explain it at the taxi rank — because every model in macroeconomics is really a story about money moving through a town.

## Subtopic: Round and Round: The Economy as One Big Taxi Route

Picture your town's economy as its taxi route. Households climb aboard carrying the only thing they sell — their work. Businesses pay wages for it; the wages ride straight back to the shops as groceries, airtime and school shoes; the shops pay wages again, and the loop repeats. Money circles the town like the taxi does — that is the circular flow. But some money leaves the route: the rand saved at the bank, the rand taxed away, the rand spent on goods made elsewhere. Those are the LEAKAGES. And new money boards along the way: a business building a workshop, the state paying a grant, an export order from across the border. Those are the INJECTIONS. When the money leaving equals the money boarding, the route runs steady — leakages equal injections, and the town is in equilibrium.

The MULTIPLIER is what happens when one big new passenger boards. Say a firm spends eight million rand building a depot. The builders earn it and spend three quarters of it in town; the shopkeepers earn that and spend three quarters again; and the ripples keep going. If seventy-five cents of every rand is spent, the arithmetic says divide one by the twenty-five cents that leak — the multiplier is four, and the depot's eight million becomes thirty-two million of income around town.

And the BUSINESS CYCLE is the route through the year: full taxis in the busy season, the peak, the quiet months, the trough — all around a slowly rising trend, because the town itself keeps growing. One camp says the potholes come from outside and the road mends itself; the other says the swings start in the passengers' own moods and the town council must act. Those are your monetarists and your Keynesians, in taxi language.

Quick check — questions on this part are coming to you right now: leakages against injections, the multiplier as rippling spending, and the phases of the cycle.

## Subtopic: The Nation's Purse and the Price of the Rand

The government is the town's biggest household, and the budget is its purse. Money in: mostly personal income tax, the value-added tax riding on nearly every purchase, and company tax. Money out: schools, grants, clinics, police — and interest on debt, the line that swells whenever the purse borrows. The three-year plan is the household's long view; the October statement is its mid-year honesty check. And the Laffer curve is a tuckshop truth: sell the cooldrinks for nothing and the till stays empty; price them so high nobody buys and the till stays empty too; somewhere between sits the price that fills it. Tax rates obey the same arc — squeeze past the peak and the takings shrink.

Now the rand. An exchange rate is nothing grander than the PRICE of one money in another, set by buyers and sellers like mangoes at a street stall. Who queues to buy foreign money? Importers, travellers heading out, investors leaving. Who brings foreign money to the stall? Exporters, tourists arriving, investors coming in. When the buyers crowd the stall and sellers are few, foreign money gets expensive — the rand has weakened. And a weak rand cuts both ways: our fruit and wine become bargains abroad and the guest houses fill up, but petrol and imported medicine bite harder at home. Hold both sides — that balance is exactly what evaluation questions pay for.

The balance of payments is simply the town's till slip with the rest of the world: the current account is the everyday buying and selling, the financial account is the investment page. When the slip runs short, the fixes are household fixes — earn more by exporting, spend less on imports, borrow carefully, or let the stall price of the rand adjust until the queue thins.

Your questions for this part are up now: the purse, the tuckshop price, who queues at the rand's stall, and reading the till slip with the world.

## Subtopic: Growing the Pie: Trade, Plans and Report Cards

Should the town buy its school shoes from the cobbler on the corner or from the cheaper container shipment at the depot? Protect the cobbler with a levy on imported shoes and his three workers keep their jobs — but every family pays more for shoes. Open the depot and shoes get cheaper for everyone — but the cobbler must sharpen up or close. That is protection against free trade in one pair of shoes, and the settled answer is balance: shelter the genuinely young workshops for a while, open the road for the rest, and sign sensible agreements with the neighbouring towns — which is what the customs union and the regional community are at country scale.

Growth against development is a bigger pie against more people actually eating. The town's income can climb while the same families stay hungry — growth without development. The nation's plans, from the programme that brought taps and houses after 1994, through the discipline years, to the plan aiming at 2030, are all attempts to bake the pie bigger AND slice it wider. The zones and corridors — the coastal industrial zones, the cross-border corridor to the neighbouring port — are attempts to carry the ovens to where the unemployed already live.

And the indicators are the town's report card. Inflation is the price column, unemployment the jobs column, life expectancy and clean water the wellbeing columns. The reading habit that earns marks: never judge one column alone. A town can score high on income and fail on sanitation; the card only makes sense read whole, the way you would read a learner's report — for the pattern, not the single mark. And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the shoe debate, growth against development with the plans in order, and the report card read as a whole. Walk into your final exams knowing the skeleton never changes — only the numbers do.
