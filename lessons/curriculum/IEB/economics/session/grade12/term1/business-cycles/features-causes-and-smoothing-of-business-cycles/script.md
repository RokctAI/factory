# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow gave us an economy at rest at its equilibrium, and the multiplier gave us the arithmetic of a shock. This session studies what those shocks add up to over years: the long swell and slump of economic life. No real economy grows in a straight line — activity rises and falls in repeating waves called BUSINESS CYCLES, and the topic demands four skills of you: label the wave, explain where it comes from, operate the levers government uses to flatten it, and interpret the instruments analysts use to anticipate the next turn. South Africa has passed through every phase of this story within your own lifetime, so nothing in this lesson needs an imaginary example.

## Subtopic: The Anatomy of the Cycle

Begin with the definition, because every later idea hangs on it. A BUSINESS CYCLE is the successive periods of expansion and contraction in aggregate economic activity — measured by real GDP — around a long-term growth trend. Three words in that sentence carry the marks. SUCCESSIVE, because the phases arrive in a fixed order, one after the other. REAL, because we count physical output, not the price tags on it. TREND, because the whole wave rides on a slowly rising path that shows where the economy is going over decades.

Now build the diagram in your mind, because you must be able to draw it and label it under time pressure. Time runs along the horizontal axis; the level of real output climbs the vertical axis. Draw a gently rising straight line first — that is the TREND LINE, the economy's long-run growth path. Around it weaves the wave. Start at the bottom of a dip: the TROUGH, the lowest point of the cycle, the moment activity finally stops falling. From there the UPSWING, also called the expansion: output, incomes, employment and spending all climbing together, the wave cutting up through the trend line. At the top sits the PEAK, the highest point, where activity can rise no further — factories close to capacity, skilled labour scarce, prices starting to accelerate. Then the DOWNSWING or contraction, with output, incomes and jobs falling away until the wave bottoms out in the next trough. The peak and the trough are the TURNING POINTS — the two moments where the direction of the whole economy reverses.

Two measurements taken off that drawing are always asked. The LENGTH of the cycle is horizontal: the time from one peak to the next peak, or from trough to trough. The AMPLITUDE is vertical: the distance between the trend line and a peak or a trough. Amplitude measures the INTENSITY of the cycle — a big amplitude means a wild boom or a punishing slump, and the rule of thumb is that the larger the amplitude, the more powerful the shock behind it and the longer the economy takes to shake off its effects.

Attach the vocabulary of severity. A RECESSION is conventionally recognised after two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. A DEPRESSION is a recession that is both deep and long. South Africa hands you dated illustrations: the contraction of two thousand and nine, imported from the global financial crisis; and the collapse of two thousand and twenty, when the pandemic lockdowns produced the deepest trough in the modern record before the recovery upswing began. The years of severe load-shedding show something subtler — an economy held below its own trend line without necessarily meeting the formal recession test.

Pause here, because this section's questions are with you now: the definition word by word, the four phases in their fixed order, the two turning points, and length and amplitude read off the diagram you have just built.

## Subtopic: Why Cycles Happen — Causes and Types

The explanation question is where the topic becomes a debate, and the material sorts the answers into two rival camps, then adds a catalogue of named cycles.

EXOGENOUS explanations put the cause OUTSIDE the market system. Drought scorches the maize crop; a war closes a shipping lane; an oil shock raises every firm's costs on the same morning; a pandemic empties the shops by law. This is the position associated with the MONETARISTS, and it comes with a strong claim: markets themselves are stable and self-correcting, so if the economy lurches, look for the disturbance outside — and, monetarists insist, look hardest at GOVERNMENT, whose own conduct is the biggest shock of all: money supply pumped up and then strangled, budgets loosened before elections and tightened after them. Their policy conclusion follows directly: not cleverer intervention, but stable, predictable, rules-based policy that stops being the problem.

ENDOGENOUS explanations put the cause INSIDE the market system. This is the KEYNESIAN position: markets do not reliably correct themselves, because prices and wages are sticky and because expectations feed on their own momentum. Confidence improves, firms invest, the multiplier magnifies the new spending, and the rising demand itself induces further investment through the accelerator — an upswing that manufactures its own fuel. Then confidence snaps, projects are shelved, the multiplier grinds into reverse, and the downswing deepens itself by the same logic. If the instability is built in, the conclusion is unavoidable: government MUST lean against the cycle. Learn both camps with their policy conclusions attached — the contrast between them is a classic discussion question.

Then the catalogue: four cycle types, each named for its discoverer, ordered from shortest to longest. KITCHIN cycles run roughly three to five years and are driven by firms building up and then running down INVENTORIES. JUGLAR cycles run roughly seven to eleven years, driven by waves of investment in MACHINERY and equipment. KUZNETS cycles run roughly fifteen to twenty years, driven by BUILDING and construction. KONDRATIEFF waves stretch forty-five to sixty years, driven by deep TECHNOLOGICAL revolutions — steam power, railways, electricity, and in our own generation the digital wave. The short cycles ride on top of the long ones, which is why no two downturns ever look quite the same.

Stop for this section's questions now — exogenous versus endogenous with the monetarist and Keynesian labels correctly pinned on, each school's cure, and the four named cycles matched to their lengths and their drivers.

## Subtopic: Leaning Against the Wave — Monetary and Fiscal Policy

No modern government simply rides the wave; it leans against it with two sets of levers, and you must be able to work each lever in both directions.

MONETARY POLICY is the business of the South African Reserve Bank, whose constitutional job is to protect the value of the currency — in practice, to keep inflation inside the three to six percent target band. Its principal lever is the REPO RATE, the rate at which the Reserve Bank lends to the commercial banks. Move the repo and every other interest rate in the country moves with it: bond rates, vehicle finance, overdrafts. Behind the repo stand the supporting instruments: OPEN-MARKET TRANSACTIONS, in which the Bank sells government securities to soak cash out of the banking system or buys them back to release it; the CASH RESERVE REQUIREMENT, the slice of deposits banks must keep unlent; and MORAL SUASION, the quiet pressure of the Governor's word. In a DOWNSWING the Bank turns EXPANSIONARY: in two thousand and twenty it cut the repo to a record low of three comma five percent so that cheap credit could carry households and firms across the pandemic trough. In an overheating UPSWING it turns CONTRACTIONARY: the run of repo increases through two thousand and twenty-two and twenty-three was the Bank answering inflation that had escaped above the top of the target band.

FISCAL POLICY is the business of the National Treasury, working through the budget. In a downswing, EXPANSIONARY fiscal policy: lift government spending, cut taxes, let the deficit widen, and allow the multiplier to convert the injection into a larger rise in national income. In a boom that is straining capacity, CONTRACTIONARY fiscal policy: hold spending back, raise taxes, close the deficit. And beside the deliberate decisions stand the AUTOMATIC STABILISERS — progressive income tax, which automatically takes a smaller bite as incomes fall, and social grants and the Unemployment Insurance Fund, which automatically pay out more as jobs disappear. They cushion the cycle without a single new announcement, which is exactly why they act faster than any stimulus package that must first be debated.

Now state the limits, because the evaluation is where the top marks live. Both levers operate with LAGS — a repo change takes months to reach the tills, and an infrastructure budget takes years to become wages — so a well-meant intervention that lands late can amplify the very wave it was meant to damp. Deficits financed by heavy borrowing pile up debt-service costs and can crowd private borrowers out of the capital market. And no demand-side lever can repair a supply-side fault: no interest rate, however low, generates a single megawatt.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the Reserve Bank's four instruments, both policies run in both directions with dated South African examples, the automatic stabilisers, and the limits honestly stated.

## Subtopic: The New Economic Paradigm and Reading the Future

The topic closes with two modern pieces: the current consensus on smoothing, and the forecaster's toolkit.

The NEW ECONOMIC PARADIGM is the answer to an old trap. Governments that chased employment with pure demand stimulus bought it at the price of inflation, and eventually got the inflation without the employment. The new paradigm targets stability itself: smooth the cycle by pairing moderate, PREDICTABLE demand-side policy with active SUPPLY-SIDE reform. On the demand side, inflation targeting and disciplined budgets, so that firms and workers plan around policy instead of gambling on it. On the supply side, everything that raises the economy's capacity and efficiency — infrastructure, skills, competition, cheaper and simpler ways of doing business — so that the trend line itself steepens and demand can grow further before it slams into the inflation ceiling. Notice the modest ambition: smoothing does not abolish the cycle. It narrows the amplitude.

Then FORECASTING, whose features you must know by name. Economic INDICATORS are data series that move with the cycle in a known time order. LEADING indicators turn BEFORE the economy does — new manufacturing orders, building plans passed, share prices, business confidence surveys. The Reserve Bank compiles a composite leading indicator that is watched closely precisely because it tends to bend months before the economy follows. COINCIDENT indicators turn WITH the economy — retail sales, industrial production, the readings straight off the tills and factory floors. LAGGING indicators turn AFTER the economy — unemployment above all, which keeps worsening after the trough because firms only rehire once they trust the recovery. A COMPOSITE indicator blends many series into a single index so that the noise in any one of them cancels out.

Three techniques finish the kit. The TREND LINE projects the long-run path forward. EXTRAPOLATION stretches an observed pattern into the future — genuinely useful, and built on the one assumption that turning points exist to destroy: that the future will resemble the past. MOVING AVERAGES tame a jumpy series by averaging each period with its neighbours, filtering out the short-term static until the underlying direction shows through. Add the cycle's own LENGTH and AMPLITUDE from the first section — an upswing that has already run unusually long and high is itself a warning — and you hold every feature that underpins forecasting.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the new paradigm as steady demand plus stronger supply, the three indicator families with correctly ordered examples, and extrapolation and moving averages each with its use and its flaw.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chapter from underneath — because a business cycle is not a squiggle on a chart; it is a fat year and a lean year arriving at every till in the same town at nearly the same time.

## Subtopic: A Town That Breathes

Picture a platinum-belt mining town, the kind where one mine's siren sets the rhythm for every shop on the main road. Watch it through one full breath.

Start at the bottom. The metal price has been low for two years. The mine runs skeleton shifts, the hostel has empty beds, the hardware store owner dusts shelves he has not restocked in months. Nothing is getting worse anymore — it is just flat and quiet. That is the TROUGH.

Then the platinum price lifts. The mine reopens a section and calls back a shift; the contractor wins a maintenance tender and hires welders; pay-day queues return to the ATMs. Those wages are spent at the taxi rank, the shisanyama and the furniture shop, and THOSE takings get spent again — the town climbing month by month on its own spending. That is the UPSWING, and the climb-feeding-the-climb is the multiplier running upward.

Then the town maxes out. Overtime on every roster, not a spare boilermaker between here and the next town at any wage, rents on the main road jumping every renewal. The town is producing all it can, and the strain shows up as rising prices. That is the PEAK.

Then something turns the price — demand for the metal softens overseas, or costs climb until marginal shafts stop paying. The mine cancels overtime, then a shift. The welder's wages vanish from the shisanyama's till; the furniture shop stops ordering; each loss knocks over the next. That is the DOWNSWING, and it runs until things stop getting worse and the town sits quiet again — a new trough, waiting for the next lift.

Keep one picture: the town's activity is a wave, and under the wave runs a slow slope — a little higher each decade as the road is tarred and skills accumulate. The wave is the cycle. The slope is the trend. How far the wave swings away from the slope is the amplitude.

Quick check before we go on — the questions on the town are coming to you right now: the four phases spotted in the story, the two turning points, and the wave told apart from the slope it rides.

## Subtopic: The Thermostat and the Budget

Somebody watches that wave and leans against it — two somebodies, in fact, holding two different levers.

The Reserve Bank holds the price of borrowed money. Treat the repo rate as a THERMOSTAT for the country's spending. Town frozen — trough, idle shafts, empty tills? Turn the dial DOWN: bond instalments shrink, the contractor can finance a bakkie, the mine can afford the loan that reopens a section. That is what the record-low rate of two thousand and twenty was — heat pumped into a frozen economy. Town running a fever — everything maxed out, prices sprinting? Turn the dial UP: repayments bite, big purchases get postponed, the sprint slows to a walk. That is what the hikes of two thousand and twenty-two and twenty-three were. One dial, two directions.

The Treasury holds the budget, and it leans the same two ways. In the lean year government can spend more and tax less — resurface the road, hire the clinic nurses, accept a wider deficit — and every rand of it goes around the town's tills more than once. In the fat year it does the opposite and banks the difference. And one part of the budget leans with nobody touching it: the day the welder is retrenched, he stops paying income tax and starts drawing from the UIF, so his family's spending falls by less than his wage did. The safety net doubles as a shock absorber.

But say the honest part too, because honest evaluation is what a strong Grade 12 answer sounds like. Both levers are SLOW — a rate cut in March reaches the shops around December, and a road budget only becomes wages when the graders actually arrive. Medicine that lands after the fever has broken can start a new fever. And neither lever can mine an ounce of platinum or generate a megawatt: if the town's real problem is a flooded shaft or a dead substation, cheap loans and big budgets just park money in a queue behind the fault.

Your questions for this part are up now: the thermostat turned both ways with the real years attached, the budget leaning both ways, the automatic shock absorber, and both honest limits.

## Subtopic: Reading the Signs Before the Wave Turns

Last piece — how anyone sees the turn coming, and how to say it like an economist.

The town broadcasts its own early warnings. Before the good times arrive, some signs move FIRST: the mine's order book for equipment, house plans lodged at the municipality, the contractor pricing tenders again, managers sounding cheerful in surveys. Economists call these LEADING indicators — the things people do TODAY because of what they expect TOMORROW. Other signs move WITH the town — the tills themselves, retail sales, tonnes hoisted: COINCIDENT indicators. And some arrive LATE: the mine converts contractors to permanent staff only months after the recovery began, exactly as unemployment nationally keeps rising after the trough has already passed — LAGGING indicators.

Two tricks turn noisy signs into readable ones. A single week's takings bounce around — a pay-day weekend, a funeral, a storm — so smooth them: average each month with the months on either side, and the jagged line settles into a curve you can trust. That is a MOVING AVERAGE. Then slide that curve forward along its current direction to guess next quarter — that is EXTRAPOLATION, and it works right up to the turning point, which is precisely where it breaks. Write that sentence in an essay and you sound like a professional: extrapolation assumes the future repeats the past, and cycles exist because sometimes it refuses to.

And the modern wisdom in one line: government has stopped trying to abolish the wave and now tries to keep it SMALL — steady, predictable policy on the demand side, and on the supply side everything that lifts the slope the wave rides on: skills, transport, reliable electricity, easier business. Narrow the amplitude, raise the trend.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: leading, coincident and lagging signs sorted from the town's story, the moving average and extrapolation each with its limit, and the smoothing idea written in your own words. Keep this wave in your head — business cycles feed straight into the public sector, fiscal policy and every growth essay you will write this year.
