# Part 1 — Expert

The four-sector circular flow left one participant half-explained: the foreign sector that buys our exports and sells us imports. Today that sector gets the full treatment: why nations bother to trade at all, the national bank statement on which every cross-border rand is entered — the balance of payments — the market where the rand itself carries a price, and the toolkit a country reaches for when its foreign accounts fall persistently out of balance. This is the machinery behind every headline about the rand weakening, and one of the richest sources of data questions you will face.

## Subtopic: Why Nations Trade — Absolute and Comparative Advantage

Begin with the reasons for trade, which come from both sides of the counter. On the DEMAND side: no country makes everything its people want. South Africa grows no coffee beans, builds no passenger aircraft, refines too little of its own fuel — and differences in income, population and taste create wants that home production cannot satisfy. On the SUPPLY side: countries are unequally endowed — we hold platinum, coal, sunshine and a long coastline; others hold oil, forests, or dense pools of engineering skill; capital, technology and climate differ too. The logic of specialisation follows: make what your endowments favour, buy the rest, and both partners end up consuming more than they could alone.

Two technical terms sharpen the logic. ABSOLUTE advantage: producing a good with fewer resources than the other country — simply more output per worker. COMPARATIVE advantage is the subtler and stronger idea: specialise where your OPPORTUNITY COST is lowest — where producing that good costs you the least in other goods forgone — and this rule pays off even when one country is absolutely better at everything.

Prove it with a two-country table. With identical resources, South Africa can produce eighty tons of citrus or twenty tons of electronics, while South Korea can produce one hundred tons of citrus or two hundred tons of electronics. Korea beats us at both — absolute advantage across the board. Now price each good in the other good. A ton of electronics costs South Africa four tons of citrus, but costs Korea only half a ton of citrus. A ton of citrus costs Korea two tons of electronics, but costs South Africa only a quarter of a ton of electronics. Korea's smallest sacrifice is in electronics; ours is in citrus. Let each specialise where its sacrifice is least, and trade at any ratio between the two opportunity costs — say one ton of citrus for one ton of electronics — and BOTH countries consume beyond their own production frontiers. Mutual gain with no requirement that anyone be best at anything: that is the deepest theorem in the topic.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: demand-side and supply-side reasons for trade, absolute set against comparative advantage, and the opportunity costs extracted from a two-country table.

## Subtopic: The Balance of Payments

Every transaction between South Africa and the world lands in the BALANCE OF PAYMENTS — the country's bank statement with everyone else, compiled by the Reserve Bank. Learn the accounts in order, because a data question will hand you the statement and ask where an item lives.

The CURRENT ACCOUNT records trade and income flowing NOW. Merchandise trade first: minerals, vehicles and fruit going out; oil, machinery and electronics coming in — the difference is the TRADE BALANCE. Then services: freight, insurance, tourism — remember that a foreign visitor spending money here counts as a service EXPORT, because their money flows in. Then income: dividends and interest crossing the border in both directions, plus current transfers such as workers' remittances. South Africa's current account usually shows a modest deficit: the import bill plus the dividends we pay the foreign owners of our companies tend to outweigh what our commodity exports earn.

The CAPITAL TRANSFER account is a sliver — debt forgiveness, migrants moving assets. The FINANCIAL ACCOUNT is the current account's mirror: it shows HOW the net imports were paid for. DIRECT investment: a foreign carmaker building a plant here — lasting ownership, hard to reverse. PORTFOLIO investment: foreigners buying our shares and government bonds — reversible at the click of a screen, hence the nickname HOT MONEY. OTHER investment: cross-border loans and deposits. The accounting discipline: a current-account deficit must be financed by a financial-account surplus — we buy more from the world than we sell to it, and the gap is settled by foreigners acquiring South African assets. Last line: the official GOLD AND FOREIGN-EXCHANGE RESERVES held by the Reserve Bank, the shock absorber that moves so the whole statement balances.

Standing behind the world's payment systems is the INTERNATIONAL MONETARY FUND — the IMF — with one hundred and ninety member countries and a pooled fund of currencies. It is the lender a country approaches when it can no longer pay its way, and its loans arrive wearing CONDITIONS: spending cuts, tax reform, liberalisation. That conditionality is why an IMF rescue is simultaneously a lifeline and a controversy.

Stop for this section's questions now — the accounts in order, sample items placed correctly, the deficit-needs-financing logic, hot money named and explained, and the IMF with its conditions.

## Subtopic: The Foreign Exchange Market and How the Rand Is Priced

A rand buys nothing in Seoul. International trade therefore needs a market where currencies themselves are traded: the FOREIGN EXCHANGE MARKET — not a building anywhere, but the world's banks dealing with each other around the clock. The price that emerges is the EXCHANGE RATE: how many rands one unit of foreign money costs — say, nineteen rand to the dollar.

The rate is a market price, so construct its two curves in words. The DEMAND for dollars is everyone who must PAY the world in dollars: importers of fuel and electronics, South Africans touring abroad, our companies remitting dividends or servicing dollar debt, residents buying foreign shares. The SUPPLY of dollars is everyone converting dollars INTO rands: foreign buyers of our coal, cars and citrus, tourists stepping off planes at Cape Town International, foreign investors purchasing our bonds or building factories. Draw it exactly like any market: quantity of dollars along the horizontal axis, the rand price of one dollar up the vertical; demand slopes down, supply slopes up, and the crossing is today's rate.

Now move the curves. Suppose a drought forces heavy wheat imports: importers need more dollars at every price, demand shifts right, the rand price of the dollar climbs — the rand DEPRECIATES. Suppose a bumper citrus season or a wave of bond-buying brings dollars flooding in: supply shifts right, the dollar cheapens — the rand APPRECIATES. Guard the vocabulary: under a floating system, market moves are called depreciation and appreciation; DEVALUATION and REVALUATION describe deliberate resets by a government running a fixed rate. Mixing the pairs costs an easy mark.

Three SYSTEMS complete the theory. FREE-FLOATING: the market alone prices the currency — South Africa's arrangement, with the Reserve Bank intervening only in exceptional moments. MANAGED FLOATING: the central bank trades in the market to hold the rate inside an unannounced corridor. FIXED or PEGGED: the rate is declared and defended with reserves. The trade-off runs one way in each direction: floating absorbs shocks and leaves the central bank free to fight inflation, but delivers volatility; fixing gives traders certainty, but eats reserves and invites speculators to test the peg.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the two queues of dollar users, the diagram with its axes, shifts producing depreciation and appreciation, the guarded vocabulary, and the three systems weighed.

## Subtopic: Correcting a Disequilibrium

A balance of payments is in DISEQUILIBRIUM when deficits — or surpluses — persist beyond what routine financing can cover. Chronic DEFICITS bleed reserves, stack up foreign debt and end in crisis if ignored. Chronic SURPLUSES sound like winning but mean the nation consumes less than it produces while its partners accumulate the matching deficits. Learn the four correction channels as a toolkit, each tagged with its price.

Channel one: the EXCHANGE RATE. Under floating, correction begins automatically — a deficit is excess demand for foreign currency, so the rand slides; our exports become bargains abroad while imports grow dear at home; export volumes expand, import volumes contract, and the gap narrows. The price: imported inflation, since fuel, machinery and food all cost more in rand — and a lag, because the higher import bill lands immediately while export volumes need seasons and contracts to respond.

Channel two: DEMAND management. Tighten monetary and fiscal policy — raise interest rates, restrain the budget — and total spending cools; since imports are bought out of income, the import bill shrinks with it, while higher rates pull in foreign portfolio money to finance what remains. The price: slower growth and more unemployment. The cure works by making the patient poorer.

Channel three: DIRECT controls. Tariffs and quotas on imports, and EXCHANGE CONTROL — rationing residents' access to foreign currency, a tool South Africa applied strictly for decades and still keeps in a relaxed form. The price: retaliation from trading partners, smuggling and evasion, the deadweight of a rationed market — and the discipline of World Trade Organisation rules, which cap how far this channel may be pushed.

Channel four: BORROWING and RESERVES. Run down the reserve cushion, or approach the IMF, to finance the gap while slower corrections take hold. The price: debt, and conditionality.

For a stubborn SURPLUS, operate the toolkit backwards: let the currency appreciate, stimulate domestic demand, open the import door wider. And the mark of a top answer is diagnosis before prescription: a deficit caused by an overvalued currency calls for channel one; a deficit caused by an overheated, over-importing economy calls for channel two.

The final questions of this part are with you now: disequilibrium defined on both sides, each channel with its mechanism and its cost, and the cure matched to the cause.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic at the scale of one street — because a trading nation is a household enlarged: it earns, it spends, it borrows, and its money must be changed at the border.

## Subtopic: The Braai Exchange

Saturday braai on one street, two gardens. Karabo grows vegetables — spinach, tomatoes, chillies that frighten visitors. Elsa bakes — bread, rolls, the works. Karabo could bake, badly, at the cost of an afternoon's gardening. Elsa could garden, badly, at the cost of her baking morning. Nobody negotiates anything: he brings vegetables for both plates, she brings bread for both, and every braai on that street eats better than either household could manage alone. No law arranged it; the waste saved by swapping did.

Now the harder case — the one that decides real marks. Suppose Elsa is better at BOTH: quicker in the garden and unbeatable at the oven. Should she cut Karabo out? Count each hour in what it costs the person spending it. An hour Elsa gives to gardening costs her two loaves she would have baked; an hour Karabo gives to gardening costs him almost nothing, because his baking would hardly have risen anyway. Elsa's gardening hour is expensive TO ELSA. Karabo's is cheap TO KARABO. So Elsa still wins by leaving the vegetables to Karabo and pouring every hour into the bakery where nobody touches her — and Karabo wins a customer. Being better at everything never means it pays to DO everything: do what costs you least, trade for the rest. Countries are Karabo and Elsa with anthems — we send the world platinum and oranges and buy oil and aircraft, though with enough sacrifice we could fumble at both ourselves.

One snag waits at the garden gate: Elsa will not accept Karabo's homemade vegetable vouchers as money forever — and South Korea will not accept rands. Between any two traders stands the question of WHOSE money settles the deal. Keep that thought warm; it is the next section.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the braai trade are coming to you right now: why the swap needs no permission, the both-gain result when one side is better at everything, and every choice priced in what it costs the chooser.

## Subtopic: The Rand Has a Price Tag Too

Everything on the shop shelf has a price in rands. Now the loop that makes first-time students blink: the RAND ITSELF has a price, quoted in other money — call it nineteen rand to the dollar — and that price moves for precisely the reason the price of tomatoes moves: buyers and sellers.

Who queues to BUY dollars with rands? Everyone with a bill from the world: the fuel importer, the electronics chain restocking phones, the parents paying a son's fees at a foreign university, the pension fund adding American shares. Who queues to SELL dollars for rands? Everyone the world is paying: the coal exporter paid by India, the citrus farmers paid by Europe, the tourists changing money at the airport, the foreign fund buying South African government bonds. When the buying queue is long and the selling queue short, the dollar grows dearer — the rand WEAKENS, depreciates. Swap the queue lengths and the rand FIRMS — appreciates.

Now test it against the news. A drought forces massive wheat imports — the buy queue swells — rand weakens. A ratings downgrade spooks foreign funds into dumping our bonds — the sell queue empties and joins the buy queue — rand weakens sharply. A record citrus harvest lands in European supermarkets — export dollars stream into the sell queue — rand firms. An interest-rate rise makes our bonds juicier — foreign money queues to come in — rand firms. Four headlines, one picture: two queues.

Who sets the price? In South Africa, nobody — the rand FLOATS and the Reserve Bank lets the queues decide, stepping in only on wild days. Other countries FIX their currency's price and spend their reserves defending the number — which holds until the day the whole world lines up on one side of the counter, betting the defence will crack. Floating is bumpy but honest; fixed is calm until it snaps.

Your questions for this part are up now: both queues staffed with real members, weakening and firming read straight off the queue lengths, four headlines explained, and floating against fixed in one sentence each.

## Subtopic: When the Family Spends More Than It Earns

Run one household's kitchen like a country, and the balance of payments turns friendly.

The family EARNS from the outside world: two salaries, rent from the garage room, the odd weekend catering job. It SPENDS to the outside world: groceries, taxi money, school fees, the streaming subscription. Rule both columns in an exercise book — that book is the CURRENT ACCOUNT. In a month where spending outruns earning, the gap must be COVERED: draw down the savings account, borrow from a relative, or sell the old trailer. That covering page is the FINANCIAL ACCOUNT. And the envelope of emergency cash taped behind the kitchen cupboard is the RESERVES. Every nation keeps this exact book — ours is kept by the Reserve Bank — and South Africa's family usually spends a little more than it earns, covered by the world buying our bonds and shares: the relative's loan, at national scale.

One tight month is nothing. Every month for years is something. The savings run thin, and the lending relative begins attaching conditions — cut the streaming, show me the budget, fix the leaking geyser before you ask again. Nations have that relative too: the IMF, the lender of last approach, whose conditions — spending cuts, tax reform — are exactly as unwelcome in a cabinet meeting as across a kitchen table.

How does the family close a chronic gap? By the same four moves a country makes. Its services can become CHEAPER to outsiders — a depreciating rand performs this automatically for a nation, turning its exports into bargains and its imports into luxuries. It can TIGHTEN THE BELT — spend less across the board, the high-interest-rate cure: effective, painful. It can BAN some spending outright — cancel the subscription; nationally, tariffs and import controls, until the neighbours retaliate against the weekend catering. Or it can BORROW properly and buy time to adjust. The examinable skill is fitting cure to disease: the family that simply overspends needs the belt; the family whose prices are uncompetitive needs the price cut.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the exercise book's pages matched to the accounts, the envelope behind the cupboard, the relative with conditions, and the four family cures paired with the four national channels. Keep the queues and the exercise book close — they carry you through trade policy next term and every rand headline between now and your final exams.
