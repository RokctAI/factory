Hi, Mandy here — the four-sector circular flow can look like a plate of spaghetti the first time: boxes, arrows both ways, six letters flying around. Take a breath, because underneath it is one sentence and three little formulas.

The sentence: money leaks out through saving, tax and imports, and gets injected back through investment, government spending and exports — and the economy sits still when the two totals match. S plus T plus M equals I plus G plus X. Say it in the shower until it says itself. That single line answers the equilibrium question, the disequilibrium question, and most policy questions too, because every policy you will ever name is just someone nudging one of those six letters.

The classic trap: assuming each pair must balance on its own — saving with investment, tax with government spending. They don't have to. Only the TOTALS must match. Question-setters love handing you figures where one pair is deliberately lopsided to catch the students who judge before they add. Add first. Then judge.

The three formulas are free marks if you write them down before touching any numbers. Factor cost equals market prices minus product taxes plus subsidies. GNI equals GDP plus primary income received from abroad minus primary income paid out. Real equals nominal times one hundred over the deflator. Formula, substitute, calculate — in that order, every time, because the method earns credit even when the arithmetic wobbles.

And in the essay, define before you use: say what a leakage is before listing three, say what equilibrium means before testing it. Definitions are the cheapest marks on the page, and they are the first thing nervous students skip.
