**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and honestly it's mine too: if R2 000 of spending really becomes R8 000 of income, why doesn't government just spend without limit and multiply us all rich?

**Tutor:** Because the multiplier multiplies SPENDING, and spending only turns into wealth when there are idle resources for it to wake up. Compare the two worlds. In a town with an unemployed fitter and a butchery running below capacity, the injection is answered with real production: pipes actually get laid, more chicken actually gets sold, and the eight thousand rand of income corresponds to eight thousand rand of goods and services that did not exist before. Everyone is genuinely better off. Now inject the same money into an economy already producing everything it can. The arithmetic still runs, the incomes still change hands, the total still reads eight thousand — but no extra pipe is laid and no extra chicken exists, so the rising spending simply bids up the prices of the same goods. The multiplier has multiplied inflation. And there is a second brake: the money has to come from somewhere. If it is borrowed, it accrues interest and must be repaid, and heavy state borrowing can push interest rates up until private firms cancel their own projects — crowding out, quietly deleting part of the gain. So the formula tells you how far a rand travels; it never promises that anything new is made along the road. Pair the calculation with the conditions, always.

**Mandy:** Next: the simple formula gave us a multiplier of five, but the open-economy one gave two. In an answer, how do I know which to use?

**Tutor:** Let the data decide, and then say why in one sentence. If a question hands you only an MPC or an MPS, it is testing the simple model: k equals one over one minus the MPC. The moment it also mentions a marginal propensity to tax or to import, it wants the open-economy denominator with all three leakages added first. But the gap between five and two is more than arithmetic — it is a description of South Africa worth a full paragraph. Our tax system takes a meaningful bite of each additional rand, and we import a large slice of what we consume, along with nearly all our machinery and fuel. Every round of the chain therefore surrenders about half of itself before the next pocket is reached, which is why our real-world multiplier sits near the small end. The policy consequence follows directly, and you can state it with confidence: what a stimulus BUYS decides what it achieves. A programme of local labour — construction, maintenance, early-childhood posts — keeps its rands circulating at home for many rounds; a programme dominated by imported equipment exports much of its own value in round one. Same headline number in the budget speech, very different chain.

**Mandy:** This one started a fight at lunch: if saving shrinks the multiplier, is saving bad? Because every adult I know tells me to save.

**Tutor:** Both sides of that fight are holding a real piece of the truth, and the resolution is the difference between the short run and the long run. Short run first, especially in a downswing: if every household suddenly saves harder, total spending drops, shops sell less, firms cut output and jobs, and incomes fall — with the bitter twist that a country of poorer households may end up saving no more in total than before. That is the paradox of thrift, and naming it precisely earns respect in an essay. But run the clock forward and the verdict flips. Saving is the raw material of the financial market: it funds the loans that build factories, roads, machinery and skills, and that investment is what grows the economy's capacity to produce tomorrow. A nation that saves too little must import the savings of foreigners to invest at all — which is exactly South Africa's structural position, and a genuine vulnerability whenever foreign money gets nervous. So the mature sentence goes: saving withdraws demand today and builds capacity for tomorrow, and which effect dominates depends on whether the economy's current disease is too little spending or too little capacity. Diagnose first, then judge the saving.

**Mandy:** Last one: can a multiplier ever be less than one? Someone in class swears their cousin's textbook says it can.

**Tutor:** In the models you are examined on, it cannot — and seeing why is a nice test of whether you own the formula. The denominator is the sum of the leakage propensities, and each is a fraction of a rand, so the sum sits between zero and one. One divided by a number smaller than one is always bigger than one. Push the leakages to an extreme — saving, tax and imports together claiming eighty cents of each rand — and the multiplier is one over nought comma eight, which is one comma two five: small, but still above one, because the very first round always delivers the full injection as somebody's income. What the cousin's textbook is gesturing at is the world beyond the simple model, where OFFSETS operate. Borrowing-financed spending can raise interest rates and cancel private investment; households expecting future tax rises may save more today; the new demand may suck in imports faster than the measured propensity suggests. Net of such offsets, the OBSERVED change in national income can indeed fall short of the amount spent, and careful studies sometimes find exactly that. So keep two labels ready in an essay: the calculated multiplier, which the formula gives and which exceeds one; and the net effect after offsets, which the real economy delivers and which can disappoint. Distinguishing them is the mark of a student who understands rather than recites.
