# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow told us that an injection raises national income. This session answers the follow-up question: raises it by HOW MUCH? And the answer is never rand for rand. A new rand of spending becomes somebody's income, part of it is spent onward, that spending becomes income again, and the original rand echoes around the economy several times before the echo fades. That echo is the MULTIPLIER — one of the few genuinely quantitative tools in your macroeconomics, and the reason a spending announcement is worth more than the number printed on it.

## Subtopic: Autonomous Spending, the MPC and the MPS

Three definitions must be word-perfect, because the formula is assembled from them and the definition earns its credit before any calculation begins.

AUTONOMOUS consumption is spending that does not depend on current income — the food, rent and transport a household buys even if income drops to zero, paid for out of past savings, borrowing, grants or family support. INDUCED consumption is the portion that rises and falls WITH income. As a consumption function: C = C₀ + cY, where C₀ is the autonomous part and c is the fraction of every extra rand of income that gets spent.

That fraction c is the MARGINAL PROPENSITY TO CONSUME — the MPC — defined as the change in consumption divided by the change in income: MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY. Its twin is the MARGINAL PROPENSITY TO SAVE — the MPS — the change in saving divided by the change in income. In the simple model, where every extra rand is either spent or saved, MPC + MPS = 1.

Compute one. A household's monthly income rises by three thousand rand and its spending rises by two thousand four hundred. MPC = 2 400 ÷ 3 000 = 0,8. MPS = 600 ÷ 3 000 = 0,2. Together they make one, as they must.

Two precision points that mark out careful work. First, MARGINAL is not AVERAGE. The MPC describes the extra rand only: a family might spend ninety-five percent of its whole income yet spend only eighty percent of a raise. Second, the MPC falls as income rises — a household living grant to grant spends virtually every additional cent, while a well-paid professional banks most of a bonus. Hold on to that fact; it is why WHERE an injection lands changes how hard it works, and it powers every essay on redistribution.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: autonomous versus induced consumption, both marginal propensities computed from a change in income, the sum-to-one rule, and marginal kept strictly apart from average.

## Subtopic: The Multiplier — Formula and Derivation

Build the tool from the ground up. Inject two thousand rand — a firm buys shelving, or a municipality pays for pipe repairs. The fitter banks the payment as income. With an MPC of 0,75 he spends one thousand five hundred rand, which becomes the next person's income; she spends one thousand one hundred and twenty-five; the next spends roughly eight hundred and forty-four; each round is three-quarters of the round before.

Total income created is the sum of the chain: 2 000 + 1 500 + 1 125 + 844 and onward. Because every term is 0,75 of its predecessor, the series converges, and its sum is the first term divided by (1 − 0,75): that is 2 000 ÷ 0,25 = R8 000. Two thousand rand of injection has produced eight thousand rand of income.

From that, the MULTIPLIER, symbol k:

k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), identical to k = 1 ÷ MPS

and the working equation:

ΔY = k × ΔJ, where ΔJ is the change in the injection.

Verify on our numbers: MPC 0,75 means MPS 0,25, and 1 ÷ 0,25 = 4; four times two thousand is eight thousand. Both forms agree because MPC and MPS always sum to one.

Read the formula as a sentence: the multiplier is big when people re-spend a big share of each extra rand, and small when they squirrel it away. The leakage is the chain-killer — every cent not re-spent ends that branch of the echo.

Describe the diagram too, because a graph can be asked for in words. Total spending sits on the vertical axis and national income on the horizontal, with a forty-five degree line marking every point where spending equals income. The aggregate spending line starts at autonomous spending and climbs with a slope equal to the MPC; equilibrium sits where it crosses the forty-five degree line. Now lift the spending line vertically by the injection ΔJ. Because the spending line is flatter than the forty-five degree line, the crossing point slides much further RIGHT than the line moved UP: the horizontal gain in income exceeds the vertical shift in spending, and their ratio is exactly the multiplier. Steepen the spending line — raise the MPC — and the crossing slides further right still.

Stop for this section's questions now — the chain and its sum, both faces of the formula, ΔY = k × ΔJ, and the forty-five degree diagram narrated in words.

## Subtopic: Working the Multiplier with Rand Figures

The multiplier is tested as calculation, so drill one sequence until it is automatic: formula, substitute, calculate, INTERPRET. The interpretation sentence earns credit all by itself.

Case one, forwards. Government commits an extra eight billion rand to school building, and the MPC is 0,8. Formula: k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0,8) = 1 ÷ 0,2 = 5. Then ΔY = 5 × R8 billion = R40 billion. Interpretation: national income rises by forty billion rand, five times the original outlay, because the initial spending is earned and re-spent round after round.

Case two, backwards — the variant that catches the unprepared. National income rose by ten billion rand after an injection of four billion. The multiplier is 10 ÷ 4 = 2,5, so 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 2,5, which gives 1 − MPC = 0,4 and MPC = 0,6. You have travelled from observed figures back to household behaviour: sixty cents of every extra rand was spent.

Case three, the version that describes South Africa. In an open economy with a government, each rand of income leaks THREE ways, and all three marginal propensities sit in the denominator:

k = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM)

with MPT the marginal propensity to tax and MPM the marginal propensity to import. Let MPS be 0,15, MPT 0,20 and MPM 0,15. The leakages total 0,50, so k = 1 ÷ 0,50 = 2. The same eight billion rand of school building now lifts national income by sixteen billion — not forty.

That gap is the most important sentence in the lesson. An economy that taxes a solid share of each rand and imports much of what it consumes has a SMALL multiplier, because every round surrenders half of itself to the treasury, the bank and the harbour. And so the COMPOSITION of spending matters enormously: rands paid to local builders, teachers and maintenance teams keep circulating at home, while rands spent on imported equipment leave the chain in the very first round.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the multiplier run forwards and backwards, the three-leakage version applied, and the full interpretation sentence written out.

## Subtopic: Leakages, Limits and the Reverse Gear

The multiplier is powerful, and it is conditional. A discussion answer that skips the conditions reads as naive, so state them.

Condition one: SPARE CAPACITY. The multiplier converts spending into real output only where idle workers, empty workshops and unused land exist to answer the extra demand. At full capacity the same injection collides with a fixed supply of goods and multiplies PRICES instead of production. South Africa's vast pool of unemployed labour is the standing argument for demand-side stimulus here — but the binding constraints may not be labour at all: if electricity, rail and ports cannot carry more activity, the multiplier hits their ceiling first.

Condition two: TIME. The rounds are sequential, not simultaneous. Income received this quarter is spent over the next, so the full effect unfolds across a year or more — long enough for the stimulus to arrive after the downswing it was aimed at.

Condition three: FINANCE. An injection funded by heavy state borrowing can push interest rates upward, discouraging the private investment that would otherwise have happened. That crowding out claws back part of the multiplier's gift.

Then the reverse gear, which is forgotten in half of all answers. The multiplier is symmetrical: it pulls DOWN as hard as it pushes up. Let exports fall by four billion rand in an economy whose multiplier is 2, and national income falls by eight billion: the factory drops a shift, the retrenched trim their spending, the shops re-order less, and the contraction compounds round by round. Every recession is a multiplier in reverse — and automatic stabilisers, the progressive tax that lightens as incomes fall and the unemployment insurance that pays out when work stops, exist precisely to weaken that downward echo by holding spending steadier than it would otherwise be.

One last partner: the ACCELERATOR. When consumer demand keeps rising, firms respond by investing in extra capacity, and that investment is itself a fresh injection — which multiplies in turn. Multiplier and accelerator feeding each other is the standard account of why upswings and downswings build their own momentum, and it links this lesson directly to the business cycle.

The final questions of this part are with you now: all three conditions, the downward multiplier computed on a fall in exports, automatic stabilisers as dampers, and the accelerator tied to the cycle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea at street level — because the multiplier is nothing more than the biography of one payment after it lands.

## Subtopic: One Rand, Many Pockets

A Limpopo municipality replaces the burst water pipe along a small town's main street and pays a local crew two thousand rand for the job. Follow the money.

The pipe fitter takes the two thousand home. He sets aside five hundred for his son's college fund and spends one thousand five hundred — chicken and vegetables at the butchery, electricity units, a taxi to town. That fifteen hundred is now the butchery's income. The butcher holds back a quarter and spends one thousand one hundred and twenty-five — flour from the bakery, a repaired apron from the seamstress. The baker holds back a quarter and spends about eight hundred and forty-four. The seamstress spends part of hers with the taxi owner, and the taxi owner buys airtime at the tuckshop.

Count what one payment has done. It has been INCOME to a pipe fitter, a butcher, a baker, a seamstress, a taxi owner and a tuckshop keeper — six pockets filled by one job. Add every round of the chain and the town's total income rises by eight thousand rand: two thousand, then fifteen hundred, then eleven twenty-five, then eight forty-four, shrinking until the amounts vanish into cents.

That is the whole concept. A rand is not finished when it is spent — it becomes somebody's income and sets off again. Economists call the total effect the multiplier; the town's shopkeepers have always known it. Ask anyone trading near a big construction site what opening day does to their takings.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two thousand rand are coming to you right now: the chain of six pockets, the shrinking rounds, and the total the town gained.

## Subtopic: Why the Chain Gets Shorter Each Time

Now the crucial half: WHY does every round shrink, and what sets the speed of the shrinking?

Because at each pocket, a slice of the money steps out of the chain through one of three exits. Some is SAVED — resting in an account instead of crossing a counter. Some is TAXED — VAT at the till, income tax off the payslip. And some buys IMPORTS — an imported phone, imported petrol, imported cooking oil — and that slice leaves South Africa altogether, never to be anyone's income here again.

Widen those exits and the chain dies young. Suppose fifteen cents of each rand is saved, twenty cents is taxed and fifteen cents buys imported goods: fifty cents of every rand exits at every stop. Now the two thousand rand pipe job adds only four thousand to national income, not eight. Same pipe, same crew — but half the money escapes each round instead of a quarter.

Two conclusions follow, and together they explain most budget arguments. First, WHO receives the money matters. A rand landing in a household that has nothing is spent almost entirely, so the chain runs long; the same rand landing in a wealthy household largely turns into savings, so the chain stops early. Second, WHAT the money buys matters. Wages for local fitters, cleaners and teachers flow on through local tills; payment for an imported machine leaves the country in one step, and the domestic chain ends at link one.

And the chain has a reverse gear, which is the painful part. When the town's biggest employer cuts a shift, the lost wage is only the beginning: the butchery loses a customer, the bakery loses an order, the taxi loses a fare. One retrenchment letter, several thinner tills.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three exits, how wider exits shorten the chain, why destination and content change the total, and the chain running in reverse.

## Subtopic: What the Chain Means on Budget Day

Last piece — converting the story into the sentences that earn credit.

When any budget promises new spending, ask three questions and you are reasoning like an economist. Question one: is there SPARE CAPACITY? Idle workers and half-empty workshops mean the new demand gets answered with new production — real output, real jobs. An economy already flat out — or throttled by power cuts and broken rail — cannot produce more, so the extra money simply bids up PRICES. Identical rand, opposite outcome.

Question two: how much LEAKS? Total up the saving slice, the tax slice and the import slice. Small combined leak, long chain, large total. Big leak, and the money is effectively gone within a round or two. This is also why "buy local" is a piece of economics and not just a slogan: the import exit is the one leak a shopper can personally narrow.

Question three: how LONG will it take? Each round of the chain takes weeks or months, so today's announcement reaches the tills across the coming year — and sometimes the emergency is over before the money arrives.

Then raise the honest objection that strong answers never skip: where was the money found? If it was borrowed, it carries interest and must be repaid, and heavy public borrowing can lift interest rates until private firms shelve their own projects — one hand injecting while the other withdraws.

So the closing summary. A rand of spending creates more than a rand of income, because it travels. How much more depends on the width of the leaks, the room to produce, and the way the spending was financed. Put those three tests to any spending announcement and you have written a complete Grade 12 evaluation.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: capacity, leakage and timing applied to a new announcement, plus the financing objection in your own words. Carry this tool forward — the multiplier reappears in business cycles, in fiscal policy and in every growth essay you will meet this year.
