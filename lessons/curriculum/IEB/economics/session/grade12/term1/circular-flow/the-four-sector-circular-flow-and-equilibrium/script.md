# Part 1 — Expert

In Grade 10 the circular flow was a picture with arrows. In Grade 12 it becomes ARITHMETIC — the working model on which all of macroeconomics stands. Today we cover four things: the closed and open economies and the four participants who keep the flow turning; the leakages and injections whose totals must balance; the three places the national accounts cut the flow to measure it, with the conversions between the aggregates; and the skill of reading an economy that has slipped out of equilibrium. Every essay you will write this year on business cycles, fiscal policy or the balance of payments is built on top of this one diagram.

## Subtopic: Closed, Open, and the Four Participants

Definitions first, because the definition earns its own credit before any diagram is drawn.

A CLOSED economy contains no foreign sector — only households and businesses, with government joining in the three-sector version. An OPEN economy adds the FOREIGN SECTOR, and South Africa, which ships out platinum, citrus and vehicles and ships in oil, electronics and machinery, is about as open as economies come. The complete model is therefore the FOUR-SECTOR model: households, businesses, government and the foreign sector.

Give each participant its job description. HOUSEHOLDS own the factors of production — labour, land, capital, entrepreneurship — and rent them out to businesses; in exchange they receive wages, rent, interest and profit, which they partly spend, partly save, and partly surrender as tax. BUSINESSES hire those factor services, produce the country's goods and services, pay out the incomes, and INVEST by buying capital goods. GOVERNMENT collects taxes, spends on goods, services and transfer payments, and supplies public goods. The FOREIGN SECTOR takes our exports and supplies our imports.

These four meet in four MARKETS, and listing them correctly is straightforward credit. The FACTOR market, where labour and the other factors are hired. The PRODUCT market, where finished goods and services change hands. The FINANCIAL market — banks, pension funds, the stock exchange — which gathers savings and passes them on to borrowers. And the FOREIGN EXCHANGE market, where rands are swapped for dollars, euros and yuan so that cross-border deals can settle.

Finally, the two opposing currents that make the diagram close. The REAL flow carries actual things — factor services travelling one way, goods and services travelling back. The MONEY flow carries rands in the opposite direction — incomes one way, expenditure the other. Every real movement has a money movement facing it; mixing the two currents is the classic first-week error, so keep things in one ring and rands in the other.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: closed versus open, each participant's job, the four markets by name, and the real flow held strictly apart from the money flow.

## Subtopic: Leakages, Injections and the Equilibrium Condition

Not every rand a household earns comes straight back to a local till, and not every rand arriving at a till started as local income. The exits from the flow are LEAKAGES, also called withdrawals; the entrances are INJECTIONS.

The three LEAKAGES: SAVING, written S — income parked in the financial market instead of spent; TAXATION, written T — income redirected to government; and IMPORTS, written M — spending that crosses the border to pay foreign producers. The three INJECTIONS: INVESTMENT, written I — business spending on capital goods, drawn out of the financial market; GOVERNMENT SPENDING, written G; and EXPORTS, written X — foreign money buying South African output.

Pair them by the door they use. Saving exits through the financial market and investment re-enters through the same door. Taxation exits to government and government spending returns from it. Imports exit to the foreign sector and exports return from it. Three doors, each with traffic in both directions.

Now the condition itself. The economy is in EQUILIBRIUM when total leakages equal total injections:

S + T + M = I + G + X

Underline the word TOTAL. The individual pairs are under no obligation to match — tax can exceed government spending while investment exceeds saving — and equilibrium still holds so long as the three-against-three totals agree. Questions are regularly built around exactly that misunderstanding, so add before you judge.

Work an example, in rand billions. Saving two hundred and fifty, taxation five hundred and fifty, imports three hundred: leakages of one thousand one hundred. Investment three hundred, government spending five hundred, exports three hundred: injections of one thousand one hundred. The totals agree, so national income holds steady — production, income and expenditure are telling the same story, and no pressure inside the flow is pushing output up or down.

The same statement can be written from the spending side as Y = C + I + G + (X − M), with Y as national income and C as household consumption. Two notations, one fact: what is spent equals what is earned equals what is produced.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three leakages and three injections paired to their doors, the totals rule, and an equilibrium tested from a fresh set of figures.

## Subtopic: Measuring the Flow — Three Methods, Three Aggregates

A river can be measured at any bridge, and the national accounts measure this one at three. The PRODUCTION method adds up the value ADDED by every producer — value added, never turnover, otherwise the flour inside the bread is counted once at the mill and again at the bakery. The INCOME method adds up what the factors of production were paid: compensation of employees, net operating surplus, plus consumption of fixed capital. The EXPENDITURE method adds up final spending: C + I + G + (X − M). Done properly, the three bridges report the same river.

Then the conversions, which are reliable credit if you write the formula before touching the numbers. GDP at MARKET PRICES values output at what buyers actually hand over — VAT and excise included, subsidies netted off. Remove the product taxes and restore the subsidies and you have GDP at FACTOR COST, what producers themselves receive:

GDP at factor cost = GDP at market prices − taxes on products + subsidies on products

With market-price GDP of seven thousand billion rand, product taxes of eight hundred billion and product subsidies of one hundred and twenty billion: seven thousand minus eight hundred plus one hundred and twenty gives six thousand three hundred and twenty billion rand at factor cost.

Next, DOMESTIC versus NATIONAL. GDP counts everything produced INSIDE South Africa's borders, no matter who owns the factory. GNI counts what SOUTH AFRICAN-owned factors earn, wherever on earth they operate: take GDP, add primary income received from the rest of the world, subtract primary income paid out to it. With ninety billion received and two hundred and sixty billion paid: seven thousand plus ninety minus two hundred and sixty equals six thousand eight hundred and thirty billion rand. Because more foreign capital works inside South Africa than South African capital works abroad, our GNI sits persistently BELOW our GDP — a structural fact worth one confident sentence in any essay.

Last, NOMINAL versus REAL. Nominal figures wear current prices and inflate with them; REAL figures are deflated to a base year, and only real growth means more actual goods. Nominal GDP of seven thousand billion against a deflator of one hundred and twenty-five on a base of one hundred: seven thousand times one hundred over one hundred and twenty-five is five thousand six hundred billion rand in real terms. Spread that over roughly sixty-three million people and real GDP per person is about eighty-nine thousand rand — and hold on to the discipline this enforces: if real GDP grows more slowly than the population, output per person is FALLING even while headlines announce growth.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the three methods, both conversions worked formula-first, and real per capita output kept apart from nominal totals.

## Subtopic: Reading a Disequilibrium

Equilibrium is a balancing act, not a resting place, and the skill that earns real credit is narrating what happens when the balance tips.

Let leakages EXCEED injections. Take our figures and let exports slip from three hundred to two hundred and forty: injections fall to one thousand and forty against leakages of one thousand one hundred. More is draining from the flow than entering it. Businesses notice it as shelves that stay full: stock accumulates, so they cut production and shed workers. Incomes fall — and now the leakages themselves start shrinking, because poorer households save less, pay less tax and import less. The contraction continues until the shrunken leakages once again equal the injections. Equilibrium returns, but at a LOWER national income with more people out of work. That is a downswing, told entirely in circular-flow language.

Run the film the other way. Let injections outrun leakages — an infrastructure programme lifts G, or a commodity boom lifts X. Stocks run down, firms expand output and hire, incomes climb, and climbing incomes automatically generate more saving, more tax and more imports until the leakages catch up. Equilibrium returns at a HIGHER national income. But the composition of that gain depends on capacity: an economy with idle machines and unemployed workers converts the extra spending into output and jobs; an economy already straining converts it mainly into INFLATION. Same injection, two endings.

Three finishing touches that mark out a strong answer. First, the flow is self-correcting in DIRECTION but not in OUTCOME: it always finds a new balance, and nothing guarantees the new balance is a good one — an economy can settle quite stably at mass unemployment. Second, this is the doorway through which all policy walks: fiscal policy moves G and T, monetary policy moves S and I through the interest rate, trade and exchange-rate policy move X and M. Every macroeconomic instrument you will ever study is an attempt to widen or narrow one of these six flows. Third, the adjustment is bigger than the shock: a rand of new injection lifts national income by more than a rand, because each recipient spends part of it onward. That amplification is the multiplier, and it gets a lesson of its own.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the contraction and the expansion each traced step by step, the capacity condition that decides output versus prices, and each policy instrument matched to the flow it moves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machine seen from a factory town at month-end — because the circular flow is not an invention of textbook writers; it is the route your family's money actually travels.

## Subtopic: The Money That Goes Around the Township

Month-end at the clothing factory. Salaries land, and by Saturday morning the town is turning. The seamstress's pay came from the factory, and the factory's money came from selling the jeans she sewed. There is the whole model in one loop: families supply WORK, the factory returns MONEY; families return the money as SPENDING, the factory returns GOODS. Two rings rotating in opposite directions — one ring carries things, the other carries rands.

Follow one salary through Saturday. Some crosses the counter at the corner shop — instantly back inside a business. Some rides away as a taxi fare; some becomes a school tracksuit; some becomes electricity units. Each of those rands lands in a business, turns into part of somebody else's pay next month-end, and sets off around the loop again. Money is not consumed when it is spent; it circulates.

Two more characters stand on that Saturday street. GOVERNMENT got there before the salary did — PAYE came off the payslip on Friday night — and it is also visibly present as the clinic sister, the teacher, the pothole team, and the grant that lands in a grandfather's account. And the FOREIGN SECTOR is on the street too: the fabric on the factory's cutting tables arrived from abroad in a container, and half the jeans leave for shops in Europe. Rands leave the town for the world, and the world's money arrives in the town.

Count the cast: four players — families, businesses, government, and the world outside — meeting in four places: where work is hired, where goods are sold, where money is banked and lent, and where rands are exchanged for other currencies.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the month-end salary are coming to you right now: the two rings turning opposite ways, the four players, and the four meeting places.

## Subtopic: Taps and Drains

Now picture the town's big water tank on its stand, taps feeding it from above and drains running out below, with the WATER LEVEL settling wherever inflow and outflow balance. The level is national income — the size of the economy.

Three DRAINS carry money out of the loop. Part of the salary is SAVED — it rests in the bank instead of reaching a till. Part is TAXED — the payslip already arrived lighter than the salary. And part buys IMPORTED goods — that money sails away in a container. Save, tax, import: the three exits.

Three TAPS feed money in. A business BORROWS from the bank and instals new sewing lines — the town's savings returning as INVESTMENT. GOVERNMENT SPENDS — the clinic extension, the teachers' salaries, the resurfaced road. And foreign shops pay for the town's jeans — EXPORT earnings flowing in. Investment, government spending, exports: the three entrances.

The single rule: the level holds when the taps TOGETHER match the drains TOGETHER. Not tap-for-drain — TOGETHER. Saving may run ahead of investment and the level still holds, provided government spending and exports cover the gap. Check it with the numbers: drains of two hundred and fifty saved, five hundred and fifty taxed, three hundred imported — one thousand one hundred out. Taps of three hundred invested, five hundred from government, three hundred exported — one thousand one hundred in. Level steady.

And the water can be measured three ways with one answer: measure what was MADE, measure what was EARNED, or measure what was SPENT. One tank, three dipsticks — which is why the national accounts publish production, income and expenditure figures and expect all three to agree.

Your questions for this part are up now: three drains, three taps, the together-not-pairs rule, and the balance verified with the figures.

## Subtopic: When the Bucket Won't Sit Still

Last piece — the one that turns news headlines into ordinary sentences.

Suppose a tap chokes: Europe's shops cut their jeans orders, and export money drops from three hundred to two hundred and forty. Now one thousand one hundred is draining out while only one thousand and forty flows in. The level falls. On the ground that reads: finished jeans stack up in the storeroom, so the factory drops a shift; a dropped shift is a thinner payslip; a thinner payslip buys less at the corner shop, so the shop trims its own orders — around and around, smaller with each turn.

Here is the insight most people never get told. The level DOES settle again — lower. As incomes shrink, the town automatically saves less, pays less tax and imports less, so the drains narrow until they match the weakened taps. Everything balances beautifully — with a shift's worth of workers at home. Balanced is not the same as well.

Reverse it and the machine runs forward: government launches a building programme, or a fruit-export boom fills the tap. Orders climb, shifts are added, payslips fatten, spending spreads, and the level rises until the widening drains catch up. One caution rides along: if the factory was already running every machine around the clock, the extra money cannot summon extra jeans — it only bids up PRICES. Same tap, different ending, decided by spare capacity.

And that is every economic policy argument you will ever hear, translated. An interest-rate cut? Someone reaching for the investment tap. A budget that spends more or taxes less? Government adjusting its own tap and drain. A weaker rand that makes our exports cheap abroad? The export tap opening. Once you see taps and drains, any headline can be placed on the diagram in a single move — and placing it is exactly the skill your assessments reward.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the falling level traced turn by turn, why a settled tank can still be a poor outcome, the capacity warning, and three headlines matched to their taps or drains.
