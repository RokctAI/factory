# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow seated government at the table as one of four participants; this session opens that participant's books. The PUBLIC SECTOR handles roughly one rand out of every three that moves through the South African economy, and the topic asks four questions about it: what it consists of and why a market economy cannot do without it; why delivering public services is genuinely difficult; how the national budget and fiscal policy operate — including the one diagram this topic owns, the Laffer curve; and what happens when the state itself is the thing that fails. It is also the chapter where the country's live debates — struggling state enterprises, the deficit, the grant system — become material you can be tested on.

## Subtopic: Composition and Necessity of the Public Sector

Composition first: three tiers, plus an outer ring. NATIONAL government — parliament and the national departments of education, health, police and defence — sets policy for the whole country. PROVINCIAL government — nine provinces — delivers the bulk of schooling and hospital care. LOCAL government — the municipalities — runs water, electricity distribution, refuse removal and municipal streets. Around the three tiers stand the STATE-OWNED ENTERPRISES: Eskom generating power, Transnet moving freight and running the ports, the SABC broadcasting, the Post Office — public bodies that produce goods and services directly.

Now the necessity question: why must any of this exist in a market economy? Because markets left to themselves fail in predictable ways, and each failure calls forth a public-sector role — learn them as matched pairs. Markets cannot deliver PUBLIC GOODS: goods that are NON-RIVALROUS, meaning one person's use subtracts nothing from anyone else's, and NON-EXCLUDABLE, meaning non-payers cannot be kept out. A safe, patrolled street or national defence protects everybody or nobody, so no private seller can bill for it — the FREE-RIDER problem — and government must fund it from taxes. Markets misprice EXTERNALITIES: costs and benefits that land on third parties. So government taxes the harmful spillovers and subsidises the beneficial ones — MERIT goods like basic education and immunisation, which markets under-supply, against DEMERIT goods like alcohol and tobacco, which they over-supply. Markets tolerate MONOPOLY, so the state polices competition. And markets pay according to productivity and property, never need, so the state REDISTRIBUTES: progressive taxation on one side, social grants on the other, reaching a large share of South African households every month. Add the stabilisation role — the fiscal policy you met in business cycles — and the state's economic job description is complete.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three tiers with their functions, the state enterprises named, public goods defined by their two properties, the free-rider standoff, and each market failure paired with its public-sector answer.

## Subtopic: Problems of Provisioning and the Objectives of the Public Sector

Public provisioning is genuinely HARD, and the reasons come as five recurring problems.

One: ACCOUNTABILITY. The wire connecting payment to service is cut. Taxpayers fund services they may never use; officials spend money that was never theirs; and where a shop is disciplined by its customers every single day, a government faces its voters once in five years. The substitutes — an auditor-general, parliamentary committees, the public protest — are weaker disciplines arriving later. Two: EFFICIENCY. With no competitor and no profit test, waste carries no automatic penalty; indeed, the department that exhausts its budget is frequently granted a bigger one. Three: ASSESSING NEEDS. A market reads wants straight off prices; the state must estimate need through censuses, surveys and political noise — and the ward that shouts loudest is not always the ward that needs most. Four: PRICING. What should the user pay for a clinic visit, a kilolitre of water, a university year? Set the price at zero and demand balloons while funding starves; set it at full cost and the poor are shut out. South Africa's characteristic answer is the stepped compromise — a free basic allocation of water and electricity, then blocks that grow dearer as use climbs. Five: the PARASTATAL problem. State enterprises live in both worlds at once, commanded to trade commercially while carrying social mandates no business would volunteer for; the strain is visible in Eskom's debt and the years of load-shedding, and in freight stuck for want of working rail. Here lives the PRIVATISATION debate: selling state assets promises efficiency and fiscal relief but risks private monopoly pricing and abandoned social mandates; NATIONALISATION promises control and social reach but concentrates every provisioning problem just listed.

Against the problems stand the OBJECTIVES — the five-line scoreboard the public sector plays for: ECONOMIC GROWTH, real GDP rising year on year; FULL EMPLOYMENT, work for all who seek it; PRICE STABILITY, inflation held inside the three to six percent target; EXCHANGE-RATE STABILITY, a rand predictable enough to plan around; and ECONOMIC EQUITY, a narrowing gap between rich and poor. Commit all five to memory — listing and explaining them is a standing discussion question.

Stop for this section's questions now — the five provisioning problems each with its mechanism, the parastatal strain with its living example, privatisation argued both ways, and the five objectives.

## Subtopic: The Budget, Fiscal Policy and the Laffer Curve

The public sector's master document is the NATIONAL BUDGET, presented by the Minister of Finance every February: a full plan of REVENUE — where the money will come from — and EXPENDITURE — where it will go.

The revenue side. PERSONAL INCOME TAX is the largest single source, levied progressively so that higher brackets pay higher rates. VAT at fifteen percent is broad and dependable, but REGRESSIVE in effect — because poorer households spend a larger share of their income, a flat rate on spending claims a larger share of a poor household's income — which is why staples such as maize meal, brown bread and rice are zero-rated. COMPANY TAX falls on profits; EXCISE duties ride on fuel, alcohol and tobacco; CUSTOMS duties on imports. Keep the classification clean: DIRECT taxes attach to income — personal and company tax; INDIRECT taxes attach to spending — VAT and excise. The expenditure side: the heaviest allocations go to social development and grants, basic education, health, debt-service costs and policing — and DEBT-SERVICE COSTS are the fastest-growing line, elbowing the others aside.

Three possible balances. BALANCED: revenue equals spending. SURPLUS: revenue exceeds spending. DEFICIT: spending exceeds revenue — South Africa's usual condition — financed by issuing government bonds that pile up as PUBLIC DEBT. A deficit during a downswing is expansionary fiscal policy on duty; a deficit that survives every phase of the cycle is a STRUCTURAL deficit, and it is the reason interest payments now compete with classrooms for the same rand.

Then the topic's own diagram, the LAFFER CURVE, in words. Horizontal axis: the tax rate, running from zero to one hundred percent. Vertical axis: tax revenue. At zero percent, revenue is zero — nothing is charged. At one hundred percent, revenue is zero again — nobody will work or declare income that is confiscated whole. Between the ends, the curve rises to a single crest and descends: revenue grows with the rate up to some optimum, then shrinks as harsher rates smother effort, chase activity into the informal economy and make avoidance and evasion pay. The examinable reading: a rate increase raises revenue only on the RISING slope; beyond the crest, a rate CUT can raise revenue. And the caveat that earns the evaluation mark: no one can observe where the crest sits, so the curve is a warning against overreach, not a recipe.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the revenue sources sorted direct against indirect, the major spending lines, the three balances and deficit financing, and the Laffer curve narrated and read correctly on both slopes.

## Subtopic: Public Sector Failure

Markets fail — that is the case for a public sector. But states fail too, and you need the concept, its causes and its effects.

PUBLIC SECTOR FAILURE is when government intervention, designed to improve on the market, ends up allocating resources LESS efficiently than the market would have — the remedy underperforming the illness. The causes grow out of the provisioning problems, sharpened by practice. MANAGEMENT failure: posts filled without the skills the work demands — the town's engineering post handed out as patronage, the treatment works nobody left can operate. APATHY: no competition, no consequence, so the queue advances at the speed of indifference. BUREAUCRACY: procedure stacked on procedure until compliance replaces purpose — the permit that takes a year, the tender that outlives the emergency it was meant to answer. POLITICS OVERRIDING ECONOMICS: projects placed where the votes are rather than where the returns are; spending timed to elections. CORRUPTION: public money rerouted into private hands — procurement fraud, and the era South Africans call state capture. And UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES of even honest policy: the price ceiling that empties the shelves, the subsidy that props up forever the firms it meant to launch.

The EFFECTS complete the picture. Resources are misallocated — spent where they produce least. Services collapse on precisely the people who depend on them, because the wealthy exit into private schools, private clinics and private security while the poor cannot. Taxpayer morality decays — why pay for what never arrives? — shrinking the base, which is the Laffer logic sneaking in through the back door. Investment stays away, since no firm builds where power, rail and licences cannot be relied on. And each failure spends some of the state's credibility, making its next intervention harder to trust.

End on the balance, because balanced judgement is what the long essays pay for: market failure justifies the public sector; public sector failure disciplines it. The real question is never state versus market in the abstract, but which failure is the worse one in the case before you — and how each is corrected: competition and regulation for the market's flaws; transparency, skills, accountability and consequence for the state's.

The final questions of this part are with you now: public sector failure defined, causes with concrete mechanisms, effects traced to those who carry them, and the balanced conclusion in your own words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic on one street in one town — because the public sector is not a theory; it is the water in the tap, the patrol at midnight, the grant that lands on the second of the month.

## Subtopic: What the Street Cannot Buy for Itself

Walk one street on a weekday morning and sort what you see into two piles: things a person bought, and things no single person COULD buy.

The vetkoek at the corner, the data bundle, the haircut — private goods. Pay and it is yours; refuse to pay and the seller keeps it. The market runs these perfectly, because non-payers can be turned away.

Now the night patrol the street has been wanting. Once a patrol walks the block, the block is safer for EVERYONE on it — the nurse coming off shift, the shopkeeper cashing up, the child asleep by the window. No house can be left out of the safety, and one family's share of it takes nothing from the next. So ask the awkward question: who volunteers to pay the patrollers? Every household runs the same private calculation — if the neighbours pay, I am protected free; if they refuse, my money is wasted alone. So everyone waits for everyone, and the patrol never starts. That standoff is the FREE-RIDER problem, and it is why safety, streetlights, storm-water drains and the road itself are bought by all of us together, through tax, or not at all.

Between the two piles sits a middle shelf. The clinic and the school COULD sell their services — private versions do. But one child's immunisation shields the whole street from an outbreak, and a street that finishes school earns more, steals less and reasons better at election time. The benefit overflows the buyer, so a street left to shop for itself buys too little schooling and too few injections. Government supplies them anyway and calls them merit goods.

And the grant on the second of the month is no purchase at all — it is the market's pay-slip arithmetic corrected, because the market pays what your labour fetches, never what your household needs.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the street are coming to you right now: the two piles separated, the patrol's two properties, the free-rider standoff retold in your own words, and why the clinic sits on the middle shelf.

## Subtopic: Why the Tap Runs Slow — the Trouble with Providing

If the state must provide these things, why does it so often provide them badly? Not because officials are wickeder than bakers — because the disciplines that keep a business sharp are missing from the job.

The chicken shop on the corner faces a daily election: dry chicken today, empty till tomorrow. The housing office faces no such ballot — its clients cannot take their custom elsewhere, its budget arrives whether the queue moved or not, and the next real reckoning is years away. Nobody chooses to be slow; the PENALTY for slowness simply is not wired in.

The chicken shop also knows precisely what to fry, because yesterday's sales say so. The housing department must guess: how many houses does this district need, how many nurses, how many megalitres? No price whispers the answer; censuses, surveys and councillors' promises must — and the loudest ward is not the neediest ward.

Then the pricing puzzle, printed on every municipal statement. Hand out electricity free and the geysers run all day while the substation rots unfunded. Charge full cost and a pensioner cannot boil a kettle. So the stepped answer: a free basic allocation of units each month, then blocks that cost more as usage climbs — necessity free, luxury expensive. Every stepped tariff you will ever see is this puzzle, solved as fairly as anyone knows how.

And the hybrids, the parastatals. Eskom is ordered to behave like a business — sell power, cover costs — while carrying duties no business would accept, like stringing lines to villages that can never repay them. Serve the villages and the balance sheet haemorrhages; serve the balance sheet and the villages stay dark. When that tension finally snapped, the whole country took turns sitting in the dark — which is why load-shedding belongs in this chapter and not only on the news.

Your questions for this part are up now: the missing daily election, guessing need without prices, the stepped-tariff compromise explained, and the parastatal tension in one sentence.

## Subtopic: February's Envelope and the Tax That Bites Back

Last piece — the budget, shrunk to something you can hold.

Every February the Minister of Finance stands up holding one enormous envelope: what will come in, and where it must go. Coming in: income tax off the country's payslips — the biggest stream — VAT riding on every till slip, tax on company profits, and the quiet extras buried in petrol, beer and cigarettes. Going out: grants, schools, clinics, police — and one line you should watch like a hawk: INTEREST on old borrowing, among the fastest-growing items in the whole envelope. When outgo beats income — most years — the shortfall is the DEFICIT, and it is borrowed. Borrowing is not a sin: a loan that builds a port can repay itself. But borrowing every single year merely to cover salaries and interest means this year's classroom is being paid for by a future taxpayer — which is to say, by you.

Now the famous curve, told as a story about the barber at the taxi rank. Tax his takings at zero percent: the state collects nothing. Tax them at one hundred percent: he locks the shop, or cuts hair quietly at home for cash — the state again collects nothing. Somewhere between the two ends lies the rate that gathers the most; push beyond it and every extra percentage point actually gathers LESS, as people work fewer hours, hide more sales, leave, or melt into the informal economy. See the shape: revenue climbing as the rate rises, cresting, then sliding down. That is the Laffer curve, and its single honest lesson is that a higher rate is not automatically higher revenue. Where the crest sits, nobody can say — write that sentence too; it is the evaluation mark.

And when the envelope is spent badly — the inflated tender, the half-built hall, the post given to a friend — return to the topic's closing balance: markets fail, which is why the state exists; states fail, which is why the state must be watched. The answer that holds both failures, and names the discipline for each, is the answer that earns distinctions.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the envelope's ins and outs, the deficit and who ultimately repays it, the barber's story told across the crest, and the closing balance in your own words.
