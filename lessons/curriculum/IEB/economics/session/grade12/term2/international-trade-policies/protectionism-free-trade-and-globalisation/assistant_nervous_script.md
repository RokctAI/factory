Hey, it's Mandy — trade policy essays frighten people because both sides sound convincing, and you sit there wondering which side you're supposed to pick. Here's the release valve: you're not supposed to pick either. The marks live in the weighing, and the weighing has a shape you can learn tonight.

Start with the instruments as one-liners, mechanism attached. Tariff: border tax, import price up, state collects. Quota: quantity capped — and the licences become gold, which is why quotas are worse. Subsidy: home costs down, protection hidden in the budget. Exchange control: no currency, no imports. Standards and red tape: the polite walls. Then the two special words — dumping, selling below cost to kill rivals, the one complaint everyone respects; and infant industry, young and promising, needing shelter with a clock on it.

For the essay, work in pairs: every argument for protection walks with its rebuttal. Infants need shelter — but infants never admit growing up. Jobs are saved — visible ones, while dearer inputs quietly cost invisible ones. Self-sufficiency buys resilience — at a permanent price. Then the free-trade list: specialisation, cheaper and wider choice, disciplined monopolies, scale, travelling ideas. Land on the mix: mostly open, plus rare, temporary, conditional shelter. That sentence is the conclusion the question wants.

The two strategies are two directions: import substitution looks inward behind walls — safe, small, ceilinged; export promotion looks outward — no ceiling, but the ports, rail and power must work. South Africa tried the first, chose the second, and is throttled by the scooter, not the treaty.

And the ladder climbs in fours: free trade area — AfCFTA; customs union — SACU; common market; economic union — the EU. Four rungs, four examples, in order, every time.

Instruments, pairs, mix, roads, ladder. Five small lists, one big essay. Go run the practice questions now — you'll be surprised how much of this you already hold.
