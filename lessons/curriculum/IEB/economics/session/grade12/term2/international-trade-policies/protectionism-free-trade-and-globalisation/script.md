# Part 1 — Expert

Term one settled WHY nations trade — comparative advantage — and how the payments balance. Now comes the political question hiding behind the economics: how OPEN should a country's borders be? Every government is pulled between two poles: FREE TRADE, where goods cross borders untaxed and unobstructed, and PROTECTIONISM, where home producers shelter behind barriers against foreign rivals. This lesson covers the instruments of protection, the arguments on both sides, the two grand strategies developing countries have used — with South Africa's own journey through both — and then the machinery of globalisation: the world trade rules, and the ladder of economic integration that Africa is currently climbing. Neither pole wins outright, and in an essay the marks always live in the weighing.

## Subtopic: The Instruments of Protection

Protection works through a toolkit, and each tool must be known by its mechanism. TARIFFS — customs duties on imports: a tax collected at the border that lifts the import's shelf price, letting home products compete more comfortably, with state revenue as a side payment. Import QUOTAS — limits on quantity: only so many units or tons may enter each year. Quotas do something tariffs do not: they hand import licences to chosen firms, and the licence itself becomes a private prize — one reason economists trust quotas even less than tariffs. SUBSIDIES to domestic producers — grants or tax relief that lower home costs so local firms can undercut imports with no visible border tax; the protection is real but buried in the budget. EXCHANGE CONTROL — rationing access to foreign currency, so would-be importers simply cannot pay foreign suppliers. Administrative and physical barriers — outright bans on certain imports, health and technical STANDARDS enforced with special enthusiasm against foreign goods, and deliberately slow paperwork at the harbour. Finally EXPORT incentives — the mirror tool, helping home firms sell abroad.

Two terms complete the kit. DUMPING: exporting at below cost, or below the home-market price, often to bankrupt competitors in the target country before lifting prices — predatory pricing that crosses a border. Anti-dumping duties are the internationally accepted answer, and South Africa uses them — imported chicken portions and certain steel products being running examples. INFANT INDUSTRY: a young domestic industry that cannot yet match the costs of established foreign giants, but plausibly could once it reaches scale — the textbook candidate for temporary shelter.

And one structural truth to anchor all later evaluation: every instrument above raises prices or shrinks choice for CONSUMERS at home. Protection is never free; it transfers money from consumers — each paying slightly more — to protected producers and their workers, each gaining a great deal. That asymmetry, losses spread invisibly thin while gains concentrate loudly, is exactly why protection is politically attractive and economically expensive at the same time.

## Subtopic: The Case for Protection Against the Case for Free Trade

The arguments FOR protection come as a set, and the essay-winning habit is to state each one fairly, then attach its standard rebuttal. INFANT INDUSTRY: young industries need shelter until scale drops their costs; rebuttal — infants seldom admit to having grown up, and shelter without an expiry date breeds permanent inefficiency. EMPLOYMENT: barriers save home jobs from import competition; rebuttal — they save the visible jobs inside the protected industry while quietly destroying others, since costlier inputs burden every downstream producer, and foreign retaliation kills export jobs. ANTI-DUMPING and unfair competition: shelter against predatory pricing, and against imports made under standards home producers may not legally breach; this is protection's strongest card — even committed free traders accept anti-dumping duties. DIVERSIFICATION and strategic self-sufficiency: a country dangerously dependent on a few exports, or on foreigners for food, medicine or defence supplies, buys resilience with protection — an argument that regained force worldwide when pandemic disruptions snapped supply chains. BALANCE OF PAYMENTS and revenue: tariffs restrain imports and fund the state — though sharper instruments usually exist for both purposes.

The case FOR free trade is comparative advantage converted into policy: specialisation lifts total world output; consumers win lower prices and wider choice; import competition disciplines home monopolies and forces efficiency; the world market opens scale that a domestic market of sixty-odd million people cannot — and ideas, technology and standards travel in the containers. The case AGAINST free trade mirrors protection's case: exposed industries shed workers faster than new industries can hire them; the gains land unevenly; and a developing country frozen into exporting raw materials may never build the industries where tomorrow's advantage will live.

What mix is DESIRABLE? Answer honestly: no country on earth practises either pole in pure form. The defensible position is broad openness — for the efficiency, the discipline and the scale — combined with targeted, temporary, conditional protection where a genuine infant, a proven dumping case, or a real strategic need exists, always with a written expiry so the shelter lapses. Protection as a scalpel, never as a blanket.

## Subtopic: Export Promotion, Import Substitution, and South Africa's Path

Developing countries have industrialised behind two grand strategies, and South Africa has lived through both.

IMPORT SUBSTITUTION — the inward road: replace imports with home production behind tariff walls. Identify what the country buys abroad, wall off that market, and let domestic factories fill the space. The attractions are real: industry starts, jobs appear, imports shrink, and the home market is guaranteed. The record, though, teaches caution. Industries raised behind walls tend to stay SMALL — the domestic market is their ceiling — and INEFFICIENT, because the wall removes the pressure to match world costs; scarce foreign currency drains away on machinery imports for the protected plants; and consumers finance the whole arrangement through higher prices. South Africa under apartheid ran precisely this model, with sanctions thickening the walls — and built industries that gasped the moment the walls came down.

EXPORT PROMOTION — the outward road: help firms SELL to the world instead of hiding from it. The tools: export incentives and finance, infrastructure that cuts exporters' costs, skills programmes, trade missions, and industrial funding tilted toward outward-facing sectors. The attractions: the world market has no ceiling, so economies of scale open fully; competing against the world forces efficiency instead of excusing its absence; and export earnings strengthen the balance of payments. The East Asian economies industrialised exactly this way. The costs and conditions: export subsidies collide with world trade rules; success stands or falls on infrastructure that actually functions — ports, rail, power; and an export-led economy rides every swing of world demand.

Democratic South Africa turned outward: tariffs were cut steeply through the 1990s, trade agreements pursued, and industrial policy now runs through sector master plans — the automotive programme the flagship, keeping global manufacturers assembling and exporting vehicles from the Eastern Cape and Gauteng; clothing, poultry and steel remain the contested files where tariff protection is still argued and still applied. The balanced judgement: South Africa officially backs export-led growth, keeps selective protection, and finds its export performance throttled less by trade policy than by the logistics and electricity constraints you met earlier in the course.

## Subtopic: Globalisation, the WTO and the Ladder of Integration

GLOBALISATION is the accelerating knitting-together of national economies — goods, services, capital, technology, information and people crossing borders at speeds and volumes never seen before. Its engines: containerisation and cheap transport, telecommunications and the internet, liberalised finance, and the deliberate dismantling of trade barriers through successive international agreements. The institutional line runs from the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade — GATT, born 1947 — through eight rounds of negotiated tariff cutting to the Uruguay Round, whose conclusion created the WORLD TRADE ORGANISATION in 1995.

The WTO, with more than 160 member states including South Africa, keeps the rulebook: NON-DISCRIMINATION — a trade benefit granted to one partner extends to all, the most-favoured-nation principle; NATIONAL TREATMENT — once inside the border, imported goods are treated like local ones; BOUND tariffs that members may not raise at will; and a DISPUTE SETTLEMENT system where members argue before panels instead of retaliating in the dark. The cumulative result is TRADE LIBERALISATION: world tariffs at a small fraction of their late-1940s levels and world trade multiplied many times over. Learn the critiques alongside: wealthy countries kept shielding agriculture — the very sector where developing countries hold advantage; the rules now restrict the industrial policies that today's rich countries themselves used while climbing; and the gains have divided unevenly between countries and within them.

Finally, the LADDER OF ECONOMIC INTEGRATION, climbed rung by rung. A FREE TRADE AREA: members drop tariffs among themselves but keep their own external tariffs — the African Continental Free Trade Area, AfCFTA, now phasing in across the continent, is the world's largest by membership. A CUSTOMS UNION adds a COMMON external tariff — the Southern African Customs Union, SACU, joining South Africa, Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini, is the world's oldest, sharing customs revenue by formula. A COMMON MARKET adds free movement of labour and capital. An ECONOMIC UNION adds harmonised policies, possibly a shared currency — the European Union with its euro is the standing case. South Africa stands on several rungs at once — SACU member, free-trade participant in the Southern African Development Community, AfCFTA signatory — and the AfCFTA is the continent's great wager: that a single market of over a billion people can finally let African industries outgrow their small national ceilings.

The final questions of this part are with you now: globalisation and its engines, GATT to the WTO with the core rules, the liberalisation critiques, and the four rungs with their African examples.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole debate moved to the taxi rank — because protection against free trade is not an abstract quarrel between economists; it is lunch, and the question of who is allowed to sell it.

## Subtopic: The Chip Stall and the Hawkers at the Rank

Inside the rank building, one chip stall has fed commuters for years, paying rent to the rank committee. Then hawkers appear along the outside fence — vetkoek, boerie rolls, fruit — cheaper, and truth be told, often fresher. Commuters stream to the fence. The stall's takings halve.

The stall owner pleads with the rank committee — and listen carefully, because her requests are the entire toolkit of protection in miniature. Charge every hawker thirty rand a day to trade at the fence: a TARIFF, lifting the outsiders' costs so the insider breathes easier. License only four hawkers, names on a list: a QUOTA — and watch the four chosen hawkers become the rule's loudest defenders, because the licence itself is now worth money. Give the stall free use of the rank's cold room: a SUBSIDY, invisible to commuters but perfectly real. Demand health certificates from the fence but not the stall: a STANDARD, sincere or convenient, take your pick. And the stall's darkest suspicion — that one hawker sells boerie rolls below cost on purpose, planning to raise prices once the stall dies: DUMPING, the one accusation that even the hawkers' friends treat seriously.

Now stand where the committee stands and count both sides of the ledger. Protect the stall, and its two workers keep their jobs, the committee keeps its rent — gains that are visible, concentrated and grateful. But two thousand commuters pay roughly two rand more for lunch every working day — a loss so thinly spread that no one marches about it, yet multiply it out and it dwarfs what the stall gains. That is protection's arithmetic everywhere on earth: the rescued producer appears on the news; the two thousand slightly lighter pockets never do.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the rank are coming to you right now: each request matched to its instrument, why the licensed hawkers switch sides, the dumping accusation, and the visible-gain, thin-spread-loss arithmetic.

## Subtopic: Raising the Young Stall Without Spoiling It

Now give the stall its strongest argument — and discover what it takes for that argument to stay honest.

Suppose the chip stall is NEW: opened last month, still learning the fryers, buying potatoes in small expensive bags while the fence hawkers buy through big wholesalers. Give it one sheltered season, says the committee chair, and its costs will drop to meet anyone's. That is the infant-industry argument, and it deserves respect: nearly every industrial country in history sheltered its young industries once — nobody learns to swim mid-shipwreck.

But watch what shelter does to effort. With the fence taxed, the stall's chips sell whether they improve or not. Why chase better oil, sharper prices, faster service? The season ends; the costs have not dropped; and the stall that called itself an infant at one month old still calls itself an infant at five years. The ladder meant for climbing has become a hammock — and everyone lying in it lobbies to keep it strung. That is the true historical record of walls kept too long: industries that stay small, expensive and loud.

So the honest version carries three rules, and they translate straight into exam sentences. The shelter must carry an EXPIRY DATE — written, published, believed. It must come with PRESSURE — cost and quality targets, or the privileges lapse early. And it must cover only genuine infants with a real prospect of growing up — never old stalls pleading youth. Shelter with a clock and a test: that is the desirable mix compressed into one line — trade mostly open; protection rare, temporary, conditional.

And the two grand strategies from the Expert half are simply the stall's two possible futures. Import substitution: seal the fence and sell to this rank's two thousand commuters forever — safe, small, ceiling fixed at the rank roll. Export promotion: make the chips so good the stall supplies OTHER ranks across the city — no ceiling at all, but it must survive open competition to get there, and the delivery scooter had better start every morning. South Africa, note well, keeps choosing the second future while its scooter — the ports, the rail lines, the power supply — keeps failing to start.

## Subtopic: One Big Rank

Last scene — widen the fence until it vanishes.

Imagine the taxi ranks of the whole metro signing an agreement: any vendor may trade at any rank, no fees between them. Overnight, the brilliant boerie-roll hawker at one rank sells at five ranks — she buys bigger, her costs fall, everyone's lunch improves. The mediocre stalls feel the squeeze: some sharpen up, some close, and their owners standing at empty counters are the agreement's real cost, deserving real help. That metro agreement is a free trade area — and Africa has just signed one across an entire continent, the AfCFTA, wagering that vendors locked inside small national ranks can never grow, while a stall serving a billion customers can.

Climb the ladder rung by rung. The metro's ranks also charge OUTSIDE vendors one identical entry fee: a customs union — southern Africa has operated one for more than a century, splitting the takings by formula. Let the vendors themselves relocate to any rank, bringing their savings and their skills: a common market. One metro trading card, one set of rules for all: an economic union — Europe went that far, single currency included.

And who referees when ranks fall out — when one rank accuses another's vendors of dumping below cost, or sneaks in a hidden entry fee? An association office where every rank committee holds a seat, with a written rulebook and a complaints desk: the world trade body, in miniature. Its rules in rank language: any privilege you give one rank, you give every rank — no favourites; once a vendor is inside your gate, treat her like your own; entry fees you promised to freeze stay frozen; and quarrels go to the desk, never to fistfights at the fence. Imperfect, certainly — the richest ranks still quietly shield their own vegetable gardens while preaching open gates, and the small ranks notice — but the desk beats the fistfight, and the metro's lunches have honestly never been better or cheaper.

That is globalisation seen from your own rank: bigger markets, better and cheaper lunches, real losers at the empty counters who deserve real help, and a rulebook forever arguing with itself. An answer holding all four of those in one paragraph is a distinction answer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the metro agreement named, the four rungs climbed in order with their African examples, the referee's four rules, and the four-part honest summary. Carry the rank with you into growth and development — the north-south divide waiting there is this same fence, drawn across a world map.
