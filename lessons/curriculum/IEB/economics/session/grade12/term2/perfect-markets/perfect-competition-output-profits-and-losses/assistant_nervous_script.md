Hey, it's Mandy — I know the perfect-competition diagram looks like five curves having an argument, and plenty of strong students freeze the first time they face it. Here's the secret: nobody draws it all at once. You build it in four layers, one line at a time, and each layer is easy on its own.

Layer one: the flat line. Price taker means one horizontal line at the market price — and you label it four ways: D, AR, MR, P. One line, four labels, and that labelling alone usually earns something.

Layer two: the tick and the U. Marginal cost dips and then climbs like a tick mark; average cost is a wide U. There is exactly one rule about how they meet: MC cuts AC at AC's lowest point. Check that crossing before anything else in your sketch — it is the first thing a marker's eye goes to.

Layer three: the decision. Find where the rising MC crosses the flat line — that crossing answers "how much should the firm produce" in every question, always: MR equals MC. Drop a dashed line to the quantity axis and the decision is made.

Layer four: the verdict. At that quantity, look up at AC. Price above AC? Economic profit — shade the rectangle. Price touching AC's minimum? Normal profit — and say the sentence that earns its own mark: normal profit is already inside the cost curves, because the owner's wage and return on capital are costs. Price below AC? A loss — but before writing "shut down", test the shallower U, average variable cost. Covering AVC: stay open and chip away at the rent. Below AVC: close.

Long run, one sentence covers it all: profit invites entry, loss forces exit, and price comes to rest on the bottom of AC — normal profit, both efficiencies.

Flat line, tick and U, crossing, verdict. Four layers, in order, never all at once. Now go try the practice questions — they're waiting for you, and you're readier than you feel.
