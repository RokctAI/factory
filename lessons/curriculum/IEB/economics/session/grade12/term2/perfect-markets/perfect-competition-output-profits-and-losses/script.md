# Part 1 — Expert

Micro-economics begins with its measuring instrument: the PERFECT MARKET, the model every real market gets held against for the rest of the year. Grade eleven gave you the parts — demand, supply, cost curves, revenue curves. This lesson bolts them together into one working machine: the assumptions perfect competition demands, the relationship between an industry and one business inside it, the diagram that decides output, profit and loss, and the South African institutions that push real markets back toward the model when they drift. Every idea today has a picture attached, and you should leave able to draw each picture from its sentence and say each sentence from its picture.

## Subtopic: Perfect Competition — Assumptions, and the Industry Against the Business

Five conditions define perfect competition, and each one deserves its own line in your answer. A VERY LARGE NUMBER of buyers and sellers, each so small that no single one can budge the price. A HOMOGENEOUS product — unit for unit identical, so no buyer has any reason to favour one seller. FREE ENTRY AND EXIT — nothing legal, financial or technical blocks a newcomer or traps anyone inside. PERFECT KNOWLEDGE — every participant knows every price on offer. And MOBILE factors of production, with no collusion among sellers and no government hand on the price. Nowhere on earth meets all five at once; a fresh-produce market and the share market come nearest, which is why textbooks reach for them.

Everything else in this topic flows from one consequence of those assumptions: the INDUSTRY decides the price, and the individual BUSINESS simply lives with it. Keep two diagrams side by side in your mind. On the left, the industry: market demand sloping down, market supply sloping up, meeting at an equilibrium price — say twelve rand a unit. On the right, one small business inside that industry: at twelve rand it can sell every unit it can produce, but ask twelve rand and five cents and it sells nothing, because perfectly informed buyers walk straight to an identical rival. The demand curve FACING THE BUSINESS is therefore a horizontal line at the market price. The business is a PRICE TAKER, and the only variable left under its control is QUANTITY.

Now name that horizontal line properly, because it wears three labels at once and the identity carries the whole topic. It is the firm's demand curve. It is the AVERAGE REVENUE curve, because if every unit fetches twelve rand, revenue per unit is twelve rand. And it is the MARGINAL REVENUE curve, because each additional unit brings in exactly twelve rand more. In perfect competition, D equals AR equals MR equals P — one flat line, four names.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the five assumptions, the price-taker conclusion, the two side-by-side diagrams, and the flat line with its full set of names.

## Subtopic: Costs, Revenue and the Profit-Maximising Output

Next, slide the cost curves in underneath that flat line — and hear the shapes precisely, because you must reproduce them from words. MARGINAL COST, MC, is the cost of one extra unit: it falls briefly at low output while specialisation still helps, then turns and climbs steeply as diminishing returns take hold — a tick shape, down then sharply up. AVERAGE COST, AC, is total cost divided by output: a broad U — expensive per unit at tiny output because fixed costs sit on few units, cheapest at some middle output, expensive again when the operation strains. And one geometric fact worth a mark on its own: MC always crosses AC exactly at AC's minimum. While the next unit costs less than the average, it drags the average down; the instant it costs more, it pushes the average up — so the crossing can only happen at the bottom of the U.

The output decision follows one rule, and the same rule will govern every market structure you meet this term: produce where MARGINAL REVENUE EQUALS MARGINAL COST, with MC rising. The reasoning is pure arithmetic. Any unit that adds more revenue than cost grows the profit — make it. The first unit that adds more cost than revenue shrinks the profit — refuse it. Stop exactly where the two are equal. Since MR here is the flat price line, the working rule becomes: produce where rising MC cuts the price line. If the price is twenty rand and the one-hundred-and-fiftieth unit is the one whose marginal cost reaches twenty rand, then one hundred and fifty units is the answer.

Whether that output earns a profit is a separate question, answered by AVERAGE cost at that quantity — and the vocabulary must be exact. NORMAL PROFIT is the minimum reward that keeps the owner in this line of business: the opportunity cost of the capital and the entrepreneurship, and it is already BUILT INTO the cost curves. When price equals average cost at the chosen output, the business earns normal profit only — an accountant sees a healthy firm; an economist says it breaks even. ECONOMIC PROFIT, also called supernormal profit, appears when price exceeds average cost at the chosen output — the owner earns more than the next-best alternative would pay. On the diagram it is a rectangle between the price line and the AC curve: width the output, height the surplus per unit. Price twenty, average cost seventeen, output one hundred and fifty: three rand times one hundred and fifty — four hundred and fifty rand of economic profit.

Stop for this section's questions now — the tick and the U described in words, the crossing at AC's minimum, the MR-equals-MC rule with its reasoning, and the profit calculation from given figures.

## Subtopic: Losses, the Shut-Down Decision, and the Long Run

Turn the picture upside down: when price lies BELOW average cost at the best available output, the business makes an ECONOMIC LOSS, and the rectangle now hangs between the AC curve above and the price line below. Here comes the question that separates strong answers from weak ones: must a loss-making business close its doors at once? No — and the reasoning is where the marks live.

Divide costs into FIXED — rent, insurance, the loan repayment — owed in full even at zero output, and VARIABLE — raw materials, casual labour, the electricity that runs the machines — which stop when production stops. In the SHORT RUN the fixed costs are unavoidable: lock the doors and the rent still arrives. So the real test uses the AVERAGE VARIABLE COST curve, a shallower U tucked beneath AC. If price covers average variable cost, every unit sold pays for its own materials and labour and leaves something over toward the rent — producing shrinks the loss. If price drops below even average variable cost, each unit sold makes the hole deeper: SHUT DOWN. The price equal to minimum AVC is the SHUT-DOWN POINT, and this is precisely why the firm's SUPPLY curve is its rising MC curve above minimum AVC — below that point it supplies nothing.

Now release the clock and let the LONG RUN arrive, because this is where the model earns its status as the benchmark. Suppose the industry's firms are enjoying economic profit. Entry is free and knowledge is perfect, so newcomers pour in; industry supply shifts right; the market price sinks; every firm's flat revenue line slides downward — and the sliding only stops when the line just touches the bottom of the AC curve, where economic profit has vanished. Run the film in reverse for losses: firms exit, supply shifts left, price rises to the same resting place. LONG-RUN EQUILIBRIUM: price equals marginal cost equals minimum average cost. Every firm produces at the cheapest possible cost per unit and earns normal profit only, and the price consumers pay equals the true resource cost of the final unit. That is ALLOCATIVE efficiency — P equals MC, resources steered exactly by consumers' wishes — and PRODUCTIVE efficiency — output at minimum average cost — delivered simultaneously. No other market structure manages both, which is exactly why this one is the yardstick.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the loss rectangle, fixed against variable cost, the AVC shut-down test, the supply curve as MC above minimum AVC, and the entry-exit story that ends in both efficiencies.

## Subtopic: Competition Policy — Pushing Real Markets Toward the Benchmark

South Africa's real markets fall short of the model, and the state runs permanent machinery to close the gap. The legal frame is the COMPETITION ACT of 1998, and its three institutions must be named with the verb that belongs to each.

The COMPETITION COMMISSION investigates: it screens proposed mergers, hunts cartels, and conducts inquiries into entire markets — data prices and grocery retail have both been examined this way. The COMPETITION TRIBUNAL adjudicates: it hears the Commission's cases, can block a merger or attach conditions to it, and can fine a guilty firm up to ten percent of its annual turnover. The COMPETITION APPEAL COURT hears appeals against the Tribunal's rulings.

What conduct does the Act target? HORIZONTAL restrictive practices — rivals conspiring instead of competing: price fixing, dividing markets, rigging bids. South African case history makes this concrete: bakeries caught fixing the price of bread, and construction firms that rigged bids on the big stadium contracts and paid penalties running to hundreds of millions of rand. VERTICAL restrictive practices — abuses down the supply chain, such as forcing retailers to observe a minimum resale price. ABUSE OF DOMINANCE — a giant pricing below cost to bury a small entrant, or refusing rivals access to a facility they cannot do without. And MERGER CONTROL — stopping or conditioning combinations that would substantially lessen competition, with a distinctly South African addition: mergers are also tested against the PUBLIC INTEREST — jobs, the prospects of small businesses, and the spread of ownership.

Why does the state spend all this effort? Answer from the model. A cartel converts a crowd of price takers into a single price MAKER: output shrinks, price floats above marginal cost, and both efficiencies leak away — monopoly outcomes without a monopoly. Competition law is the attempt to rebuild, by statute, the discipline that free entry and countless rivals impose automatically inside the model. And evaluate like a top student: enforcement moves slowly, a fine can quietly reappear in consumer prices, and competition goals sometimes tug against industrial policy — yet the deterrent is real, and the price of your groceries is lower because the machinery exists.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the Act and its three institutions with their verbs, the targeted practices with South African cases, the public-interest test in mergers, and the model-based justification for the whole system.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the identical machine, rebuilt at ground level — one seller in a long row of sellers — because perfect competition is simply what a market looks like when nobody, anywhere, controls the price.

## Subtopic: Sixty Crates of Butternuts

Saturday morning, the street market in Pietermaritzburg. Sixty crates of butternuts down one long row, and every butternut looks like every other butternut. A grower arrives with her bakkie and joins the row.

She chalks up fifteen rand a kilo while the whole row is asking twelve. Shoppers pause, glance, and drift one crate along to identical butternuts at twelve. She sells nothing. So she swings the other way — nine rand — and her crates empty before ten o'clock. Victory? No. The row would have bought everything she had at twelve; she has simply gifted three rand on every kilo she owned. By the time she packs the bakkie she understands the row's single law: twelve is the price because ALL the butternuts and ALL the shoppers together made it twelve, and no one crate can move it. One decision remains hers, and only one: how many kilos to load next Saturday.

That is what a price taker is, and the flat line from the theory is nothing but her Saturday drawn as a graph. Whatever quantity she chooses, every kilo brings twelve rand — so twelve is her price, her revenue per kilo, and the revenue from the next kilo. One number answering to three names.

And notice what holds the row honest: sixty sellers, identical produce, shoppers who can see the whole row at a glance, and nothing stopping crate sixty-one from arriving next week. Take away any one of these — brand her butternuts, blindfold the shoppers, close the market gate — and she suddenly has pricing power. The assumptions are not decoration; each one is a fence that keeps the price honest.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the market row are coming to you right now: why fifteen fails and nine wastes money, the single decision she keeps, the flat line's three names, and the fences that keep the price honest.

## Subtopic: How Many Kilos? — the One Rule

So how many kilos should she load? Think the way an economist thinks: one kilo at a time.

Every kilo sells for twelve rand. Every kilo also costs something to bring — and the cost of the NEXT kilo keeps changing. The first kilos are cheap: her own field, her own labour. Beyond that she buys from a neighbouring farm at rising prices, pays for extra fuel, pays her nephew to help at the stall — each additional kilo costs more than the one before. Somewhere along the line, the cost of one more kilo touches exactly twelve rand.

That is where she stops. Every kilo before that point earned more than it cost — money in. Every kilo past it would cost more than the twelve rand it fetches — money out. The rule in market language: keep loading kilos while the next one earns more than it costs; stop the moment the next one would cost more than it earns. That whole idea is MR equals MC. No algebra, no mystery — just refusing to sell anything at a loss, decided kilo by kilo.

Then Saturday's verdict. Add up EVERYTHING the operation costs her — and include the wage she gives up by not taking the weekend shift at the packhouse, because a Saturday at the market is a Saturday's pay forgone. If the all-in cost works out at eleven rand a kilo and she sells at twelve: one rand a kilo of genuine surplus — economic profit, better than her best alternative. If all-in cost is exactly twelve: no surplus, but she earns precisely what her time and money would earn anywhere else — normal profit, and staying at the market is still rational. If all-in cost is thirteen: every kilo subsidises the shoppers, and the packhouse shift is calling her name.

Your questions for this part are up now: the kilo-by-kilo rule in your own words, why her forgone wage counts as a cost, and the three verdicts — surplus, break-even, and the packhouse.

## Subtopic: The Street Corrects Itself

One last movement — what the row does NEXT, because a market like this never sits still.

The news travels: butternut sellers are clearing a rand a kilo above all their costs. Nothing guards the row — that is the free-entry fence working in reverse — so next month there are seventy crates, then eighty. More butternuts chase the same shoppers, and the price eases from twelve toward eleven. The surplus thins out crate by crate until selling butternuts pays exactly what the same sweat pays anywhere else — and the newcomers stop coming. Profit was the advertisement; entry was the reply; the story ends when the surplus is gone.

Now run the film backwards for a bad season: costs above price, sellers bleeding. Some give up the stall for the packhouse — free EXIT — fewer crates arrive, the price climbs, and the survivors return to break-even. The row breathes in and out around one resting point: price equal to the lowest possible cost per kilo, every seller earning a fair return and nothing extra.

Step back and admire what the row achieved without a manager: butternuts grown as cheaply as they can be grown, sold at what the final kilo genuinely cost society, by exactly as many sellers as shoppers justify. That is why economists hang this model on the wall like a spirit level — not because real markets match it, but to measure precisely how far each one tilts.

And the closing twist: suppose the sixty sellers gather after packing up and agree — from next week, nobody sells under sixteen. The row becomes one giant seller; shoppers pay more for fewer butternuts; every honest fence is down. In South Africa that agreement is against the law: a Commission exists to investigate it, a Tribunal can fine it up to ten percent of turnover, and the case files already include the bread on your breakfast table. When sixty crates start speaking with one voice, the law breaks up the conversation.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: profit as the advertisement and entry as the reply, the bad season in reverse, the resting point in a single sentence, and the after-market agreement with its legal consequences. Hold this benchmark tight — next lesson the fences come down one by one, and monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition are all measured against what you built today.
