# Part 1 — Expert

The perfect market achieved both efficiencies; the imperfect structures eroded them. This session finishes the argument with a harder claim: even a market crowded with sellers and open to entry can still allocate resources WRONGLY, systematically. MARKET FAILURE is the situation where the price mechanism, left entirely to itself, delivers the wrong QUANTITIES — too much of some goods, too little of others, and none whatsoever of a few — judged against what society genuinely values. This topic is the intellectual foundation beneath the whole public-sector side of economics, and it hands you two workhorse tools: the split between private and social costs and benefits, and cost-benefit analysis. Both appear constantly in essays and in data questions.

## Subtopic: The Concept and Causes of Market Failure

Start with a definition sharp enough to quote: market failure exists when markets fail to allocate resources EFFICIENTLY — the quantity produced and consumed differs from the socially optimal quantity, so a different allocation could raise society's welfare. Notice what the definition never says: it does not say prices are too high, nor that firms are wicked. It says the QUANTITIES are wrong.

The causes come as a list of six, each with its own mechanism — learn the mechanism, not just the name. One: EXTERNALITIES — costs or benefits that spill onto third parties who never agreed to the transaction; because the price ignores them, the quantity comes out wrong. The full machinery follows in the next section. Two: MISSING MARKETS — public goods. Recall their two defining properties: non-rivalry and non-excludability. Nobody can charge users for a streetlight, so no firm ever supplies one — the market simply never comes into being, failure at its purest. Merit goods are underprovided for a gentler reason: people undervalue education, immunisation and insurance, or cannot afford them; demerit goods — alcohol, tobacco, gambling — get over-consumed. Three: IMPERFECT COMPETITION — last lesson delivered this cause complete: a monopolist holds price above marginal cost and output below the social optimum, and the gap is the misallocation. Four: IMPERFECT INFORMATION — buyers ignorant of a product's dangers, workers ignorant of a job's risks, lenders knowing less than borrowers; wrong information produces wrong quantities. Five: IMMOBILITY of factors of production — resources that cannot reach their best use: retrenched mineworkers whose skills fit no other industry, capital sunk in the wrong region; structural unemployment is South Africa's standing illustration. Six: INEQUALITY in the distribution of income — a market weighs demand in rand, not in need, so the spending power of the wealthy pulls resources toward luxuries while urgent needs backed by no money go unanswered. A market can pass every efficiency test and still leave children hungry; efficiency and equity are separate examinations.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the definition built on wrong quantities, and all six causes, each tied to its mechanism.

## Subtopic: Externalities — Private Against Social Costs and Benefits

Externalities carry this topic's diagrams, so nail the vocabulary first. PRIVATE costs belong to the producer: wages, materials, power. EXTERNAL costs land on bystanders: the dust, the fumes, the contaminated stream. SOCIAL COST is the sum — private plus external. The benefit side mirrors it: private benefit to the buyer, external benefit to third parties, SOCIAL BENEFIT the total. Markets equate demand with supply — curves built from PRIVATE benefit and PRIVATE cost only. Society's ideal output sits where SOCIAL benefit equals SOCIAL cost. Whenever externals exist, those two output levels part company: that gap is the failure.

Take the negative production externality first, narrated for drawing. A brick kiln operates outside a Highveld town, its smoke drifting over the houses. Quantity runs along the bottom, price up the side. Demand slopes down. The supply curve — the kiln's marginal PRIVATE cost — slopes up. But every thousand bricks also burdens the neighbours: washing dulled by soot, asthma pumps and clinic queues, crops dusted grey. Stack that external cost on top of the private cost and a second supply curve appears — marginal SOCIAL cost — running parallel ABOVE the first, the vertical distance between them equal to the external cost per thousand bricks. The market settles where demand meets the PRIVATE cost curve: a big output at a low price. Society's best point is where demand meets the SOCIAL cost curve: fewer bricks at a higher price. The market OVER-produces the smoky good and under-prices it — the town pays part of the kiln's bill with its lungs and its laundry.

Now the positive consumption externality, the mirror image. Training and education: the student who completes a trade qualification gains a lifetime of higher earnings — private benefit — but the gains do not stop with her: employers find skills, her township gains a business, the tax base widens, her children start further ahead. The market demand curve counts only her private benefit; the SOCIAL benefit curve runs parallel ABOVE it. The market settles where private demand meets supply; society prefers the larger quantity where social benefit meets supply. Left alone, the market UNDER-produces education, training, immunisation, research — every good whose benefits leak generously onto others.

One sentence rules the section: goods carrying external costs get over-produced, goods carrying external benefits get under-produced, and in both cases the market price fails to tell the truth about what the good really costs or really gives.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four cost-and-benefit terms, both diagrams narrated with their two crossing points, and the over- and under-production conclusions.

## Subtopic: Consequences and the State's Toolkit

Consequences first, stated by name. Resources misallocate: too many bricks, too little training — society's welfare sits below its possibility frontier. The environment absorbs whatever carries no invoice — the free sky fills with soot, the free river with waste. Public and merit goods arrive scarce or not at all: the dark street, the unbuilt clinic. Inequality deepens, because those least able to move or complain usually live closest to the smoke. And micro-failures compound into macro-costs: structural unemployment from immobile factors, disease burdens from demerit goods, growth forfeited when human capital goes unbuilt.

Now the state's toolkit, instrument by instrument, each aimed at a named failure. TAXES on external costs: charge the producer, per unit, an amount equal to the external cost — the polluter now carries the social cost, the private cost curve lifts onto the social cost curve, and the market itself selects the smaller, correct quantity. South Africa's carbon tax, the fuel levy, the excise on alcohol and tobacco, the levy on sugary drinks — all are this one instrument wearing different uniforms. SUBSIDIES on external benefits: pay colleges, clinics and researchers the value of the benefits they scatter beyond their customers — no-fee schools, free primary healthcare, subsidised student funding. DIRECT PROVISION where the market never forms: the state supplies public goods — defence, street lighting, courts — out of tax revenue. REGULATION where prices are too blunt an instrument: emission limits, banned substances, zoning, licensing, compulsory information — the ingredients label answers imperfect information head-on. PRICE INTERVENTIONS: maximum prices to keep essentials within reach — accepting the textbook side effect of shortages when set below equilibrium; minimum prices, the minimum wage the leading case, to protect suppliers of labour — accepting the risk of surpluses, meaning unemployment, when set above equilibrium. And REDISTRIBUTION — progressive taxation funding grants — aimed squarely at the inequality failure.

Every tool carries its own cost, and stating that cost is the evaluation mark: a corrective tax set at the wrong level corrects nothing; subsidies must be financed by someone; direct provision inherits every public-sector weakness you have studied; controls distort quantities; and each intervention risks replacing a market failure with a government failure. The state repairs the market using imperfect instruments held by imperfect hands — so each intervention must argue its own case, not borrow a blanket one.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the consequences named, each instrument matched to its target failure with a South African example, and the cost attached to each.

## Subtopic: Cost-Benefit Analysis

When government weighs the new dam, highway or power plant, no market can hand it the answer — too many of the costs and benefits are external, unpriced, or arriving decades from now. The instrument built for the job is COST-BENEFIT ANALYSIS, CBA: a disciplined comparison of ALL social costs against ALL social benefits of a project, private and external together.

Five steps, in order. One: identify every cost — construction and operation, but equally the households displaced, the farmland taken, the wetland drained. Two: identify every benefit — the output produced, floods prevented, hours of travel saved, accidents avoided, lives extended. Three: attach money values, even to the unpriced — and this is the craft: travel time valued at the going wage, a wetland at the cost of the treatment plant doing the same purification, risk to life through measured willingness to pay. Four: DISCOUNT the future — a rand of benefit twenty years away is worth less than a rand today, so distant flows are shrunk by a discount rate before the adding starts. Five: compare — proceed when social benefits exceed social costs, and when budgets are tight, rank rival projects by their benefit-cost ratios.

Why is CBA the right instrument for public decisions? Because it writes into the ledger exactly what markets leave out: the third parties, the unpriced goods, the spillovers. A private toll company asks what the road earns at the boom; CBA asks what the road does to everyone — the bypassed villages and the avoided accidents included.

Now the limits, delivered like an evaluator. Valuing the unpriceable is contested ground — what is a wetland worth, or a life, and who decides? The discount rate is a moral judgement dressed as arithmetic — set it high and future generations fall silent, which is why climate-related projects turn entirely on that dial. Project champions systematically overstate benefits and understate costs — optimism bias, with more than one South African mega-project as the cautionary tale. And CBA totals rands without asking WHOSE rands — a project can clear the test while loading its whole cost onto the poorest; distribution must be judged beside the total. Practised honestly, CBA disciplines politics with arithmetic; practised cynically, it dresses politics in arithmetic. A top essay carries both sentences.

The final questions of this part are with you now: why public projects need CBA, the five steps in sequence, discounting explained, and the four limits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic from one dusty corner of one township — because market failure is not first a theory; it is grit on the washing line, an inhaler on a child's bedside table, and a bill that never arrives at the right address.

## Subtopic: The Bill Nobody Sends

A coal depot operates on the edge of a township; trucks rumble in and out from before dawn. The depot sells coal at ninety rand a bag, and at ninety rand it sells mountains of it — the market humming, apparently.

Now stand one street downwind. Coal dust settles on everything. Families wash clothes twice — more soap, more water, more time. Windowsills get repainted a year early. Children with tight chests need inhalers, clinic visits, taxi fare to the clinic, days off school. Tally it honestly and every bag of coal costs the neighbouring streets perhaps seven rand in coping. But no invoice for that seven rand ever reaches the depot or the coal buyer. The customer across town pays ninety and believes that is what a bag of coal costs. It is not. A bag COSTS ninety-seven — ninety paid at the depot gate, seven paid in soap, paint, inhalers and taxi fare by people who never bought coal at all. That missing seven rand is an external cost, and the ninety-rand price is a lie of omission.

Watch what the lie does next: because coal LOOKS seven rand cheaper than it truly is, buyers buy MORE of it than they would at the honest price. The market over-produces precisely the thing that coats the township — not out of cruelty, but out of a bill that was never sent.

Now flip the coin. Two blocks away, a retired boxer runs a gym in a converted container: sixty rand a month. Members gain fitness — they pay, so the market counts that. But the whole neighbourhood gains too: teenagers busy training are teenagers off the corners; evenings grow calmer; small shops stay open later because the street feels safer. Those gains land on households that never paid sixty rand. Priced honestly, a month of that gym is worth far more than sixty rand — so at sixty, too few teenagers join and the container stands half-empty. The neighbourhood under-buys exactly the thing that protects it most.

One unsent bill makes too much coal dust; one unsent thank-you makes too few boxers. That is market failure, complete.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the depot and the gym are coming to you right now: the honest cost of a bag of coal, why the lie raises sales, the gym as the mirror case, and the one-sentence pattern.

## Subtopic: Fixing the Price Until It Tells the Truth

If the disease is a lying price, the cure is to make the price tell the truth — and nearly every government instrument you have ever heard of is one version of that cure.

Deliver the missing bill: place a seven-rand levy on every bag the depot sells, sized to the streets' real coping costs. Now the depot's own arithmetic contains the dust. Coal sells at ninety-seven; buyers buy less; fewer trucks, less grit — and the levy money can buy the street sweepers and the clinic stock. That is all a carbon tax is; that is what the tobacco excise is: the hospital bill collected in advance, at the till. Admire the design — government never orders the depot to do anything; it corrects one number and lets the market do the rest.

Deliver the missing thank-you: subsidise the gym down to thirty rand a month, because the neighbourhood gains more than the member does. The container fills. That is every no-fee school and every free clinic: society paying for the slice of the benefit that society keeps.

Some failures need more than a corrected number. The streetlight cannot bill anybody — so the state simply buys it for everyone: direct provision. And sometimes the price is left alone and the CONDUCT is ruled outright: loads must be covered, no hauling past the school at drop-off hour, full stop — regulation, for harm too serious to merely tax. Poison has no correct price.

But carry the earlier warning in your pocket: the hand that sets the levy can set it wrong, spend it badly, or steer the street-sweeping tender to a friend. Repairing a lying price with a lying process rescues nobody. The honest summary: markets fail, and governments fail at repairing them — sound policy is choosing the smaller failure, case by case.

Your questions for this part are up now: the levy as a delivered bill, the subsidy as a delivered thank-you, when provision and outright rules beat both, and the smaller-failure sentence.

## Subtopic: Should the Toll Road Be Built?

Final scene. The province proposes a toll highway past the township: freight to the port in half the time, jobs during construction — and a strip of houses expropriated, with the old shopping street bypassed. The community hall fills with shouting. How is such a thing DECIDED? Not by market prices — half of what matters here carries no price at all. The adult method is to count everything, both sides, honestly. Watch it work.

Costs: the tar, the bridges, the machines — but also the forty households bought out and moved, the farm cut in two, the hawkers on the old route whose passing trade dies, the noise that never stops. Benefits: the toll revenue, yes — but also thousands of hours not spent in traffic, fuel unburnt, crashes that never happen on the safer road, the factory that locates here because the port is suddenly near. Money values go onto all of it, even the awkward entries: an hour saved is worth roughly the wage it could have earned; a life spared is counted through what society demonstrably pays to reduce risk.

Then the step everyone forgets: the costs land NOW, while the benefits dribble in over thirty years — and a promised rand in thirty years is not worth a rand today. So future benefits are shrunk before counting: discounted. Turn that shrink-dial carelessly and you can make any highway look golden or worthless — which is why the dial itself deserves argument.

Count, shrink, compare: if the all-in benefits beat the all-in costs, build; if not, don't — and when three projects chase one budget, rank them by benefit per rand. That is cost-benefit analysis: every unsent bill and every unsent thank-you, finally gathered onto one page.

Close with the caution that finishes the essay. The counting can be gamed — the road's champions will round benefits up and costs down, every time. And the page adds rands without asking whose: the highway can pass the total test while the forty moved households and the bypassed hawkers carry the whole cost — so ask about the losers, and about compensating them, before anything is signed. Done honestly, this page is the closest economics comes to fairness on a big decision. Done dishonestly, it is a lie wearing a spreadsheet. Your task — in the exam and long after it — is to tell the two apart.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: why the highway cannot be judged by market prices, the counting on both sides, the shrinking of future rands, ranking by benefit per rand, and the two-line caution. This topic hinges the whole public-sector story — everything from state spending to environmental sustainability turns on what you built today.
