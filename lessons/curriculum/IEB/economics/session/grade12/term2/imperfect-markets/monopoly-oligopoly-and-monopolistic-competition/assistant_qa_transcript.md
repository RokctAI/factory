**Mandy:** Question time before we pack up! First one from the class: the lesson said the monopolist can charge whatever it likes — but then said it can't. Which is it? Where does a monopolist's power actually stop?

**Tutor:** The power stops at the demand curve, and grasping that boundary is the whole monopoly question. A monopolist owns the SUPPLY side of its market completely — no rival, no substitute, no entry. It owns nothing on the DEMAND side. Buyers still hold three quiet weapons: they can buy less, they can find a rougher substitute, or they can go without. Push the ferry fare high enough and people drive around the lagoon, share lifts, or stop crossing — the deck empties. So the monopolist never chooses price AND quantity; it chooses one point on a curve that offers only trade-offs: high price with few buyers, or low price with many. That is what price maker really means — the freedom to pick the point, not to abolish the curve. And its choice is disciplined further by its own arithmetic: cutting price to win extra buyers discounts every existing buyer too, which is why marginal revenue sags below price, and why the chosen point sits where MR meets MC rather than at the highest price imaginable. In an evaluation paragraph, add the elasticity insight: a monopolist facing very inelastic demand — insulin, a water utility — holds terrifying power, which is why those are precisely the markets where regulators cap prices; a monopolist facing elastic demand — one cinema in a town full of other entertainment — holds far less than the word monopoly suggests. Power over supply, never over demand: that sentence keeps every monopoly answer honest.

**Mandy:** Next: the kinked demand curve says oligopoly prices barely move. But petrol prices change every single month. Doesn't that break the whole model?

**Tutor:** Beautiful catch — and the answer teaches you how to handle every model in this subject. First, separate the two kinds of price movement. Fuel prices move monthly because a regulated formula passes on the world oil price and the exchange rate — that is COST passing through under regulation, not firms choosing strategy. The kink is a claim about STRATEGIC pricing: whether a firm chooses, on its own, to move away from the ruling price to grab advantage. Watch the markets where firms genuinely set prices — banking fees, mobile contract tariffs, cement, bread before the cartel case — and you see exactly what the kink predicts: long spells of stillness, rivalry pouring into advertising and loyalty programmes instead, and when a change does come, everyone moving together within days. Second, know the model's honest limits, because stating them earns evaluation marks: the kink explains why a ruling price STAYS still, but not how that price was found in the first place; and where firms follow a price leader, prices can move quite smoothly — that is tacit coordination, a different oligopoly story. So the mature exam sentence runs: the kinked demand curve captures one real oligopoly behaviour — the fear of moving alone — and its prediction of sticky strategic prices and vigorous non-price competition matches much of what we observe; where it seems to fail, check first whether the price move is cost pass-through, regulation, or leadership rather than lone strategy. A model is a lens, not a law — hold it up, note what it sharpens, admit what it blurs.

**Mandy:** Here's one that started an argument in class: my cousin's tiny braai stand near the taxi rank charges more than the shop next door and still keeps its customers. Is a braai stand seriously a kind of monopoly?

**Tutor:** A monopoly the size of a teaspoon — and that is precisely the idea monopolistic competition was built to capture. Ask what your cousin's stand owns that the shop next door cannot copy tonight: her marinade, her exact corner as the taxis unload, the fact that regulars trust her and she knows their orders. Over THAT bundle — her particular version of lunch — she is the only supplier on earth. So she holds real pricing power: her regulars will pay a few rand extra rather than switch. But now measure how short the teaspoon is. Let her push the premium from a few rand to fifteen and watch the regulars discover that the street holds a dozen near-substitutes; her demand curve slopes, but gently — loyalty stretches, then snaps. And here is the part that completes the structure: her power is fenced in TIME as well as price. The corner spot, the marinade, the friendliness — all imitable. If her margins fatten visibly, a second braai stand appears fifty metres along within months, because entry is free — no licence wall, no massive capital. Each imitator shaves her regulars until she is back to earning what her effort would earn elsewhere: normal profit at the tangency. So the full answer for the exam: yes, every differentiated small business is a miniature monopoly over its own variant — that is the monopolistic half — but its pricing power is small, because substitutes crowd close, and temporary, because entry is open — that is the competition half. Two words, one structure, and your cousin's stand demonstrates both.

**Mandy:** Last question: if monopoly is supposedly so harmful, why does the state actually run some monopolies itself instead of breaking them all up?

**Tutor:** Because the case against monopoly has one principled exception, and the exam rewards students who can state the rule and the exception in the same paragraph. The standard charge sheet first: a monopolist restricts output below the competitive level, prices above marginal cost, keeps profit that entry cannot erode, and — freed from rivals — may let costs bloat and innovation doze. That justifies competition policy nearly everywhere. Now the exception: NATURAL monopoly. Some industries are almost all fixed cost — the transmission grid, the water network, the rail track, the pipeline. Building the network devours billions; carrying one extra user along it costs almost nothing; so average cost keeps falling across the whole size of the market. One network serving everyone is then genuinely the cheapest way the service can exist. Force two competing grids down every street and you double the country's poles and pipes to split the same customers — competition would RAISE costs, not cut them. Break-up destroys the very scale that makes the service affordable. So the intelligent response flips: keep the single network, but chain its pricing — state ownership, or private ownership under a regulator who caps tariffs and polices service quality. That is why the electricity grid and water mains sit under public or regulated control worldwide. Then close with the modern refinement that earns the extra mark: technology keeps redrawing the boundary of what is natural. Electricity GENERATION now supports many competing producers — only the WIRES remain the natural monopoly — which is why reformers split the industry: competition wherever it works, regulation over the bottleneck that remains. Monopoly is the disease in most markets and the correct prescription in a few — and knowing which market you are standing in is the actual skill.
