# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson built the benchmark; this lesson dismantles it, assumption by assumption, and studies what takes its place. Strip out every rival and MONOPOLY appears. Leave just a handful and you have OLIGOPOLY. Bring back many sellers but let each one make its product slightly its own and MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION emerges. Between them, these three imperfect structures describe most of the economy you actually live in, and the method for studying them is always comparison: every feature gets measured against the perfect market. So keep last lesson's machinery idling underneath everything today — MR equals MC, the cost curves, normal against economic profit — because all of it returns with new settings.

## Subtopic: Monopoly — One Seller, and the Price Maker's Diagram

A MONOPOLY is a market with ONE seller of a product that has NO close substitutes, guarded by BLOCKED entry. The barriers deserve as much attention as the definition, so name them: legal barriers — patents, licences, exclusive franchises; control of an essential resource — the classic diamond-mine example from South Africa's own history; NATURAL monopoly, where a single network genuinely costs less than two — one electricity transmission grid, one water pipe down the street; and economies of scale so deep that no newcomer could ever match the incumbent's costs.

Because the monopolist IS the whole industry, the downward-sloping market demand curve belongs to it alone — and that single fact generates the entire analysis. The monopolist is a PRICE MAKER: it may pick any point on the demand curve — a high price with few buyers or a low price with many — but it can never escape the curve itself.

Now the technical heart of the topic, told so you can draw it. When the demand curve slopes downward, MARGINAL REVENUE lies BELOW average revenue. The reason: to sell an extra unit, the monopolist must cut the price — and the cut applies to EVERY unit, not just the last one. Sell twenty units at fifty rand each, or twenty-one at forty-nine: the twenty-first unit carries a forty-nine rand price tag, but it costs one rand off each of the other twenty, so marginal revenue is forty-nine minus twenty — twenty-nine rand, far beneath the sticker price. On paper: a downward demand curve labelled D equals AR; beneath it an MR curve starting at the same point on the price axis and falling twice as steeply; then the familiar tick-shaped MC and U-shaped AC.

Equilibrium obeys the universal rule — produce where MR equals MC — but with one trap laid for the careless: the PRICE is read off the DEMAND curve, vertically above that quantity, never off the MR curve. Then compare price with AC at that quantity for the profit verdict, exactly as before. Against perfect competition, three differences stand out: price sits ABOVE marginal cost; output is SMALLER than a competitive industry would supply; and because the entry door is bolted, economic profit can SURVIVE into the long run — no entry, no erosion. That surviving profit and shrunken output form the standing welfare charge against monopoly: buyers pay more for less, and the gap between price and marginal cost measures the resources sent to the wrong addresses.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the definition and its barriers, the arithmetic that pushes MR below AR, where output and price are read on the diagram, and the three contrasts with the perfect market.

## Subtopic: Oligopoly — the Few, the Kink, and the Cartel Temptation

An OLIGOPOLY is a market ruled by a FEW large, mutually aware sellers — think of the big banks, the fuel wholesalers, the mobile networks; two firms alone make the special case of a DUOPOLY. The product may be uniform, like cement, or differentiated, like smartphones. Entry is hard: scale, brand weight, licences.

The defining trait is INTERDEPENDENCE: each firm's best move depends on what its rivals will do about it, so oligopolists play strategy — the only structure where firms study each other more closely than they study the market. Theory freezes the result into the KINKED demand curve; hear it for drawing. The firm's demand curve bends at the ruling price. ABOVE the kink it runs flat-ish — elastic — because a firm that RAISES its price raises alone; rivals hold still and its customers pour away. BELOW the kink it turns steep — inelastic — because a firm that CUTS its price gets matched within days; volumes barely shift while everyone's revenue sags. Raise and you bleed alone; cut and you gain nothing. Hence the signature prediction: oligopoly prices are STICKY. The kink even snaps the MR curve, leaving a vertical gap below the ruling output, so moderate cost changes move nothing at all.

If cutting prices is collective self-harm, rivalry escapes down other channels: NON-PRICE competition — brand-building and advertising, loyalty schemes, product features, service quality, sponsorship of teams and festivals. That is why network operators flood your feed with campaigns while their tariffs barely twitch.

And there is a darker escape from interdependence: stop competing altogether. COLLUSION. Overt collusion — a CARTEL — has the few agree on prices or carve up territories, acting jointly as a monopolist and sharing the spoils; between countries an oil producers' club does it openly, but inside South Africa the bread and construction cases ended before the Commission and the Tribunal. TACIT collusion is quieter — price leadership, where the dominant firm moves first and the rest follow without a word exchanged. Cartels also carry a built-in flaw worth stating in any essay: every member gains by secretly shaving price below the pact, so cartels tend to eat themselves. While they last, though, consumers face monopoly outcomes wearing a competitive disguise — which is why collusion stands first on the Competition Act's target list.

Stop for this section's questions now — the features of oligopoly, the two arms of the kink side by side, sticky prices and the non-price war, and collusion in both forms with the flaw inside it.

## Subtopic: Monopolistic Competition — Many Sellers, Each Slightly Special

Now the hybrid filling every main road: MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION — MANY sellers, FREE entry and exit, but every seller offering a DIFFERENTIATED product: takeaways, salons, guesthouses, clothing labels. Differentiation — genuine, or manufactured by branding, location, service, packaging — hands each seller a miniature monopoly over its own version: customers who prefer this particular takeaway will absorb a slightly higher price before they defect.

That customer loyalty tilts the firm's demand curve: no longer flat, but gently downward-sloping — much flatter than a monopolist's, because near-substitutes crowd close by. A sloping demand curve drags an MR curve below it, so the short-run diagram is a pocket-sized monopoly picture: produce where MR equals MC, read price off the demand curve, compare with AC. Short-run economic profit is entirely possible.

But the entry door stands open — and this is the structure's signature scene. Profit invites copies: a rival chicken outlet opens over the road; a new salon undercuts down the block. Each arrival shaves customers off the incumbent, nudging its demand curve LEFTWARD, and the shaving continues until the demand curve just TOUCHES the average cost curve — tangent — at the chosen output. At that tangency, price equals average cost: normal profit only. A monopoly-shaped diagram delivering perfect competition's long-run verdict.

Two efficiency findings live at the tangency, and both get asked. Because the demand curve slopes, the touch lands on the DOWNHILL side of the AC curve, left of its minimum: the firm carries EXCESS CAPACITY, permanently producing less than its cheapest possible scale — the quiet mid-afternoon shop as a structural fact, not bad luck. And price still exceeds marginal cost, so allocative efficiency fails too, gently. The repayment is VARIETY: identical stalls give efficiency without choice; a road of different takeaways gives choice without full efficiency. Consumers pay slightly more and receive a menu.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — features and examples, the gently tilted demand curve, entry shaving profit to the tangency, excess capacity, and the variety trade-off.

## Subtopic: The Four Structures Side by Side

The comparison table is the topic's harvest; recite it in words, structure by structure, across five tests.

NUMBER OF SELLERS: perfect competition, very many; monopolistic competition, many; oligopoly, few; monopoly, one. NATURE OF PRODUCT: homogeneous; differentiated; homogeneous or differentiated; unique with no close substitutes. ENTRY: free; free; difficult; blocked. CONTROL OVER PRICE: none — pure price taker; slight; considerable but hemmed in by rivals; full — price maker, restrained only by the demand curve. DEMAND CURVE FACING THE FIRM: horizontal; gently downward-sloping; kinked at the ruling price; the market demand curve itself.

Then the performance row, where evaluation marks concentrate. LONG-RUN PROFIT: normal only; normal only, because entry shaves it away; economic profit possible, because entry is hard; economic profit persistent, because entry is blocked. EFFICIENCY: the perfect market alone achieves allocative and productive efficiency together; monopolistic competition misses both narrowly, buying variety at the cost of excess capacity; oligopoly restricts output and channels resources into persuasion, though its scale and innovation partly compensate; monopoly restricts hardest and prices furthest above marginal cost — with the famous qualification attached: where one network truly is cheapest, natural monopoly plus a regulator beats forced competition, and large sheltered profits sometimes bankroll research no small rival could afford.

Close with working method, because it collapses the whole topic. Every structure answers the same three questions with the same tools: WHERE to produce — MR equals MC, always; WHAT price — read upward to the firm's demand curve; WHAT profit — compare price with AC there, remembering that normal profit already hides inside the cost curves. Structures differ in just two settings: the SLOPE of the demand curve the firm faces, and the EASE of entry that decides whether profit outlives the short run. Slope and door. Turn those two dials and you can generate all four diagrams from one.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five-test recitation, the long-run profit row explained through entry, the efficiency verdicts with monopoly's qualification, and the two dials that build every structure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole zoo at walking pace — one lagoon, one town, and its eating streets — because you already trade in all four market structures every week without once naming them.

## Subtopic: The Only Ferry Across the Lagoon

A holiday town sits across a wide lagoon, and one company runs the only ferry. Ride, or drive ninety minutes around.

Feel how different this is from the butternut row. The butternut seller who tried fifteen rand watched her shoppers drift one crate along. The ferry has no next crate: push the fare up and most passengers sigh and tap their cards. The ferry company chooses its own fare — a price MAKER — though never without limits, because at eighty rand a crossing, real numbers of people will do the ninety-minute drive, share lifts, or stay on their own side. The company picks its spot on the town's willingness to pay: high fare and emptier decks, or low fare and full ones. That downward slope is the only leash it wears.

Now catch the wrinkle that carries the diagram. When the ferry drops the fare from thirty rand to twenty-eight to tempt extra passengers aboard, EVERY passenger pays twenty-eight — including the whole crowd that was happy at thirty. The new passengers bring twenty-eight rand each, but the two-rand discount to everyone already aboard quietly eats into the gain, so the true extra revenue per new passenger is well below the fare on the board. Selling more always costs a monopolist something invisible. That invisible subtraction is exactly why marginal revenue sits below price on the monopoly diagram.

And why does nobody launch a second ferry? Sometimes a licence forbids it; sometimes the company owns the only decent jetty; and sometimes a second ferry would be genuine waste — one boat can carry the whole town more cheaply than two half-empty ones ever could. That last case is the natural monopoly, and it is why no one wants two power grids down one street: the wise answer is not a rival boat but a watchful eye on the fare — a regulator standing at the gangway.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the ferry are coming to you right now: price maker against price taker, the invisible cost hiding in every fare cut, why the fare still has a ceiling, and the natural-monopoly case for a regulator instead of a rival.

## Subtopic: Three Bakeries and a Staring Contest

Inland now: a farming town fed by exactly three bakeries, their delivery vans crossing paths every dawn.

Bakery A considers lifting its bread price by a rand. It already knows the ending: B and C hold still, every spaza and café switches supplier within a week, and A's ovens cool alone. So A does not lift. Then A considers CUTTING a rand to grab the town. It knows that ending too: B and C match by Thursday — they have no choice — and by month-end all three sell the same loaves to the same customers at less, each simply poorer. Up, you lose alone; down, you gain nothing. So the price of bread stands still for a year at a time — not because anyone shook hands, but because each bakery is playing chess against the other two. That frozen price is the kinked demand curve wearing an apron.

Where does the rivalry go if not into price? Everywhere else. Fresher morning deliveries, a van that never misses a spaza, sponsor boards at the school sports day, a new seeded loaf, neater packaging — the war of everything-except-the-price. When you notice banks and networks advertising endlessly while charging almost identically, you are watching this same contest at national scale.

Then the temptation. One afternoon at the agricultural show, the three owners find themselves at one table. Someone murmurs: why are we fighting? Two rand on every loaf, all three of us, from Monday. Suddenly the town has one bakery in three buildings — customers pay more with nowhere to turn. Two forces eventually crack the pact. Greed inside: each owner is tempted to quietly discount after hours and steal volume — the cheating wired into every cartel. And the law outside: in South Africa that table talk is a case file; the Competition Commission investigates it and the Tribunal can fine each bakery up to a tenth of its turnover — the country's real bread producers learnt exactly that. The staring contest is legal; the murmur at the table is not.

Your questions for this part are up now: why the price freezes without any agreement, where the competition migrates, the table at the show, and the two forces that crack the pact.

## Subtopic: A Street Full of Takeaways

Back to the coast, to the taxi-rank street where fourteen takeaways trade elbow to elbow — and every single one is busy being different. One fries the crispiest chips; one is strictly halaal; one stays open till the last taxi; one loads extra atchar without being asked; one belongs to the auntie who remembers every customer's usual order.

Each takeaway owns a tiny ferry: ITS regulars will pay five rand more before crossing the street, because to them the difference is real. So each sets a price slightly its own — a small price maker. But the ferry route is short: fifteen rand more and even the loyal ones try the rival two doors down, because thirteen close substitutes wait within a hundred metres. The demand curve tilts gently — a slope, not a cliff.

Then the butternut row's old logic walks back in, because entry is FREE. Say the chip shop's queue lengthens and its margins fatten. Within months, two new takeaways open on the same street — as predictable as the tide. Every newcomer slices a few regulars away; the incumbents' curves edge left; and the fattening stops precisely where each owner again earns what her hands and savings would earn anywhere else. The special recipe stays special only until it is copied.

Now read the street as an economist. Nobody rich; nobody ruined; and look closely — every takeaway stands half-idle through the mid-afternoon lull. Fourteen half-busy fryers where nine full ones would cost less. That idle fryer is excess capacity, the standing price of a street where everyone differs. And what do customers buy with it? CHOICE — the crisp chips, the halaal counter, the late hours, the extra atchar, the auntie who knows your order. The butternut row was efficient and identical; the takeaway street is slightly wasteful and human. Both are markets obeying their own structures to the letter.

So carry the zoo home in one sweep: sixty identical crates — no power, no profit that survives. One ferry — full power, profit lasting as long as the jetty stays exclusive. Three bakeries staring — power held carefully, frozen prices, war by delivery van and sponsor board. Fourteen takeaways — a pinch of power each, melted by the next arrival, and paid out to you as variety.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the takeaway's short ferry route, entry as the melter of fat margins, the idle fryer named and priced, and all four businesses matched to their structures. Keep the zoo caged and labelled — market failure, up next, begins exactly where these structures leave buyers paying more than the last unit truly cost.
