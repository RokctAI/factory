**Mandy:** Class questions before we wrap up! The first one comes from the group at the window: my mom runs a small catering business, and she says her costs went up way more than the six percent the news talks about. Is the official number just wrong for people like her?

**Tutor:** The official number is right about the average and silent about your mother — and both halves of that sentence matter. The CPI prices one national basket, weighted by what households as a whole spend. A caterer's personal basket is nothing like it: her spending is concentrated in food, cooking oil, gas and transport, exactly the categories that have often outrun the headline rate. When food inflation runs at twelve percent and food is half of your outgoings, your lived inflation sits far above the average even while the official number is perfectly accurate. Statistics South Africa acknowledges this itself by publishing separate indices for different expenditure groups, and the index for lower-spending households frequently runs hotter than headline CPI when food and transport lead the increases. Add one psychological effect: we remember the prices that leapt and never notice the ones that crept — data prices, for instance, have had long stretches of standing still, and nobody celebrates a price that did not move. So in an answer, say it precisely: the CPI is a weighted average of a representative basket; individual households experience different rates depending on how their spending differs from the weights; both the average and the experience can be true simultaneously.

***

**Mandy:** Next question, and it is a good one: the Reserve Bank is supposed to protect us from inflation, but when it raises the repo rate my dad's bond payment goes up. How is making us poorer protecting us?

**Tutor:** You have just described the mechanism, not a malfunction — and seeing that clearly is what evaluation questions reward. The repo rate cools inflation BY making borrowing expensive: bond instalments, car repayments and overdrafts all climb, households trim spending, firms delay expansion, and with less money chasing goods, the pulling pressure on prices eases. The cost of that medicine lands immediately and unevenly — on your father's bond, on the small business surviving on credit, on the worker whose employer cancels a shift when orders slow. The benefit arrives later and thinly spread, as prices stabilise for everyone. That timing gap is why rate hikes are always unpopular and often still correct: unchecked inflation would eventually rob the same households more deeply, and a central bank that hesitates teaches unions and firms to expect high inflation, which writes the next round of contracts at higher numbers and makes the eventual cure harsher. But keep the lesson's sharpest edge in your answer: this defence holds when the inflation is demand-driven. When prices are being pushed by tariffs, fuel and drought, the repo squeeze extracts real pain for very little cooling — and that is when your father's complaint is not just understandable but economically sound.

***

**Mandy:** Third one: if inflation makes borrowers better off and the government is the biggest borrower of all, doesn't the government secretly want inflation? Why fight it at all?

**Tutor:** That suspicion has excellent historical credentials — governments through the centuries have quietly used inflation to shrink their debts, and economists even have a name for the gain: it works like a hidden tax on everyone holding money. But follow the logic one step further and the trick collapses. Lenders are not fools: the moment they expect inflation, they demand higher interest rates on new government bonds to compensate, so the state's future borrowing gets more expensive precisely because it inflated away the past. Push harder and you arrive at the cases where states financed themselves with the printing press — Zimbabwe in 2008 is the example you know — and the currency itself died, taking tax revenue, savings and the economy down with it. That is exactly why South Africa's design separates the powers: an independent Reserve Bank, with a public 3-to-6-percent target, that cannot be instructed to print money for the treasury. The independence is not decoration; it is a promise to lenders and citizens that the borrower with the most to gain from inflation does not control the machine that creates money. In an essay, that point — inflation as a temptation the system deliberately locks away from government — turns a list of consequences into an argument.

**Mandy:** Last question before the bell: in the weighted-basket calculation, why do we multiply each price increase by the weight? Why not just average the three percentages?

**Tutor:** Because a plain average pretends every item matters equally in your life, and your spending says otherwise. Take the lesson's basket: food up twelve percent with a weight of forty, transport up four with thirty-five, education up eight with twenty-five. A plain average of twelve, four and eight gives eight percent — but that treats transport, where you spend thirty-five rand of every hundred, the same as it would treat an item where you spend one rand. The weighted version multiplies each increase by the share of your money it touches: nought comma four times twelve, plus nought comma three five times four, plus nought comma two five times eight, giving eight comma two percent. The weight is the answer to a simple question — how much of my money does this price rise actually reach? A fifty percent jump in the price of something that takes half a percent of your budget barely moves your cost of living; a five percent rise in rent moves it seriously. That is also the deep reason different households live different inflation rates: the arithmetic is identical, but the weights are personal. Check your weights sum to one, show the multiplication line by line, and the marks follow the working.
