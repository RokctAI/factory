# Part 1 — Expert

Walk into any supermarket in January and watch the staff with the pricing gun, relabelling shelf after shelf. Multiply that scene across every till, every taxi rank and every rental office in the country, and you have the phenomenon this session dissects: inflation. One official number summarises all that relabelling, it is built from an index called the CPI, and it steers the repo rate, wage talks, social grants and the interest on your family's bond. By the end of this session you will know what inflation is, how the CPI is assembled and calculated, what causes prices to run, who gets hurt, and what the authorities can actually do about it. Few topics in Economics repay study more reliably in your final exams.

## Subtopic: Inflation Defined and the CPI Basket

Begin with the definition, and treat every word in it as load-bearing. INFLATION is a sustained and significant increase in the GENERAL price level over time. Three tests hide inside that sentence. SUSTAINED means the rise must keep going: a single jump in prices after a fuel-levy increase, followed by flat prices, fails the test. GENERAL means the average of prices across the whole economy must rise: if school shoes get more expensive while televisions get cheaper, that is relative prices shuffling, not inflation. OVER TIME means the increase is always a comparison with an earlier date — usually the same month one year before. Beside inflation stand its two cousins. DEFLATION is a sustained fall in the general price level, rare and quietly destructive, because buyers wait for cheaper prices and producers cut back while they wait. DISINFLATION is inflation slowing down: a move from eight percent to five percent means prices are still climbing, only less steeply — a distinction that costs students marks every year when they call it deflation.

Now the instrument that measures all this. The CONSUMER PRICE INDEX is a weighted index of the prices of a representative BASKET of goods and services that households buy. Statistics South Africa assembles it in four stages. Stage one: expenditure surveys ask thousands of households what they actually spend money on, producing a basket of several hundred items — from samp and cooking oil to prepaid electricity, data bundles and burial-society premiums. Stage two: each item gets a WEIGHT equal to its share of total household spending — housing and utilities take roughly a quarter of the basket, food and non-alcoholic drinks a little under a fifth, transport around a seventh. Stage three: field workers and online collectors record tens of thousands of prices every single month, across provinces and store types. Stage four: the weighted average of all those prices is converted into an index, with the BASE YEAR set equal to 100.

Learn the family of indices too. HEADLINE CPI is the all-items number for urban areas — the one quoted on the news. CORE inflation removes food, fuel and energy, the jumpiest items, to expose the underlying trend. And the PRODUCER PRICE INDEX, the PPI, measures prices where goods leave the factory or the farm, before retail. The CPI prices the cost of living; the PPI prices the cost of production — and because factory-gate increases usually reach the shelf a few months later, analysts read the PPI as tomorrow's CPI arriving early.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three tests inside the definition, deflation separated from disinflation, how the basket and its weights are built, and the CPI set against the PPI.

## Subtopic: Calculating with the CPI

Calculations carry guaranteed marks in this topic, so drill both of them until they run without thinking.

First calculation: building the index. Suppose the representative basket cost R5 000 in the base year, when the index was set at 100. Three years later the identical basket costs R5 400. The index now reads 5 400 divided by 5 000, times 100 — which is 108,0. Hold onto what that number is: not a price, not a percentage, but a ratio to the base year. An index of 108 says that what cost R100 in the base year costs R108 today.

Second calculation: the inflation rate, which is the PERCENTAGE CHANGE in the CPI between two dates. The formula is new index minus old index, divided by the OLD index, times 100. Say the CPI climbs from 108,0 to 114,5 in a year. The change is 6,5 index points. Divide by the old value of 108,0 and multiply by 100: 6,02 percent, call it 6,0. Write the formula, substitute, compute, attach the percentage sign — the working earns marks step by step. Two traps wait here, and both are famous. Trap one: dividing by the new index instead of the old one, which answers a question nobody asked. Trap two: reporting the point change as the rate — the index moved 6,5 points, but the inflation rate is 6,0 percent, and points are not percentages.

Because the CPI is a weighted average, you must also be able to weight one yourself. Take a simplified basket of three categories. Food carries a weight of 40 percent and food prices rose 12 percent. Transport carries 35 percent and rose 4 percent. Education carries 25 percent and rose 8 percent. Weighted inflation is 0,4 times 12, plus 0,35 times 4, plus 0,25 times 8 — that is 4,8 plus 1,4 plus 2,0, giving 8,2 percent. Notice the lesson inside the arithmetic: the biggest single price rise does not automatically dominate; the weights decide. And that is exactly why a low-income household, spending a far larger slice of its money on food, lives a harsher inflation rate than the national average admits.

Finally, run the machine in reverse and in real terms. If the CPI stood at 105,0 last year and inflation ran at 8 percent, this year's index is 105 times 1,08 — that is 113,4. And use inflation to deflate money values: a nurse whose salary rises 5 percent in a year of 7 percent inflation is earning roughly 2 percent LESS in real terms. More rands, smaller trolley.

Stop for this section's questions now — constructing the index, the rate formula forwards and in reverse, the weighted basket, and nominal converted honestly into real.

## Subtopic: Types and Causes of Inflation

Not all inflation is the same animal, and diagnosing the type correctly is the skill every discussion question on this topic secretly tests — because the type dictates the treatment.

DEMAND-PULL inflation begins on the spending side: total demand grows faster than the economy can produce, and too much money chases too few goods. Trace its engines through the components of spending — households borrowing cheaply and consuming, government running wide deficits, firms in an investment boom, exports surging and pouring income into the country. Aggregate demand shifts to the right against a supply curve that cannot stretch fast enough, and sellers, facing queues, raise prices. The symptoms are unmistakable: sold-out stock, waiting lists, an economy running warm.

COST-PUSH inflation begins on the production side: input costs rise, and producers pass them down the line to survive. The classic culprits: wage settlements that outrun productivity, a depreciating rand making imported components and oil dearer, fuel-price shocks, administered prices such as electricity tariffs marching upward, drought pushing up food prices, and firms with market power padding margins. Cost-push is the crueller type because it pairs rising prices with FALLING output and rising unemployment — the miserable combination economists call STAGFLATION.

You must be able to tell the two stories in curve language, in words. Demand-pull: the aggregate demand curve shifts right along aggregate supply — price level up, output up. Cost-push: the aggregate supply curve shifts left — price level up, output DOWN. The till slip looks identical; the machinery underneath moves in opposite directions.

Complete the vocabulary with the extremes. HYPERINFLATION is inflation that has escaped control entirely — Zimbabwe in 2008 remains the textbook case, when prices doubled within days, banknotes carried rows of zeros, and people abandoned the currency for barter and foreign notes. At the other end, CREEPING inflation of a few percent is considered compatible with healthy growth — which is precisely why South Africa's target is a positive band rather than zero.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — demand-pull and cost-push with causes sorted onto the correct side, both curve stories, stagflation, and hyperinflation.

## Subtopic: Consequences and the Fight Against Inflation

Why do central banks treat inflation as an enemy? Because it rearranges wealth without permission, hits the defenceless hardest, and corrodes the machinery of growth.

Take the redistribution first. Inflation shifts wealth from CREDITORS to DEBTORS: a loan taken today is repaid later in rands that buy less, so the lender loses purchasing power and the borrower quietly pockets the difference. People on FIXED INCOMES — pensioners, grant recipients — watch each month's money buy a little less unless payments are adjusted upward. Savers lose whenever their interest rate sits below inflation, because the REAL interest rate — nominal minus inflation — turns negative and their capital melts. Organised workers can bargain for increases; workers without that power absorb a pay cut nobody ever announced. That asymmetry is why inflation is often described as the harshest tax on the poor.

Then the damage to efficiency. Uncertain prices make firms postpone investment. When domestic prices rise faster than those of trading partners, exports lose competitiveness and the trade balance suffers. Pay increases that merely match inflation can still push taxpayers into higher brackets — bracket creep, a tax increase no parliament voted for. Worst of all, inflation feeds on EXPECTATIONS: once everyone anticipates seven percent, wage deals and price lists are written at seven percent, and the expectation delivers itself. Breaking that loop is the central task of policy.

South Africa's chosen weapon, since the year 2000, is INFLATION TARGETING. The Reserve Bank is mandated to hold CPI inflation inside a band of 3 to 6 percent, and in practice it steers toward the middle of that band. Its instrument is MONETARY POLICY through the repo rate — the rate at which it lends to commercial banks. Lift the repo and prime follows within days; loans cost more, spending and borrowing cool, and demand-pull pressure drains away. The Bank's independence is part of the design: a central bank that cannot be instructed to print money for the state is the historical safeguard against hyperinflation. But state the limit as clearly as the strength, because evaluation marks live there: the repo rate operates on DEMAND. Aim it at cost-push inflation — an oil shock, a tariff hike, a drought — and it cannot lower a single production cost; it can only crush spending around the shock, sacrificing output and jobs for little relief. The Phillips curve formalises the trade-off: in the short run, buying lower inflation tends to cost higher unemployment.

The rest of the toolkit is fiscal and structural: government restraining its own spending and borrowing, holding administered price increases down, and building competition and productivity so supply can grow into demand. Direct wage and price controls have been tried elsewhere and the syllabus expects you to know their record — they suppress the symptom while shortages and black markets multiply beneath the freeze.

The final questions of this part are with you now: who loses and who gains from the redistribution, bracket creep and expectations, the target band with the repo mechanism, and the honest limits of that mechanism against cost-push.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again, starting from a supermarket trolley in Polokwane — because the CPI is nothing grander than the nation's shopping, weighed and priced on repeat.

## Subtopic: The Trolley That Tells the Truth

Picture one trolley packed to stand for every household in the country. What goes in? Whatever South Africans genuinely spend on: maize meal and cooking oil, a taxi-fare allowance, prepaid electricity, a data bundle, rent, school uniforms, a burial-society premium. Statistics South Africa did not guess this list — it asked thousands of households, and the answers decided both the contents and the SPACE each item occupies. Housing claims the biggest section of the trolley. Food claims a large one. A movie ticket squeezes into a corner.

Every month, collectors price that exact same trolley again. In the starting year it cost R5 000, and we stamp that moment with the number 100 — not rands, just a starting line. Three years later the identical trolley rings up R5 400. Divide 5 400 by 5 000 and multiply by 100: the stamp now reads 108. That stamp is the CPI. It never tells you what anything costs; it tells you how far prices have walked since the starting line.

The inflation rate is simply the speed of the walk over the past year. Last year the stamp said 108. Today it says 114,5. It moved 6,5 points, and measured against the 108 where the year began, that is a 6 percent journey. That is the number the news reads out — and now you know it was not felt, voted on or invented. One trolley. Priced twice. Compared.

One honest footnote. Your household's trolley is not the national trolley. A family that spends nearly half its money on food takes food inflation at double strength; a household with a long daily commute feels every fuel increase twice. So when someone insists the official rate must be wrong because their own shopping jumped far more — both can be true at once. The official number is an average, and no real family is average.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the trolley are coming to you right now: how the basket is chosen, what the index stamp means, and how a moving stamp becomes the inflation rate.

## Subtopic: Two Fires, Two Hoses

Prices can catch alight from two different directions, and separating the two is the exact skill the essay question wants.

The first fire starts out front, at the till. Money is flowing — bonuses paid, credit cheap, government projects hiring, exports booming — and everybody is buying. Shelves empty faster than they refill, and when many hands grab at few goods, sellers lift prices simply because they can. That is DEMAND-PULL: buyers pulling prices upward. Its fingerprint is the busy shop — stock sold out, order books full, delivery dates slipping.

The second fire starts around the back, at the loading bay. The rand weakens and imported parts cost more. Diesel climbs, so every delivery costs more. The electricity tariff jumps. A drought shrivels the maize crop. Nothing about customers has changed — they are not spending more — but the producer's own bills have risen, so the shelf price rises in self-defence. That is COST-PUSH: costs shoving prices up from behind. Its fingerprint is the quiet shop with rising prices — the double misery called stagflation.

Why does the diagnosis matter so much? Because the country's main firehose — the Reserve Bank raising the repo rate — only reaches the FIRST fire. Costlier loans make households spend less, and less spending stops the pulling. But turn that hose on a cost fire and watch what happens: diesel does not get cheaper because bond repayments went up. The hose drenches the shoppers while the kitchen keeps burning — sales fall, jobs go, and the cost pressure stays exactly where it was. Carry that sentence into your final exams: the repo rate fights demand-pull well and cost-push badly.

Your questions for this part are up now: sorting causes into the correct fire, the fingerprint of each, and why the interest-rate hose reaches only one of them.

## Subtopic: Who Wins and Who Loses When Prices Run

The inflation number is not neutral — it quietly slides money between pockets, and the last skill of the topic is naming whose.

Start with the losers. The pensioner: her income arrives fixed while prices do not, so each month's envelope stretches thinner — which is why grant and pension adjustments are announced with the CPI in the same breath. The saver: money earning 5 percent while inflation runs 7 is shrinking at 2 percent a year in real terms, politely and invisibly. The worker with no union behind him: no negotiated increase means an unannounced pay cut. And every taxpayer meets the sneakiest loss of all — a raise that merely matches inflation can lift you into a higher tax bracket, so the state takes a bigger share of buying power that never grew.

Now the surprising winners. The borrower: borrow today, repay in lighter rands tomorrow — inflation gnaws at debts just as hungrily as it gnaws at savings. The largest borrower in the country, the government itself, benefits the same way. And people who hold real assets — property, shares, stock in a business — ride the price wave upward while cash under the mattress sinks.

This is why the 3-to-6-percent band exists. Not zero, because a small, steady rise keeps the economy's wheels turning. Never double digits, because that road ends where Zimbabwe stood in 2008 — wheelbarrow money that could not buy bread by sunset. The band is the compromise: inflation low enough and predictable enough that a family can sign a lease, a spaza owner can price stock, and a matric learner can plan next year's fees without the ground shifting under the numbers.

So read the next inflation headline like an economist. You know where the number came from — one trolley, priced twice. You can ask which fire is burning — pull from the till or push from the loading bay. You can predict the Bank's response — repo up if demand is the culprit, agony if costs are. And you can name who is bleeding — the pensioner, the saver, the unorganised — and who is quietly smiling — the borrower. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: winners and losers sorted, the case for the band, and the full chain from trolley to repo told in your own words. Master this topic properly — it anchors the inflation essay and walks straight into business cycles, monetary policy and exchange rates.
