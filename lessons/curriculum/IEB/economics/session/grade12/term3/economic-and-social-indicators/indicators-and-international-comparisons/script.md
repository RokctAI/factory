# Part 1 — Expert

A pilot never flies on a single dial. Altitude, airspeed, fuel, heading — each instrument answers a different question, and ignoring any one of them ends badly. An economy is flown the same way, on INDICATORS: statistics published on a fixed calendar that reveal how production, prices, money, labour and human wellbeing are moving. This session assembles the full instrument panel the syllabus requires — the economic indicators, the social indicators, and the discipline of comparing countries without deceiving yourself — and closes with the two global institutions, the IMF and the World Bank, that watch these panels for the whole world.

## Subtopic: Reading the Dashboard: Indicator Types and Timing

An ECONOMIC INDICATOR is a regularly published statistic that signals the performance of the economy. Statistics South Africa, the Reserve Bank and the treasury release them on a public schedule, so that government, business and unions all negotiate from the same numbers rather than from rival guesses.

The first way to classify indicators is by TIMING, because they do not all describe the same moment. LEADING indicators turn BEFORE the economy turns — building plans passed, job advertisements placed, new vehicle sales, share prices, factory orders. Nobody submits a building plan by accident; someone intends to build next year, so these series peer into the future, and the Reserve Bank compiles a composite leading indicator watched precisely because it bends months ahead of the business cycle. COINCIDENT indicators move WITH the cycle — industrial production, retail sales, real GDP itself — reporting where the economy stands this quarter. LAGGING indicators confirm a turning point only AFTER it has passed: unemployment is the famous one, because firms retrench late and rehire cautiously; inventories and insolvencies behave the same way. Lagging series never predict; their job is to certify that a turn already happened.

Classification two: LEVEL against RATE. The level of GDP measures the economy's size; the growth rate measures its direction. Keep them apart under pressure — a giant economy can be stalling while a small one sprints, and a data question that hands you both is testing exactly this refusal to confuse them.

Classification three: NOMINAL against REAL. Anything measured in rands inflates along with prices, so honest comparison across time strips inflation out. Real growth equals nominal growth minus inflation, roughly — and the correction bites: if nominal GDP expanded 7,5 percent while inflation ran 5,5, actual production grew only about 2 percent. Rands stretched; output barely did.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: leading, coincident and lagging with correct examples, level kept apart from rate, and nominal converted to real.

## Subtopic: The Economic Indicators

Now walk the panel gauge by gauge, as the syllabus lists it.

PRODUCTION: real GDP growth leads the panel — the annual percentage change in the volume of final goods and services produced. Pair it immediately with GDP PER CAPITA, because living standards are output per person: if GDP grows 0,9 percent while the population grows 1,5, the average South African is getting poorer inside a growing economy. That one subtraction explains years of national frustration.

PRICES: the CPI at the till and the PPI at the factory gate — each has its own lesson, but on this panel remember their role: consumer inflation is the gauge the Reserve Bank is mandated to hold inside the 3-to-6-percent band.

MONEY AND CREDIT: the money supply comes in nested sizes. M1 is the most liquid layer — notes, coins and demand deposits. M2 adds short and medium-term deposits; M3, the broadest aggregate, adds long-term deposits and is the one the Bank tracks. Next to it sits credit extended to the private sector. Rapid growth in money and credit is an early smoke signal of inflation ahead, which is why both are monitored even though formal money-supply targets were abandoned long ago.

INTEREST RATES: the repo rate is the policy lever, prime is what banks charge their best clients, and the REAL interest rate — nominal minus inflation — is what savers truly earn and borrowers truly pay.

THE EXTERNAL GAUGES: the rand's exchange rate, the current account balance, and the TERMS OF TRADE — an index of export prices divided by import prices. When the prices of what South Africa sells — platinum, coal, iron ore, fruit — rise relative to what it buys — oil, machinery, electronics — the terms of trade improve, and the country earns more without shipping one extra ton. It is a raise the world pays us.

LABOUR: the unemployment rate — the unemployed as a percentage of the economically active population — with its two definitions held clearly apart. The STRICT definition counts only people actively searching for work; the EXPANDED definition adds discouraged work-seekers who have stopped looking. The gap between the two runs to several percentage points, and that gap is itself a measurement — of surrendered hope. The Quarterly Labour Force Survey supplies the employment side of the gauge.

PRODUCTIVITY: output per worker per hour. It is the only fountain of rising wages that does not feed inflation; when pay outruns productivity, the excess reappears as cost-push pressure on prices.

Stop for this section's questions now — the money aggregates in order, terms of trade read correctly, strict against expanded unemployment, the per-capita subtraction, and productivity's link to inflation.

## Subtopic: The Social Indicators

Economic gauges measure the machine; SOCIAL indicators measure the people the machine exists for — whether growth is actually reaching human lives. The syllabus groups them, so group them.

DEMOGRAPHICS: population growth and LIFE EXPECTANCY at birth. Life expectancy is development's great summary number, compressing nutrition, clinics, clean water and safety into a single figure. South Africa's own curve tells a policy story in one line: it collapsed during the years when AIDS went untreated, then climbed back by more than a decade as antiretroviral treatment reached millions — proof that a health decision shows up in a national statistic within a generation.

NUTRITION AND HEALTH: the INFANT MORTALITY RATE — deaths before the first birthday per thousand live births — and under-five mortality are the sharpest poverty gauges in existence, because when water, food and clinics fail, the youngest die first. Child stunting, HIV prevalence, and access measures such as health workers per thousand people complete the group.

EDUCATION: literacy, enrolment, completion — and, the modern refinement, actual LEARNING outcomes, because a full classroom where little is learned flatters the gauge without changing a life. Education sits on both sides of the panel: a social indicator today, the economy's skills supply tomorrow.

SERVICES AND HOUSING: the share of households with piped water, sanitation, electricity and formal housing. These are the gauges where South Africa's post-1994 progress is most visible — household electrification rose from roughly two-thirds to about nine in ten — and where every service-delivery protest is a data point filed by the households still waiting.

URBANISATION: the share of people living in cities — a development signal, because opportunity concentrates in towns, and a stress signal when cities grow faster than their pipes, buses and clinics.

Two composite numbers tie the threads together. The HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX, published by the UN Development Programme, folds life expectancy, education and income per person into one score between 0 and 1 — invented precisely to stop GDP being the only scoreboard of progress. And the GINI COEFFICIENT measures inequality on a scale from 0, perfect equality, to 1, one person holding everything. South Africa's Gini, around 0,63 to 0,65, ranks among the highest ever recorded — the number underneath the familiar sentence that this is one of the most unequal societies on earth.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — life expectancy and infant mortality as summary gauges, the services indicators, the HDI's three ingredients, and the Gini read correctly.

## Subtopic: Comparing Countries: PPP, Pitfalls and the Global Institutions

Comparing countries is where indicator work gets technical, and three corrections keep the comparison honest.

Correction one: PER CAPITA. China's total GDP towers over Switzerland's, yet a Swiss resident is many times richer than a Chinese resident. Total output measures a country's weight in the world; output per person measures its people's living standards. Divide by population before saying anything about wellbeing.

Correction two: PURCHASING POWER PARITY. Converting incomes at market exchange rates makes poor countries look poorer than they live, because money goes further at home: a hundred rand in Mthatha buys a cooked meal, a taxi ride and change, while its exchange-rate equivalent barely buys a coffee in London. PPP conversion reprices each country's income by what money actually buys locally, and it can double or triple a developing country's measured income. Any table you quote should declare which conversion it used.

Correction three: COVERAGE. Official statistics miss the informal sector, subsistence farming and unpaid household work — activities proportionally far larger in poorer countries — so measured gaps between rich and poor nations overstate the real gaps. Definitions differ too: an unemployment comparison across borders means first checking whose definition is stricter.

Then the two institutions the syllabus attaches to this global bookkeeping, both created at Bretton Woods in 1944. The INTERNATIONAL MONETARY FUND is the world's financial paramedic: it monitors member economies through surveillance and lends to countries in balance-of-payments crises — countries that can no longer pay for imports or service foreign debt. Its loans come with CONDITIONALITY: required policy changes, frequently austerity, which is why every IMF rescue is simultaneously a lifeline and a controversy. The WORLD BANK is the development financier: long-horizon loans and technical expertise for infrastructure, education, health and reform in developing countries — and the publisher of much of the comparative data this whole lesson runs on. The clean one-line separation: the IMF handles short-term crisis finance and stability; the World Bank funds long-term development. South Africa belongs to both, and during the 2020 pandemic drew about 4,3 billion dollars from the IMF's emergency facility — a rare and much-debated use of the paramedic.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the per-capita and PPP corrections, the informal-sector blind spot, and the IMF cleanly separated from the World Bank.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole panel again from behind one steering wheel — because every indicator is just a way of asking how the journey is going.

## Subtopic: Headlights, Speedometer, Mirror

Put the economy in a minibus running the N3 from Durban to Johannesburg at night. The driver reads three kinds of information, and so does a country.

HEADLIGHTS light up the road ahead. For the economy: building plans approved, job adverts published, orders placed with factories, new vehicles bought. Each one is a promise about next year — nobody orders parts for a factory they intend to close. When the headlights brighten, better times are probably coming; when they dim, brake before the bend. LEADING indicators.

The SPEEDOMETER reads this instant. Tills ringing, factories producing, GDP itself — the speed the economy is actually doing right now. COINCIDENT indicators.

The REAR-VIEW MIRROR shows the road already travelled — and here sits the surprise that catches everyone: JOBS are in the mirror, not the headlights. Think like an employer. When business sours, you hold on to staff for a while, hoping the dip passes. When business improves, you wait to hire until you are certain. Either way, employment moves LAST. By the time unemployment finally falls, the recovery is already old. LAGGING indicators confirm the past; they never announce the future.

Two driving habits complete the section. Never confuse the size of the bus with its speed — a fifteen-seater can overtake a truck; a huge economy can be crawling. And never accept a reading taken in rands without asking what prices did: if takings rose 7,5 percent while inflation ran 5,5, the journey truly advanced only 2 percent. Rands stretch in the heat; kilometres do not.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the dashboard are coming to you right now: sorting indicators into headlights, speedometer and mirror, and the two driving habits.

## Subtopic: The Passengers' Report

Now turn around in the driver's seat and look at the PASSENGERS — because a bus setting speed records while its passengers are sick and hungry is not a journey worth boasting about. Social indicators are the passengers' own report.

Ask the eldest passenger: how long do people live where you come from? LIFE EXPECTANCY folds food, clinics, water and safety into one number — and South Africa's version of that number fell hard in the untreated-AIDS years and rose by more than ten when treatment arrived. A single statistic, carrying an entire policy history.

Ask the mother with the baby: do infants here reach their first birthday? INFANT MORTALITY is the most honest poverty gauge on the panel, because when the taps and clinics fail, the smallest passengers are the first to pay.

Ask the learner in uniform: are you in school — and is the school teaching you anything? Enrolment without learning is a register full of names and a gauge full of nothing.

Ask the household in the back row: is there a tap in the yard, a toilet that flushes, a switch that works? Water, sanitation and electricity are development you can touch with your hand — and the fact that about nine homes in ten now have electricity is as real as the protests staged by the tenth.

Then two clever summaries. The HDI blends health, schooling and income into one score out of 1, so countries can be ranked by the lives their people live rather than only the money they make. And the GINI asks the bluntest question on the bus: however much cash is on board, how is it SHARED? Zero means equally; one means a single passenger holds the lot. South Africa scores about 0,63 — near the top of the world's unfairness rankings. Two children on this same bus can be travelling through two different countries that happen to share one border post of skin and suburb.

## Subtopic: Comparing Taxis Honestly

Final skill: when two countries park side by side and compare dashboards, three honest rules stop the comparison from lying.

Rule one: divide by the heads on board. A taxi banking R12 000 a month with eighteen passengers is poorer per person than one banking R5 000 with three. China's economy is enormous; the average Swiss resident is far richer than the average Chinese resident. Say PER CAPITA before you say anything else.

Rule two: price the money where it lives. A hundred rand changed into pounds disappears in a London café, but at home it buys a plate of food, a ride and airtime. Raw exchange-rate comparisons make poor countries look poorer than their daily lives are. The repair is purchasing power parity — repricing each income by what it buys at home — and it can lift a developing country's measured income two or three times over. Before quoting any table, check which method built it.

Rule three: respect what the meter never sees. The vetkoek stand with no card machine, the neighbour fixing cars in his yard, the vegetables grown behind the house, the gogo raising three grandchildren — all real production, all invisible to official statistics, and all a bigger slice of life in poorer countries. The measured gap between rich and poor nations is real, but the meter stretches it.

And who referees the comparisons worldwide? Two institutions from the same 1944 meeting, kept apart in one breath each. The IMF is the paramedic: when a country cannot pay its foreign bills, the Fund lends — with strict conditions stapled to the loan, which saves the patient and starts the argument. The World Bank is the builder: patient, long-term finance for dams, schools, clinics and reforms, plus the statistics this lesson has leaned on throughout. South Africa carries both membership cards, and used the paramedic once in living memory — about 4,3 billion dollars in the pandemic emergency.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the three honest rules, the paramedic told apart from the builder, and one sentence on why a fast bus still needs its passengers checked.
