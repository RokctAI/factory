# Part 1 — Expert

Every other export South Africa produces must be dug, grown or built, then shipped away for good. Tourism runs the transaction backwards: the customer crosses the world TO the product, spends foreign currency inside our borders, and departs leaving the product intact for tomorrow's customer. Economists therefore file tourism under exports — an invisible export that earns very visible rands — and a country carrying mass unemployment studies it with the same seriousness it gives mining or manufacturing. This session runs the syllabus circuit in full: the precise definitions, the reasons tourism exploded worldwide, its effects on GDP, jobs, households and the state, South Africa's own tourism profile including indigenous knowledge systems, and the policies that could grow the industry further.

## Subtopic: What Counts as Tourism

Definitions open the topic, and they are tested clause by clause. On the UN World Tourism Organisation's definition, a TOURIST is a person travelling outside his or her usual environment, for less than a year, for leisure, business or other purposes not connected with earning money at the destination. Each clause earns its place. OUTSIDE the usual environment excludes your daily commute and weekend shopping. LESS THAN A YEAR excludes migration. NOT REMUNERATED at the destination excludes taking a job there — a consultant flown in and paid by a Sandton firm is a worker, while a delegate whose salary comes from home attending a Durban conference is a business tourist. One more split: a visitor who sleeps over at least one night is a tourist proper; one who arrives and leaves the same day is a DAY VISITOR, or excursionist — counted, but spending a fraction as much.

Then the three directions of flow, with their trade logic. DOMESTIC tourism is residents travelling inside their own country — a Pretoria family driving to Ballito in December: rands circulating at home, supporting jobs, but bringing no new money in. INBOUND tourism is foreigners arriving here — a Japanese couple at the Table Mountain cableway — and every rand they spend is an EXPORT, earning foreign exchange. OUTBOUND tourism is South Africans travelling abroad — your neighbour's trip to Thailand — an import, leaking spending out. Net the columns and you have the tourism balance; South Africa's runs at a surplus, earning more from arrivals than residents spend away.

Purpose categories complete the definitions: leisure and holidays; business tourism, including the MICE market — meetings, incentives, conferences and events; VISITING FRIENDS AND RELATIVES, the VFR category that dominates domestic travel; and the specialist streams — medical, sports, religious, educational, cultural. Each category spends differently, peaks in different seasons and needs different infrastructure, which is why marketing targets them separately.

## Subtopic: Why Tourism Has Grown

International tourism swelled from a few million trips a year in 1950 to more than a billion before the pandemic — one of the steepest expansions in economic history — and the syllabus wants the causes sorted into sides.

The DEMAND side: real incomes rose across the world, and the new middle classes of Asia in particular converted travel from a luxury into an expectation. Paid annual leave and pensions supplied the time; longer, healthier lifespans created a generation of retired travellers with decades to spend; smaller families and city living sharpened the appetite for getting away.

The SUPPLY side: the price of distance collapsed. Jet aircraft, then budget airlines, turned a journey that once cost a year's wages into one costing a week's. The price of knowing collapsed too: film and television sold the world's destinations, then the internet and the smartphone let any traveller research, compare, book and review without an agent, while package operators industrialised the holiday itself.

Then the enablers: peace, open borders and convertible currencies, because tourism follows safety the way water follows a slope. When the Cold War ended, half a continent opened to visitors. South Africa's own hinge year was 1994: with apartheid and sanctions gone, an isolated pariah became one of the world's fastest-growing destinations inside a decade — the clearest possible demonstration that politics writes tourism's growth curve.

Hold the shadow of the growth story too, because it is the mirror image evaluators love: tourism is a luxury purchase, first to be cut in hard times, and it collapses under shock. In 2020 the pandemic closed world tourism almost to zero within a single quarter, wiping out millions of livelihoods — and in South Africa silencing an industry that had supported roughly one job in every twenty. Rapid growth, fragile floor: carry both.

## Subtopic: What Tourism Does to an Economy

Now the effects, arranged exactly as the syllabus marks them: GDP, employment, externalities, and the benefits flowing to households, businesses, infrastructure and the state.

GDP: tourist spending on beds, transport, meals, guiding and curios is final demand feeding straight into production. Before the pandemic, tourism contributed around 3 percent of South African GDP directly, and closer to 8 or 9 percent once indirect effects are counted. The gap between those numbers is the MULTIPLIER at work: the lodge buys vegetables, laundry services and security; the waiter spends his wages at the spaza; every round of re-spending adds another layer of income. It is the circular-flow chain from earlier in the course — and tourism's chain runs long because so many of its inputs are bought locally.

EMPLOYMENT: tourism is LABOUR-INTENSIVE. Beds are made, meals cooked and trails guided by people, not machines, so a million rand of tourism demand creates more jobs than a million rand of mining or banking. Better yet for South Africa's unemployment profile: the jobs absorb semi-skilled workers, employ high shares of women and young people, and appear in rural districts where no factory will ever locate. A game lodge is an export industry standing in a village.

The four beneficiary groups: HOUSEHOLDS earn wages and small-business income; BUSINESSES from airlines to guesthouses earn revenue; INFRASTRUCTURE built for visitors — airports, roads, communications — keeps serving residents long after checkout; and the STATE collects VAT on every bed-night, taxes on every operator, and dedicated tourism levies, funding services far beyond the reserves.

Then the cost column, because evaluation questions live in it. Externalities: seasonal crowding and congestion; pressure on scarce water and land; litter and trampling in fragile places; and the risk of CULTURAL COMMODIFICATION — traditions rehearsed as products until the meaning drains out. LEAKAGES: when the hotel is foreign-owned, the food imported and the package sold overseas, much of each tourist rand exits the country before it can multiply — the classic critique of enclave resorts. And SEASONALITY makes tourism work precarious: the coast hires for December and retrenches in February. A strong answer weighs both columns openly.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — direct against multiplied GDP contribution, why labour-intensity matters here, the four beneficiary groups, and the cost column.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Profile, IKS and Policy

What is actually in South Africa's shop window? SCENERY AND WILDLIFE anchor it: the Kruger Park and private reserves, the Garden Route, the Drakensberg, Table Mountain — with the Big Five as the global calling card. HERITAGE AND CULTURE: ten UNESCO World Heritage Sites, among them Robben Island, the Cradle of Humankind, iSimangaliso Wetland Park and uKhahlamba-Drakensberg; township routes, battlefield trails, the winelands. EVENTS AND BUSINESS: conference centres in Cape Town and Durban, sports tours, festivals. CLIMATE: reliable sunshine, sold by the week to northern winters.

The syllabus gives special billing to INDIGENOUS KNOWLEDGE SYSTEMS — IKS: the accumulated knowledge, practices and expressions of indigenous communities, treated as a tourism asset in its own right. Traditional healing and plant knowledge, San tracking and rock-art interpretation, storytelling, beadwork and pottery whose patterns carry meaning, indigenous cuisine and dance — culture converted into livelihood through cultural villages, heritage routes and craft markets that earn income in places gate fees never reach. Attach the two policy duties that come with it: AUTHENTICITY — the culture must stay owned and told by its own communities, performed with them rather than staged about them — and BENEFIT-SHARING — the community, not only the operator, must bank the earnings from its knowledge.

The policy architecture: the 1996 White Paper on the Development and Promotion of Tourism set the framework — government leads, the private sector delivers, communities must benefit. The Tourism Act of 2014 governs the sector; SOUTH AFRICAN TOURISM markets the country abroad, funded partly by the TOMSA levy of one percent on tourism services; grading systems police quality; and the National Development Plan lists tourism among its priority growth sectors.

Policy suggestions — the improvement question this topic loves — work only when tied to named obstacles. Visas are slow: extend e-visas to high-growth markets such as China and India. Crime perception brakes bookings: police tourist routes visibly and fix the airport-to-hotel first impression. Flights are scarce and dear: negotiate more direct routes. Skills are short: train guides, chefs and managers ahead of the growth. Benefits pool in famous corridors: market and develop beyond the Cape and the Kruger, into township, rural and IKS-based products. And keep electricity and water running, because service failures travel the world as videos faster than any advertising campaign.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four product pillars, IKS with its two policy duties, the White Paper's division of roles, and matched obstacle-suggestion pairs.

## Subtopic: The Export That Walks In

Think about how this country usually earns foreign money: hoist coal onto a ship at Richards Bay and watch it vanish over the horizon forever. Now run the arrow the other way. A Canadian couple lands at Cape Town International with dollars in their account. They sleep in a Langa guesthouse, ride the cableway, drive the Garden Route, buy beadwork at a roadside stall, tip a guide at Addo, taste wine in Franschhoek — and fly home. The coal is gone for good. Table Mountain is still standing, ready to be sold again at sunrise.

That is why tourism is called an INVISIBLE EXPORT: the buyer travels to the product, the foreign money stays behind, and the product never leaves the country. Every foreign visitor is a shipping container that walks through passport control on its own legs.

Keep the three directions straight with one family photo. Your aunt from Mamelodi holidaying in Margate: DOMESTIC — rands moving around inside the national family, feeding jobs but adding no new money. The Canadian couple: INBOUND — new money in, a true export. Your cousin flying to Bangkok: OUTBOUND — our money out, an import. A country wants the inbound column standing tallest.

Now audit what the couple's money touched in one week: an airline, a guesthouse, a cableway, a car hire, a farm stall, a restaurant, a game reserve, a wine estate. Eight industries invoiced by one holiday. No container of coal does that on its way to the harbour.

## Subtopic: One Busload, Many Pay Packets

Now park a safari vehicle outside a lodge in the Waterberg and count the pay packets it fills, ring by ring — because this is the employment argument in miniature.

The DIRECT ring is on the payroll: the receptionist, the housekeepers, the chef, the guides, the tracker, the maintenance team. The INDIRECT ring sells to the lodge: the farmer who delivers vegetables, the village laundry, the security firm at the gate, the builder repairing the deck, the fuel supplier. Then the INDUCED ring: every worker in the first two rings spends wages at the spaza, the taxi, the salon, the tavern — the multiplier from the circular-flow lesson, now wearing a lodge uniform. One vehicle of guests; three rings of pay packets.

Study the character of those jobs. They are made of hands and hospitality — beds, meals, stories, safety — so they employ PEOPLE in numbers machines cannot replace: rand for rand, tourism out-hires mining. They appear precisely where our unemployment is deepest: no assembly plant is coming to a village beside a game reserve, but the reserve is already there, immovable, an export industry with a village attached. And they open doors for the groups our labour market fails hardest — women running kitchens and guesthouses, young people starting as trainee guides.

Now the honest warning: follow the rand and watch for the LEAK. If the lodge is owned abroad, the food trucked in from far away, and the package paid for in London, the vehicle arrives full and the village stays poor — the multiplying happened in someone else's economy. That is why policy keeps shouting LOCAL: local ownership, local suppliers, community shares in lodges. It is the difference between tourism that visits a village and tourism that feeds one.

## Subtopic: Selling What Only We Have

Final piece: what does the visitor actually cross the planet for — and what should policy repair first?

Beaches and hotel beds exist everywhere on earth. The eleven-hour flight is bought by what exists only here: the Big Five moving wild, Table Mountain, the Drakensberg escarpment, Robben Island's cell, the Cradle of Humankind — and the living cultures. That last shelf is the syllabus's honoured guest: indigenous knowledge. A San tracker reading yesterday's sand, a healer naming the veld's pharmacy, a storyteller carrying a grandmother's version of the story, beadwork whose colours spell messages — knowledge no competitor can copy, earning in villages that gate fees never reach. Two rules keep it honest: the community must OWN THE TELLING — culture performed with its people, never staged about them — and the community must BANK THE EARNINGS, not just wave as the bus pulls off.

Then run the obstacles like a businessperson, each with its matching fix. Visas crawl — extend e-visas to the giant new markets, China and India. Visitors fear crime — make tourist routes visibly safe and fix the airport-to-hotel first hour, where reputations are made. Flights are few and pricey — land more direct routes. The power flickers — and one load-shedding video circles the globe faster than a marketing budget; keeping the lights on IS destination marketing. Skills run short — train the chefs, guides and managers before the growth arrives, not after. And spread the map — the Cape and the Kruger are told; the Wild Coast, the Karoo and the battlefields are still waiting for their audience.

Close on the frame that holds every answer in this topic: tourism is the export that walks in, multiplies through three rings of pay packets, sells what only we have — and grows exactly as fast as we make the visit easy, safe and shared.
