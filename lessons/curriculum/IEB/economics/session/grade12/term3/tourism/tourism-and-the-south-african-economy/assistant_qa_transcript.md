**Mandy:** Question round before we close! First one: if tourism is an export, why doesn't it show up on a ship or in a container? My brother says calling a holiday an export is just economists playing with words.

**Tutor:** Tell your brother to follow the money instead of the cargo, because trade is defined by payments, not by packaging. An export is any transaction where a foreigner's money flows in to buy something we produced. When a mine sells coal to Rotterdam, euros flow in and coal flows out. When a Canadian couple sleeps in a Langa guesthouse, dollars flow in and — here is the only difference — nothing flows out, because the product they bought is a stay, a view, a guided walk, all produced and consumed on our soil. On the balance of payments, both transactions sit on the same side of the ledger: foreign currency earned. That is why the statistics call tourism an invisible export — invisible because no customs officer stamps a crate, an export because the payment direction is identical. And notice the two advantages the walking version has over the sailing version. The coal can be sold exactly once; the mountain is sold again tomorrow, undepleted. And the coal leaves as one payment to one company, while the couple's week scatters payments across an airline, a guesthouse, a car hire, a farm stall and a game reserve — eight invoices instead of one. So the phrase is not wordplay; it is precise accounting with a good story attached, and using it correctly in a definition question signals that you understand what exports actually are.

**Mandy:** Next: the lesson said tourism jobs go where no factory will ever go. But those same jobs vanished overnight in 2020. Should a village really bet its whole economy on tourists?

**Tutor:** You have put your finger on the deepest trade-off in the topic, so let us weigh it honestly. The case for the bet: a game reserve is an immovable asset — the village beside it holds a natural monopoly no competitor can relocate, and the jobs it creates suit exactly the workers our economy strands: semi-skilled, rural, young, disproportionately women. For many villages the realistic alternative to lodge work is not factory work; it is no work, or a bus ticket to an overcrowded city. The case against: 2020 proved the floor can vanish in a quarter — a shock nobody in the village caused emptied every bed, and seasonality repeats a small version of that shock every winter. So the mature answer is neither embrace nor avoid, but structure the bet. First, diversify within tourism itself: a village serving international safari guests, domestic weekenders, school groups and conference retreats has four demand streams that rarely fail simultaneously — domestic visitors were, notably, the first to return after the pandemic. Second, deepen the local supply chain: a village that grows the food, does the laundry and owns a share of the lodge keeps more of every rand in fat years and holds productive assets — farms, laundries, skills — that survive lean ones. Third, treat tourism income as a ladder, not a hammock: wages and levies that fund training and small enterprise convert a fragile stream into permanent capability. Bet on the reserve, but build the village so it can survive the reserve's worst year — that balanced sentence is the evaluation answer.

**Mandy:** Third one, and it made a few people uncomfortable: is there something wrong with tourists paying to watch traditional dancing? Where's the line between sharing culture and selling it?

**Tutor:** The discomfort is the right instinct, and economics gives it a usable edge. The danger has a name from the lesson: cultural commodification — a tradition rehearsed as a product until the performance replaces the meaning, schedules replace ceremonies, and a community becomes scenery in its own story. But notice the line is not drawn at money changing hands; cultures have always traded their crafts, feasts and music. The line runs through two questions, and they are the two IKS duties. Question one — who owns the telling? When the dancers decide what is performed, what is explained, what stays private and how visitors behave, culture is being shared on its custodians' terms; when an outside operator scripts the show for the bus schedule, the community has become an exhibit. Question two — who banks the earnings? When gate fees, craft sales and guiding wages stay in the community — funding the school, keeping young people home, making the grandmother's knowledge a career her granddaughter wants — tourism is paying culture's keep and strengthening its transmission. When the operator banks the margin and pays performance wages, the community bears the cultural cost while someone else takes the return. So the test for your essay is ownership and benefit, not commerce itself: authenticity plus benefit-sharing turns the uncomfortable question into a well-designed answer — culture performed WITH its people, paid TO its people.

**Mandy:** Last question: for the policy section, can I just write 'the government should promote tourism more'? It feels like that covers everything.

**Tutor:** It covers everything the way a weather forecast of 'weather expected' covers tomorrow — true, empty, and worth almost nothing. Promotion answers earn marks through a mechanism: name the obstacle, name the fix, show how the fix removes the obstacle. Watch the difference. Weak: promote tourism in Asia. Strong: Chinese and Indian visitors face slow paper visas, so extend the e-visa system to those markets, because every week of waiting diverts bookings to rivals with instant approval. Weak: improve safety. Strong: crime perception is the industry's biggest brake, and first impressions form on the airport-to-hotel journey, so put visible policing on tourist routes and secure that first hour — reputation is built exactly there. Weak: fix infrastructure. Strong: a load-shedding video travels the world faster than a marketing campaign, so reliable power in tourist towns IS destination marketing, rand for rand more effective than advertisements it would otherwise cancel out. Weak: train people. Strong: growth will demand chefs, guides and managers, so train them ahead of the demand or watch service quality ration the boom. See the pattern? Each suggestion is an if-then with an obstacle in the if. Build five pairs like that, and close by spreading the map beyond the Cape-Kruger corridor — then your policy section reads like someone who could actually run the department, which is precisely the impression that collects the marks.
