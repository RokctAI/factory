# Part 1 — Expert

The very first lesson of this course defined economics as the management of scarce resources — and the final topic of the syllabus turns that definition on its author. What if the scarcest resource of all is the atmosphere, the rivers, the soil, the living systems that every other resource depends on? None of these passes through a market. None carries a price tag. All belong to everyone and therefore to no one — which is why the same price system that allocates bread and bricks with elegant efficiency allocates clean air catastrophically badly. This session completes the syllabus circuit: why environmental damage is an economic problem in the strictest sense, the state of the environment at home and abroad, the toolkit of measures that make sustainability pay, and the ladder of international agreements from Montreal to Paris and the summits beyond.

## Subtopic: Why Markets Fail the Environment

Anchor the topic on its constitutional definition. SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT, formulated by the Brundtland Commission in 1987, is development that meets the needs of the present WITHOUT compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It rests on three legs — economic, social and environmental — and the analytical move the syllabus wants is to treat nature as CAPITAL: rivers, soil and biodiversity are assets, and a country that grows by consuming them is not earning an income, it is auctioning off its inheritance and calling the proceeds profit.

Why does a free market, so good at so much, wreck the environment? Three distinct failures, and they organise everything that follows.

Failure one: EXTERNALITIES. Picture a tannery discharging into a river. Downstream, the water must be boiled, the fish disappear, the clinic treats stomach illness. Every one of those costs is real and payable in rands — but none appears on the tannery's books, because the people who bear them are not party to any of its transactions. The tannery's PRIVATE cost sits below the true SOCIAL cost, its leather is priced too cheaply, and the market cheerfully produces too much of it. Pollution is not the market misfiring; it is the market working precisely, on prices that are missing a component.

Failure two: PUBLIC GOODS. Clean air and a stable climate are NON-EXCLUDABLE — no one can be fenced out of breathing — and NON-RIVAL — my lungful leaves yours undiminished. Nothing that cannot exclude non-payers can be sold, so no firm will ever supply clean air for profit, and every citizen is tempted to FREE RIDE on the restraint of everyone else.

Failure three: MISSING PROPERTY RIGHTS — the TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS. Consider the abalone beds off the Cape coast: owned by nobody, they are raced to depletion by divers who each reason, correctly, that whatever they spare tonight will be taken by another boat tomorrow. What everybody owns, nobody protects — the same logic that has emptied fishing grounds, stripped communal grazing and treats the atmosphere as a free dumping ground.

Hold the three failures in one hand and the entire policy programme falls out of them: make invisible costs visible, put prices on the unpriced, and give the ownerless either an owner or an enforceable rule.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the Brundtland definition word-perfect, externalities and social cost, public goods and free riding, and the tragedy of the commons.

## Subtopic: The State of the Environment

The syllabus asks for the damage catalogued clearly, globally and locally — evidence, not lament.

CLIMATE CHANGE heads the list. Greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels trap heat, and the planet has warmed more than one degree Celsius since industrialisation, loading the dice toward harsher droughts, floods and storms. South Africa is exposed twice over. Our electricity comes overwhelmingly from coal, making this one of the most carbon-intensive economies on earth — a liability as trading partners begin pricing carbon at their borders. And southern Africa is warming at roughly double the global average, with rain-fed agriculture directly in the line of fire. Cape Town's countdown to Day Zero and the flood damage along the KwaZulu-Natal coast are local receipts for a global bill.

WATER: South Africa receives about half the world's average rainfall, yet its rivers carry acid drainage from old mines, sewage from broken municipal treatment works, and fertiliser runoff that chokes dams with algae. Wetlands — the landscape's kidneys — have been drained, ploughed and paved.

AIR: on the Mpumalanga Highveld, the coal belt breathes air ranked among the world's dirtiest, and the externality has a medical invoice: asthma, lost working days, premature deaths — costs with names and addresses attached.

LAND AND BIODIVERSITY: overgrazing and erosion eat productive soil; forests shrink globally by millions of hectares a year; species vanish at rates ecologists compare to the planet's ancient mass extinctions. Read biodiversity as an asset register — the genetic source code for future medicines and crops, and the living inventory that the tourism industry sells to the world.

WASTE: leaking landfills, plastic riding every river to the sea, and a produce-use-discard economy that treats the earth as simultaneously an endless mine and a bottomless bin.

Now the economic translation, which matters more than the catalogue itself: every entry is future GDP quietly leaking away — harvests that fail, tourists who book elsewhere, workers off sick, cities rebuilt after floods. Environmental damage is not a competing priority to growth; left unmanaged, it is a compounding tax on all future growth.

Stop for this section's questions now — the climate mechanism and South Africa's double exposure, the water and air specifics, biodiversity as capital, and damage read as future GDP lost.

## Subtopic: Making Sustainability Pay: the Policy Toolkit

Government's instruments split into command and market, and strong answers know both families with examples.

COMMAND AND CONTROL is direct regulation: emission limits for smokestacks and exhausts, zoning that keeps factories off wetlands, environmental impact assessments before any mine or mall breaks ground, protected areas, and quotas — fishing quotas, water-use licences, hunting permits. Its strengths are certainty and enforceability where the danger is acute: when a pollutant kills, you ban it, you do not tax it. Its weaknesses: inspectors cost money, a fixed standard gives no reward for beating the minimum, and one uniform rule ignores that compliance is cheap for some firms and crippling for others.

MARKET-BASED MEASURES write the missing price tag so the polluter pays. ENVIRONMENTAL TAXES lead the family: South Africa's CARBON TAX — in force since 2019, the first of its kind in Africa — prices every ton of carbon dioxide emitted; the plastic-bag levy and the tyre levy apply the same logic at the till. Once the social cost sits inside the price, every firm saves money by polluting less, and the incentive works every hour of every day, not only when the inspector visits. SUBSIDIES reverse the sign — rebates for solar water heaters, and the renewable procurement programme whose bidding rounds have planted wind farms and solar fields across the Northern Cape and Eastern Cape. TRADABLE PERMITS cap total pollution and let firms buy and sell the allowances beneath the cap: the cap secures the environmental outcome, the trading finds the cheapest reductions, and Europe's emissions trading scheme is the standing example. And where feasible, ASSIGNING PROPERTY RIGHTS cures the commons directly: hand a community legal ownership of its wildlife or its stretch of river, and the asset acquires a defender — the economics behind community conservancies on the borders of the great parks, where an animal that funds the village clinic is an animal the village guards.

Complete the toolkit with the supporting rank: education and information — recycling habits, eco-labels that let shoppers vote with their trolleys — and the state cleaning its own yard: green procurement, efficient public buildings, and restoration programmes like Working for Water, which hires unemployed workers to clear thirsty alien vegetation from catchments, purchasing water security and wages with a single budget line.

Carry the mixing rule as your evaluation sentence: commands set the floor, prices move the average — ban what kills, tax what harms, cap-and-trade what must be capped, and give owners to whatever can be owned.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — command tools with examples, the carbon tax and the levies, permits with their cap-plus-trade logic, property rights as the commons cure, and the mixing rule.

## Subtopic: The World's Promises: International Agreements

The atmosphere ignores borders, so one nation's virtue cannot secure it — hence the treaty ladder, and the syllabus wants it rung by rung.

MONTREAL, 1987: the existence proof. Faced with CFCs shredding the ozone layer, the world agreed to phase them out; chemists found substitutes, the phase-out held, and the ozone layer is measurably healing. It remains the most successful environmental treaty ever signed — produce it whenever someone claims global cooperation is a fantasy.

RIO DE JANEIRO, 1992 — the Earth Summit: sustainability's founding convention. Three instruments came out of it: Agenda 21, the worldwide action plan for sustainable development; the Convention on Biological Diversity; and the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, the UNFCCC — the parent treaty under which every later climate negotiation happens. Its organising principle: COMMON BUT DIFFERENTIATED RESPONSIBILITIES — every nation must act, but those who built their wealth on carbon must carry the heavier end.

KYOTO, 1997: the first attempt at binding numbers — quantified emission cuts, but imposed only on developed countries. The United States never ratified, the fast-industrialising giants carried no targets, and the design taught its own lesson: binding-for-some cannot bend a global curve.

JOHANNESBURG, 2002: the World Summit on Sustainable Development, hosted on South African soil, which pulled implementation into focus and tied environment to poverty, water and energy access.

PARIS, 2015: the current architecture. Nearly every country on earth submits NATIONALLY DETERMINED CONTRIBUTIONS — emission targets it sets itself and must ratchet tighter over time — in pursuit of holding warming well below two degrees and chasing one and a half. Where Kyoto was binding but partial, Paris is universal but voluntary — one design choice that is simultaneously its strength and its weakness, and saying both is the evaluation answer.

The annual COPs have kept adding rungs. Glasgow, 2021, secured the first explicit agreement to phase DOWN coal and completed the carbon-market rulebook. Sharm el-Sheikh, 2022, established the LOSS AND DAMAGE fund to compensate vulnerable countries for harm already unavoidable. Dubai, 2023, delivered the first global stocktake, calling on the world to transition away from fossil fuels. And South Africa stands inside the newest chapter personally: the JUST ENERGY TRANSITION PARTNERSHIP, unveiled at Glasgow, under which wealthy nations pledged 8,5 billion dollars to help this coal-fired economy decarbonise without discarding its coal workers and coal towns — the phrase JUST TRANSITION carrying sustainability's social leg straight into energy policy.

The final questions of this part are with you now: Montreal's proof, Rio's three offspring, Kyoto against Paris as designs, the recent COP additions, and South Africa's JETP.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again from the bank of a single river — because every idea in it lives in one village with an upstream and a downstream.

## Subtopic: The River Nobody Owns

A brick kiln operates upstream of a village. Its waste pipe empties into the river and pays nothing for the privilege. Downstream, mothers boil drinking water, the fish traps come up empty, and the clinic's queue lengthens every month. Open the kiln's account books and the business looks superb — because its largest production cost is being settled by families who appear nowhere in those books. Economists call that hidden invoice an EXTERNALITY: a real cost, payable in rands — firewood for boiling, lost catches, clinic fees — that lands outside the polluter's fence.

Why is the river treated in a way no one would treat their own vegetable garden? Because the garden has an owner and the river does not. Your garden's owner defends it, fences it, sues for it. The river belongs to everybody — which in practice means nobody can send the bill. And whatever everybody owns, nobody protects. Watch the two fishermen at the deep pool: each knows that any fish he spares tonight will sit in the other man's trap by morning, so both men fish the pool to the last fin. The TRAGEDY OF THE COMMONS, complete in one evening — and the same single sentence explains stripped fishing grounds, vanished forests and a heating sky: the price tag is missing.

Clean air plays an even slyer trick. Suppose the village collects money to move the kiln's chimney downwind of everyone. The air clears — for every household, including the three that refused to pay. Fresh air cannot be fenced off for paying customers only, so each household waits for the others to pay, and the collection never fills. That waiting game is FREE RIDING, and it is why clean environments never simply appear: there is no way to sell one.

So carry the diagnosis in a single line: the market damages the environment not because people are wicked, but because the damage is priced at zero. Everything still to come in this lesson is one repair, attempted four ways — put the missing price back.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the river are coming to you right now: the externality in rands, the ownerless river, and the free rider who sinks the collection.

## Subtopic: Sending the River a Bill

If the disease is a missing price, the cures all write price tags. Four tags, each with its village version.

Tag one: the RULE. The village council bans dumping outright, sets fines, and sends an inspector to the pipe. That is command and control — the emission standard, the licence, the protected wetland. Use it where the poison is deadly and certainty is everything. Its flaw: the inspector must keep returning, and a kiln that could halve its waste cheaply has no reason to do one drop better than the rule demands.

Tag two: the TAX. Charge the kiln for every barrel it releases. Overnight, the economics of the pipe reverse: filtration equipment stops being an expense and becomes an investment, because every barrel not dumped is money saved. No inspector needed — the kiln's own bookkeeper now polices the pipe, every day, forever. That is the carbon tax on every ton of emissions, and the plastic-bag levy whispering at every till: pollute less, pay less.

Tag three: the REWARD. Pay for the behaviour you want more of: rebates for the household that fits a solar water heater, contracts for the wind farms and solar fields now standing across the Northern Cape. The tax pushes the old habit; the subsidy pulls the new one.

Tag four: the OWNER. Give the commons a keeper with title deeds. Hand the community legal ownership of the riverbank — or of the wildlife on its land — together with the right to charge visitors and harvest sustainably. The arithmetic of poaching flips: an antelope that belongs to nobody is a carcass waiting to happen; an antelope whose gate fees pay the village school is patrolled by everyone who has a child. That is the economics of community conservancies beside the great parks — and of Working for Water, which pays local teams to clear the alien trees drinking the catchment: the village becomes the river's salaried bodyguard.

The strongest answers mix the tags deliberately: a hard rule where lives are at stake, a tax to keep every firm improving forever, rewards to accelerate the new technology, and owners wherever the commons can carry a title deed.

Your questions for this part are up now: the four price tags matched to their examples, why the tax polices itself, and the antelope that pays the school fees.

## Subtopic: Promises Between Countries

Final stretch: zoom out until the river becomes the sky. The atmosphere is the ultimate shared river — every nation's pipe drains into it — so the village meeting must seat nearly two hundred countries, each tempted to let the others pay for the clean-up. It is the free-rider problem at planetary scale.

Yet the meeting has succeeded before, completely. Montreal, 1987: the world agreed to stop producing the chemicals tearing open the ozone layer, substitutes were engineered, and the layer is healing above us right now. Keep that treaty loaded for any question sneering that nations never cooperate.

Climate needed more attempts. Rio in 1992 wrote the family constitution: everyone must act, and the countries that got rich burning carbon must shoulder more — common but differentiated responsibilities. Kyoto in 1997 handed strict homework to the rich alone; the biggest emitter never signed, the rising giants had no assignments, and the global curve barely noticed. Paris in 2015 inverted the design: every country writes its OWN promise, publishes it for the world to see, and must strengthen it every five years. Everybody is inside the tent; nobody is compelled — name that trade-off from both sides and the evaluation marks are yours.

The yearly summits since have added three developments worth citing by name: an agreement to phase down coal, a loss-and-damage fund paying countries already suffering harm they did little to cause, and a first global stocktake telling humanity, in writing, to move away from fossil fuels.

And South Africa is the case study the world is watching. A coal-powered country, offered 8,5 billion dollars by richer nations to rewire its energy system — with the condition stitched into the partnership's name: the transition must be JUST. Retrain the coal miner, bring new industry to the coal town, keep the lights on while the grid turns green. Which is the Brundtland definition come home to roost: meet today's needs — jobs, power, growth — without spending the inheritance of the learners who will write exams after you.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: Montreal's lesson, Kyoto against Paris in one sentence each, the newest additions from the summits, and the just transition explained to a coal town. Hold onto the river and its bill — this topic closes the course by finally pricing what the first lesson called scarce.
