# Part 1 — Expert

Follow one ton of South African chrome ore. It leaves Steelpoort on a truck, sails from Richards Bay for a modest price, and returns months later inside imported stainless-steel appliances costing many multiples of what the ore earned. The distance between those two invoices — between digging and making — is the entire subject of this session. INDUSTRIAL development is government's deliberate campaign to grow manufacturing so the making happens here; REGIONAL development is its twin campaign to spread that making across the map, instead of letting it pile up in Gauteng, eThekwini and Cape Town while the rest of the country ships out its young people. The syllabus wants South Africa's actual named strategies — and, at grade twelve level, the harder skill of judging whether they work.

## Subtopic: Why Countries Industrialise

INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT means growing the secondary sector — manufacturing, construction, processing — relative to merely extracting and exporting raw materials. Why should a government push this deliberately instead of trusting the market? Four arguments build the essay.

Argument one: VALUE ADDED. Every stage of processing multiplies the worth of the original material — chrome ore becomes ferrochrome, ferrochrome becomes stainless steel, stainless steel becomes surgical instruments. Whichever country performs a stage keeps that stage's wages, profits and taxes. Processing raw materials at home before export is called BENEFICIATION, and it is the declared ambition of South African policy: export the instrument tray, not the ore.

Argument two: EMPLOYMENT. Factories absorb semi-skilled workers at a scale that modern mechanised mining cannot and high-skill finance will not. For a country with mass unemployment, manufacturing is the historical ladder that carried farm workers into steady wage work everywhere industrialisation has succeeded.

Argument three: STABILITY. A commodity exporter lives strapped to the roller-coaster of world prices — flush when platinum and coal boom, gutted when they slump. A diversified factory base spreads national income across many products and many markets, smoothing the ride.

Argument four: LINKAGES. A factory is never alone. BACKWARD linkages reach to its suppliers — component makers, toolrooms, transporters, packaging plants; FORWARD linkages reach to the firms that use and sell its output. A single vehicle plant anchors a web of hundreds of businesses, which is why manufacturing is called a growth engine rather than merely a sector.

Now fix the counterweight in place, because evaluation cuts both ways. Industrial support is never free: protecting an infant industry taxes consumers through higher prices, subsidies consume scarce public money, and the historical record is full of infants that never grew up because the support never ended. Every strategy in this lesson must eventually answer one question — is the industry becoming competitive, or merely comfortable?

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: beneficiation and value added, the employment and stability arguments, linkages in both directions, and the infant-industry caution.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Industrial Strategies

The syllabus expects strategies by name and date, so anchor these firmly.

The umbrella is the NATIONAL INDUSTRIAL POLICY FRAMEWORK of 2007, implemented through successive INDUSTRIAL POLICY ACTION PLANS — the IPAPs — maintained by the Department of Trade, Industry and Competition. The IPAPs select priority sectors — automotives, clothing and textiles, agro-processing, metals, chemicals, green industries — and aim support at them rather than spraying protection everywhere.

Know the flagship instruments individually. The AUTOMOTIVE PRODUCTION AND DEVELOPMENT PROGRAMME — the APDP — rewards manufacturers for building and investing locally, through import-duty credits earned by production and allowances earned by investment; it is the reason seven global carmakers assemble vehicles here and vehicles stand among the country's leading manufactured exports. The CLOTHING AND TEXTILES COMPETITIVENESS PROGRAMME funds upgrading in a labour-intensive industry hammered by cheap imports. Section 12I tax allowances reward large greenfield and expansion investments in industry. The BLACK INDUSTRIALISTS SCHEME finances black-owned manufacturers, welding industrial policy to transformation. And patient state capital flows through the INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT CORPORATION — the IDC — which backs industrial projects commercial banks judge too slow or too risky.

Add the demand-side instrument: LOCALISATION. Through designated public procurement, state entities are required to buy specified products — buses, rail stock, cables, uniforms — from local factories. The state uses its own enormous shopping list to guarantee manufacturers a market.

Above everything sits the NATIONAL DEVELOPMENT PLAN'S goal of lifting manufacturing's share of output and jobs, with small-business support and incubation programmes underneath — because today's global component supplier began as somebody's workshop.

For balance, name the headwinds honestly: electricity that fails and costs more each year, congested ports and unreliable rail, scarce technical skills, and Asian competitors with decades of scale. A strategy that reads beautifully on paper still has to survive a load-shedding schedule — placed correctly, that sentence earns evaluation marks.

Stop for this section's questions now — the NIPF and IPAPs, the APDP and the clothing programme, the IDC and the Black Industrialists Scheme, localisation through procurement, and the headwinds.

## Subtopic: Regional Development: Spreading the Map

Industry clumps. Left to itself, a firm settles where suppliers, customers, ports and engineers already are — which is why Gauteng, on roughly two percent of the land, generates about a third of national output. REGIONAL DEVELOPMENT is the state's deliberate counter-pull: policies that steer economic activity toward neglected regions, partly to unlock their resources, partly to carry work to where people live rather than forcing every work-seeker into overcrowded metros.

History gives South Africa a scarred version of this policy. Apartheid's decentralisation programme paid industries to relocate to homeland border areas — and it mostly failed, because no subsidy could compensate for missing infrastructure, missing markets and missing skills. When the payments stopped, the factories died. That failure is the standard cautionary tale: location incentives without genuine economic foundations purchase temporary factories, never development.

Democratic South Africa redesigned the instruments. First, SPATIAL DEVELOPMENT INITIATIVES — SDIs — concentrate public investment along corridors with real economic potential. The flagship is the MAPUTO DEVELOPMENT CORRIDOR: the N4 toll road, rail and border-post upgrades linking Gauteng's industrial heartland to Maputo's harbour, financed through public-private partnership — giving Mpumalanga's steel, petrochemicals and citrus a port hundreds of kilometres closer than Durban.

Second, INDUSTRIAL DEVELOPMENT ZONES, converted by the SEZ Act of 2014 into SPECIAL ECONOMIC ZONES. An SEZ is a purpose-built, serviced industrial precinct tied to a port or airport, offering hard infrastructure plus a sweetener package: corporate tax at 15 percent instead of the standard rate, building allowances, employment tax incentives, and duty-free import of inputs for export production. Learn the names and anchors: COEGA outside Gqeberha with the deep-water port of Ngqura — the largest; EAST LONDON, wrapped around vehicle assembly; RICHARDS BAY, minerals and metals; DUBE TRADEPORT beside King Shaka International, flying out perishables and electronics; SALDANHA BAY, oil-and-gas services; and Tshwane's automotive SEZ feeding the Rosslyn plants.

Third, the modest rank: industrial parks and township and rural enterprise programmes — reviving the old decentralisation-era estates and pulling light industry toward the deepest unemployment.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — why industry clumps and why the state counter-pulls, the apartheid decentralisation lesson, the Maputo Corridor, and the SEZ package with named zones.

## Subtopic: Judging the Strategies

The grade-twelve instruction is EVALUATE: are South Africa's strategies appropriate? Judgement needs criteria, so build the answer on four benchmarks.

Benchmark one: international best practice. The countries that industrialised successfully — South Korea, Malaysia, lately Vietnam — followed a recognisable recipe: support tied to EXPORT PERFORMANCE, so firms must prove themselves against world competition; support that is TEMPORARY and withdrawn from failures; massive parallel investment in skills and infrastructure; and a stable macroeconomy underneath. Score South Africa against that recipe. The APDP passes the export test — carmakers earn their credits by producing for world markets — and it is consistently the strategy assessors rate highest. Open-ended protection of uncompetitive sectors rates lowest.

Benchmark two: results. Interrogate each strategy with the outcome questions. Has manufacturing's share of GDP risen? No — it has slid over two decades from above 19 percent toward 12 to 13 percent: deindustrialisation, the single most damning aggregate fact in the topic. Have the SEZs drawn private money beyond the state's own spending? Unevenly — Coega and Dube TradePort hold real tenants, jobs and exports; several other zones remain serviced land waiting for occupants. Has employment grown? Vehicle assembly holds its own; clothing employment collapsed under import pressure despite dedicated support. A balanced verdict names successes and failures specifically.

Benchmark three: binding constraints. The best-designed incentive cannot outweigh power cuts, rail that loses freight to trucks, ports where ships queue, and skills that emigrate. Appropriateness is therefore partly a question of sequencing — repairing electricity, transport and training may matter more than any new subsidy. That is the strongest single evaluation point available in this topic.

Benchmark four: cost and governance. Incentives spend public money, so ask the price per sustainable job. A zone that needs permanent subsidy is the homeland mistake wearing modern branding. And clean procurement, policy certainty and coordination are themselves location factors: investors price political risk exactly as they price freight.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the best-practice recipe, the deindustrialisation fact, named successes and failures, the binding-constraints argument, and cost per job as a criterion.

## Subtopic: The Bakkie and the Bag of Ore

Stand on the dock and watch two exports leave. First, a bag of iron ore: the country banks a few hundred rand and the wages of the digging — that is the whole story. Second, a bakkie: the country banks hundreds of thousands of rand, and folded inside that price are the wages of the furnace operator, the presser, the welder, the seat-stitcher, the electrician, the sprayer and the driver who delivered it — plus the profits and taxes of every firm along the chain. Same underlying metal. The difference is which country did the MAKING.

That picture is the entire case for industrial development. Each making step — ore into steel, steel into parts, parts into bakkie — is ADDING VALUE, and doing those steps at home before exporting is BENEFICIATION. A country that only digs is selling the cheapest link of the chain and buying back the most expensive one, forever.

And no factory stands alone. Behind an assembly plant queue the firms that feed it — tyres, glass, wiring harnesses, pressed steel, logistics. In front of it stand the dealers, the mechanics, the parts counters, the finance houses. Hundreds of businesses hang from one production line — which is why a town that loses its plant loses far more than one employer, and a town that wins one wins an ecosystem.

So the state leans on the scale: production rewards for carmakers, tax allowances for big industrial investments, patient loans from the state's industrial financier, and a rule that government's own fleet of buses, trains and uniforms must be bought locally. But keep the warning taped to all of it: help must come with a finish line. An industry that can only live behind protection is a permanent tax on every consumer, collected in higher prices. Support the infant to grow up — never to stay an infant.

## Subtopic: Why the Factory Went to Coega

Now the map question: why is the factory never in YOUR town? Because factories follow factories. The suppliers sit in Gauteng, the port is in Durban, the toolmakers are where the other toolmakers are — so money keeps piling up in three city-regions, and everywhere else exports its school-leavers to them by bus.

Government's counter-move: build the missing foundations in a chosen place, then sweeten the arithmetic. Watch it at Coega, outside Gqeberha. The state built a deep-water port — Ngqura — and a fenced, fully serviced industrial precinct: roads laid, power reticulated, water connected. Then it makes investors an offer: locate here and pay company tax at 15 percent instead of the standard rate, claim building allowances, claim a tax break for hiring young workers, and import your inputs duty-free provided you export what you make. That bundle is a special economic zone — and versions of it anchor vehicle assembly at East London, fly perishables and electronics out of Dube TradePort beside King Shaka, and serve minerals at Richards Bay.

Sometimes the sweetener is a road rather than a fence. The Maputo Corridor rebuilt the N4 from Gauteng through Mpumalanga to Mozambique's harbour, and suddenly steel, petrochemicals and citrus had a shorter route to a ship — while farms, depots and border towns along the whole line woke up. A corridor lifts a string of towns; a zone lifts a point.

But keep history's warning lamp on. Apartheid paid factories to move to the homelands, far from customers, suppliers and skills. Subsidies flowed; factories stood; subsidies stopped; factories died. The lesson in one line: you can pay a factory to ARRIVE anywhere, but it only STAYS where the business actually works. Zones and corridors succeed where they fix real foundations — port, power, transport, training — and fail where they are a fence and a discount in an empty field.

## Subtopic: Marking the Government's Homework

When the instruction says EVALUATE, answer like the person holding the memo — four moves, in order.

Move one: state the test before the verdict. Worldwide, industrial policy succeeds when support is tied to exporting, is temporary, and stands on working infrastructure and skills. Open with that checklist and every later sentence has something to lean on.

Move two: credit the wins by name. The automotive programme earns its keep — global brands assemble here because the rewards are earned by producing and exporting, and vehicles rank among the country's top manufactured exports. Coega and Dube TradePort hold genuine tenants, jobs and cargo. The Maputo Corridor carries genuine freight. Never claim everything failed; the person marking knows otherwise.

Move three: name the failures with the same steadiness. Manufacturing's slice of the economy has shrunk for twenty years — from about a fifth toward an eighth — so the aggregate scoreboard reads deindustrialisation. Clothing employment collapsed despite a dedicated programme. Some zones are serviced land with too few tenants: public money went in, private money declined the invitation. Never claim everything worked, either.

Move four: point at the real bottleneck, because the top marks live here. No incentive package outbids a power cut. Factories stay where electricity flows, trucks move, ships load and technicians can be hired — so the most appropriate industrial policy right now may be repairing electricity, rail, ports and training, the foundations every factory in every province needs at once, before inventing new sweeteners.

Then close with a one-sentence verdict: the direction is defensible and the flagships deliver, but the strategy underachieves while the foundations crumble — fix the constraints, and the incentives can finally bite.
