**Thandi:** Class questions coming in! First: if ceilings cause shortages and black markets, why would any government ever use one instead of just paying a subsidy?

**Tutor:** Because the two tools ask for different things the government may not have: money and time. A subsidy is the economist's favourite — it moves the supply curve rather than fighting the crossing point, so it delivers cheapness WITHOUT a shortage — but every rand of it must come out of the budget, and a treasury in crisis may simply not have the rands. A ceiling costs the state almost nothing on paper: pass the regulation, print the notice. In a sudden emergency — a war, a failed harvest, a currency collapse — a ceiling can also be imposed overnight, while building a subsidy system takes months. So ceilings are the tool of empty treasuries and urgent moments, and the shortage is the hidden invoice: the state pays not in money but in queues, black markets and quality decay. The three-question habit exposes exactly that — what it was for, what the diagram predicts, and what else follows — and lets you compare the visible cost of the subsidy against the hidden costs of the cap.

**Thandi:** Next one: with the sugary drinks levy, who really ends up paying it — the cooldrink companies or us buying at the tuck shop?

**Tutor:** Both of you, and the diagram even tells you roughly in what proportions. The levy shifts supply leftward; the shelf price rises, but by LESS than the full levy — so the buyer pays some of it in the higher price, and the producer swallows the rest as a thinner margin. What decides the split is how each side can dodge. Buyers of sugary drinks dodge easily: water, juice, cheaper brands — so push the price too hard and they walk, which forces producers to absorb more. That is also why the same levy on something buyers cannot dodge — like fuel for a commuter with no alternative — lands mostly on the buyer. And notice the policy's clever twist: with a health levy, the dodging IS the goal. Every buyer who switches to water is not an escape from the tax; it is the tax succeeding. Revenue raised on the litres still sold, health improved on the litres abandoned — one instrument, two victories.

**Thandi:** Third question, about the floor: if the state ends up owning a warehouse full of potatoes it must sell at a loss, hasn't the policy simply failed? Why keep floors at all?

**Tutor:** Judge it by what it was FOR, not only by what it costs — that is question one of the habit. The floor's purpose is to keep growers growing. Food production has a rhythm no market can hurry: if low prices bankrupt farms this season, next season's planting simply does not happen, and a country can find itself importing staples at world prices with a weak rand — a far more expensive disaster than a full warehouse. The floor is effectively an insurance premium: society pays the storage bills and the export losses in exchange for a farming sector that survives bad years. Whether the premium is worth it is a real debate — floors can also fatten large agribusinesses that never needed rescue, and the money has an opportunity cost in clinics and classrooms. So the answer is not "floors good" or "floors failed"; it is: the surplus is the known, budgeted cost of a survival guarantee, and the honest argument is about whether the guarantee is priced fairly.

**Thandi:** Last one, the supper-table classic: my aunt says the minimum wage rescued her family; my neighbour says it closed his car wash. The lesson says both can be right — but then how does the country ever DECIDE?

**Tutor:** By counting instead of shouting — which is precisely what separates economics from a family argument. Your aunt and your neighbour are both single data points, each perfectly real; policy needs the whole distribution. So researchers track what actually happens when the floor is set or raised: employment in low-wage sectors before and after, poverty rates among the employed, how many jobs slip into the informal shadows, whether small firms close or adapt. The evidence then feeds the design, because a wage floor is not a yes-or-no switch — its LEVEL can be set with the data, raised gently and watched; enforcement can be phased for small employers; grants and public employment can catch those the floor prices out. That is the mature version of the whole topic: the diagram warns you where to look, the evidence tells you how much, and the design answers both your aunt and your neighbour at once. Decide with the three questions and the numbers — never with the loudest voice at the table.
