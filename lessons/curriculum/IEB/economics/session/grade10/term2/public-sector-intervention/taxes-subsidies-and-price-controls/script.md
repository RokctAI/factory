# Part 1 — Expert

Markets form prices, allocate, inform, ration and connect — and yet society regularly overrules them. PUBLIC SECTOR INTERVENTION is the state stepping into markets on purpose: taxing some goods dearer, subsidising others cheaper, capping certain prices, propping up others, and setting a legal floor under the price of labour itself. This session defines each instrument, traces its consequences honestly on the market diagram — side effects included — and covers the welfare tools that bypass the market entirely. One discipline runs through everything: state what was intended, what the diagram predicts, and what else follows.

## Subtopic: Why the State Steps In — and With What

The market's rationing rule — whoever is willing and able to pay — sometimes produces outcomes society refuses to accept: staple foods priced beyond the poorest households. And markets misfire in well-documented ways: externalities go uncharged — the factory's smoke appears on nobody's invoice; harmful goods are overconsumed; merit goods like education are underconsumed; and incomes can be so unequal that the FOR WHOM question gets a brutal answer. Intervention is the public sector's reply, and Grade 10 studies its instruments in three families.

INDIRECT TAXES: taxes levied on goods and services rather than directly on incomes — VAT on most purchases, excise duties on alcohol and tobacco, the fuel levy, and the levy on sugary drinks. They raise revenue AND steer behaviour deliberately: taxing a good makes it dearer, and dearer means less consumed — which is exactly the intention with tobacco and sugar. SUBSIDIES: taxation running in reverse — the state pays part of the cost of producing or providing a good so that it reaches consumers cheaper than the market alone would manage: subsidised commuter transport, support for emerging farmers, the funded allocation of free basic water and electricity. WELFARE: provision and transfers that sidestep prices altogether — social grants placing buying power directly into poor households, public clinics and no-fee schools delivering services free at the point of use.

And the fourth family, the centrepiece of today's diagrams: PRICE CONTROLS — the state legislating the price itself. Maximum prices to shield buyers, minimum prices to shield sellers, and the national minimum wage to shield workers. Each is a direct overrule of the market's crossing point — which is precisely why each carries consequences the diagram will expose.

## Subtopic: Taxes and Subsidies on the Diagram

An indirect tax operates through the SUPPLY curve. The tax is an amount sellers must pay over to the state on every unit — so at any quantity, sellers now require a higher price before offering what they offered before. The supply curve shifts LEFTWARD, or equivalently upward by the size of the tax. Read the new crossing: equilibrium price HIGHER, equilibrium quantity LOWER. And note the sharing, because strong answers always do: the price typically rises by LESS than the full tax. Sellers absorb part of it and buyers pay the rest — the burden is split between the two sides of the counter. For the sugary drinks levy, the fall in quantity IS the policy's goal: fewer litres of sweetened cooldrink consumed. And the same mechanism explains zero-rating: staples like brown bread and maize meal are spared VAT precisely so this price-raising machinery never touches the poorest households' basket.

A subsidy is the exact mirror. The state pays producers per unit, cutting their effective cost — supply shifts RIGHTWARD: equilibrium price LOWER, quantity HIGHER. Commuters ride subsidised trains cheaply, and more of them ride; supported farmers plant more, and food reaches the shelves cheaper than it otherwise would. State the honest costs too: every subsidy is funded by taxation somewhere else — opportunity cost never sleeps; and both instruments can misfire — set a tax too high and smuggling and illicit trade bloom; pay a subsidy carelessly and producers may pocket the margin instead of passing it on.

Diagram discipline, always: taxes and subsidies SHIFT the supply curve — they do not simply relabel the price. Draw the shift, mark both equilibria, state the burden split.

Stop for this section's questions now — tax and subsidy on the diagram: direction of shift, both new equilibrium values, the sharing of the burden, and one honest cost of each.

## Subtopic: Maximum Prices — the Ceiling

A MAXIMUM PRICE — a price ceiling — is a legislated price ABOVE which a good may not be sold, set BELOW equilibrium so that an essential stays affordable: cooking oil capped during a supply crisis, rent capped in a housing squeeze, paraffin protected in winter.

Follow the diagram's sequence. At the ceiling price, quantity demanded is large — the low price invites everyone in — but quantity supplied is SMALL: producing no longer pays properly. Demanded exceeds supplied: a SHORTAGE, and a permanent one for as long as the ceiling binds, because the one force that could clear it — a rising price — is now against the law. The market's rationing function has been switched off deliberately, so something else must ration instead: queues form, and first-come-first-served replaces willingness to pay; sellers ration informally — regular customers favoured, two bottles per family; and the pressure leaks into the BLACK MARKET, where illegal sales happen above the ceiling and the desperate pay more than the free market would ever have asked. Quality often sags too: sellers forbidden from raising prices quietly cut corners instead.

Close with the honest verdict a complete answer requires: the ceiling delivers cheap cooking oil to those who actually GET cooking oil — and it manufactures a shortage that decides who gets it by luck, queueing or connections. The intervention succeeds at its price goal and fails at its quantity goal. That trade-off is why states often prefer subsidies or direct grants — which move curves or incomes — over ceilings, which wrestle the crossing point directly.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the ceiling placed below equilibrium, the shortage mechanics, and where the black market comes from.

## Subtopic: Minimum Prices and the Minimum Wage — the Floor

Now the mirror image: a MINIMUM PRICE — a floor — a legislated price BELOW which a good may not be sold, set ABOVE equilibrium to protect SELLERS' incomes. Historically it is farm produce that gets floors: a guaranteed price meant to keep growers on the land.

The diagram mirrors too. At the floor, quantity supplied is generous — the rewarding price pulls production in — but quantity demanded shrinks back. Supplied exceeds demanded: a SURPLUS. Stock piles up unsold, and the price is legally barred from falling to clear it — so the state usually ends up BUYING the surplus, storing it in silos and warehouses, exporting it at a loss, or watching it spoil. The policy's bill lands squarely in the national budget.

Then the floor South Africans debate at every supper table: the MINIMUM WAGE — a price floor in the LABOUR market, where households sell labour and firms buy it. Set above the market wage, the diagram's mechanical prediction reads: more people offer themselves for work — the better wage attracts them — while firms hire fewer — the higher cost deters them. A surplus of labour: pressure toward unemployment. State that prediction plainly; it is the diagram's honest output. But a full answer argues both sides. FOR the floor: an unregulated wage for unskilled work can settle below what survival costs — the floor defends dignity, cuts working poverty, and the extra wages flow straight back into township tills and taxi ranks through the circular flow; steadier, better-paid workers can also be more productive workers. AGAINST the floor: the workers priced out first are usually the youngest and least skilled — the very group the policy hoped to lift — and some employment slips into the informal shadows beyond any protection. The mature conclusion: a genuine trade-off between protecting the employed and risking the excluded, whose actual size is an empirical question — measured in the real world, country by country, not settled by the diagram alone.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the floor's mechanics, the state's surplus problem, and the minimum wage argued honestly in both directions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now every instrument again, from the street where you already know them — the price on a cooldrink, the train ticket that costs less than it should, the stories of capped prices and empty shelves, and the wage debate that divides families.

## Subtopic: The Referee Enters the Game

Picture the market as a weekend soccer match: buyers and sellers playing, the score changing by itself, no adult interference — and most Saturdays, the game runs fine. But some Saturdays the crowd starts shouting for the referee. When the play injures people — smoke drifting over the fence, bread priced past the pensioners — the STATE walks onto the pitch. Not to kick the ball; to change the rules of play.

This referee carries four calls. Call one: make it DEARER — load a tax onto the harmful thing. Every sugary drink now carries a levy; the shelf price climbs; some buyers switch to water. One call, two results: money raised AND behaviour nudged. Call two: make it CHEAPER — the state quietly pays part of the cost of the good thing. Your train ticket doesn't cover what your trip really costs; the difference is paid from the budget so that workers can afford to reach work. Call three: freeze the SCORE itself — declare a legal maximum nobody may exceed, the ceiling, or a legal minimum nobody may undercut, the floor. Call four: abandon the match entirely and hand the ball over — grants paid straight into households, clinics that charge nothing at the door, schools with no fees. No price, no market; the need is simply met.

Why blow the whistle at all? Because the game's natural rule — whoever can pay, plays — is a rule society sometimes refuses to apply to bread, to medicine, or to the wage a worker must live on.

## Subtopic: Ceilings Make Queues, Floors Make Piles

The two score-freezing calls have famous side effects, and one picture will hold them in your memory for good.

The CEILING: the state presses the price DOWN below its resting point — cooking oil capped cheap during a shortage crisis. Shoppers cheer and pour in; but watch the producers: at the capped price, pressing and bottling oil barely pays, so they produce LESS. Many wanting, few supplying: the shelves go bare. And here is the cruel mechanism: the price is not ALLOWED to rise and call more oil in — that is now illegal. So who gets the cheap oil? Whoever queued from dawn. Whoever knows the shop owner. And behind the shop, someone sells bottles quietly at DOUBLE — the black market, born in the space between what the law permits and what the shelves hold. Cheap oil for some, no oil for many, dear oil in the shadows. A ceiling makes QUEUES.

The FLOOR: the state holds the price UP above its resting point — say a guaranteed potato price to keep growers farming. Growers cheer and plant every field; but buyers at the dear price take fewer bags. Much offered, little bought: the warehouse fills. And the price may not drop to empty it — illegal again. So the state buys the mountain of potatoes itself, pays for storage, sells abroad at a loss, or watches them rot — and the account arrives at the treasury. A floor makes PILES.

One memory trick locks it in: a ceiling is pressed down LOW over the market — and under a low ceiling, people wait in lines outside; a floor is jacked up HIGH — and on a high floor, unsold stock stands stacked. Queues under ceilings; piles on floors.

Your questions for this part are up now: ceiling or floor, queue or pile — and the back door where the shadow market trades.

## Subtopic: The Wage Floor at the Factory Gate

Now the floor that starts arguments in every household: the minimum wage — a floor under the price of WORK.

Stand at the packhouse gate before sunrise. With no rule at all, the wage for unskilled work settles where the crowd of work-seekers meets the firm's hiring plans — and in a country where the crowd is enormous, that resting wage can drop below what rent and food actually cost. So the state rules: below this line, no one may be paid.

Hold both truths at once, because both are true. Truth one: for every worker employed at the floor, life genuinely changes — school shoes bought, rent covered, dignity restored — and those wages are spent at the spaza, the salon and the rank, feeding the whole neighbourhood's circular flow. Truth two: at the costlier wage, the packhouse mechanises the sorting line or hires fewer hands — the crowd at the gate grows — and the first ones squeezed out are the youngest and least experienced, exactly the people the floor was built to protect. Some work also slides off the books entirely: jobs below the line, in the shadows, with no protections at all.

So who wins the supper-table argument — the uncle whose family the wage rescued, or the neighbour who says it shut the small firms? Quite possibly BOTH, about different people — and saying so is not fence-sitting; it is precision. The genuine question is the balance: how many protected against how many priced out, and that is measured year by year in the real world, not shouted into settlement. The diagram gives you the SHAPE of the trade-off; only evidence gives you its SIZE.

And that is the whole topic folded into one habit: for every intervention, ask three questions — what was it FOR, what does the diagram predict, and what ELSE follows? Tax, subsidy, ceiling, floor, wage floor — the same three questions, every single time.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the wage floor argued both ways, and the three-question habit run on an intervention you have never seen before. Master the habit, and the public sector's whole toolbox opens to you.
