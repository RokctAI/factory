# Part 1 — Expert

Term one gave scarcity and opportunity cost their definitions; this session gives them their picture. The PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE — the PPC — draws the outer edge of what an economy can produce with everything it currently owns, and packs scarcity, choice, opportunity cost, efficiency, unemployment and growth into a single diagram. It is the subject's chosen instrument for making the basic economic problem visible, and diagram questions on it reward learners who can both draw it and read it.

## Subtopic: The Curve That Draws Scarcity

Every model simplifies, and this one is honest about it — state the assumptions, because complete answers begin there. The economy produces only TWO goods; its RESOURCES are fixed in quantity and quality; TECHNOLOGY is fixed; and every resource is FULLY and efficiently employed. Under those four assumptions, every possible division of the economy's effort between the two goods can be tabulated.

Take a district of mixed farms dividing fixed land and labour between wheat and wool. The schedule: option A — no wheat, 40 bales of wool; B — 10 tonnes of wheat, 38 bales; C — 20 and 34; D — 30 and 27; E — 40 and 16; F — 50 tonnes of wheat, no wool. Put wool on one axis and wheat on the other, plot the six options, join them — and the PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE emerges: a boundary bowing outward from axis to axis.

Read carefully what the curve IS. Every point ON it is a maximum: the greatest amount of one good obtainable once the quantity of the other is chosen, with everything employed. The curve is scarcity made visible — the economy may pick any point on the boundary but can reach nothing beyond it. And it is choice made visible too: moving from B to C gains ten tonnes of wheat and surrenders four bales of wool. No movement along the curve is free; the gain on one axis is always paid on the other. The diagram outlaws free lunches by its very construction.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the four assumptions, the schedule plotted, and why every point on the boundary is a maximum.

## Subtopic: Reading the Points — On, Inside, Beyond

Three zones, three verdicts — the reading exercise this topic is famous for.

ON the curve: EFFICIENT and attainable. Every resource working, nothing idle; the district squeezes all possible output from what it has. Which on-curve point is "best" the model refuses to say — that is society's choice between efficient options, the WHAT-to-produce question from term one appearing as a position on a boundary.

INSIDE the curve: ATTAINABLE but INEFFICIENT. Producing 20 tonnes of wheat but only 27 bales of wool, when the boundary offers 34 at that wheat level, means resources are standing idle or badly used — shearers without work, paddocks unstocked, tractors parked. The economy carries scarcity's burden without collecting scarcity's full reward. Inside points are where recessions and unemployment live on this diagram.

BEYOND the curve: UNATTAINABLE — today. No reshuffling of present resources and technology reaches those combinations. Desirable, certainly; reachable only when the boundary itself moves.

Along the curve, OPPORTUNITY COST turns into a number you can compute. From A to B, the first 10 tonnes of wheat cost 2 bales of wool; from B to C, the next 10 cost 4 bales; then 7, then 11, then 16. Wheat's price in wool RISES as more wheat is grown — INCREASING opportunity cost, and the very reason the curve bows outward. Resources are not all equally versatile: the first paddocks ploughed for wheat are the ones best suited to grain; push further and you are ploughing land that carried sheep brilliantly but grows wheat grudgingly, so each extra tonne costs more wool than the one before. A straight-line PPC would claim resources switch jobs perfectly; the outward bow tells the truth.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three zones classified, opportunity cost computed between points, and the bowing explained.

## Subtopic: What Moves the Curve

The boundary is fixed only for a moment — it is drawn for TODAY'S resources and technology. Change those, and the boundary itself relocates. The syllabus sorts the movers into internal and external factors.

INTERNAL factors arise within the economy: the QUANTITY of resources — a growing labour force, capital accumulated through investment, new mineral or water discoveries; the QUALITY of resources — education and training sharpening labour's skill, better management; TECHNOLOGY — methods that coax more output from unchanged inputs; and efficiency reforms that move practice closer to best use. Improvements push the PPC OUTWARD: combinations that were unattainable yesterday come within reach. That outward march IS economic growth in its diagram form — not a step along the boundary, but the boundary itself advancing.

EXTERNAL factors arrive from beyond the economy's control: climate and weather — good rains stretch the agricultural frontier, drought drags it back; international conditions — access to export markets, the world prices our goods fetch, imported machinery and capital; and shocks — pandemics, wars and disasters that destroy resources outright. Favourable externals push the curve outward; destruction hauls it INWARD, leaving a smaller boundary and a poorer productive capacity.

Now guard the topic's classic distinction. A movement ALONG the curve is reallocation — more wheat, less wool, same capacity. A SHIFT of the curve is a change in capacity itself. And a move from an inside point TOWARD the curve is neither: it is recovery — putting idle resources back to work. Falling unemployment moves the economy toward its boundary; only the boundary advancing is growth. Three improvements, three entirely different pictures — confuse them and the answer collapses.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — internal and external movers sorted, outward against inward, and along-versus-toward-versus-shift kept rigorously apart.

## Subtopic: Consequences of Inefficiencies

The topic ends where South African reality begins: the cost of producing inside the curve.

First name the gap. An economy at an inside point produces less than its capacity allows, and the distance to the boundary is measurable loss — output that could exist and does not. Every unemployed worker, silent machine and unplanted field is a slice of that gap, and the gap is concrete: houses never built, food never grown, services never delivered, while the means to deliver them stand ready and waiting.

Then follow the chain of consequences. Lost output means lost INCOMES — wages the jobless never earn, profits idle plants never generate. Lost incomes shrink SPENDING, and the circular flow turns more slowly for everyone. Shrunken spending and profit mean lost TAX REVENUE — the public purse thinnest exactly when social needs are heaviest. And prolonged idleness CORRODES the resources themselves: skills fade through long unemployment, machinery rusts, the discouraged give up the search — today's inside point quietly drags tomorrow's boundary inward. Name the social consequences too, as the subject requires: poverty, inequality and exclusion, concentrated among the very people the idle resources represent — South Africa's stubbornly high unemployment is this country's visible distance from its own curve.

The policy lesson falls out cleanly. An economy inside its boundary can raise output WITHOUT a single new resource — by employing what it already has: absorbing the unemployed, running idle capacity, matching skills to vacancies. That is recovery toward the curve, the cheapest growth on offer. Reaching beyond today's boundary then calls for the shifters — investment, education, technology. Two tasks, strictly in order: first reach the curve; then move it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the gap named, the consequence chain traced, and the two policy tasks separated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again from a sewing workshop before opening time — two products, one room of machines, and every big idea stitched into a working day.

## Subtopic: One Workshop, Two Uniforms

A small workshop sews school uniforms: one room of machines, three machinists, one working day. It can sew plain school shirts, rugby jerseys, or a mix — and the day's possibilities form a little menu. All shirts: 60 shirts, no jerseys. Mostly shirts: 54 shirts and 12 jerseys. Balanced: 45 and 24. Leaning jersey: 33 and 36. Nearly all jerseys: 18 and 48. All jerseys: none and 60.

Spot the iron rule stitched into the menu: every dozen jerseys is PAID FOR in shirts. The machines' day is fixed; the machinists' hands are fixed — push jerseys up and shirts must come down. That is scarcity, not as a definition to memorise but as a menu to choose from: many combinations on offer, and every single one is a trade.

Now draw the menu: shirts up the side, jerseys along the bottom, each combination a dot, the dots joined. Out comes a curved boundary — the edge of the possible day. Everything the workshop can achieve at full hum lies ON that line; nothing outside it can be sewn with this room and these hands. That line is the production possibility curve — and a country's PPC is the same picture with a bigger room: fixed resources, two goods for simplicity, and a boundary announcing "this much, no more — now choose your mix."

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the menu are coming to you right now: the fixed day, the trades between garments, and what the boundary line declares.

## Subtopic: Lazy Days and Dream Days

The picture sorts every possible day into three kinds — and you have lived all three.

The FULL day: machines humming from open to close, all three machinists busy, fabric flowing. That day lands ON the line — the most this workshop can produce, in whatever mix the owner chose. No point on the line outranks another in the diagram's eyes; more shirts or more jerseys is the owner's judgement call. The line's only promise is that each choice is a maximum.

The LAZY day: one machinist off sick, a machine jammed for an hour, a roll of fabric left in the storeroom. That day lands INSIDE the line — perhaps 36 jerseys but only 27 shirts, when the full day offered 33 shirts alongside. The workshop did not shrink; it simply failed to use what it had. Inside the line is where waste lives — and for a country, inside the line is unemployment's address: willing hands, work left undone.

The DREAM day: 60 shirts AND 48 jerseys — wonderful, and impossible. That combination floats BEYOND the line, unreachable by any honest arrangement of this room. Not shameful — just tomorrow's target, waiting for the workshop itself to change.

And the menu whispers one more secret: jerseys grow dearer as you sew more of them. The first dozen jerseys cost only 6 shirts; the final dozen costs 18. Why? The machinists quickest at jersey work switch across first; push further and you are moving your best shirt-hands onto jersey work they do slowly. Hands and machines are not all-purpose — which is exactly why the boundary bows outward like a filled sail instead of running flat.

Your questions for this part are up now: full, lazy and dream days placed on the picture, and the rising price of jerseys explained.

## Subtopic: Buying the Second Machine

Last piece: how dream days become reachable — and what lazy days truly cost.

The line moved for the workshop the month the owner bought two extra machines and sent a machinist on an advanced course. Suddenly every mix improved: more shirts AND more jerseys within reach; yesterday's impossible orders now quotable. The boundary itself stepped outward. That is growth — and a nation grows the same way: more machines and workers, better-trained hands, smarter methods, and friendly conditions beyond the gate, from good rains to strong export demand. The line can also step BACKWARD: a machine seized by breakdown, a machinist emigrating, a fire in the storeroom — capacity lost, the possible day shrunk, and no clever mix can sew what capacity no longer allows.

Keep the three improvements strictly apart, because they masquerade as each other. Choosing a different MIX is a walk along the line — reallocation, not growth. Fixing a lazy day is a walk from inside back TO the line — recovery: more of everything, using only what was always there. Only the line itself advancing is growth.

And now the sharpest lesson for our own country. A workshop that runs lazy days loses more than the day's garments. The unpaid wages never reach the taxi rank or the tuck shop; the unsewn orders pay no tax toward the clinic; and machinists who sit idle too long lose their speed — today's laziness quietly shrinks tomorrow's full day. That is South Africa's unemployment drawn as a distance: millions of willing hands inside the line. The first, cheapest growth is simply walking back to the boundary — employing what we already have. THEN we buy the second machine.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: walks along the line, walks back to the line, and moves of the line itself told apart — and the lazy day's full cost counted. Hold this picture, and the term's whole story — markets, intervention, and the edge of the possible — fits on one page.
