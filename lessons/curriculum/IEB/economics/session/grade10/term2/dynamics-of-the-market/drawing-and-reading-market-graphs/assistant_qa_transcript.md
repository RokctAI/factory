**Thandi:** Time for the class questions! First one, from at least five people: why do we bother with the graph at all if every answer is already sitting in the table?

**Tutor:** Because the table only speaks at its own five prices, while the graph speaks at every price in between. Ask the table what happens at seventeen rand and it goes silent — no row. Ask the graph and your level sweep still returns two readings and a gap, because joining the points filled in the whole continuous relationship. The graph also shows shape at a glance: how steep demand is, where the curves converge, how fast a surplus shrinks as the price falls toward equilibrium. And there is a practical reason: interpretation tasks hand you a diagram and expect readings from it directly. The table is the boss of the facts, but the graph is the instrument you will actually be handed — so you must be fluent in both directions: table to graph, and graph back to numbers.

***

**Thandi:** Next: is it wrong if my demand curve comes out as a perfectly straight line? Mine looked like a ruler drew it and I panicked.

**Tutor:** No panic needed — your ruler told the truth. The shape of the curve belongs to the data, not to you. This schedule falls by exactly fifty units for every five-rand step, so its picture is a perfectly straight, evenly falling line; a different schedule might fall steeply at first and flatten later, giving a genuine curve. Economists say "demand curve" as a family name, the way a rugby coach says "the ball" whether it is new or worn. What WOULD be wrong is bending a straight schedule into an artistic swoop or straightening a curved one — that is the graph disobeying the table. Plot the dots faithfully, join them smoothly, and accept whatever honest shape appears. The marks sit in faithful plotting and correct reading, never in decorative curviness.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the one I got wrong myself: my friend and I both read the gap at twenty rand and got different answers — 100 and 150. How do two people read one graph differently?

**Tutor:** Almost always through the crooked sweep — and I suspect one of you flew it. The rule is that both readings must come from the same height: the horizontal line at twenty rand touches demand at 100 and supply at 200, gap 100. The classic slip is taking supply at twenty rand but demand at fifteen or twenty-five — then you are subtracting quantities from two different worlds, and any number can come out, like comparing your height today with your friend's height from three years ago. There is a second, sneakier source: reading against an uneven scale, where the same distance means different quantities in different places. So when two readers disagree, run the audit: same price for both touches? Even steps on both axes? Dots checked against rows? One of those three will expose the culprit — the graph itself never lies if it was built honestly.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: you said this exact skill comes back later in the subject. Where exactly will we meet it again?

**Tutor:** Everywhere — this lesson is the entrance fee to the rest of Economics. Next term, when the state intervenes in markets, you will read shortages under price ceilings and surpluses above price floors — the identical level sweep, now with a law blocking the price from moving. In the grades ahead, cost and revenue curves decide a firm's best output, elasticity turns on how quantities respond along the very curves you plotted today, and macroeconomics draws aggregate demand and supply on the same frame with the same conventions. The deeper skill is trusting data enough to draw it honestly and drawing it well enough to read it — and that pays outside the classroom too, every time a chart appears in the news. Five practice graphs now, and every diagram for the next three years becomes an old friend.
