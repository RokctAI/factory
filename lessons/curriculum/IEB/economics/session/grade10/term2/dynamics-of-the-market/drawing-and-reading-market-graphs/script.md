# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds a skill rather than a theory — the quiet, dependable skill of turning a table of numbers into an accurate market graph and then reading answers directly off it. Our working example is a demand and supply schedule for a product at prices from five rand to twenty-five rand, and by the end you will plot both curves, locate the equilibrium, and read off the excess supply at twenty rand.

## Subtopic: Reading the Table Before You Draw

Here is our table — economists call it a schedule. Each row records, for one price, two quantities: what buyers would take and what sellers would offer.

At five rand, quantity demanded is 250 units and quantity supplied is 50 units. At ten rand, demand is 200 and supply is 100. At fifteen rand, demand is 150 and supply is 150. At twenty rand, demand is 100 and supply is 200. At twenty-five rand, demand is 50 and supply is 250.

Before your pencil touches paper, question the table — this habit catches errors before they exist. Run down the demand column: as price climbs from five to twenty-five rand, quantity demanded falls from 250 to 50. Shrinking as price rises — the law of demand, so this column must become a downward-sloping curve. Now the supply column: as price rises, quantity supplied grows from 50 to 250. Growing with price — the law of supply, an upward-sloping curve.

And notice the remarkable row: at fifteen rand, both columns read the same number — 150 and 150. Flag that row in your mind; the entire graph is going to pivot on it.

One further habit: note the ranges. Prices span five to twenty-five rand; quantities span 50 to 250. Those ranges dictate how the axes will be scaled in a moment. A table properly interrogated is a graph already half built.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two columns read for direction, each law named, and the special row spotted.

## Subtopic: Axes, Scales and Plotting the Points

Now the construction — where presentation either earns or leaks marks.

Rule one: in economics, price occupies the vertical axis and quantity the horizontal axis, without exception. It is the subject's standing convention; follow it automatically.

Rule two: choose even scales that fill the space. Prices run five to twenty-five, so mark the vertical axis in equal steps of five: five, ten, fifteen, twenty, twenty-five. Quantities run 50 to 250, so mark the horizontal axis in equal steps of 50: fifty, one hundred, one hundred and fifty, two hundred, two hundred and fifty. Equal steps throughout — never compress some values and stretch others. An uneven scale bends curves that should be straight and corrupts every reading you take from them afterwards.

Rule three: label everything. "Price (R)" on the vertical axis, "Quantity" on the horizontal, a clear heading above the graph, and names on the curves themselves. Labels are cheap to write and expensive to omit.

Now plot the demand schedule, row by row. Each row is one point: five rand with 250 — up to five on the price axis, across to 250, mark it. Ten rand with 200. Fifteen with 150. Twenty with 100. Twenty-five with 50. Join the points smoothly and name the line D. It runs downhill, exactly as the column promised.

Then the supply schedule on the same axes: five rand with 50, ten with 100, fifteen with 150, twenty with 200, twenty-five with 250. Join and name it S. Uphill. Two curves on one frame — a complete market in a picture.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three construction rules, and rows turned into plotted points.

## Subtopic: Finding Equilibrium on the Graph

Study your finished drawing. The two curves cross at exactly one point. Read its coordinates: price fifteen rand, quantity 150. That crossing is the market equilibrium — the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

Now verify it against the table, because graph and table must always agree: at fifteen rand, demand reads 150 and supply reads 150. They match. If your curves cross anywhere except a row where the two columns are equal, one of your points sits in the wrong place — return to the table and check each point against its row.

Present the equilibrium properly: a dotted line from the crossing point across to the price axis, striking it at fifteen rand, and a dotted line down to the quantity axis, striking 150. Label the point E. Those dotted lines are working lines, not decoration — they demonstrate that you can read a graph, and they routinely carry marks of their own.

Be precise about what this point means economically: at fifteen rand the market clears. Every unit offered finds a buyer; every buyer willing and able to pay finds a unit. No surplus, no shortage, no pressure on the price. At any other price the market is out of balance — and, as the next section shows, the graph will tell you by exactly how much.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the crossing read, the dotted lines drawn, and the graph-against-table check explained.

## Subtopic: Reading Off Excess Supply at R20

Now the classic task: determine the excess supply at twenty rand. This is the moment your graph stops being a picture and becomes a measuring instrument.

Locate twenty rand on the price axis. Travel horizontally across the graph at that height. Your line strikes the demand curve first — drop straight down: quantity demanded is 100. Continue across to the supply curve — drop down again: quantity supplied is 200.

At twenty rand, then, sellers offer 200 units while buyers take only 100. The market fails to balance: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded by 200 minus 100 — 100 units. That gap is the excess supply, also called a surplus. On the graph it is the horizontal distance between the two curves at the height of twenty rand. Bracket that stretch and label it "excess supply = 100 units".

Why does it appear at twenty rand? Because twenty rand lies above the equilibrium of fifteen. Any price above equilibrium tempts sellers into offering generously while pricing many buyers out — surplus is the inevitable result. And the market's own response follows: unsold stock presses the price down toward fifteen rand.

The mirror-image skill: asked for excess demand at ten rand, use the identical technique — across at ten, demand reads 200, supply reads 100: a shortage of 100 units. Below equilibrium, buyers outrun sellers.

Before finishing, run the verification routine — every time. One: does each plotted point match its table row exactly? Two: does demand slope down and supply slope up? Three: does the crossing agree with the row where the columns are equal? Four: for any reading, did you take BOTH quantities at the SAME price before subtracting? The most frequent error in this task is reading the two curves at different heights — the horizontal line at twenty rand must touch both curves at that one height.

A graph with even scales, full labels and dotted reading lines is an instrument: feed it any price and it returns the surplus or shortage. This same instrument, unchanged, will handle every curve this subject gives you in the years ahead — and this part's questions are with you now: readings taken, gaps named and measured, the routine run in full.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the identical skill with the anxiety removed — because a market graph is nothing more than a photograph of a table, and tables have never frightened you.

## Subtopic: The Table Is the Boss

Here is the calming secret: the graph invents nothing. Every dot on it walks straight out of a table row, and whenever graph and table disagree, the table wins — the graph is only its portrait, and portraits must resemble their subject.

So interview the table before drawing anything. Ask the demand column what it does as price climbs: it shrinks — 250, 200, 150, 100, 50. So the finished picture MUST show a line stepping downhill. Ask the supply column: it grows — 50, 100, 150, 200, 250. A line climbing uphill. You now know the shape of the picture before touching a pencil — which turns you into your own marker: if your demand line ends up climbing, the table already told you something is wrong, and you can fix it before anyone else sees it.

While interviewing, hunt for the handshake row — the one price where the two columns agree. There it is: fifteen rand, where both sides say 150. One price at which buyers' plans and sellers' plans match exactly. Circle it mentally: your two lines are going to cross precisely there, and if they cross anywhere else, some dot wandered from its row.

Table first, picture second, and the table stays the boss forever. That single habit eliminates most graphing disasters before they begin.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on interviewing the table are coming to you right now: directions, ranges, and the handshake row.

## Subtopic: Building the Picture Frame

Now the frame — because a trustworthy graph is mostly good scaffolding, and scaffolding is pure routine.

Three habits carry everything. Habit one: price stands UP the side, quantity runs ALONG the bottom — every economics graph, every time, no exceptions. Do it on autopilot. Habit two: even steps. Five, ten, fifteen, twenty, twenty-five up the side — each step the same width of paper. Fifty, one hundred, one-fifty, two hundred, two-fifty along the bottom — identical spacing again. Why the strictness? Because uneven steps warp straight lines into curves and curves into lies — like a measuring tape with some centimetres squashed and others stretched: whatever it tells you afterwards is worthless. Habit three: name everything — "Price (R)" up the side, "Quantity" along the bottom, a title on top, and D and S written on the lines themselves. Naming feels like fussiness; it is actually marks lying on the pavement waiting to be picked up.

With the frame standing, plotting becomes deliveries. Every table row is a drop-off point with two coordinates: five rand and 250 — ride up to five, across to 250, leave a dot. Five dots for demand, joined smoothly, signed D. Five dots for supply, joined, signed S. If every dot landed at its correct coordinates, the downhill line and the uphill line appear on their own — you never had to force them; the two laws drew themselves.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three habits, and dots delivered to their coordinates.

## Subtopic: The Answer Machine

Final piece — the payoff. A graph built in its frame is a machine: feed it a price, and it hands back an answer.

First, where do the lines cross? Up at fifteen rand, across at 150 — drop dotted lines to both axes like a plane's landing gear. That crossing is the market's resting place: at fifteen rand every unit offered is bought, every willing buyer served. The handshake row from the table, now standing visibly as a crossing.

Now feed the machine its test question: what happens at twenty rand? Put your finger on twenty up the side and slide it straight across — one flat, level sweep. First touch: the demand line — look down: 100. Keep sweeping to the supply line — look down: 200. Same height, two readings: at twenty rand, sellers bring 200 and buyers take 100. The stretch of air between your two touch-points — 100 units — is the excess supply, physically visible as the gap between the lines at that height. Above the resting place, sellers always overshoot buyers.

And the mirror trick below: sweep across at ten rand — supply touches at 100, demand at 200 — buyers overshoot sellers by 100: excess demand. One sweeping rule keeps every reading honest: BOTH touches on ONE height. The classic blunder is reading one line at twenty rand and the other at some other height — a crooked sweep. Flat sweep, two touches, subtract.

That is the entire skill: table interviewed, frame built with three habits, dots delivered, machine fed questions with a level sweep. None of it is talent — it is routine, and five practice graphs make the routine permanent.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: crossings located, gaps swept and sized, and the crooked-sweep error caught before it costs anything. Draw your five graphs and this skill stays with you for the rest of school.
