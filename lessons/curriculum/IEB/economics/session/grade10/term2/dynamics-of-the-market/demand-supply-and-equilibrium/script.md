# Part 1 — Expert

Every price on every shelf is the outcome of two forces pulling against each other: demand and supply. This session teaches you to state both laws precisely, build the market diagram, decide which curve moves when the world changes, and locate the new equilibrium. Our running case study comes straight from recent South African life: an outbreak of bird flu forces egg farmers to cull millions of laying hens — and the price of eggs climbs within weeks.

## Subtopic: The Law of Demand

Begin with the buyers. Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at each possible price. Both conditions must hold. A learner who dreams of the newest phone but has no savings adds nothing to demand; desire only becomes demand when money stands behind it.

The law of demand states: as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and as the price falls, the quantity demanded rises — other things remaining equal. You live this law already: when data bundles get pricier, households ration their gigabytes; when a special drops the price, baskets fill up.

On the diagram, price sits on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. Plot the price and quantity pairs, join them, and the demand curve appears, running downhill from left to right. Label it D.

Now the distinction that separates strong answers from weak ones: a movement along the curve versus a shift of the entire curve. If the price of eggs itself changes, buyers respond along the same demand curve — a change in quantity demanded. The relationship did not change; we merely read a different point on it. But when a non-price factor changes — household income, the price of a substitute, tastes, the size of the population — buyers behave differently at every price, and the whole curve relocates: rightward for an increase in demand, leftward for a decrease. Memorise the rule: the good's own price moves you along the curve; any other cause shifts it.

In our bird flu story, take careful note: nothing happened to buyers' incomes or tastes. That detail tells you the demand curve will not move. Keep it in mind.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the law of demand stated fully, and the movement-versus-shift distinction with its deciding rule.

## Subtopic: The Law of Supply

Now cross the counter to the sellers. Supply is the quantity of a good that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each possible price.

The law of supply states: as the price rises, the quantity supplied rises; as the price falls, the quantity supplied falls — other things equal. Why does supply run in the same direction as price? Because price is the producer's incentive. When eggs fetch more per tray, it pays a farmer to expand the hen houses, buy more feed, and keep the packing line running longer. The supply curve therefore climbs from left to right. Label it S.

The along-versus-shift distinction applies here identically. A change in the good's own price moves sellers along the existing supply curve — a change in quantity supplied. But when something other than the price changes — feed and fuel costs, production technology, taxes and subsidies, the number of producers, and natural events such as disease or weather — the entire supply curve shifts. Lower costs or better technology shift supply rightward: more offered at every price. Higher costs or destruction shift it leftward: less offered at every price.

Apply this to the outbreak. Ask the two diagnostic questions every market answer needs. First: which side of the market was struck — buyers or sellers? The disease hit the farms, so the supply side. Second: did the price of eggs cause the change? No — a virus did. Therefore we shift the whole supply curve rather than sliding along it. Direction? With millions of hens culled, farmers can offer fewer eggs at every conceivable price: the supply curve shifts leftward, from S to S1.

The diagnosis is complete: demand curve untouched, supply curve shifted left — and that piece of reasoning carries a large share of the marks in any market question.

Stop for this section's questions now — the law of supply, the list of shift factors, and the two diagnostic questions run on the outbreak.

## Subtopic: Equilibrium — Where the Market Settles

Draw both curves on one set of axes and they intersect exactly once. That intersection is the market equilibrium — the single most important point on the diagram.

At the equilibrium price, the quantity buyers plan to purchase equals the quantity sellers plan to offer. The market clears: no trays of eggs left over, no shoppers turned away empty-handed. Label the equilibrium price P and the equilibrium quantity Q, with dotted lines to each axis.

Why does the market come to rest there and only there? Test both alternatives. Suppose the price sits above equilibrium. The generous price draws plenty of stock onto the shelves, but buyers pull back. Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded — a surplus. Trays approach their sell-by dates unsold, and retailers mark prices down to move them. The price sinks toward equilibrium.

Now suppose the price sits below equilibrium. Shoppers pour in, but sellers see little reward and offer little. Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — a shortage. Shelves empty early, and buyers who really want eggs are prepared to pay more; the price is bid upward.

From above, the price falls; from below, it rises. Equilibrium is the only price under no pressure to change — like water finding its level in a container. Notice what is absent: no minister, no committee, no head office decides this price. The tug of surplus and shortage does all the work. Economists call this price formation through market forces.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — surplus, shortage, and the reasoning that makes equilibrium the only resting place.

## Subtopic: The Bird Flu Diagram — Working the Full Example

Now assemble the complete answer to our case study, step by step, exactly as a full-mark response would present it.

Step one: axes. Price of eggs vertical, quantity of eggs horizontal, both labelled.

Step two: the original market. Demand curve D sloping down, supply curve S sloping up, intersection marked as the original equilibrium with price P and quantity Q, dotted lines to both axes.

Step three: the shock. Bird flu destroys a large share of the national laying flock — a supply-side event with no connection to the egg price. Draw S1 to the left of S, and add an arrow from S to S1 so your reasoning is visible on the page.

Step four: state explicitly that household incomes and tastes are unchanged, so the demand curve stays exactly where it is. Writing down why the other curve does not move earns reasoning marks that silent diagrams forfeit.

Step five: read the new equilibrium. S1 crosses D at a new intersection: price P1, quantity Q1, dotted lines again. Compare the two: P1 stands higher than P, and Q1 stands smaller than Q.

Step six: narrate the adjustment, because the diagram alone is half an answer. At the old price, the shrunken flock leaves quantity demanded exceeding quantity supplied — a shortage. Competing buyers bid the price upward. As it climbs, two movements unfold simultaneously: some households cut back on eggs or switch to other proteins — a movement along the unchanged demand curve — and farmers push their remaining hens and re-stock as fast as biology allows — a movement along the new supply curve S1. The climb ends where the shortage vanishes: the new, higher equilibrium price P1, with fewer eggs traded than before.

Final self-check: does the conclusion match reality? During the actual outbreak, South Africans met exactly this — emptier egg shelves and steeper prices. When your diagram, your paragraph and the shops all agree, your method is sound — and this part's questions are with you now: the six steps, applied to the outbreak and to fresh shocks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two forces told from the school gate at break time — because demand and supply were never invented by textbooks; they are the oldest tug-of-war on any pavement: buyers pulling the price down, sellers pulling it up, and the price settling where the pulling stops.

## Subtopic: The Vetkoek Table at the School Gate

A gogo sets up her table of fresh vetkoek at the school gate every morning. Watch the buyers first. At twelve rand each, only the truly hungry buy. At eight rand, more hands reach out. At five rand, half the grade wants one. Nobody at that gate has studied economics, yet the law of demand runs the queue perfectly: the cheaper the vetkoek, the more get bought, because every learner quietly weighs the price against the coins in their pocket.

And hear the rule hiding in that queue: HUNGER is not demand. The boy staring at the table with empty pockets wants a vetkoek desperately — but the market cannot hear him. Demand is hunger plus money ready to spend: willing AND able, always both.

Now watch the seller's side. When vetkoek fetch five rand, gogo mixes one small batch of dough — barely worth the early morning. At eight rand, two batches. At twelve rand, she calls her daughter to help fry, because now every vetkoek rewards the work handsomely. That is the law of supply on a table: the better the price, the more sellers bring, because price is the seller's payslip.

So the two sides lean in opposite directions — buyers say cheaper means more of us buy; sellers say dearer means more we bring. Two laws pulling against each other. Something must give, and what gives is the price — that story comes next.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the vetkoek table are coming to you right now: both laws from the gate, and the willing-AND-able rule.

## Subtopic: Where the Argument Ends

So where does the vetkoek price finally land? Watch the gate sort itself out with nobody in charge.

Suppose gogo tries twelve rand. Excited by the price, she fried a mountain of vetkoek — but the buyers hold back. By second break, cold vetkoek sit stacked under a cloth, going stiff. What does any seller do with stock going stale? Cut the price. Eleven rand... nine... and the stack shrinks faster with every cut.

Now the opposite morning: she tries four rand. The vetkoek vanish before the bell — a queue still stretching, the table bare. Does she charge four rand tomorrow? Never. Learners were jostling to buy; plenty would have paid more. Up goes the price.

Somewhere between the stale stack and the jostling queue lies a price where the morning works perfectly: the last vetkoek sells to the last willing buyer — nothing left over, nobody sent away. That price is equilibrium, the resting place. Priced too high, the leftovers push the price down; priced too low, the queue pulls it up. And notice the deepest fact in this whole topic: NOBODY chose that price. Not the school, not the gogo, not a committee. Thousands of small choices — buy or walk past, fry more or fry less — settled it between them. Every price you have ever paid was settled the same way.

Your questions for this part are up now: too high, too low, and the resting place — tell each story with the stack and the queue.

## Subtopic: The Egg Shelf at the Spaza

Last piece: what happens when the world changes — the bird flu story, told at the spaza shop.

Bird flu sweeps the poultry farms and millions of laying hens are culled. First question, every single time: WHOSE side of the market got hit — buyers or sellers? Shoppers' wallets are unchanged; the FARMS took the blow. Sellers' side. Second question: did the egg PRICE cause this? No — a virus did. So nobody is sliding along a curve here; the whole selling side has changed. At every price you could name, there are simply fewer eggs in the country. The supply curve packs its bags and moves left.

Now play it forward at the spaza. At the old price, the egg shelf empties by mid-morning — more buyers than trays: shortage. Customers who need eggs for the week bid against each other, and the shopkeeper's price climbs. As it climbs, watch two things happen at once: some families switch to beans or buy six eggs instead of thirty — buyers sliding along their unchanged demand curve — and farmers squeeze every tray they can from the hens that survived — sellers stretching along their new, thinner curve. The climbing stops where the smaller supply meets the trimmed-back demand: a new resting place, higher price, fewer eggs sold. Exactly what the whole country saw during the outbreak. The diagram simply draws what the spaza already knows.

And the method, one final time: whose side, and did the own price do it? Answer those two questions correctly, shift or slide accordingly, and every market question in this subject opens for you.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: fresh shocks — a maize price spike raising feed costs, a health craze for eggs, a new mega-farm opening — each diagnosed with the two questions and traced to its new resting place.
