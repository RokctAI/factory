# Part 1 — Expert

Demand and supply only make full sense once three deeper foundations are in place: what VALUE is and where it comes from, how MARKETS are classified — perfect and imperfect — and what markets actually DO for an economy, including the electronic, world-spanning markets of our own decade. This session lays all three foundations, and each one carries its own family of questions: precise definitions, careful classification, and functions explained with examples.

## Subtopic: Value, Price and Utility

The starting concept is UTILITY: the satisfaction or usefulness a consumer derives from a good or service. Utility is SUBJECTIVE — it exists in the person, not in the object. A raincoat delivers immense utility in a Durban downpour and none on a cloudless day; a calculus textbook matters enormously to the learner preparing for a test and very little to someone who finished school years ago. Because utility differs between people, places and moments, the same object carries different worth for different owners — and that difference is what makes exchange possible at all: trade happens precisely because a thing is worth more to the buyer than to the seller.

From utility flow two distinct kinds of VALUE. VALUE IN USE: how useful a thing is to whoever holds it — water's value in use is beyond measure, since life itself depends on it. VALUE IN EXCHANGE: what a thing fetches when traded — and here stands the centuries-old paradox your syllabus asks you to resolve: water, essential for survival, trades for cents, while diamonds, mostly decorative, trade for fortunes. The resolution runs through SCARCITY relative to wants, and through the utility of the NEXT unit rather than the total. Water is abundant in most places, and one more glass to a person with plenty adds almost nothing; diamonds are rare, and even one more still dazzles. Abundance cheapens; scarcity makes dear — no matter how noble the good.

Finally PRICE: value in exchange expressed in money. Money prices translate every exchange value into one shared language, which is what makes comparison, calculation and bookkeeping possible across an entire economy.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: utility's subjectivity, the two values separated cleanly, and the water-diamond paradox resolved through scarcity.

## Subtopic: Perfect and Imperfect Markets

A market is any arrangement that connects buyers and sellers for trade — a physical square, a phone call, an app. Economics sorts markets by COMPETITIVE STRUCTURE, and the measuring stick is the PERFECT market, defined by strict conditions: MANY buyers and many sellers, none big enough to sway the price alone; a HOMOGENEOUS product — one farmer's tomatoes indistinguishable from the next farmer's; PERFECT KNOWLEDGE — every participant informed about prices and quality; and FREE ENTRY AND EXIT — no barriers stopping newcomers or trapping leavers. Where all conditions hold, every participant is a PRICE TAKER: the market's crossing point sets one price, and each buyer and seller simply accepts it. Reality only approximates the model — fresh-produce markets and the securities exchange come nearest: standardised products, crowds of participants, publicly visible prices.

Most real markets are IMPERFECT: one or more conditions fail, and someone acquires influence over the price. The MONOPOLY: a single seller — the only rail freight operator, the holder of a patented seed variety — facing no direct competition and choosing its price, limited only by what buyers will tolerate. The OLIGOPOLY: a handful of large sellers — the big banks, fuel companies, cellular networks — each moving with one eye on the others, often competing on everything except price. MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION: many sellers of SIMILAR but differentiated products — coffee shops, gyms, clothing brands — each holding a sliver of pricing power built from brand, location or loyalty. Classification runs on three questions: how many sellers; how similar the product; how free the entry. And the consequences scale with the answers: fewer sellers and higher barriers pull the price further from the perfect benchmark, turning the seller into a PRICE MAKER.

Stop for this section's questions now — the perfect conditions listed completely, the three imperfect structures sorted, and price taker set against price maker.

## Subtopic: World Markets and the Effects of Electronics

Markets stopped respecting borders long ago. WORLD MARKETS trade commodities, currencies and manufactured goods across the planet: the gold price is settled by global buying and selling, not in Johannesburg; the rand's value against the dollar is a world market's minute-by-minute verdict. For South Africa, a commodity-exporting economy, world prices are household realities — every platinum belt town rises and falls with numbers set continents away.

Your syllabus highlights the ELECTRONIC transformation of markets, and its effects deserve a disciplined list. Electronics WIDENED markets: a bidder in Mthatha competes with one in Singapore; a beadwork artist reaches buyers abroad through a platform page. They ACCELERATED markets: currency and share prices adjust within seconds of news, around the clock. They strengthened KNOWLEDGE: prices are now visible to anyone holding a phone — a livestock farmer checks auction prices in a group chat before the buyer's bakkie arrives; a shopper compares four retailers in one minute. Perfect knowledge, once a textbook fiction, has crept toward everyday fact. They lowered ENTRY barriers: a home baker with a social-media account joins the cake market without renting premises. And they built NEW market forms entirely: online marketplaces, auction platforms, and app services that match buyers to sellers by algorithm.

Then balance the ledger, as a complete answer must: electronics concentrate power in the platform owners standing at the new gates; they expose small local sellers to competition from giant foreign ones; and they demand data and devices that many South Africans still cannot afford — the digital divide operating as a modern barrier to entry. Markets became wider, faster and better informed; nothing guarantees they became fairer.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — world prices as local realities, the electronic effects listed in both directions, and the knowledge condition revisited.

## Subtopic: The Functions of Markets

The topic closes with what markets DO — their functions in an economy. Five, each one already visible in your earlier lessons.

One: PRICE FORMATION — markets discover prices through the meeting of demand and supply; every crossing point you have drawn is this function at work. Two: ALLOCATION of resources — once formed, prices steer the factors of production: a rising price calls producers in, drawing labour and capital toward what society wants more of; a falling price releases resources from what it wants less. The WHAT-to-produce question answers itself without a central planner. Three: INFORMATION — a price is a compressed message: one number carrying news about scarcity, costs and tastes to millions of strangers simultaneously; no newsletter could brief an economy so cheaply. Four: RATIONING — when a good runs short, its rising price decides who receives it: those willing and able to pay. Harsh at times, but automatic, and it clears without queues. Five: LINKING buyers and sellers — markets join people who will never meet: the Karoo wool farmer and the Milanese tailor, connected through a chain of markets neither can see.

Bind the five together in one sentence: markets form prices, and prices then allocate, inform, ration and connect. And attach the honest footnote this term keeps returning to: markets serve those with buying power — where genuine need has no income behind it, or where costs spill onto bystanders, society calls in the state's corrections. That is precisely where the next topic goes: public sector intervention.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five functions named and illustrated, and the bridge to intervention stated.

## Subtopic: Why Water Costs R30 at the Festival

One bottle of water: nine rand at the corner café, thirty rand inside the festival grounds on a blazing afternoon. The water is identical — same label, same litre. So what changed? YOU changed. You are hot, dancing, dehydrated, and fenced in with no other option — the satisfaction that one bottle delivers right now is enormous. Economists call that satisfaction utility, and its first law is this: utility lives in the person, never in the product. Same bottle, different moment, completely different worth.

That idea cracks open a riddle philosophers argued about for centuries: water keeps every human alive and costs next to nothing, while a diamond keeps nobody alive and costs a fortune. The trick is to separate two kinds of worth. Usefulness-worth — value in use — water wins effortlessly. But trading-worth — value in exchange — runs on a different engine: SCARCITY. Water flows from taps everywhere, and one more glass to someone with plenty is almost worthless — so nobody offers much for it. Diamonds are genuinely rare, and even one more is a prize — so people bid fortunes. The rule underneath everything: plentiful things trade cheap, scarce things trade dear — regardless of how essential they are. And the festival is the rule demonstrated in reverse: fence the crowd in, and water INSIDE the fence becomes scarce — its trading-worth more than triples on the spot.

Price is simply trading-worth translated into rands — one common language so that everything, from water to diamonds, can be compared and counted.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the festival bottle are coming to you right now: where utility lives, the two worths, and the riddle cracked.

## Subtopic: The Tomato Market and the Big Four Banks

Spend one Saturday morning in town and you can classify every market structure in the syllabus.

Start at the fresh-produce market at sunrise: forty growers selling tomatoes, a crowd of buyers moving down the rows. Every crate looks like every other crate. Ten minutes of walking shows you every price on offer. Any grower may pitch up tomorrow; any may stay home. The result: one going rate for tomatoes, and no individual can bend it — ask double and buyers drift to the next stall; offer half and growers wave you off. Everybody TAKES the price the whole market built together. That is the perfect market, or as near as the real world manages.

Now walk into town. Four big banks handle nearly all the country's accounts — a few giant sellers, each pricing its fees with one eye locked on the other three, competing through adverts and apps more than through price: an oligopoly. The company holding the patent on a drought-resistant seed variety — one seller, no substitute, choosing its price subject only to what farmers can bear: a monopoly, the textbook price MAKER. And the street of coffee shops — many sellers, each slightly different: this one's roast, that one's couches, that one's playlist — each holding a small loyal crowd willing to pay a few rand extra: monopolistic competition.

The sorting kit, street edition: how many sellers? Is the product identical or dressed up different? How easily can a newcomer open shop? Three questions, and you can classify any market you walk past — and predict who takes the price and who makes it.

## Subtopic: The Market in Your Pocket

Final piece: the phone in your pocket is not just a way to reach markets — it IS one, and it has rewritten the old rules.

A generation ago, a livestock farmer knew only what the visiting buyer offered at the kraal gate. Today she scrolls the auction group before the bakkie even turns in — and negotiates armed with the going price. A matric learner compares laptop prices across four retailers during break. A home baker sells celebration cakes through a social-media page — no shopfront, no rent, customers she has never met. And the giant markets — gold, shares, currencies — flicker on the same screen, their prices updated by the second from across the planet. The textbook's "perfect knowledge" condition used to be pure fiction; the phone has dragged it halfway into daily life — and knowledge is bargaining power in every deal.

But audit it like an economist: the platforms hosting these new markets have owners — a few enormous companies standing at the gates and setting the rules of entry; the home baker now competes with factory confectioners three provinces away; and data costs real money — whoever cannot afford it stands outside the electronic marketplace entirely, locked out by a new kind of gate. Wider, faster, better informed — not automatically fairer.

And beneath it all, pocket-sized or planet-sized, markets still perform the same five jobs: they FORM prices where buyers meet sellers; those prices STEER the economy's effort toward what people actually want; every price is a one-number MESSAGE about scarcity that millions read at once; rising prices RATION whatever runs short; and markets CONNECT total strangers — the wool farmer and the tailor, the baker and the birthday party — through chains nobody planned. One sentence carries the lot: markets make prices, and prices do the rest.
