# Part 1 — Expert

Every topic this year has carried a quiet second question underneath it: production, but produced by whom? Income, but flowing to whom? Markets, but open to whom? ECONOMIC REDRESS answers that second question directly — it is the deliberate, policy-driven correction of economic injustice inherited from the past. This lesson identifies the economically marginalised groups and the mechanisms that excluded them, organises redress through the FACTORS OF PRODUCTION, explains how economic decision-making itself has been democratised — labour law, public participation, NEDLAC and self-regulating bodies — and closes with the macro-economic frameworks around it all and an honest, two-sided assessment.

## Subtopic: Economically Marginalised Groups

To be MARGINALISED is to be held at the margin of the economy: shut out of ownership, formal employment, skills, credit and the rooms where decisions are made. In South Africa this exclusion was written into statute. It was not the by-product of markets working badly; it was the intended product of laws working exactly as designed — and because economic position is inherited, the pattern outlives the laws that built it.

Name the groups, and always attach the mechanism, because a group without its mechanism is a list, and a group with its mechanism is an analysis. BLACK South Africans: stripped of land ownership by statute, confined in where they could live and trade, given deliberately inferior schooling, and funnelled into migrant labour at wages set by the absence of alternatives. WOMEN — and Black women carried both exclusions at once: barred by law and custom from owning property, signing contracts and accessing credit, loaded with unpaid care work that still limits the hours they can sell, and clustered today in the lowest-paid occupations. RURAL communities: settled by design in territories with the least infrastructure, the thinnest markets and the longest distances from employment. PEOPLE WITH DISABILITIES: excluded by inaccessible schools, transport and workplaces, and by employer assumptions that persist long after the ramps are built. And the YOUTH: the newest arrivals in a labour market with too few open doors, inheriting every earlier exclusion as their starting position.

One analytical step lifts an answer above the average: these categories INTERSECT. A young woman with a disability in a remote village does not face four separate barriers queued politely one behind the other — she faces a single compounded wall. Strong answers name the groups, name the mechanisms, and notice the compounding.

## Subtopic: Redress Through the Factors of Production

Here is the most useful structure in the whole topic: organise redress by the FOUR FACTORS OF PRODUCTION, because what marginalisation removed was precisely access to those factors. For each one, ask three questions in sequence — what was denied, what instrument answers it, and what support the instrument needs before it produces a livelihood.

NATURAL RESOURCES, meaning above all LAND. Dispossession sits at the root of the whole story, so land reform sits at the root of redress, and it runs on three tracks: RESTITUTION, which returns land or pays compensation to those dispossessed after nineteen thirteen; REDISTRIBUTION, which opens access for people who were never permitted to own; and TENURE REFORM, which converts insecure occupation — the family that has farmed a plot for fifty years with no paper to prove it — into rights the law defends. And the economics adds a warning the politics often skips: a transferred hectare with no finance, no equipment, no training and no route to a buyer generates neither income nor output. Transfer plus support is redress; transfer alone is a ceremony.

LABOUR. What was denied here was the better job and the schooling that leads to it. The instruments: employment equity law, which obliges designated employers to dismantle barriers and to make their workforces — critically, at MANAGEMENT and professional level, where exclusion bit hardest — reflect the society around them; and skills development, where a levy on payrolls funds learnerships, apprenticeships and artisan training, rebuilding the qualifications pipeline that was deliberately narrowed.

CAPITAL. Generations barred from owning productive assets arrive in the present with nothing to invest and nothing to borrow against. Broad-based Black Economic Empowerment works on the ownership side: equity stakes, management representation, developing enterprises and suppliers, and preferential procurement that steers large buyers toward transformed firms. Development finance institutions work on the credit side, lending where commercial banks see no collateral and no history.

ENTREPRENEURSHIP. The right to build a business was itself restricted — by law, by location and by exclusion from networks and capital. Redress here means incubation, mentorship, simplified compliance for small firms, and set-asides that guarantee new entrants a first customer.

Four factors, four denials, four instruments — and one refrain: every instrument needs its support conditions attached, or the answer has named the machinery and forgotten the fuel.

Pause for this section's questions now: each factor with its denial, its instrument, and the support without which the instrument fails.

## Subtopic: Democratising Economic Decisions

Ownership is half of economic power; the other half is DECISION-MAKING, and redress reaches it too. Democratisation of economic procedures means widening the circle of people whose voices count when economic rules are written.

Begin with LABOUR LEGISLATION, because the rights to organise, to bargain collectively and to be consulted before retrenchment are democratic rights exercised inside the economy: they turn workers from people that decisions happen TO into people decisions are negotiated WITH. Then PUBLIC PARTICIPATION: parliamentary committees must take written submissions before economic laws pass; municipalities must consult residents when drawing up development plans; regulators must hold hearings before approving electricity tariffs or licence conditions. Whenever a notice invites public comment on a price increase, you are watching this principle operate.

The institution to know in detail is NEDLAC — the National Economic Development and Labour Council. Four constituencies hold seats: GOVERNMENT, ORGANISED BUSINESS, ORGANISED LABOUR, and ORGANISED COMMUNITY, the last representing civic bodies, youth, women's organisations and rural interests. Its defining feature: major labour legislation and significant economic policy are debated and negotiated at NEDLAC BEFORE they reach Parliament. The design principle is social dialogue — consensus built among those who must live with a policy, rather than a policy imposed and then resisted. State its strength and its limit together: agreements arrive with buy-in, which makes them durable; but consensus is slow, and a constituency only speaks as loudly as it is organised, so the weakly organised are quietest exactly where they most need volume.

Finally, SELF-REGULATING BODIES: professional councils for accountants, engineers, health professionals, and industry bodies for advertising and the press, which write and enforce their own codes within a framework the law sets. This devolves regulation to the practitioners who understand the work — with public accountability attached, so self-regulation does not decay into self-protection.

Take this section's questions now: the participation channels, NEDLAC's four constituencies with its function and honest limit, and self-regulation defined with its safeguard.

## Subtopic: Macro-economic Adaptations and Judging Redress

Redress does not float free; it operates inside macro-economic frameworks whose aims have stayed remarkably constant even as the programme names changed. Know the aims as a set: PERFORMANCE — growth strong enough to create jobs and fund the state; EMPLOYMENT as a stated objective, not a hoped-for side effect; equitable DISTRIBUTION, pursued through progressive taxation, social grants and public services in education, health and housing; INTERNAL STABILITY — inflation contained and public debt sustainable, because instability taxes the poor first and hardest; and EXTERNAL STABILITY — a balance of payments and a currency the economy can live with.

Now the tension, which is where evaluation marks live: the aims pull against one another. Redistribution needs revenue; revenue needs growth; growth may call for investment incentives that reward those who already hold capital; stability disciplines the borrowing that ambitious social programmes would require. There is no setting of the dials that maximises everything at once — policy IS the trade-off, and the term-one distinction between positive and normative statements returns here in full force: evidence tells you what each dial does; values tell you where you want it set.

How should thirty years of redress be judged? With both ledgers open. The achievements are genuine and measurable: millions of households connected to water, electricity and housing; grants that hold back destitution; a large Black professional and middle class where the law once forbade one; ownership and senior management that no longer mirror the old exclusions; and an architecture of rights and institutions that did not exist before. The shortfalls are equally genuine: unemployment stuck at levels few economies have ever managed; income and wealth inequality among the most extreme recorded anywhere; land reform behind every target it has set, with transferred farms too often left unsupported; and empowerment benefits that have sometimes pooled among a connected few — the very criticism that pushed the phrase broad-based into the legislation. The disciplined answer writes both columns, attaches mechanisms to each entry, and keeps evidence separate from preference.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five macro-economic aims, the tensions among them, and redress judged on both ledgers with mechanisms named.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic told at ground level — because redress is not a chapter heading in this country; it is the difference between two front doors on the same street.

## Subtopic: Starting the Race at Different Lines

Two young people, both twenty-two, both bright, apply for the same junior post in town. The first borrows his mother's car for the interview, prints his CV at home, and mentions that his uncle knows the manager. The second wakes at four, takes two taxis paid for with borrowed money, and knows nobody inside any building in that town. Same post. Same interview. Different starting lines.

Where did the lines come from? Not from effort, and not from chance. For most of a century, the law itself drew them. The law said who could own a house and who could only rent a matchbox with no title deed. The law said whose school got laboratories and whose got a chalkboard and sixty desks. The law said which families lived near the factories and which were moved two taxi rides away. The law said who could open a shop on the main road. That is what marginalised means: pushed to the edge on purpose, by rules.

Now the step that separates economics from slogans: scrapping those rules did not scrap their results. The house that could never be bought is still not owned, so there is still nothing to borrow against. The grandfather who was denied a trade still had no trade to teach. The family address is still two taxis from the work. Advantage and disadvantage are both inherited — through property, schooling, contacts and location — which is why a country can have equal laws and a deeply unequal economy at the same time. Redress is simply the name for closing the distance between those two facts.

Quick check before we move on — this section's questions are with you now: who was pushed to the edge, by which rules, and why removing the rules did not remove the results.

## Subtopic: Four Things You Need to Build a Livelihood

Meet Naledi. She wants to start an egg business in her village — a few hundred layers, supplying the local shops. Watch what she needs, and you will watch the four factors of production walk past in order — and every redress programme in the country is aimed at one of them.

She needs a PLACE. Her family has worked a plot for two generations, but there is no title deed, so she cannot fence it, build on it with confidence, or use it to raise a loan. Securing that plot is tenure reform. Giving land to a family that never had any is redistribution. Returning land that was taken is restitution. And note the hard lesson the whole country has learned: a plot with no borehole, no feed supplier and no buyer is not yet a business. Land plus support, always.

She needs SKILLS. Nobody in her family was ever allowed near the courses that teach bookkeeping or poultry management. So redress builds the ladder back: learnerships, apprenticeships and technical training paid for through a levy on employers — and employment equity opens the jobs themselves, all the way up to management, so that skill has somewhere to go.

She needs MONEY. Her family owns nothing a bank recognises, so the loan officer smiles and says no. Redress answers on two fronts: ownership — shares, procurement channels that make big buyers purchase from businesses like hers — and finance designed for people without collateral, through development funders and small-business schemes.

She needs the CHANCE TO RUN IT. Registration that does not take six months, a mentor who has done it before, and a first guaranteed order — perhaps a school feeding scheme set aside for local suppliers — because the first customer is the hardest one to find.

Place, skills, money, chance. Four needs, four families of redress. And one test for any programme you are ever asked to judge: did it hand Naledi an object, or did it leave her with a working livelihood?

## Subtopic: Who Sits at the Table

Last idea, and it is about voice: an economy is only fully democratic when the people affected by its decisions help make them.

Start small. When Naledi eventually employs three workers, they have rights her grandmother's generation never had: to join a union, to bargain, to be consulted before retrenchment — labour law giving workers a chair at the employer's table. Step up a level: when her municipality plans next year's spending, it must call meetings and hear residents; when the electricity regulator considers a tariff increase, it must invite objections first. Those notices in the newspaper and on the community hall door are democracy reaching into economics.

Then the national table: NEDLAC. Four chairs — government, organised business, organised labour, and organised community, which is where civic groups, youth structures, women's organisations and rural voices sit. Big economic laws stop at this table before they travel to Parliament, so the arguments happen early, among the people who will live with the outcome. It is deliberately slower than a minister deciding alone — that slowness is the point. And keep the honest footnote: the table hears organised voices best, so the least organised South Africans are also the least heard, which is a real weakness worth naming.

Add self-regulation — engineers, doctors and accountants policing their own professional standards inside the law — and the picture is complete: decisions pushed outward and downward, to workers, residents, constituencies and practitioners.

And then judge the whole project like an economist: both columns, always. Column one, real gains — services and grants reaching millions, a professional class that was once illegal, ownership slowly shifting, rights where there were none. Column two, real shortfalls — unemployment still massive, inequality still extreme, land reform slow and under-supported, benefits sometimes captured by a few. Write both columns with the mechanisms attached and you are doing economics; write only one and you are doing propaganda.

The last questions of the lesson are with you right now: the tables and channels named, and redress judged with both columns open.
