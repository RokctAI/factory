**Thandi:** Questions from the class before the year closes! First one, asked almost every time this topic comes up: wouldn't it be simpler and fairer to skip all these programmes and just give poor households money directly?

**Tutor:** It is a serious proposal — economists debate it constantly — so let us test it properly rather than dismiss it. Cash transfers do one thing superbly: they relieve POVERTY, immediately, with low administration and full respect for the household's own judgement about what it needs. That is why grants already form a central pillar of our system, and the evidence says they work — child nutrition, school attendance and local spending all improve. But now look at what cash does not touch. Redress aims at the STOCKS that generate income year after year: land, skills, business ownership, access to credit and networks. A monthly transfer keeps a family fed while the underlying position — no assets, no qualifications, no collateral — reproduces itself into the next generation. Cash also cannot open a profession that will not hire you, secure a plot you occupy without title, or put your firm into a supply chain that has never bought from anyone like you. So the two instruments answer different questions: transfers answer how does this household survive this month; redress answers why does this household start every month at the back. A mature answer holds both: grants as the floor beneath survival, redress as the ladder toward ownership and earning — and it notes the fiscal link between them, because the taxes that fund the floor depend on how many people the ladder eventually carries into work and business.

**Thandi:** Next question, and it came with some heat: a learner said her neighbour calls empowerment a scheme for the politically connected. How should we answer that in an exam without just picking a side?

**Tutor:** By doing what economics does with every strong claim: separate the mechanism, the evidence and the conclusion. The mechanism the neighbour is pointing at is real and has a name — narrow enrichment. Early empowerment deals often transferred shares to a small circle of prominent individuals, because transacting with famous names was the fastest way for firms to earn points, and the same names recurred deal after deal. That criticism was made loudly INSIDE the policy debate, and it changed the law: the shift to broad-based empowerment widened the scorecard beyond ownership to employment equity, skills spending, supplier development and support for small enterprises, precisely so that benefit would have to flow past the connected circle. So the honest evidence reads: the capture happened, the design responded, and implementation still varies — some sectors show genuinely broad supplier and worker benefit, while fronting, where a firm fakes transformation on paper, remains a prosecuted offence because the temptation remains. The disciplined conclusion is neither the programme is a scam nor the programme is beyond criticism. It is: the objective — spreading productive ownership after generations of legislated exclusion — remains economically sound, and the test of any specific deal is the one from the lesson: did it build livelihoods broadly, or decorate a letterhead? Answer like that and you have shown evaluation, not allegiance.

**Thandi:** Third one: if NEDLAC really lets everyone negotiate before laws are passed, why do we still see strikes and protests every year? Doesn't that mean the table has failed?

**Tutor:** Only if you expected the table to abolish conflict — which was never its promise. Distinguish the levels. NEDLAC negotiates the RULES of the economic game: labour legislation, major policy frameworks. Within those rules, individual unions and employers still bargain over wages firm by firm and sector by sector, and a lawful strike is part of that bargaining machinery working, not evidence of its breakdown — the right to strike is one of the democratic rights the framework deliberately protects. So a wage strike at a mine no more proves NEDLAC failed than a court case proves Parliament failed. The fairer tests of the table are these: are major laws arriving with fewer surprises and more durability because the parties shaped them early? Often, yes — significant labour statutes have held for decades because every constituency owns their fingerprints. Does consensus sometimes produce slow, watered-down outcomes? Also yes — that is the honest cost of the design, and you should say so. And the sharpest critique from the lesson applies here too: the unemployed and the informally employed sit at that table only indirectly, through the community chair, which is the weakest-organised seat. Protest in the streets is often the voice of exactly the people the boardroom hears least. So the mature answer: the table has reduced and structured conflict, it cannot and should not eliminate it, and its real weakness is not the strikes you see but the voices it under-represents.

**Thandi:** Last question of the whole year: we have spent four terms learning how much is wrong — scarcity, unemployment, inequality. Honestly, what was the point, if we're too young to change any of it?

**Tutor:** Let me push back on the premise before answering the question, because too young to change any of it is empirically false in at least three ways. First, the near horizon: within a handful of years, this class will be choosing courses, trades and first jobs — and the single most reliable finding in this whole syllabus is that skills matched to real demand transform an individual's position. Understanding structural unemployment is not abstract for you; it is a map of where the open doors are. Second, the middle horizon: some of you will hire, lend, buy and build. A shop owner who pays fairly, a manager who trains, a buyer who gives a new supplier its first order — these are redress mechanisms operating at household scale, and thousands of such decisions are what the aggregate statistics are made of. Third, the long horizon, and the real answer: every policy this year described — grants, public works, land reform, empowerment, NEDLAC itself — exists because enough citizens understood a problem well enough to demand a mechanism, and then kept demanding that it work. Economies are not weather; they are the sum of rules people chose and can re-choose. What this year gave you is the ability to tell a mechanism from a slogan, a shortage from scarcity, a diagnosis from a blame — and a country's future is decided substantially by how many of its citizens can make exactly those distinctions when it matters. You are not too young to change it. You are, as of this year, equipped to start.
