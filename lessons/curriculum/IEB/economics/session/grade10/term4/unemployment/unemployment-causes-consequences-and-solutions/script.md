# Part 1 — Expert

Term three built the machinery of the labour market — who counts, who hires, what the wage does. This term begins with the machinery's most visible failure: UNEMPLOYMENT, the problem that shadows every other number in South African economics. Today's route: the nature and measurement of unemployment; the five TYPES, because each type has its own cause and therefore its own cure; the causes and consequences, sorted so no level is forgotten; and the three standing approaches to solving it — growing production, public employment programmes, and unemployment insurance. Hold one sentence through everything: this topic is diagnosis before prescription.

## Subtopic: The Nature and Measurement of Unemployment

Definitions first, stated so precisely they cannot leak marks. The STRICT definition counts a person as unemployed if they are of working age, WITHOUT work, AVAILABLE to start, and ACTIVELY SEEKING. The EXPANDED definition adds the DISCOURAGED work-seekers — people who want work but have given up the search, usually because the searching itself costs money and hope. The UNEMPLOYMENT RATE divides the unemployed by the LABOUR FORCE — employed plus unemployed — and never by the whole population; get the denominator wrong and every calculation after it collapses.

Rather than memorising percentages that age, learn the CHARACTER of South African unemployment — six features, each with a consequence. One: it is chiefly STRUCTURAL — it survives upswings, so the business cycle alone cannot explain it. Two: it is YOUTH-HEAVY — rates among people under thirty-five run far above the national figure, and first-time seekers wait longest for a first yes. Three: it is SKILL-SORTED — joblessness collapses as qualifications rise; the economy queues for artisans and engineers while queues of unskilled workers stand unmet. Four: it is GEOGRAPHIC — deepest in the former homelands and outer townships, where apartheid planning parked people far from economic centres. Five: it is LONG-DURATION — a large share of the unemployed have searched for over a year, and every month out of work rusts skills, thins networks and drains confidence, making the next month harder. Six: it carries a wide DISCOURAGEMENT band, which is why the expanded rate sits several percentage points above the strict one — and why you must always ask which measure a headline is quoting.

Two implications before moving on. Growth without labour-absorption will not clear this: an economy can expand through machines and skilled sectors while the queue stands still. And because the young and the long-term unemployed are the core of the problem, remedies must reach people the market currently walks past.

## Subtopic: The Types of Unemployment

Sort unemployment by CAUSE, because the cure always follows the cause. Five types; for each, own the definition, an example and the matched remedy.

FRICTIONAL: the short gap between jobs in a working labour market — the graduate whose post starts in three months, the clerk who resigned to search for better. Partly voluntary, always temporary, present in every healthy economy because matching takes time. Remedy: better INFORMATION — vacancy platforms, employment centres, career guidance. You shorten the search; you neither can nor should abolish it.

STRUCTURAL: the deep MISMATCH between what workers offer and what employers need — in skills, or in location, or both. The machinist whose factory closed and whose craft no growing industry wants; the school-leaver in a district whose only advertisements ask for experience she has never been allowed to gain; the willing worker three taxis from the nearest industrial park. This is South Africa's dominant type, and it does not respond to job boards or upswings — only to structural medicine: education and technical training, retraining for the displaced, and policies that shrink the physical distance between people and work.

CYCLICAL: the downswing arriving at the payroll. Orders fall, production follows, retrenchments follow that — term one's business cycle translated into empty overalls. Remedy: restored DEMAND — public spending, easier credit, export growth — because when buying returns, the derived demand for labour returns with it.

SEASONAL: work with a calendar — harvests, festive-season retail, tourism's high months. Predictable and recurring. Remedies: diversifying local economies and building off-season income or training.

TECHNOLOGICAL: tasks absorbed by machines — the ticket clerk replaced by an app, the packhouse line replaced by a sorter. It recurs with every generation of technology, and its remedy matches structural's: retrain toward the work machines create and cannot do, rather than mourning the work they removed.

Pause for this section's questions now — five types diagnosed from scenarios, each with cause and matching remedy.

## Subtopic: Causes and Consequences

CAUSES first, stacked in layers so an essay can walk down them. The historical-structural layer: an economy assembled around capital-intensive mining and heavy industry, which generates few jobs per rand of output; a schooling system that still under-produces the technical skills employers advertise for; and the spatial design that placed labour far from work — geography as an inherited cost. The demand layer: growth too slow to absorb each year's new work-seekers; investment hesitant; downswings that destroy jobs faster than recoveries rebuild them. The supply layer: a young population delivering large new cohorts to the labour market every January. And the friction layer: job information that does not reach the township or the village, search costs — data, transport, printing — that tax the poorest seekers hardest, and the network decay that long unemployment inflicts.

CONSEQUENCES next, sorted across three levels so none is dropped. For the INDIVIDUAL and the household: income lost and poverty entrenched; dependence on grants and relatives; skills and confidence eroding with each idle month; and the documented toll on health, dignity and family life — joblessness is a condition people live inside, not just a statistic they appear in. For the ECONOMY: output permanently forgone — the country operating inside its production possibility curve; weaker aggregate demand as the jobless cannot spend, which feeds back into weaker production; a fiscus squeezed from both ends, collecting less tax while needing more social spending; and the future capacity loss as unused skills decay. For SOCIETY: deeper poverty and inequality; the exclusion of a generation of young people from adult economic life; and the strain that prolonged, concentrated joblessness places on social stability everywhere it occurs.

And name the LOOP that makes it self-feeding: no wage means no spending, no spending means no orders, no orders mean no hiring. Every serious solution is an attempt to cut that loop somewhere.

Take this section's questions now — causes by layer, consequences by level, and the loop stated in one breath.

## Subtopic: Approaches to Solving Unemployment

Three standing approaches; each attacks a different part of the problem, and none suffices alone.

GROWTH OF PRODUCTION. More output requires more hands — derived demand working in your favour for once. The policy set: investment in productive capacity, infrastructure that cuts the cost of doing business, deliberate support for LABOUR-ABSORBING sectors — agriculture, tourism, construction, personal services — plus small-enterprise development and export promotion. Strength: growth-created jobs are durable and self-financing — a wage paid by a customer, not a budget. Limit: the COMPOSITION of growth decides everything. A capital-intensive boom can lift output impressively while hiring almost nobody; growth helps the queue only when it is the kind of growth that needs people.

PUBLIC EMPLOYMENT PROGRAMMES. The state hires directly for labour-intensive work — maintaining roads, clearing waterways, caring for public spaces, assisting in schools and clinics — with the Expanded Public Works Programme as the standing national example. Strengths: immediate income into households that have none; a first work record for people the formal market keeps refusing; and public assets built or maintained. Limits, stated honestly: the posts are temporary and modestly paid; every rand is a budget rand with an opportunity cost; and unless the programme connects onward — to a skill, a certificate, a next employer — it relieves symptoms without touching causes. Judge it as a BRIDGE, and ask always what the bridge lands on.

UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE. The Unemployment Insurance Fund collects one percent of earnings from the worker and a matching one percent from the employer, and pays temporary benefits to contributors who lose work — with maternity, illness and dependant benefits attached. Strengths: it softens the income shock, buys time for a proper job search, and steadies household spending in downswings, which cushions the circular flow itself. Limits: benefits are partial and time-bound — and the fund reaches ONLY former contributors. The young person who has never held a formal job, the largest single face in South Africa's queue, stands entirely outside it.

The mature close: DIAGNOSE, then prescribe. Cyclical joblessness answers to demand; frictional to information; structural — our dominant strain — answers only to skills, mobility and labour-absorbing growth, with public programmes bridging and insurance cushioning along the way. Any answer proposing one instrument for all five types has failed the diagnosis.

The final questions of this part are with you now: three approaches with strengths and limits, and remedies matched to diagnosed types.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from inside it — because in this country unemployment is not an abstract noun; it lives in nearly every family WhatsApp group, and understanding it clearly is a form of respect.

## Subtopic: The Queue That Doesn't Move

When a new supermarket opened outside town last year, it advertised forty posts. By the closing date, more than three thousand applications had arrived. Three thousand. For forty. Stand in that number for a moment: it means two thousand nine hundred and sixty letters of regret — most of them sent to people under thirty, holding certificates, who did everything they were told to do.

That pile of applications is the statistics made visible, and four things about it explain the whole topic. It is YOUNG: the front of South Africa's unemployment numbers is people under thirty-five, most seeking a FIRST job — and trapped in the oldest loop there is: every post wants experience, and experience needs a post. It is SPLIT BY SKILL: across town, an engineering workshop has re-advertised the same artisan post three times — three thousand applications for shelf-packing, three for millwrighting. Same economy, opposite queues. It is MAPPED BY HISTORY: in some districts the nearest real employer is two taxi rides away, and the fare to submit one CV costs a family's supper. And it is LONG: many applicants have been applying for over a year — and each empty month rusts a skill, cools a contact, and makes the next interview harder to walk into.

One more truth, and it is the one the headlines skip: some people no longer apply at all. Not because they found something — because a hundred silences taught them to stop. The STRICT count loses sight of them the day they stop searching; only the EXPANDED count still sees them. So whenever you hear the rate, ask which count — the people who stopped applying are not the smallest part of this problem; they are its deepest part.

## Subtopic: Five Different Reasons for the Same Empty Hands

Here is the error that wrecks most arguments about unemployment: speaking about it as ONE thing with one villain and one fix. Empty hands hold at least five different stories, and mixing them up guarantees the wrong medicine.

Story one: Lerato finished her diploma in June; her internship starts in September. For three months she is unemployed — but only in the GAP between finishing and starting. Every economy contains people mid-hop. Fix: faster matching — platforms, career offices. Nothing is broken.

Story two — the South African headline: Jabu operated a machine for eleven years until the plant relocated; nothing within a hundred kilometres wants his particular skill. Or Zanele, whose brand-new matric certificate meets a town where every advert demands three years' experience. Neither laziness nor a bad quarter explains them — their skills and their places do not MATCH what employers need and where. That is structural unemployment, and no job advert cures it. Only training, trades, transport and time do.

Story three: Pieter's construction crew was cut when the downturn froze building projects across the province. His job is not gone forever — it is gone until cranes return. Fix: demand — when the economy spends again, crews are rehired.

Story four: Naomi packs fruit from December to April, then the packhouse locks until the next season. Predictable, yearly, calendar-shaped. Fix: something for the other seven months — different local work, or training scheduled into the quiet.

Story five: Sizwe collected tickets until an app collected them instead. The machine did not end work; it ended his TASK. Fix: the same as story two — retrain toward what the machine still cannot do.

Five stories. Five different fixes. Anyone selling one cure for all five — whatever the cure — has skipped the diagnosis, and diagnosis is the entire skill.

## Subtopic: Bridges, Engines and Nets

Last stretch: what actually helps — with the honest limits attached, because false promises are their own kind of harm.

The ENGINE is growth. When production rises, hiring follows — that is derived demand, and it is real. But interrogate the KIND of growth before celebrating it: a highly mechanised expansion can add billions to output and mere dozens to payrolls, while a farm, a guesthouse, a building site or a busy small-business street hires many hands per rand. So never ask only is the economy growing — ask is it growing in ways that need people. That is why support for small firms, agriculture, tourism and construction carries such weight in every serious jobs plan.

The BRIDGE is public employment. The state pays people directly to do needed work — patch roads, clear storm drains, assist at clinics and schools. Real money reaches households that had none; a first payslip starts a CV that had no first line; and the community keeps the repaired road afterwards. But keep both eyes open: the posts are short and the pay modest; the money is tax money with an opportunity cost; and a bridge that ends in mid-air — no skill gained, no next job connected — must be redesigned, not just repainted. A bridge is judged by where it lands.

The NET is the UIF. One percent from every payslip, matched by the employer, pools into a fund that pays you for a stretch if you lose your job — so that a retrenchment does not become an eviction, and so that spending does not vanish from the local shops overnight. Necessary, genuinely. And honestly holed: you can only claim against contributions from a job you HAD. The three thousand applicants at that supermarket who have never been employed have never contributed a cent — and can never claim one. The largest group in the queue stands exactly where the net is not.

So: an engine that must grow the labour-hungry way, a bridge that must land somewhere, and a net with its hole where the young stand. Hold all three with their limits and you can talk about this subject the way it deserves — precisely.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: engine, bridge and net matched to what each fixes and what each misses, and one honest paragraph on why no single answer is enough. Study this one properly — some of you will spend careers shortening that queue.
