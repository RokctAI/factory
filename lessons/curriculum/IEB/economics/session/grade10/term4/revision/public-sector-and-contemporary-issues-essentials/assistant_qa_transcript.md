**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the ceiling diagram says shortages and black markets, but during the pandemic the government capped prices on masks and sanitiser and most people supported it. Was that bad economics?

**Tutor:** It was defensible economics — provided you can say precisely WHY, because the case for a ceiling is conditional, never general. Run the conditions. First, the shock was sudden and temporary: demand for masks exploded overnight while supply needed weeks to respond, and in that window sellers could charge almost anything — rationing by price would have meant the nurse and the pensioner going without while the wealthy stockpiled. A cap swapped rationing by price for rationing by availability, which society judged fairer for an essential in an emergency. Second, the duration was short: the danger of ceilings is cumulative — the longer a price is pinned below equilibrium, the more suppliers exit, cut corners or divert stock to back channels, so a cap that is tolerable for three months becomes destructive over three years. Third — and this is the step most answers miss — the real solution was always on the SUPPLY side, and it arrived: factories retooled, imports flowed, supply shifted right, and the market price fell below the old cap, at which point the ceiling stopped binding and quietly became irrelevant. That is the textbook life cycle of a good ceiling: shield briefly, while supply is mobilised to make the shield unnecessary. So the mature answer is neither ceilings are bad nor price limits are kind: it is a ceiling below equilibrium always creates shortage pressure; whether it is justified depends on the essentialness of the good, the length of the intervention, and whether supply-side repair is underway. Conditions, not slogans — that is how the evaluation marks are earned.

**Thandi:** Next: if money is just whatever everyone accepts, what stops it failing? My gran keeps saying money was worth more in her day — was it?

**Tutor:** Your gran is remembering something real, and the story stage about money explains it exactly. Money has no value of its own — a hundred-rand note is paper; the value lives in shared confidence that everyone else will accept it tomorrow at roughly today's worth. That confidence is maintained, not automatic: it is why the stage where GOVERNMENT ENTERS matters so much — a central authority issues the currency, limits how much of it exists, and stands behind it. Now connect that to the four functions. When prices rise year after year — inflation — money keeps doing its go-between job almost indefinitely: notes still change hands. But its STORE OF VALUE job erodes first: the hundred rand your gran saved in her day buys a fraction of what it did, because every year of rising prices quietly shaved its purchasing power. That is the precise sense in which money was worth more then — not that the notes were different, but that each rand commanded more goods. Watch economies where inflation runs completely loose and you see the functions fail in order: first saving in that currency becomes foolish, then pricing in it becomes impossible as numbers change daily, and finally people refuse it as a medium altogether and retreat to barter or foreign currency — the whole evolution of markets running in REVERSE. That reversal is the strongest proof of the story you revised: money is not a thing but a working agreement, and the agreement needs guarding. It is also why stable prices are treated as a national priority rather than a technical detail — the alternative is Sipho back at the riverbank, holding a goat.

**Thandi:** Third one: the dispute ladder sounds slow — talks, then conciliation, then arbitration, then court. Wouldn't everything be faster if disagreements just went straight to a judge?

**Tutor:** Faster for one dispute, perhaps — catastrophic for the system, and the design teaches something deep about how economies handle conflict. Consider what the ladder actually optimises. The bottom rungs are CHEAP and CONSENSUAL: bargaining and conciliation cost little, keep the relationship alive, and produce settlements both sides chose — which they then implement willingly, without appeals. Courts are the opposite: expensive, slow, adversarial, and they end with a winner and a loser who must still work together on Monday. Route every wage disagreement, every dismissal dispute, every shift-roster quarrel straight to judges and three things happen: the courts silt up within months, so justice slows for everyone; legal costs price workers — the weaker party the law exists to protect — out of the process entirely; and workplaces learn to litigate rather than negotiate, poisoning exactly the relationships production depends on. The ladder inverts all three: the CCMA's conciliation resolves most disputes at almost no cost within weeks; arbitration catches most of the rest with a binding ruling that avoids court formality; and the Labour Court handles only what genuinely needs law decided — its scarce judicial time reserved for questions that set precedent. Notice the economics hiding in the design: it is the principle of allocating a SCARCE resource — judicial capacity — to its highest-valued use, the same scarcity logic from the first week of the year, applied to justice itself. And the procedural rule you revised has the same shape: a strike after the ladder has been climbed is protected precisely because the system wants pressure released through channels it can see. So: not slow — sequenced. The ladder is what fast looks like when everyone must use it at once.

**Thandi:** Last one: this sweep covered the state in markets, and unemployment, and redress. In the exam they feel like three separate topics — is there one thread that ties them together, so it all stays in my head?

**Tutor:** There is exactly one thread, and holding it will organise not just this sweep but the whole year: EVERY INTERVENTION IS A TRADE-OFF, AND THE MARKS LIVE IN NAMING BOTH SIDES. Watch it run through everything we revised. The tax on sugary drinks: public health gained, but a market shrunk and jobs in it squeezed. The zero-rating of staples: the poor household's basket cheapened, but revenue forgone that clinics and classrooms needed — opportunity cost, term one's oldest idea, standing inside a VAT table. The ceiling: affordability won today, shortage and black market bred tomorrow. The floor and the minimum wage: dignity for the employed, pressure on the number employed. The labour statutes: protection and voice for workers, cost and caution for the small employer. Public works: income and experience now, budget spent that cannot be spent twice. The UIF: a net under the retrenched, nothing beneath the never-employed. Redress: assets and access moved toward the excluded, with the standing risk of capture and the constant need for support conditions. Not one policy on that list is free, and not one is worthless — each buys something society values by paying something society also values. Second-rate answers pick a side and cheer; first-rate answers state what is bought, what is paid, who carries each, and then judge whether the purchase is worth it under the given conditions. That single sentence structure — bought, paid, who, worth it — will convert nearly every evaluation question this examination can ask. Carry the thread, and the three topics walk in as one.
