# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the second revision sweep before your final examination — the material of terms two, three and four gathered into four structures. Structure one: the state's instruments in the market — taxes, subsidies, ceilings and floors. Structure two: the long road from barter to money to industry. Structure three: the labour force, labour law and the machinery for disputes. Structure four: the twin contemporary issues — unemployment and economic redress. Every definition sharpened, every diagram rebuilt in words, every list welded to the mechanism that makes it worth marks.

## Subtopic: The State in the Market — Taxes, Subsidies and Price Controls

The state enters markets when their outcomes collide with what society will accept: essentials priced beyond the poor, producers wiped out by volatility, harmful goods flourishing. Four instruments, each with a verbal diagram you must be able to rebuild under pressure.

A TAX on a product loads an extra cost onto every unit sold, shifting the supply curve LEFT and upward by the amount of the tax. Result: higher price, smaller quantity, with the burden split between buyers and sellers. This is why the excise on cigarettes and the levy on sugary drinks exist — the state deliberately shrinking a market it distrusts. A SUBSIDY runs the mechanism in reverse: the state carries part of the cost, supply shifts RIGHT and downward, price falls, quantity grows. The VAT system uses the same logic in its zero-rating: staples such as brown bread, maize meal and rice carry no VAT while most goods carry the standard fifteen percent — a targeted cheapening of the poor household's basket.

A MAXIMUM PRICE — a CEILING — is pinned BELOW equilibrium to keep an essential affordable. Rebuild the diagram in words: at the ceiling, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, and the gap is a SHORTAGE that will not close, because the price is legally forbidden from rising to close it. The consequences follow like clockwork: queues, rationing schemes, and a black market where the good changes hands illegally above the ceiling. And a technical point that separates strong candidates: a ceiling set ABOVE equilibrium binds nothing — the market never reaches it.

A MINIMUM PRICE — a FLOOR — is pinned ABOVE equilibrium to protect producers. At the floor, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a standing SURPLUS the state must buy up, store, or export at a loss. The MINIMUM WAGE is the labour market's floor, and it must always be argued with both hands: it lifts the earnings of workers who remain employed, and, set above the market-clearing wage, it can reduce the number of workers firms hire. Protection and pressure, in the same instrument.

Hold it there — this section's questions are with you now: each instrument's shift or line placed correctly, ceilings breeding shortages, floors breeding surpluses, and the minimum wage argued from both sides.

## Subtopic: From Barter to Industry — the Evolution of Markets

Structure two is a story in five stages, and the marks sit in knowing what PROBLEM each stage solved.

Stage one, SELF-SUFFICIENCY: every household produces all it consumes. No surplus, no exchange, no market — and no specialisation, which is why self-sufficient societies stay poor. Stage two, SURPLUS and BARTER: improved tools yield more than the household needs, and the excess seeks a trade. But barter drags an anchor called the DOUBLE COINCIDENCE OF WANTS: exchange happens only when each party wants exactly what the other offers, at the same time and place. The herder with a goat to trade must find a grain farmer who wants a goat this very week — so trade stays small, slow and local.

Stage three, MONEY: the anchor cut loose. Money is whatever a community generally accepts in exchange, and its four FUNCTIONS carry the marks: MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE — it dissolves the double coincidence, because everyone accepts it; UNIT OF ACCOUNT — one measuring rod prices everything from goats to grain; STORE OF VALUE — this season's surplus can wait safely for next season; STANDARD OF DEFERRED PAYMENT — debts can be written down and settled later, which makes credit and contracts possible. With money working, markets stretch from the village square to the whole world.

Stage four, GOVERNMENT ENTERS: expanding markets need what no trader supplies alone — a trusted currency, enforced contracts, honest weights and measures, roads and courts — and the state taxes trade to fund them. Markets and government grow together, each enabling the other.

Stage five, INDUSTRIALISATION: machines and factories multiply output per worker; wage labour replaces family production; towns swell. South Africa entered this stage through its MINERAL REVOLUTION — first diamonds, then gold — which dragged railways, foreign capital and hundreds of thousands of workers inland, built an economy centred on mining, and entrenched the migrant labour system whose scars the redress topic still traces today.

Pause for this section's questions now: five stages in order, each with its problem solved, the double coincidence explained through the herder, and money's four functions.

## Subtopic: Population, Labour and the Rules of Work

Structure three begins with careful counting. The working-age population spans ages fifteen to sixty-four. The LABOUR FORCE — the economically active — contains everyone in that span who is either employed or unemployed AND actively seeking. Learners, full-time homemakers, retirees and the discouraged stand outside the fence. Then let the market price what the labour force sells: DEMAND for labour comes from employers, and it is DERIVED demand — a bakery hires because customers buy bread, never for the pleasure of hiring — while SUPPLY comes from households offering hours. Where they meet, the wage forms; scarce skills clear at high wages, abundant unskilled labour queues at low ones.

Next, the rules — which exist because a single work-seeker facing a large employer negotiates from weakness, and the law deliberately rebalances the contest. Three statutes anchor the topic. The BASIC CONDITIONS OF EMPLOYMENT ACT lays the floor under every job: limits on ordinary working hours, rights to leave, notice periods, and prohibitions on child labour. The LABOUR RELATIONS ACT writes the rules of the collective contest: workers may form and join unions, bargain collectively, and strike under proper procedure — while employers hold the counterweight of the lock-out. The EMPLOYMENT EQUITY ACT polices fairness in appointment and promotion: unfair discrimination prohibited, and measures required to correct the exclusions the past engineered.

Finally, when negotiation deadlocks, the dispute climbs a LADDER rather than descending into chaos. Unions and employers bargain — often within bargaining councils that cover whole sectors. If talks fail, the CCMA offers CONCILIATION: a commissioner mediates toward settlement. If conciliation fails, ARBITRATION: the commissioner hears both sides and issues a binding ruling. Questions of law go further, to the Labour Court. A strike that has followed the procedure is protected; one that skips the ladder is not.

Stop for this section's questions now — the labour force fenced precisely, demand for labour derived not direct, three Acts matched to three jobs, and the ladder climbed rung by rung.

## Subtopic: Unemployment and Economic Redress

Structure four, part one: unemployment stripped to its load-bearing walls. Definitions: the STRICT measure counts those without work, available for it, and actively seeking; the EXPANDED measure adds the discouraged, who want work but have stopped searching. The RATE divides the unemployed by the labour force — never by the whole population. The FIVE TYPES matter because each names its own remedy: FRICTIONAL, the normal gap between jobs — remedied by faster information and matching; STRUCTURAL, the mismatch of skills and places against what employers need — South Africa's dominant type, remedied only by education, training and shrinking the distance between people and work; CYCLICAL, the downswing's retrenchments — remedied by restored demand; SEASONAL, work that exists only part of the year — remedied by diversification and off-season alternatives; TECHNOLOGICAL, tasks taken over by machines — remedied by retraining toward what machines still cannot do. Consequences sort across three levels — the person, the economy, society — and the three standing responses are growth of production for durable jobs, public employment programmes as a bridge, and unemployment insurance as a net that catches only former contributors.

Part two: redress, organised by the FOUR FACTORS OF PRODUCTION, because what exclusion removed was access to the factors themselves. LAND: restitution for the dispossessed, redistribution for those never allowed in, tenure security for those occupying without rights. LABOUR: employment equity reaching into management, and levy-funded skills development. CAPITAL: broad-based ownership, preferential procurement, and finance for the collateral-less. ENTREPRENEURSHIP: training, funding, lighter regulation and first-customer set-asides for small business. The evaluation sentence that lifts an answer: an instrument without its support fails — land without finance, a qualification without a vacancy, a tender without working capital — so judge every programme by the livelihood it leaves, not the object it hands over.

And the decision-making layer completes it: unions and labour law give workers a voice; hearings give communities one; and NEDLAC seats government, business, labour and community before major economic legislation reaches Parliament.

The final questions of this part are with you now: definitions and types diagnosed from scenarios, remedies matched to types, redress argued factor by factor, and the table of decision-makers named.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ground at walking pace: a referee at a school tournament, a herder who invented shopping, and one factory gate where three terms of economics queue together.

## Subtopic: Referees, Ceilings and Floors

A school soccer tournament with no referee is not freedom — it is shin-kicking. Markets are the same game: mostly self-running, occasionally vicious, and so the state walks on with a whistle. It has four moves, and each one has a consequence you should see coming before it arrives.

Move one: make something dearer on purpose. Slap a tax on it — cigarettes, sugary drinks — and the till price climbs, so less of it sells. The state shrinking a market it distrusts. Move two: make something cheaper on purpose. Subsidise it, or strip the VAT off it — which is why brown bread, maize meal and rice carry no VAT while most things carry fifteen percent. The state widening a market it wants the poorest household inside.

Move three: the CEILING. The state pins a price down, below where the market would have set it, so an essential stays reachable. But watch the mechanics: at the pinned-down price, buyers multiply and sellers shrink back — and the space between them becomes a queue outside the shop. Ceilings make queues. Then rationing. Then the side door, where the desperate quietly pay more than the law allows. The whistle stopped the price; it could not stop the shortage.

Move four: the FLOOR. The state pins a price up, above the market's level, so producers stay standing. Now sellers pile goods high while buyers step back — floors make piles, and somebody must buy, store or export the pile. Put the floor under wages instead of maize and you have the minimum wage: genuine protection for every worker who keeps a job, and — say the second half every time — a higher price on labour can mean fewer workers hired. Two truths, one instrument, both required.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are with you right now: the four moves, why ceilings make queues and floors make piles, and the minimum wage in two honest halves.

## Subtopic: From Swapping Goats to Swiping Cards

Begin with a herder named Sipho, three centuries back, standing at a riverbank with a fat goat and a problem. His family needs grain. The grain farmer across the river needs a plough blade, not a goat. The blacksmith upstream would take a goat — but needs no grain to give. Sipho walks home with his goat. That wasted afternoon has a technical name — the DOUBLE COINCIDENCE OF WANTS — and it is the reason pure barter economies stay small: every trade needs two people whose wants mirror each other exactly, at the same moment.

Then somebody's clever idea ends the walking: agree on ONE thing everyone will always accept. Cattle served first; cowrie shells, gold and coins followed; printed notes after that; today it is a card tapped or a phone code — the form keeps changing, the job never does. And it is really four jobs at once. The GO-BETWEEN: Sipho sells the goat for money and buys grain anywhere, from anyone. The MEASURING ROD: one scale prices the goat, the grain and the plough blade, so bargaining has numbers. The STORAGE JAR: this year's fat season can wait, as money, for next year's thin one. The PROMISE-KEEPER: buy now, pay at month-end — credit exists because money lets a debt be written down.

With money working, the market outgrows the riverbank — and two newcomers arrive. First the chief, in modern dress the STATE: minting the coins and guarding their value, enforcing the promises, checking the scales, taking a share in tax, and building the roads and courts the traders need. Then the machines: factories doing in an hour what hands did in a month, pulling people from fields into towns — and in South Africa the pull came from underground, as diamonds and gold dragged railways, money and workers inland and built our economy around the mines.

Take this section's questions now — Sipho's wasted afternoon, money's four jobs, and the chief and the machines arriving in the right order.

## Subtopic: The Gate, the Team and the Starting Line

One factory gate, first light. Everything from terms three and four is standing in that image — walk through it in four looks.

Look one: who counts as the team? Everyone from fifteen to sixty-four who is working or genuinely hunting for work — that is the labour force. Your classmate in school, the granny on pension, the parent home by choice: outside the fence, and never counted as unemployed. The wage at the gate is a price like any other, set where the employers' need meets the workers' offer — and remember the employers' need is second-hand: the factory hires when its orders grow, never before.

Look two: the rules pinned to the gate. Because one work-seeker against one big employer is no contest, the law evens it: a floor of basic conditions under every contract — hours, leave, notice, no child labour; the right to combine into a union and bargain with one voice, and to strike by the book; and a ban on unfair discrimination in hiring and promotion. When talks jam, there is a ladder on the wall: a peacemaker first, a referee with a binding decision second, a specialist court above both.

Look three: the queue is really five queues wearing one face. The matriculant between school and a January start — a gap, not a crisis. The retrenched mine worker whose skill fits nothing within a hundred kilometres — the mismatch, our country's heaviest queue, unmoved by adverts, moved only by training and transport. The worker cut in a downswing, who returns when orders do. The fruit-season worker in winter. The cashier whose till became a screen — retraining, or nothing. Five diagnoses, five prescriptions; one-size answers fail them all.

Look four: the starting line behind the queue. Some arrived at this gate carrying title deeds, savings and surnames the manager knows; others arrived carrying the opposite inheritance, because the law once decided who could own, learn and live where. Redress is the answer under construction: land returned, secured or opened; skills built; ownership and finance unlocked; small businesses walked to their first customer — each instrument needing its partner, because the field without seed money is still not a farm. And above it all, the big table where government, business, labour and community argue the rules before Parliament writes them.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the team fenced, the rules and the ladder, five queues diagnosed, and redress argued factor by factor. Carry the gate into the examination — most of what it can ask is standing somewhere in that queue.
