**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: a few of us keep writing that scarcity will end once the economy grows enough. It got marked wrong — why exactly?

**Tutor:** Because growth moves the boundary; it never removes the boundary — and that distinction is the whole first term in one sentence. Picture the production possibility curve you revised: growth is the curve shifting outward, so combinations that were dreams become possible. But the new, bigger curve still has an edge, and at that edge the old questions return with full force: more hospitals or more housing, more teachers or more trains. Notice that the richest economies on earth still hold budget debates every single year — if wealth ended scarcity, those debates would have quietly disappeared decades ago. Instead they get fiercer, because richer societies discover richer wants: faster networks, cleaner energy, longer retirements. What growth DOES end, sometimes, is a specific SHORTAGE — a shortage is one market, one price, one moment, and it is curable by more supply or a higher price. Scarcity is the permanent backdrop those shortages play out against. So write it this way: growth relieves particular shortages and expands options, but scarcity — limited resources against unlimited wants — survives every growth rate ever recorded. And its shadow survives with it: even in the wealthiest economy, every choice still carries an opportunity cost, because the next best alternative never stops existing. Separate the curable from the permanent and this question type never costs you again.

**Thandi:** Next one: in the egg example, the price went up AND the quantity went down. But last term someone showed us a case where price went up and quantity went UP. How can both be true?

**Tutor:** Beautifully spotted — and the answer is the most powerful diagnostic in the subject: ask WHICH curve moved. Case one, the avian flu: the SUPPLY curve shifted left. The demand curve stayed put, so the market slid up along an unchanged demand curve — higher price, smaller quantity. Case two, from last term: suppose a health craze makes everyone want eggs. Now the DEMAND curve shifts right while supply stays put, and the market slides up along the supply curve — higher price, LARGER quantity. Same rising price, opposite quantity stories, because different curves did the moving. This is why the price went up is never accepted as an answer on its own — the price is the visible symptom, and the curves are the diagnosis underneath. Here is the method to lock in: read the scenario and find the FIRST event. Ask: does it change sellers' costs or conditions? Then supply moved. Does it change buyers' income, tastes or alternatives? Then demand moved. Only after naming the curve and its direction do you trace price and quantity, and the trace will never betray you. One extra tip for tables and diagrams: when both price and quantity rose, demand shifted right; when price rose but quantity fell, supply shifted left. Those two fingerprints identify the culprit instantly — most learners never notice the fingerprints, and the ones who do collect the marks.

**Thandi:** Third question: my brother works as a mechanic from our yard, cash only. Is he inside GDP or outside it? And does it matter?

**Tutor:** A perfect real-world test of the measurement section — let us apply the definitions honestly. In PRINCIPLE, your brother belongs in GDP: he produces final services, within the borders, this year — every box ticked. In PRACTICE, statisticians can only count what leaves a record: invoices, VAT returns, payrolls, till data. A cash mechanic in a yard leaves almost none, so agencies ESTIMATE informal activity through surveys and assumptions — and estimates undercount. Some of his work therefore enters the number approximately, and some vanishes from it entirely. Does it matter? Enormously, on three levels. For accuracy: South Africa has a large informal sector, so our published GDP understates true production, and understates it MORE in exactly the townships and villages where informal work dominates — meaning the statistics make poorer communities look less productive than they are. For policy: if officials plan only from recorded activity, they under-provide for traders, mechanics and stokvels — infrastructure, banking, training — because the spreadsheet cannot see them. For your exams: this is the limitation of national accounts figures made flesh — alongside unpaid household work, distribution and environmental costs, the uncounted informal economy is a standard evaluation point, and a lived example like your brother's workshop, used precisely, is worth more than a memorised list. So: he is real production, partially visible, and the gap between real and visible is itself an economic fact worth writing about.

**Thandi:** Last one: with the exam coming, should we actually care which indicators are leading or lagging, or is that just a list to memorise the night before?

**Tutor:** Care about it — it is the difference between reading the economy and reading about it, and it is also where easy marks hide. Start with why the classes exist. Decisions face FORWARD: a firm deciding whether to build, a bank setting rates, a matriculant choosing a field — all of them need tomorrow's economy, not yesterday's. But most statistics describe the past. So economists sorted indicators by their timing. LEADING indicators move before the economy turns, because they capture commitments made today for activity tomorrow: building plans approved this month become construction jobs next year; new orders placed now become factory shifts later. COINCIDENT indicators — retail sales, electricity consumption, current production — tell you the economy's speed right now. LAGGING indicators move after the turn, and unemployment is the textbook case for a mechanical reason worth writing in any answer: firms retrench only after a downswing has squeezed them for months, and rehire only after an upswing has convinced them it will last — so the labour market confirms history rather than predicting anything. Now the practical payoffs. In the exam: classification questions are pure method — ask does this statistic capture a commitment to future activity, the present pace, or a delayed reaction, and the class names itself; no midnight memorising required. Beyond the exam: when a headline says unemployment worsened, you will know that is the rearview mirror — and you will look for the building plans to see the road ahead. That habit — checking the headlights before believing the mirror — is what this whole revision sweep was for.
