# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the first of two revision sweeps before your final examination: everything from terms one and two, rebuilt as four load-bearing structures rather than forty scattered pages. Structure one, the economic problem and the curve that draws it. Structure two, the circular flow and the national accounts. Structure three, demand, supply and equilibrium. Structure four, markets, value and the business cycle. Nothing today is new — today is about compression, order, and the precise sentences that separate a pass from a distinction.

## Subtopic: Scarcity, Choice and the Economic Problem

Begin at the foundation. Economics studies how societies with LIMITED resources satisfy UNLIMITED wants — and the crucial word is permanent. SCARCITY is not a bad season or an empty shelf; it is the standing condition of every economy, rich or poor, because wants regenerate faster than resources ever can. From scarcity comes CHOICE, and from choice comes OPPORTUNITY COST — defined with surgical precision as the value of the NEXT BEST alternative sacrificed. Not the sum of everything forgone: the single best thing. A learner who takes a Saturday shift at the car wash gives up sleep, series and soccer — but if the soccer trial mattered most, the opportunity cost of the shift is the trial, and only the trial.

Every society answers THREE fundamental questions: WHAT to produce with its resources, HOW to combine labour, capital and technology to produce it, and FOR WHOM the output is destined. Keep the two lenses distinct as well: MICROECONOMICS examines single decision-makers — a household budgeting, a firm pricing, one market clearing; MACROECONOMICS examines the aggregates — national output, total employment, the general price level.

Now the diagram, described so completely you can redraw it blind. The PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE puts one product on each axis — say fruit on the vertical and furniture on the horizontal. The curve bows outward between the axes, tracing every MAXIMUM combination available when all resources are fully and efficiently employed. Read positions like a map. ON the curve: efficiency — nothing idle. INSIDE the curve: underutilisation — workers unemployed, machines dark; South Africa, with unemployment above thirty percent, produces inside its curve every single day. BEYOND the curve: impossible with today's resources. And the CURVE ITSELF moves outward when resources expand or technology improves — that outward march is the picture of economic growth. The slope carries meaning too: moving along the curve toward more furniture surrenders fruit, and that surrender is opportunity cost drawn as geometry.

Hold it there — this section's questions are with you now: scarcity separated from shortage, opportunity cost defined by the next best alternative, the three questions, and every position on, inside and beyond the curve.

## Subtopic: The Circular Flow and the National Numbers

Structure two. Strip the economy to two participants: HOUSEHOLDS, who own all factors of production, and FIRMS, who hire them. Households sell labour, land, capital and enterprise on the FACTOR market and receive wages, rent, interest and profit; firms transform the factors into goods and services and sell them back on the PRODUCT market. Two loops spin in opposite directions — money one way, goods and factors the other — and the economy is that spinning.

But the loop is not sealed. Three LEAKAGES drain spending out of it: SAVING, which parks income instead of spending it; TAXATION, which routes income to the state; and IMPORTS, which send spending to foreign producers. Three INJECTIONS pump spending in: INVESTMENT by firms, GOVERNMENT expenditure, and EXPORT revenue arriving from abroad. The rule of motion: injections above leakages and the flow expands; leakages above injections and it contracts. South Africa's loop is wide open to the world — one move in the oil price walks straight into every taxi fare in the country, which is the openness made visible.

The national accounts measure the loop. GDP — GROSS DOMESTIC PRODUCT — is the market value of all FINAL goods and services produced WITHIN a country's borders in a year. Both italicised ideas hide a trap. Final only: count the bakkie, not also the steel in it, or the steel is counted twice. Within the borders — the WHERE rule: a German-owned plant in Gqeberha produces inside South Africa, so its output is South African GDP. GNI runs on the WHO rule instead: it follows income to the nationality of the factor owners, adding what South Africans earn abroad and subtracting what foreign owners remit home. Divide either total by population for PER CAPITA comparisons — then recite the blind spots before trusting the number: unpaid household work uncounted, informal activity undercounted, distribution invisible, environmental damage booked as nothing.

Pause for this section's questions now: three leakages against three injections, the where rule against the who rule, and the blind spots of the big number.

## Subtopic: Demand, Supply and Equilibrium

Structure three, and the one that decides the most marks. The LAW OF DEMAND: price up, quantity demanded down, all else constant — a curve falling from top left to bottom right. The LAW OF SUPPLY: price up, quantity supplied up — a curve climbing from bottom left to top right. Their crossing is EQUILIBRIUM: the single price at which buyers' plans and sellers' plans agree, leaving neither surplus nor shortage.

Now the distinction that sits at the very heart of this subject: MOVEMENT along a curve versus SHIFT of the curve. Only a change in the product's OWN price causes movement along its curve. Everything else — income, tastes, the prices of substitutes and complements, the number of buyers, input costs, technology, weather — relocates the ENTIRE curve. Train the reflex sentence: the product's own price never shifts the product's own curve.

Rehearse the standard shock until it is muscle memory. Avian flu strikes poultry farms. Supply of eggs shifts LEFT: at every price, fewer trays reach the shops. At the old price, quantity demanded now exceeds quantity supplied — a shortage — and the shortage bids the price upward. Buyers slide up along the demand curve until a new equilibrium settles: higher price, smaller quantity. Four steps, every time: which curve shifts, which direction, which excess appears at the old price, where the new equilibrium lands.

And when the data arrives as a TABLE, obey the table. Equilibrium is the row where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At any price above that row, subtract to find EXCESS SUPPLY; below it, excess demand. Name the row, name the units, show the subtraction — the method earns the marks even before the answer does.

Stop for this section's questions now — both laws, movement against shift, the egg shock in four steps, and a table read row by row.

## Subtopic: Markets, Value and the Business Cycle

Structure four gathers three sets of distinctions. First: UTILITY, VALUE and PRICE. Utility is the satisfaction a good delivers — personal and unmeasurable from outside. Value is the importance attached to the good, and it takes BOTH utility and scarcity to create it: water carries immense utility but little market value where it is abundant, while diamonds — nearly useless — command fortunes through scarcity alone. Price is simply value translated into currency on an actual market.

Second: market STRUCTURE. A PERFECT market requires many buyers and many sellers, a homogeneous product, complete information and free entry and exit — conditions so strict that only some fresh-produce and share markets approach them; no single participant there can move the price. IMPERFECT markets break at least one condition: MONOPOLY, a single seller facing the whole demand curve; OLIGOPOLY, a handful of large firms watching each other — think of our banking or mobile network sectors; and MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION, many sellers differentiating similar products, like takeaway food. Remember what markets DO as well: discover prices, transmit information, allocate resources, and connect buyers and sellers — today across the planet from a phone.

Third: the BUSINESS CYCLE, the economy's long wave through four phases in fixed order — RECOVERY, output and hiring turning upward; PROSPERITY, the peak where capacity strains; RECESSION, spending and employment falling; DEPRESSION, the deep floor. Track the wave with three classes of INDICATORS: LEADING indicators turn before the economy does — building plans approved is the classic, a promise of construction to come; COINCIDENT indicators, like current retail sales, move with the economy in real time; LAGGING indicators, unemployment chief among them, confirm a turning point only after it has passed. Causes split into internal forces — waves of business and consumer confidence, credit expanding and contracting — and external shocks, and for a mineral exporter like South Africa, swings in world commodity prices reach our cycle with full force.

The final questions of this part are with you now: utility, value and price separated; perfect and imperfect structures classified; the four phases in order; and the three indicator classes matched to their timing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same half-year, told again through three things you can picture without trying: a month-end wallet, a Saturday main road, and a vetkoek stand at the school gate.

## Subtopic: One Wallet, Three Questions

Month-end in the kitchen. The wallet holds three hundred and fifty rand; the list on the fridge adds up to five hundred and eighty. Nobody in the house did anything wrong — the gap is just how economic life is, every month, in every household on earth, and even in every treasury on earth. Wants outgrow money. Permanently. That permanent gap has a name: scarcity — and notice it is not the same as the bread running out at the shop, which is temporary and fixable. Scarcity never runs out.

Because the gap is permanent, choosing is permanent. And every choice carries a hidden second price. Say the family spends the money on new soccer boots. The till slip says three hundred and fifty rand — but the real cost is the BEST thing that money would otherwise have bought, maybe the month's data and taxi fare. Economists are strict about this: not everything on the list, only the top item you walked away from. One choice, one true cost.

A household with that wallet answers three questions: what to buy, how to stretch the rands, and who in the family gets what. Scale it up and a whole country answers the same three: what to produce, how to produce it, for whom. The wallet is the economy, shrunk.

Now picture the country's capacity as one sewing room with ten machines that can make school shirts or dresses. All ten machines humming, in any mix — that is the best the room can do, and the boundary of best has a shape: a curve bowing between shirts on one axis and dresses on the other. Every machine busy puts you ON the curve. Two machines standing broken or idle puts you INSIDE it — the room producing less than it could, which is precisely what an unemployment rate above thirty percent means for a country. Wanting output BEYOND the curve is a wish — until you buy more machines or train faster hands, and then the whole curve itself moves outward. That outward move is what the news calls growth.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are coming to you right now: the permanent gap, the hidden second price, the three questions, and the sewing room read inside, on and beyond its curve.

## Subtopic: The Circle of Money on One Street

Saturday morning on a small-town main road. The fish shop pays its two workers; one walks to the hardware store for paint, the other to the salon for braids. The hardware owner banks some takings and pays her assistant, who buys fish and chips for lunch. Money is running a relay around the street — households handing firms their work, firms handing back wages, wages coming straight back over the counters. Draw that relay as a circle and you have drawn the whole macroeconomy: the circular flow.

Now watch the circle's water level, because it leaks and it refills. Three leaks: money SAVED in the bank instead of spent on the street; money TAXED away to Pretoria; money spent on IMPORTED goods — the phone from overseas takes its rands with it. Three refills: a firm INVESTING — the fish shop buying a second fryer; GOVERNMENT money arriving — the clinic being repainted, the teacher's salary; and EXPORT money — the town's rooibos sold abroad and paid for in foreign cash converted to rand. Refills stronger than leaks, and the street hums; leaks stronger than refills, and shutters come down early.

Then once a year, count the street. Every finished thing made and sold — every haircut, every tin of paint, every parcel of fish — at its price. Only FINISHED things: count the parcel of fish and chips, not also the potatoes inside it, or the potatoes are counted twice. Do that for the whole country and the total is GDP — everything produced inside the borders, whoever owns the shop; the foreign-owned franchise on the corner counts, because it produces HERE. Chase the incomes home instead — what our people earn anywhere on earth, minus what foreign owners send home — and the total is GNI. And keep the humility: the number never sees gogo minding the grandchildren, half the informal traders, who got which share, or the smoke drifting off the dump.

Take this section's questions now — three leaks, three refills, the finished-goods rule, the where rule against the who rule, and what the count misses.

## Subtopic: The Argument That Sets the Price, and the Economy That Breathes

A vetkoek stand at the school gate, Monday morning. Priced at fifteen rand each, the tray is still full at ten o'clock — buyers walked past, and by tomorrow the price will have quietly come down. Priced at three rand, the tray is empty before assembly and latecomers are begging — and tomorrow the price will drift up. Somewhere near seven rand, the last vetkoek meets the last hungry learner: no leftover tray, no queue turned away. Nobody chaired a meeting. The daily argument between buyers and sellers found the crossing point on its own — that crossing point is equilibrium.

Two very different mornings can change that stand, and telling them apart is the single most tested skill in this half-year. Morning one: the seller herself changes the price — buyers simply slide along their own willingness to buy. Movement ALONG the curve. Morning two: something else changes first — exams end and the school empties, or the price of cooking oil doubles, or a second stand opens across the road. Now the whole relationship has been rewritten: an entire curve SHIFTS, and the argument settles at a new crossing. The reflex: own price moves you along; everything else moves the curve.

Finally, lift your eyes from the stand to the whole economy and feel it breathe. The in-breath: shops rehiring, overtime returning, cranes appearing on the skyline — recovery climbing into prosperity. The out-breath: orders thinning, short time, retrenchment letters — recession sliding toward the floor of depression, until the breath turns again. Four phases, fixed order. And read the breath with three instruments: building plans approved are the headlights — they light the road ahead; today's retail sales are the speedometer — the economy's speed right now; unemployment is the rearview mirror — it confirms the hill only after you have climbed or fallen down it.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the vetkoek tray at fifteen rand and at three, movement against shift in one clean sentence, and the four phases matched to headlights, speedometer and mirror. Walk into the examination carrying the structures, not the pages — the structures answer everything.
