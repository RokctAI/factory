# Part 1 — Expert

For two terms we have examined the economy as a finished machine; this term begins by asking where the machine came from. Today we walk the long road of economic development: households that once produced everything for themselves; agriculture creating the first surplus; barter and the frustrations that doomed it; the arrival of money; markets deepening through specialisation, transport, law and new forms of business; the state stepping in as regulator, tax collector and builder; and at last industrialisation, when machines and harnessed energy rebuilt production from the ground up. Treat the story as a chain of cause and effect: every stage exists because it SOLVED a problem the stage before it left behind.

## Subtopic: From Self-Sufficiency to Surplus and Barter

The oldest economic unit was the SELF-SUFFICIENT household or community. Hunting, gathering, later keeping herds — each group made every single thing it used. There was no trade, no market and no money, because there was nothing to spare and nobody to spare it for. Understand the trap in that arrangement: a household consumes only what its own hands produce, so every craft must be practised — badly — under every roof, and no skill is ever pursued far enough to become excellent.

The AGRICULTURAL revolution sprang the trap. Once people settled, planted, harvested and stored, a season could finally yield SURPLUS — output beyond what the producers themselves required. Hang the entire subject on that word, because surplus is the pivot on which economic history turns: it frees hands from food production. With the fields feeding more than the farmers, some people could give their whole working lives to other crafts — smithing, weaving, building — and a person who spends all day making what they do not intend to consume has no choice but to EXCHANGE it. Specialisation creates trade as surely as rain creates rivers.

The first form of trade was BARTER — goods handed directly for goods: a sheep for sacks of millet, baskets for a hoe. Its four defects must be stated exactly, because they are the reasoning that explains money. First, the DOUBLE COINCIDENCE OF WANTS: a deal needs a partner who both HAS what you seek and WANTS what you carry — a search so costly it throttles exchange. Second, INDIVISIBILITY: you cannot hand over half a sheep for a small knife; large goods cannot settle small purchases. Third, NO COMMON MEASURE of value: with no shared yardstick, every pairing of goods demands its own haggled ratio — how many baskets to a hoe, how many hoes to a sheep? Fourth, NO STORE of value: surplus that is food spoils and surplus that is livestock must be fed and herded — wealth cannot simply be kept. Each defect is a vacancy waiting for the invention that fills it.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the self-sufficiency trap, surplus as the pivot, and barter's four defects stated precisely.

## Subtopic: Money and the Evolution of Markets

MONEY is best defined by its FUNCTIONS, because each function repairs one of barter's defects. As a MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE, money is accepted by everybody — so the double coincidence dissolves: sell your goods to whoever wants them, then buy from whoever has what you need. As a UNIT OF ACCOUNT, money puts one measuring rod under every value: goods carry PRICES instead of a tangle of pairwise ratios. As a STORE OF VALUE, money lets wealth pause: sell the perishable harvest today, hold the money, spend it months later. And through DIVISIBILITY, money makes change — purchases of any size become possible. The earliest moneys were commodities in their own right — cattle, salt, cowrie shells, metal rings — and the survivors shared four properties: durable, divisible, portable and scarce. Precious metals scored highest on all four, and the line ran on through minted coin, paper notes, and the electronic balances you tap with a card today. Southern Africa's own history offers the anchor: for centuries cattle served this region as money and as stored wealth at once.

Once money circulated, MARKETS evolved along several tracks simultaneously, and you must be able to name each track. SPECIALISATION deepened into the fine-grained division of labour — whole trades, then single tasks within trades — multiplying output per worker; and specialisation and markets reinforce one another, because a specialist depends on a market both to sell in and to buy from. TRADE AND TRANSPORT advanced in step: paths became roads, ships grew, railways followed — and every widening of transport widened the circle of customers a market could reach, stretching local exchange into regional and international commerce. MERCANTILE AND COMMERCIAL LAW arose because strangers cannot trade on kinship and memory: contracts, standard weights and measures, and courts turned personal trust into institutional trust. BUSINESS FORMS evolved to gather capital: the sole trader, then partnerships pooling resources, then the COMPANY — ownership sliced into shares, risk spread across many holders, and sums assembled that no single merchant could ever risk alone. And through every track ran TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS, each better tool raising what markets could produce and carry.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four functions of money matched one-to-one against barter's four defects, and the market's evolutionary tracks named with the logic of each.

## Subtopic: Governments Enter the Market

Markets did not grow alone; the STATE grew alongside them and into them. History records three great entries, and you need all three with their motives.

Entry one: INVOLVEMENT IN TRADE. Rulers policed marketplaces, licensed and chartered trading companies, held ports and controlled routes, and offered merchants protection — for a reason that is pure economics: trade could be TAXED, and taxed trade financed the ruler's power. The chartered companies of the mercantile age — armed, monopoly-holding, operating under state licence — are the vehicles that carried European commerce to this coastline, with consequences written deep into South Africa's history.

Entry two: TAXATION itself — the state's standing claim on economic activity. It began with customs at the border and levies on land and harvests, and grew into the taxes on income and spending you will meet on every payslip and till slip. Taxation rewired incentives on both sides: a ruler who taxes an economy gains a permanent interest in that economy GROWING, since more activity means more revenue — while the taxed gain a permanent interest in watching how the revenue is spent.

Entry three: FINANCING INFRASTRUCTURE. A market needs things no individual trader will ever build alone — roads, bridges, harbours, and in later centuries railways, power grids and communication networks. Infrastructure costs enormously, benefits everybody, and pays its builder back only indirectly — which is the textbook justification for public investment. States that built it enlarged their markets and prospered; states that neglected it watched trade route around them. The thread runs unbroken to the present: the infrastructure lines in every modern national budget are this same ancient function, still running.

Hold the analytical thread as you learn the three entries: each one answers a LIMITATION of the market — promises that need enforcing, common goods that nobody funds voluntarily — the same intervention logic you met in term two, now wearing historical dress.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the three entries, each with its motive and its modern descendant.

## Subtopic: Industrial Development

The last stage rebuilt the world: INDUSTRIALISATION — production migrating from hand and hearth to machine and factory, first in Britain in the seventeen hundreds, then everywhere.

Learn its four components as a set. MANUFACTURING: production at factory scale — where a workshop finished a dozen items, the factory finished thousands, and prices fell as volumes climbed. TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS: knowledge cast into machinery — spinning frames, power looms, machine tools precise enough to build further machines. The MOBILISATION OF ENERGY — the true rupture in the story: work had always been limited by muscle, human or animal; now water wheels, then coal-fired steam, then electricity and oil drove production past every limit a living body imposes. And MECHANISATION: machines absorbing task after task across agriculture, transport and industry — the plough behind a tractor, the wagon replaced by rail, the bench replaced by the assembly line.

The consequences spread in every direction. Productivity leapt: one machine-equipped worker now outproduced dozens working by hand. URBANISATION followed, as factories pulled workers into dense new cities — the labour force you studied earlier this term was assembled in exactly this movement. Two new social classes formed and faced each other — the owners of machines and the operators of machines — and their disputes over wages, hours and safety became the labour relations you have just studied. Transport and communication revolutions stitched national markets into world markets, laying globalisation's foundations. And the bill arrived alongside the benefits: brutal factory conditions, children at machines, fouled air and water — the very abuses that called labour law and regulation into existence.

South Africa's entry into the industrial age came through MINING. Diamonds, then gold, industrialised the economy at extraordinary speed: railways were flung inland to the diggings, capital poured in, and labour was concentrated on the mines under the migrant labour system — an arrangement whose social costs the country still carries today. Here as everywhere, development arrives as gains and costs braided together — and separating the strands honestly is the analytical habit this term demands.

The final questions of this part are with you now: industrialisation's four components, its consequences on both sides of the ledger, and South Africa's route through mining.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole ten-thousand-year story again — but shrunk to a single village in a river valley, growing up stage by stage. Everything that happened to the world happened here first, in miniature.

## Subtopic: The Village That Fed Itself — Then Grew Extra

Begin in the river valley, long ago. Every family in the village does every job for itself: catches its fish, grows its millet, weaves its own sleeping mats, shapes its own hoes. Nobody trades with anybody — not out of unfriendliness, but because no family ever has anything left over. And no family ever CAN have anything left over, because a family doing every job does no job well. Survival takes the whole day, every day.

Then one farmer cracks it: when to plant, how to store grain against the dry months — and one harvest, her granary holds MORE millet than her family can eat before the next planting. More than needed. That little word — extra — is the seed of everything else in this subject. Extra millet means someone else's hands are released from the fields. Her brother, who was always better with metal than with millet, stops farming entirely and works iron all day — and hoes made by a man who makes hoes from sunrise to sunset soon shame everyone else's. Across the path, a neighbour gives her days to weaving mats; down at the river, a boy becomes the village's full-time fisherman. Each person is now excellent at ONE thing — and therefore each person NEEDS all the others. The village has created specialists, and specialists cannot survive without trade.

Their first trade is swapping — hoes for millet, fish for mats — and swapping turns out to be misery four times over. Misery one: the hunt — you must find the one person who wants YOUR fish and has THE hoe you need, on the same day. Misery two: the big-for-small problem — a whole sheep is too much to pay for one clay pot, and you cannot pay with a leg. Misery three: every deal is an argument, because nobody agrees how many mats one hoe is worth — there is no yardstick. Misery four: you cannot SAVE — fish rots by tomorrow, and wealth that grazes can wander off or sicken. Hold all four miseries in mind, because one invention is about to end every one of them.

A quick check before we move on — questions on the valley village are reaching you right now: why no-extra meant no-trade, and the four miseries of swapping.

## Subtopic: The Invention That Fixed Everything

One market morning the village elders make an announcement: from today, these COPPER BANDS — scarce along the river, lasting forever, light in a pouch, and made in large bands and small — count as payment for anything in the village. By sunset, all four miseries are dying.

The hunt is over: the fisherman sells fish to ANYONE holding copper and buys his hoe from ANYONE who forged one — the two halves of every swap have been sliced apart, and that is the secret of what money actually is: half a trade that you can keep in your pocket until you are ready for the other half. The big-for-small problem is over: big bands for a sheep, small bands for a pot — payment now comes in every size. The arguments are over: everything in the market gets its price IN COPPER — one yardstick under every value, so comparing a hoe to a mat to a basket of fish becomes counting instead of quarrelling. And saving is finally possible: sell the catch while it is fresh, keep the copper, buy grain in the hungry month — wealth has learned to wait. Test any money in history against the village's four requirements — lasting, splittable, scarce, carryable — and you will find cattle, salt, shells, gold coins, printed notes and the balance on a banking app all passing the same exam. The costume changes every few centuries; the four jobs never change at all.

With copper working, the market bursts its banks. A cleared path becomes a road; the road reaches the next valley; a jetty rises on the river — and every improvement in getting-there pulls more villages, more specialists and more goods into one growing web. Soon strangers are trading with strangers, and a handshake between strangers needs backup — so the village writes RULES: stamped weights so scales cannot cheat, promises that must be kept, an elder appointed to judge disputes. Trust stops depending on knowing someone's family and starts living in institutions. And the boldest ventures grow too big for one purse: first two traders share one boat, then twenty families each buy a SHARE of a trading expedition — profits divided, and no single family destroyed if the boat is lost. Look at what the valley now holds: money, roads, rules and shared ownership. That is not a primitive economy. That is YOUR economy, at its smallest scale.

Your questions for this part are up now: the four jobs of copper matched to the four miseries, and the market's expansion — roads, rules, shares — in the right order.

## Subtopic: The Chief, and Then the Machines

Two arrivals finish the village's story — and both are still on the news every night.

First, the CHIEF walks into the market — the state, in its earliest costume. Watch his three moves, and notice that every national budget still makes all three. Move one: he REGULATES — the river route is patrolled, the big trading expeditions need his licence, cheats are punished; the market gets order, and the chief gets control. Move two: he TAXES — a measure of grain from every granary, a copper band from every big deal. And taxing changes the chief himself: from that day he wants the market to THRIVE, because a busier market fills his stores faster — the ruler has become the economy's interested party. Move three: he BUILDS what no trader would ever build alone — the stone bridge across the river, the jetty wall, the great road over the ridge. Everyone profits from a bridge; no single trader could ever afford one; only everyone's tax, gathered together, can pay for it. Regulate, tax, build — three jobs invented in a river valley, still the state's three jobs in this year's budget.

Second, the MACHINES arrive. For all its copper and roads, the valley still makes everything by hand, and hands can only do so much. Then someone sets a wheel in the river's current, and later a coal engine thumps where the wheel turned — and cloth that took a weaver a week now rolls out of the mill in an afternoon. Work leaves the huts and gathers under the mill roof; families leave the valley slopes for the streets growing around the mill. One machine-minder outproduces a whole row of hand-weavers — so cloth becomes cheap, the town becomes a city, and across the mill floor two brand-new kinds of people study each other: the family that OWNS the mill, and the hundreds who WORK in it. Their quarrels — pay, hours, safety, dignity — are precisely the labour relations you studied earlier this term; and the mill's smoke, and the children once sent to its looms, are precisely why the rulebooks had to be written.

South Africa's mill-day did not come from a river wheel — it came out of the ground. Diamonds, then gold: mines that industrialised a country at a gallop, railways chasing the ore inland, and men summoned from every distant valley to the compounds under the migrant labour system. Enormous gains, heavy costs, permanently entangled — which is exactly the honest, two-column lens this whole term keeps asking you to use.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the chief's three moves traced into the modern budget, the machine-day's gains and costs both counted, and the valley's full journey — extra, specialists, copper, market, chief, machines — told in its proper order. Ten thousand years in one valley: carry it with you.
