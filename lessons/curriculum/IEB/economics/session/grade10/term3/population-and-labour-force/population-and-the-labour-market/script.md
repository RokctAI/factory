# Part 1 — Expert

Strip an economy down to its foundations and you find people: people producing, people earning, people spending. This lesson is about counting those people carefully. We trace how populations expand and move through the demographic cycle, how migration rearranges them across a map, which slice of the population qualifies as the LABOUR FORCE, what South Africa's workforce looks like by age and by province, and finally how the labour market operates as a genuine market — where the supply of workers meets the demand of firms and a wage emerges. Master today's definitions, because the lessons on labour relations and on unemployment both stand on them.

## Subtopic: Population Growth and the Demographic Cycle

Only two forces can change the size of a population. The first is NATURAL GROWTH: births minus deaths. The second is MIGRATION: people arriving minus people leaving. We express natural growth per thousand of the population. Suppose a country records thirty births and twelve deaths for every thousand residents in a year: its natural growth rate is eighteen per thousand — one comma eight percent annually — and a rate like that, held steady, roughly doubles a population within forty years.

As societies develop, their birth and death rates follow a predictable four-stage journey called the DEMOGRAPHIC CYCLE. Stage one, HIGH STATIONARY: births are plentiful but so are deaths — infection, hunger and hard living cut lives short — so the population hardly moves; this was the whole world before modern medicine. Stage two, EARLY EXPANDING: death rates collapse first — piped water, immunisation, antibiotics, reliable harvests — while birth rates remain high, and the widening gap between them produces explosive growth. Stage three, LATE EXPANDING: birth rates now begin their own descent — families urbanise, girls stay in school longer, contraception spreads, and a child stops being an extra pair of farm hands and becomes a set of school fees — so growth decelerates. Stage four, LOW STATIONARY: births and deaths are both low; numbers level off, and in some rich economies they now shrink unless immigrants arrive.

South Africa stands in the later expanding stage. Deaths fell decades ago; births are easing downward; the population still grows, only more slowly than before. Crucially, it remains a YOUNG population — each year a substantial new cohort crosses into working age, and that single demographic fact shapes everything else this term covers. Layer migration on top: internally, people stream from rural districts toward Gauteng and the Western Cape, reshaping provincial populations; internationally, workers from across Southern Africa are drawn toward the South African economy even as some South Africans leave for opportunities abroad.

Pause and take this section's questions, which are with you now — the two forces of change, the growth-rate arithmetic, and the four stages with the causes that drive each one.

## Subtopic: Who Is the Labour Force?

This section rewards surgical definitions, because the concepts sit inside one another like Russian dolls.

Start with the POPULATION: every person in the country. Within it sits the WORKING-AGE population — by convention everyone aged fifteen to sixty-four, the people who COULD work. Within that sits the LABOUR FORCE, also called the economically active population: working-age people who are either EMPLOYED — doing any work for pay or profit — or UNEMPLOYED under the STRICT definition, which requires three things at once: no work, AVAILABLE for work, and ACTIVELY LOOKING for it. Everyone else of working age falls outside the labour force: full-time learners, people running homes, people too ill to work, early retirees — and one group your assessment answers must handle with care: DISCOURAGED WORK-SEEKERS. These are people who want a job but have abandoned the search, convinced that nothing is out there. The strict definition drops them from the unemployment count; the EXPANDED definition restores them — and in South Africa the distance between the two measures amounts to several percentage points of lived hardship.

Two ratios complete the toolkit. The UNEMPLOYMENT RATE takes the unemployed as a percentage of the LABOUR FORCE — never of the whole population; choosing the wrong denominator is the most common error in this topic. The LABOUR FORCE PARTICIPATION RATE takes the labour force as a percentage of the working-age population — it measures what fraction of the potential workforce has actually stepped into the arena.

Sketch South Africa's labour force in durable shapes rather than perishable statistics. Shape one: it is YOUNG, and joblessness among the youth runs far above the overall rate — those newest to the market wait the longest. Shape two: it is GEOGRAPHICALLY lopsided — employment is stacked in the metropolitan engines, Gauteng foremost, while the former homeland regions hold many people and few jobs, a spatial inheritance of apartheid planning. Shape three: participation runs below what a job-rich economy would call forth, and discouragement is what holds it down.

Take this section's questions now — the nested definitions stated precisely, the strict and expanded measures contrasted, and both ratios anchored to their correct denominators.

## Subtopic: The Demand and Supply for Labour

The labour market is a real market — but the roles are reversed from the goods market. Here HOUSEHOLDS are the suppliers, offering their time and skill; FIRMS are the demanders; and the price they negotiate is the WAGE. Each side of the market obeys its own logic.

Labour demand is a DERIVED demand — learn the term, because it explains the whole curve. A firm does not hire people as an end in itself; it hires them for the OUTPUT they generate. Nobody demands electricians unless somebody first demands wiring. Consequently labour demand strengthens whenever the demand for goods strengthens — expansions, export booms, new investment — and weakens in every downswing of the business cycle you met in term one. The curve slopes downward against the wage: as labour grows dearer, firms trim hiring, mechanise, or scale production back.

Labour supply flows from the population arithmetic of the first subtopic: demographics and participation fix its size, education fixes its quality. The supply curve slopes upward: higher wages tempt more people into the market and more hours out of those already in it. Where the two curves intersect, the market wage settles — and every result from term two's market theory carries over. Hold the wage above equilibrium and some willing workers find no buyer: unemployment at that wage. A legislated minimum wage is precisely the price floor from the intervention lesson, transplanted into the labour market.

Now the distinctly South African twist: there is no single labour market — there are many, partitioned by SKILL. In the market for scarce capabilities — engineers, artisans, data specialists — demand chases limited supply: salaries climb and vacancies persist. In the market for unskilled labour, supply dwarfs demand: pay is squeezed toward the legal floor and queues lengthen. The SKILLS MISMATCH — a schooling pipeline that delivers too little of what firms are hunting for, while millions carry abilities the economy absorbs weakly — is the structural core of South African unemployment, and it is the bridge into the next two lessons.

This section's questions are in front of you now — derived demand explained in full, the logic behind each curve, and the skill-partitioned market applied to South Africa.

## Subtopic: Reading the Labour Statistics

We close Part 1 with four habits that turn a labour statistics table into marks.

Habit one: DEFINITION FIRST. No labour figure means anything until you know which definition produced it — the strict and expanded unemployment measures can stand millions of people apart. State the definition before you interpret the number.

Habit two: NAME THE DENOMINATOR. Unemployment divides by the labour force; participation divides by the working-age population; youth unemployment divides young jobless people by the YOUNG labour force. Writing the numerator and denominator explicitly earns method credit before any arithmetic begins.

Habit three: LOOK UNDER THE HEADLINE. A single national rate flattens the real story: joblessness piles up among the young, the unskilled and particular provinces; discouragement drags participation down invisibly; work itself divides into formal and informal. Data-response questions almost always live beneath the headline — a breakdown by age or by province, and your commentary on it.

Habit four: EXPLAIN THE MECHANISM. Why do young people face the worst rates? They arrive without experience or networks, precisely where labour supply floods in and entry-level demand is thinnest. Why do the former homelands carry the heaviest burdens? History placed the jobs elsewhere, and distance plus transport costs wall people off from the posts that exist. Commentary that traces a figure back to demand, supply, skill or geography is analysis; commentary that repeats the figure is merely description.

The final questions of this part are with you now — definitions declared, denominators named, structures uncovered, mechanisms explained.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same topic, but now we walk it through one street and one Sunday lunch — because every neighbourhood already runs a census, a labour survey and a wage negotiation without noticing.

## Subtopic: The Street as a Census

Take one street — call it Mvubu Street — and count it the way a statistician would. Tally the babies born this year; tally the funerals; the difference is the street's NATURAL growth. Then tally the families who moved in against the families who loaded the truck and left — that is migration. Add the two together and you hold the street's complete population change. A country is exactly this, multiplied by every street in it.

Now watch Mvubu Street's history unfold in four chapters — the same four every society writes. Chapter one, long ago: cradles everywhere, but funerals almost as frequent — sickness took children young — so the street's numbers stood still. Chapter two: the clinic opened at the corner — clean taps, immunisation days — and suddenly the funerals thinned out while the cradles kept coming: the street SWELLED, three generations under every roof. Chapter three: the daughters of that boom finished school, took jobs in town, planned two children instead of six — the swelling eased. Chapter four, still ahead, already visible overseas: few cradles, few funerals, the street's numbers frozen and its residents greying.

South Africa's streets are living chapter three: still young, still growing, but more gently — and here is the one sentence that unlocks the entire term: EVERY DECEMBER, ANOTHER LARGE MATRIC CLASS WALKS OUT OF THE SCHOOL HALL AND INTO THE JOB QUEUE. Measure that arriving class against the number of jobs opening to receive it, and you are holding the plot of the next three lessons.

A quick check before we move on — the questions on the street census are reaching you right now: the two forces of change, and the four chapters with what turned each page.

## Subtopic: Who's On the Team?

Now sort one family, seat by seat around the Sunday table, exactly as the survey-takers would — because the sorting IS the assessment.

Grandpa Joseph, seventy: counted in the population, but past working age — out of the working-age group. The nine-year-old, Neo: too young — same verdict. Ma, a nurse on night shift: working-age and EMPLOYED — she is on the team and on the field. Uncle Vusi: no job, but he lodged four applications this month and checks the community notice board every morning — not working, yet AVAILABLE and SEARCHING: on the team, sitting on the bench — officially UNEMPLOYED. Ayanda, in matric: working-age on paper, but a full-time learner — NOT on the team; learners stay off the sheet. And cousin Sipho: three years of unanswered applications, until this winter he quietly stopped sending them. He wants work; he no longer looks. The strict count strikes him off the team sheet entirely. The expanded count looks at him and says: he is plainly unemployed — count him. The space between those two counts is filled with thousands of real Siphos, and knowing BOTH measures means knowing that the truth here comes in two sizes.

The two ratios, table-side version. The unemployment rate asks: of everyone ON the team — field plus bench — what share is benched? Never divide by the whole table; Grandpa Joseph and Neo were never on the sheet. The participation rate asks: of everyone OLD ENOUGH to be picked, how many put their names forward at all? Sipho's silent withdrawal shows up here — discouragement lowers participation while leaving the strict unemployment rate untouched.

Your questions for this part are up now — sort a new family onto or off the team sheet, and divide by the right group every time.

## Subtopic: The Wage Is a Price at a Gate

The last piece is the labour market itself — pictured at a factory gate just after sunrise.

It is a market with the counter spun around: the FAMILIES stand on the selling side, offering hours, muscle and know-how, and the FIRMS stand on the buying side. The wage is nothing more than the price where the two sides shake hands. Both sides lean exactly as term two taught: lift the wage and more people arrive at the gate — supply stretches; but at that higher wage the firm takes on fewer people and eyes a machine instead — demand shrinks. Where the leanings balance, the going wage forms.

One idea powers the firm's side of the gate: a firm never wants workers — it wants what workers MAKE. No customer orders a plumber for conversation; they order one because a geyser burst. So when orders flood in, the gate swings wide; when the downswing arrives, the very same gate narrows. The textbook word is derived demand; the street translation is shorter: no orders, no shifts.

And there is never just one gate. At the artisans' gate — welders, electricians, toolmakers — the queue is short and firms outbid one another: scarce skill, strong pay. At the general-labour gate the queue bends around the block: many selling, few buying, and the wage sinks toward the legal floor. Two gates, one town, opposite worlds — separated only by SKILL. That is why a trade certificate works like a boarding pass from the long queue to the short one, and why the country's deepest labour wound now has a name you can use: the skills mismatch — crowds at the gate the economy least needs, and empty space at the gate it needs most.

The lesson's final questions arrive now — the reversed market drawn, derived demand in plain words, and the two gates with the boarding pass between them. Next lesson: what changes when the people in the queue start speaking with one voice.
