**Thandi:** Class questions before we pack up! The first one caused an argument at the back: Sipho wants a job just as much as Vusi does — so why does the strict definition get to pretend he doesn't exist? Isn't that just making the numbers look nicer?

**Tutor:** Hold both halves of that objection, because both are worth marks. The strict definition insists on active searching for a measurement reason: searching leaves EVIDENCE. A survey can ask what someone actually did in the past month — applications posted, agencies visited, adverts answered — and record it consistently, household after household, country after country. Wanting a job, by itself, lives inside a person's head; measured directly, it wobbles with mood, phrasing and pride, and no two countries would count it alike. So the strict rate is the auditable, internationally comparable core. But your instinct is also correct, and South Africa is the textbook case for why: where searching itself costs money — data, taxis, printed CVs — and where a township has watched a hundred applications sink without reply, giving up is not laziness, it is arithmetic. There the strict rate systematically understates distress. That is exactly why the official statistics agency publishes BOTH measures every quarter, side by side. The full answer in any assessment is never to pick a favourite: quote both, and then say what the gap between them measures — discouragement, pooled precisely where hope is scarcest. The numbers are honest; it is quoting only one of them that misleads.

**Thandi:** Next question: you said the demand for labour is borrowed from the demand for goods. So is a worker basically a passenger — no control, just waiting for customers to show up?

**Tutor:** Borrowed demand narrows the levers; it does not remove them — and naming the levers is precisely what a good answer does. Lever one operates through the goods market: whatever lifts the demand for what your industry makes lifts the demand for you, which is why exports, investment and the upswing are quietly employment policy, and why workers are never indifferent to growth. Lever two belongs entirely to the worker: SKILL. Demand is derived gate by gate — retrain, and you change WHICH demand you are derived from; the same economy that ignored you at the crowded gate bids for you at the scarce one. Lever three is productivity: raise what one hour of your work produces — through tools, experience, further study — and you raise the ceiling any firm can rationally pay you, whatever the state of orders. And lever four arrives in the very next lesson: workers bargaining together rather than alone, negotiating where inside the available room the wage settles. So yes, the weather comes from the goods market — but the queue you stand in, the skills you carry into it, and the voice you negotiate with are all choices. Derived demand sets the stage; it does not write the whole script.

**Thandi:** This one came with a map drawn on it: why do the former homeland areas always carry the worst unemployment? Surely a business could open anywhere?

**Tutor:** A business COULD open anywhere; it profitably opens only where the economics line up — and history rigged where they line up. The homelands were designed as labour reservoirs, deliberately distant from the mines, harbours and cities, and starved of roads, rail and power for generations. A firm choosing a site today runs cold calculations: where are my customers, my suppliers, my transport links, my pool of skills? Every calculation points at the metros — so the jobs keep landing where the infrastructure already is, not where the people are. Now feel the arithmetic from the household's side: reaching work costs precisely what the jobless don't have — taxi fare, city rent, a first month's deposit. Distance becomes a wedge driven between a willing worker and an existing wage. And a small trader who stays local faces thin local spending — the circular flow turning at low volume — so even self-employment fights uphill. When a data question hands you a provincial unemployment table, this is the analysis it wants: apartheid's spatial design meeting the firm's location logic, distance as a cost wedge, infrastructure gaps repelling investment — and the policy directions that follow, from transport subsidies to taking training and infrastructure to where people actually live.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's a big one: everyone keeps saying our population is young. Is that a blessing or a problem? Because it sounds like both.

**Tutor:** It is both — conditionally — and economists have a name for the condition: the demographic dividend, with heavy stress on the word potential. Here is the promise: for a few decades, a young country carries an unusually large working-age bulge supporting relatively few pensioners — more potential producers per dependant than any ageing society enjoys. Economies that seized that window — think of East Asia's rapid industrialisers — turned the bulge into workers, savers and builders, and transformed themselves within a generation. Here is the condition: the dividend pays out ONLY if that bulge is schooled, healthy and ABSORBED into work. Employed, the young cohort is an engine; standing at closed gates, the identical cohort becomes foregone output, stretched grants and simmering frustration — the dividend flipping into a burden. So the honest answer to blessing-or-problem is: the population pyramid poses the question, and policy answers it. Will the skills pipeline defeat the mismatch? Will investment keep pushing the production boundary outward? Will the gates open as each matric class arrives? You are not just studying this material — you ARE the cohort it describes, and the economics you are learning is the instruction manual for cashing the dividend in.
