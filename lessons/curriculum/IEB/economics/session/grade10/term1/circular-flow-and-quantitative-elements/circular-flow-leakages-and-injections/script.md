# Part 1 — Expert

Today we assemble the one diagram every economist carries everywhere: the circular flow. By the end of this session you will construct the model for an open economy with four participants — households, firms, government and the foreign sector — and you will classify savings, taxes and imports as leakages, and investment, government spending and exports as injections. Built in layers, the diagram stays simple; swallowed whole, it overwhelms. We build in layers.

## Subtopic: The Two Original Participants — Households and Firms

Strip any economy down to its skeleton and two participants remain: households and firms.

Households own the factors of production — all four of them, from earlier work: natural resources, labour, capital and entrepreneurship. Trace any factor to its source and you arrive at somebody's kitchen table. Your uncle who clocks in at the depot supplies labour. The family renting out the back room supplies property they own. The pension money invested in machines somewhere is capital. Your neighbour running her own catering business from home supplies entrepreneurship.

Firms are the production units: they hire those factors and combine them into the goods and services everyone consumes.

Now watch the circle close. Households deliver factors of production to firms through the FACTOR market. Firms pay for them with factor incomes: wages for labour, rent for natural resources, interest for capital, profit for entrepreneurship. That is loop one — factors flowing one way, income flowing back.

But nobody eats a payslip. Households take that income to the GOODS market and spend it on what firms produce. The spending becomes firm revenue, which pays for the next round of factors — and the circle spins.

One more layer of precision: every road in the diagram carries traffic in two lanes. REAL flows are physical — factors travelling to firms, goods and services travelling to households. MONEY flows run the opposite way — incomes out to households, spending back to firms. Every real flow has a money flow driving against it. Two participants, two markets, two kinds of flow: this is the simple closed economy, and drawn correctly with labelled arrows, it is the skeleton for everything that follows.

Hold it there — this section's questions are with you now: the two participants, the two markets, and real flows against money flows. Keep the arrows in your head as you answer.

## Subtopic: Leakages and Injections — Why the Circle Shrinks and Grows

If households returned every cent of income to firms as spending, the circle would spin at one constant size forever. They do not — and the model becomes realistic at exactly this point.

Suppose a household banks part of its income — six hundred rand into a fixed deposit. That money steps out of the spending stream: earned, but not passed back to firms. Any such escape from the circle is a LEAKAGE, also called a withdrawal. Savings is the first leakage.

Is the saved money gone? Not at all — and here the model shows its elegance. Banks and other financial institutions lend accumulated savings onward, and firms borrow to buy machinery, vehicles, buildings — a bakery financing its new industrial mixer. Spending on capital goods is called INVESTMENT, and it pumps money back into the circle. Any such re-entry is an INJECTION. Savings drain out; investment flows back — the financial sector is the connecting pipe.

Why do these two flows deserve so much attention? Because their balance decides whether the whole economy expands or contracts. When total leakages exceed total injections, each round returns less money to firms: production drops, incomes drop, the circle tightens. When injections exceed leakages, the circle widens — more spending, more output, more income. When the two are equal, the economy sits in EQUILIBRIUM. Keep that three-way comparison close; senior macroeconomics returns to it constantly.

And one misconception to kill early: a leakage is not "bad" and an injection is not "good". Today's savings finance tomorrow's machines. The words describe the DIRECTION of a flow, never its virtue.

Pause for this section's questions, with you now — savings, investment and the equilibrium comparison: classify every flow by direction, never by virtue.

## Subtopic: Adding Government — Taxes In, Spending Out

Participant three: the government, dealing with households and firms alike, and bringing one leakage and one injection of its own.

The leakage is TAX. Households surrender personal income tax; firms surrender company tax. Whatever SARS collects, households and firms can no longer spend — the money is drawn out of the stream exactly as savings were. Taxes are a leakage.

But the state does not hoard it. The money returns as GOVERNMENT SPENDING: salaries for teachers, nurses and police; a gravel road upgraded to tar; textbooks and medicines bought from firms; social grants paid into household accounts. Government spending is an injection — public money pumped back into the circle.

The government also delivers public goods and services — policing, street lighting, public clinics — to both households and firms. So position it at the centre of your diagram with arrows in both directions: taxes flowing in from households and firms, spending and services flowing back out to both.

Take stock of the tally. Leakages so far: savings and taxes. Injections so far: investment and government spending. An economy of households, firms and government that never trades across its borders is still a CLOSED economy. One participant remains to open the doors.

Stop for this section's questions, in front of you now — the government's arrows in both directions, and the running tally of leakages and injections.

## Subtopic: The Foreign Sector — Opening the Economy

The fourth participant is the FOREIGN SECTOR — every country we trade with. Adding it converts the closed economy into an OPEN one, which is what South Africa actually is.

Follow the money when South Africans buy foreign goods — laptops from South Korea, buses built in India. Rands earned inside our circle are paid to producers outside it. That spending exits our flow entirely: IMPORTS are a leakage.

Reverse the direction. When the world buys South African production — citrus shipped to the Netherlands, platinum to Japanese factories, rooibos to German supermarkets — foreign money streams into South African firms. EXPORTS are an injection.

Now assemble the full machine, because producing this picture on demand is the core skill: the open-economy circular flow joins four participants — households, firms, government, foreign sector — through three connecting structures: the factor market, the goods market, and the financial sector standing between savers and borrowers.

And the two master lists, ready for instant recall. Three leakages: savings, taxes, imports. Three injections: investment, government spending, exports. Here is the check that separates understanding from memorising: each leakage and its partner injection travel through the SAME channel. Savings exit through the financial sector; investment re-enters through it. Taxes exit through the government; government spending re-enters. Import payments exit through the foreign sector; export earnings re-enter. Three doors, each with traffic both ways.

Test yourself in four moves. A household buys a Korean laptop — leakage, import. The state tars the gravel road — injection, government spending. A farm ships oranges to Rotterdam — injection, export. A family locks R600 into a fixed deposit — leakage, savings. Four out of four means you are ready — for this part's questions, with you now: the complete open-economy model, the master lists, and the three doors with traffic in both directions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same diagram without the textbook — because the circular flow is really just your own neighbourhood on the last Friday of the month, drawn with arrows.

## Subtopic: Friday in the Neighbourhood

Put the notes away and watch a month-end Friday instead. Somebody in your house spent the month working at a firm — hours and effort delivered. Friday evening, the pay lands. By Saturday morning that pay is moving: groceries at the corner supermarket, a trim at the barber, cement from the hardware for the unfinished wall. And every one of those businesses uses its takings to pay ITS people, who carry their pay to other counters, whose takings pay other people... See the shape? The money never rests. It CIRCLES. Work flows out one way; money flows home the other; spending pushes it around again.

That circle IS the model. Households — every family — supply the working hands, the rented rooms, the saved rands, the one-woman businesses. Firms — the supermarket, the barber, the hardware, the catering neighbour — combine those into things worth paying for. Two meeting points connect the sides: the hiring side of life, where work finds employers — the factor market — and the till side of life, where money finds goods — the goods market.

And notice every road carries two lanes of traffic. The REAL lane moves actual things: labour riding to work in the morning, bread riding home in the evening. The MONEY lane drives the opposite way: wages coming home, spending going out. Draw one month-end Friday with arrows and you have drawn the simple circular flow — two participants, two markets, two lanes.

Quick check before we move on — questions on Friday's circle are reaching you right now: who supplies what, which market is which, and which lane every arrow uses.

## Subtopic: Three Drains and Three Taps

Now picture the circling money as water running round a channel — and notice the channel was never sealed. Three drains let water out; three taps pour it back.

Drain one: the BANK. The six hundred rand granny locks into her fixed deposit steps out of the stream — no shop sees it this month. But look up: the bank is also tap one, because deposits get lent onward — to the bakery financing its new mixer — and back the money pours, as investment. Drain two: SARS. The tax deducted before the pay even lands never reaches the counter — drained. But the state is tap two: the teachers paid, the road tarred, the grants deposited — government spending gushes back in. Drain three: the BORDER. Buy the Korean laptop and those rands leave the country's channel altogether — imports drain. But when Rotterdam pays for our oranges and Japan pays for our platinum, foreign water pours IN — exports are tap three.

Three drains: savings, taxes, imports. Three taps: investment, government spending, exports. And the memory trick that beats rote learning: each drain shares a DOOR with its tap. Bank door: savings out, investment in. Government door: taxes out, spending in. Border door: imports out, exports in. Master three doors instead of six loose facts.

Carry the lesson's warning across in street terms too: drains are not the enemy. The deposit granny makes is precisely the money that becomes the bakery's mixer. Drains and taps tell you which WAY the water is moving — never whether the movement is good.

Your questions for this part are up now: for each little story, name the drain or the tap — and the door it passes through.

## Subtopic: Reading the Water Level

Last picture: what the whole diagram is FOR. Watch the level of the water.

If the three drains together pull more out than the three taps pour in, the level falls: less spending reaches the counters, so orders shrink, so production slows, so pay packets thin — and next month, even less water circles. That is a contracting economy, and the street feels it before the news announces it: quiet tills, shorter shifts. If the taps together beat the drains, the level rises: more spending, more production, more hiring — the good spiral. Drains equal to taps? The level holds — economists say EQUILIBRIUM; the street says things are steady.

This is why the news circles endlessly around savings, budgets, imports and exports — every report is someone reading drains and taps, estimating next quarter's water level. "Treasury announces an infrastructure programme" — the government tap opening wider. "Households cutting back and saving more" — the bank drain pulling harder. "Record citrus exports" — the border tap gushing. You can now translate every such headline into the diagram.

And the classifying game that assessments play is the one you have just mastered: a school buys imported projectors — border drain. A mine lands a platinum order from Japan — border tap. SARS collects; the clinic gets its new wing — government door, both directions. Stokvel money banked — bank drain; the loan it funds for the bakery — bank tap.

And the last questions of the lesson are with you right now: read the level — drains against taps — and push each headline through its correct door. Own the three doors, and you own the most useful diagram in the subject.
