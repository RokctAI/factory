**Thandi:** Class questions before we finish! First: if my savings get lent out and come straight back as investment, why call savings a leakage at all? It sounds like the money never really left.

**Tutor:** Because the return trip is not guaranteed — and the gap between "left the stream" and "might come back" is where entire recessions live. Follow the six hundred rand carefully. The moment it enters the fixed deposit, the spending stream has lost it: no shop rings it up this month, and that loss is real and immediate. Whether it re-enters depends on a second, separate decision made by different people — a bank must choose to lend it, and a firm must choose to borrow and buy equipment with it. In confident times that chain connects smoothly and your instinct holds: out through the drain, back through the tap. But when firms fear the future, they stop borrowing no matter how much sits in the banks — savings pile up while investment dries up, leakage exceeding injection, and the circle tightens round after round. That is why economists insist on recording the two flows separately: savings and investment are decided by different actors for different reasons, and the economy's fate hangs on whether the two decisions happen to match. The label "leakage" isn't an accusation — it's an accounting honesty.

***

**Thandi:** Next: taxes and government spending go through the same door — so if the government just spends everything it collects, do they cancel out? Why track them as two flows?

**Tutor:** For the same deep reason as savings and investment: the two sides of a door are separate decisions, and their MISMATCH is the interesting part. A government seldom spends exactly what it collects. Spend more than the tax take — a budget deficit — and the government door is injecting more than it drains, pushing the water level up; that is precisely the lever states pull in a downswing, borrowing to keep the taps open while tax revenue sags. Collect more than is spent — a surplus — and the door drains on net, cooling an overheated economy. And even when the totals happen to match, the composition matters: tax drawn from one part of the stream returns as spending somewhere quite different — deducted from a Sandton salary, reappearing as a nurse's wage in Lusikisiki or a grant in a rural household. The door doesn't just recycle water; it moves it across the economy. So we track both flows because their difference is deliberate policy, and their routing is redistribution — neither is visible if you net them into one number.

***

**Thandi:** Third: imports being a leakage makes buying foreign goods sound unpatriotic. Should we read the model as saying imports are bad for the economy?

**Tutor:** No — the model classifies flows; it does not moralise them, and this is the leakage-virtue warning wearing its border-door clothes. Imports drain spending from the domestic circle, true. But look at what arrives in the real lane going the other way: machinery our factories cannot yet build, medicines, fuel, components — inputs that make domestic production POSSIBLE. Cut those off and the circle you were protecting shrinks for lack of parts. Imports also discipline domestic prices through competition and stretch every household's rand further. The sensible reading is about balance and partnership: exports earn the foreign currency that pays for imports, so a country that wants the border tap and drain in rough balance works on being good at selling to the world, not on refusing to buy from it. Notice too that our imports are another country's exports — if every nation treated imports as sin, world trade, and every export job in our citrus and platinum industries, would vanish. The model's cool language is deliberate: direction of flow, never virtue — at the bank door, the government door, and the border door alike.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in real life all six flows move at once, every day. How would anyone actually SEE whether leakages or injections are winning?

**Tutor:** You watch the consequences, because the balance announces itself in the numbers a country publishes. No statistician tallies "total leakages" on a Friday afternoon — instead, the effects surface in the indicators: when production, employment and incomes are rising quarter after quarter, injections are outrunning leakages; when they shrink, the drains are winning; the growth figures are the water level in printed form. Alongside those, each door publishes its own gauge. The government door: the budget balance — deficit means net tap, surplus means net drain. The border door: the trade figures — exports against imports, month by month. The bank door: credit extension and savings data from the financial sector. Economists read all three gauges together the way a nurse reads pulse, temperature and blood pressure — no single number is the patient, but the pattern is unmistakable. And that is the practical gift of this diagram: it turns the blizzard of published statistics into three doors and a water level — a structure your mind can actually hold while the news rushes past it.
