# Part 1 — Expert

The opening lesson defined the discipline; this one meets the problem that brought it into existence. SCARCITY — limited resources facing unlimited wants — is called the basic economic problem because everything else in the subject is some kind of answer to it. On today's route: scarcity separated cleanly from a shortage, the choices scarcity forces on every decision-maker, opportunity cost as the real price of every choice, the three questions no economy can escape, and the ethical dimension — how economic activity can promote or trample human rights and the environment.

## Subtopic: Scarcity — the Permanent Condition

Definitions first, and with full precision, because sloppy wording here costs marks all year. SCARCITY is the permanent condition in which resources are insufficient to satisfy all wants. Two properties do the heavy lifting. It is universal: wealthy and poor economies face it alike, because wants grow along with the means to satisfy them. And it is permanent: no amount of growth retires it. Now hold it against a SHORTAGE, which is a temporary market situation in which, at the current price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied — cooking oil stripped from the shelves during a supply scare, fuel queues when a refinery goes down. A shortage ends: prices adjust, the trucks arrive. Scarcity never does. A wealthy family experiences hardly any shortages and still lives under scarcity — even the largest fortune cannot purchase extra hours in a day. Confusing these two terms is the classic error of this topic; keep the definitions sharp enough to cut.

Because resources are scarce, every actor in the economy must CHOOSE. The household weighs new school uniforms against fixing the geyser. The firm weighs a delivery vehicle against an advertising push. The government weighs another teacher's post against another stretch of tarred road. Scarcity does more than limit — it forces a RANKING: which want is met now, which must wait, and which never gets its turn.

And every choice drags a shadow behind it — the thing not chosen. That shadow deserves its own section, because it is the single most powerful idea in the subject.

Hold it there — this section's questions are with you now: scarcity and shortage defined and separated, and why wealth offers no escape from the condition.

## Subtopic: Opportunity Cost — the True Price

OPPORTUNITY COST is the value of the NEXT BEST ALTERNATIVE given up when a choice is made. Note the precision: not everything forgone — only the single best alternative you would genuinely have taken instead. Ayanda has three hundred rand and buys soccer boots; her honest second choice was a month's gym membership. Her opportunity cost is one month at the gym — not the movie outing that ranked third, not the whole shop.

Now run the concept through all three levels of the economy. The HOUSEHOLD: a Sunday shift at the car wash pays one hundred and eighty rand — but its true cost is the afternoon of maths revision that did not happen, and in the term of a big test, that afternoon may be worth more than the money. The BUSINESS: Lerato has seven thousand five hundred rand and buys a second-hand display fridge for her cold-drink stall; the next best use was extra stock that would have earned its mark-up — so the fridge's real cost is that forgone trading profit. The STATE: two billion rand on a highway interchange is two billion rand withdrawn from its next-best use — rural clinics, school maintenance — and every national budget debate, heard properly, is an argument about opportunity cost. The price tells you what you PAY; opportunity cost tells you what you GIVE UP — and economics insists the second is the true measure.

Two rules of discipline for written answers. One: express opportunity cost in REAL terms — the alternative itself: "one month's gym membership", "the forgone stock profit" — never in rands, because the rands left your hands whichever way you chose. Two: only the NEXT BEST alternative counts — rank the options first, then name the runner-up. Later this year the production possibility curve will turn this idea into a diagram; today it must be solid in words.

Pause for this section's questions, with you now — opportunity cost defined, applied at all three levels, and stated correctly as the next-best alternative in real terms.

## Subtopic: The Three Questions Every Economy Must Answer

Scarcity does not only squeeze individuals; it corners entire societies — and every society, whatever flag it flies, must answer the same three questions.

WHAT to produce? Land planted with sugar cane is not growing vegetables; a plant tooled for bakkies is not building buses. Every economy must settle its basket: how much food, housing, defence, entertainment. HOW to produce? Labour-intensive methods spread work across many hands; capital-intensive methods let machines do it with few — and in a country carrying heavy unemployment, that choice carries real social weight. FOR WHOM to produce? Who ends up with the output — allocated by income and prices, by need, or by policy? This is the distribution question, and it is the most politically explosive of the three.

Societies answer through their ECONOMIC SYSTEMS. In a MARKET economy, prices do the answering: what sells at a profit gets made, the cheapest method wins, and goods flow to those willing and able to pay. In a PLANNED economy — also called a command economy — the state answers: officials set targets, choose methods and direct distribution. In a MIXED economy, markets answer most questions while the state corrects, redistributes and supplies what markets neglect. South Africa is unmistakably mixed: private supermarkets and factories operating alongside public hospitals, no-fee schools, social grants and state-owned enterprises. At this level you need the classification plus one honest observation: the pure forms are textbook endpoints; every real economy lives somewhere between them, differing only in degree.

Stop for this section's questions, in front of you now — the three questions with examples of each, and the three systems matched to whoever does the answering.

## Subtopic: Human Rights and the Environment

The topic closes on its ethical thread: economic activity can PROMOTE human rights and the environment — or VIOLATE both — and a complete answer argues each direction with evidence.

Promotion first. Production creates jobs, and decent work funds the rights our Constitution promises: food, shelter, education, healthcare — a wage is the everyday engine of dignity. Exchange knits people into networks of trust — markets reward reliability and service. Tax collected on economic activity pays for the public delivery of rights: schools, hospitals, grants. And a growing economy steadily widens what a society can afford for its people.

Violation, just as real. Production can exploit: children in work instead of school, wages beneath survival, unsafe sites — labour law exists because these abuses keep resurfacing. Markets can exclude: a person with no income sends no price signal and simply disappears from the market's view — the FOR WHOM question answered coldly. And the environment: production draws down natural resources and pushes out waste, and when those costs fall on outsiders — the valley breathing a brickworks' smoke, the village downstream of its ash — economists call them EXTERNALITIES: real costs carried by people who were never part of the transaction. Left unchecked, the chase for output eats the resource base tomorrow's production needs — which is why SUSTAINABILITY matters: meeting today's needs without stripping tomorrow's.

The assessment pattern to prepare for: a scenario — a brickworks that employs half a town while smoking up its valley and silting its stream — and the instruction to argue BOTH directions, then weigh them. Employment and incomes promoted; health and environmental rights violated; remedies found in regulation, in taxing the externality, in enforcing labour law. Economics does not make society's choice — it names the trade-off exactly, and exact naming is the whole gift of this topic.

The final questions of this part are with you now: promotion and violation argued with examples, externalities defined, and one scenario weighed on both sides.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the identical problem, seen from the kitchen table — because scarcity, choice and the cost of the road not taken get negotiated in every household before the kettle has even boiled.

## Subtopic: Not a Shortage — a Forever Thing

When cooking oil vanishes off the shelves for a week, that is a SHORTAGE — irritating, temporary, gone when the next delivery lands and the price settles. Scarcity is a different creature altogether, and telling them apart is the skill.

Scarcity is the forever-gap between what people want and what exists to satisfy them. It cannot be closed, because wants refill themselves. Get the soccer boots — now the tracksuit calls. The family finally fences the yard — now the gate looks shabby. A wealthy household and a struggling one both live inside the gap; the wealthy one's wants have simply gone upmarket — overseas flights instead of taxi fare. And TIME is scarce for absolutely everyone: no bank balance buys a twenty-fifth hour.

So run the test: can it be fixed by a delivery, a price change, or a bit of waiting? Shortage. Is it the permanent state of wanting more than there is? Scarcity. The truck fixes the oil aisle; nothing in the world fixes scarcity.

And because the gap is forever, life becomes RANKING. The household with two thousand five hundred rand left after rent never asks "what do we want?" — the answer is everything. It asks "what comes FIRST?" Uniforms before the tracksuit. Electricity before the outing. Scarcity's real power is not that it refuses you — it is that it makes you queue your wants and defend the order.

Quick check before we move on — questions on the forever thing are landing with you right now: shortage or scarcity, case by case, and what the gap forces every household to do.

## Subtopic: The Price Tag Behind the Price Tag

Every choice carries two price tags. The first is printed and visible — three hundred rand for the boots. The second is invisible and heavier: what ELSE those three hundred rand would have become. Economics calls the hidden one opportunity cost, and once your eye learns to find it, it never stops.

The rule for reading the hidden tag: name the NEXT BEST thing you would have done — only the runner-up, never the whole catalogue. You take the boots; your genuine second choice was the gym month. Then the true cost of the boots is: one month at the gym. Not three hundred rand — the money was leaving your pocket either way — but the gym month you will now do without.

Walk it around the neighbourhood. The learner who works the Sunday car-wash shift banks one hundred and eighty rand — but the hidden tag reads "one afternoon of maths revision", and in test season that tag can outweigh the cash. Lerato's seven and a half thousand rand goes into a display fridge — her hidden tag is the profit the extra stock would have earned. Even FREE things wear the tag: the free holiday soccer clinic still costs the afternoon — and that afternoon had a next-best use of its own.

That is why economists insist the true price of anything is the next-best thing surrendered for it. Money reads the front of the tag; your life is lived on the back of it. People who read both sides make visibly better calls — watch for them, at home and in the news.

Your questions for this part are up now: flip the tags — for each choice, name the runner-up, in real things, never in rands.

## Subtopic: People and the Planet in the Ledger

Last stretch: the street-level view of the topic's ethical thread — because the economy is not numbers floating somewhere; it is what happens to people and places you can walk to.

The promoting column, visible on any main road: the new packhouse hiring means wages; wages mean school fees covered, the clinic card paid, dignity funded by work. Busy shops mean choice and sharper prices. And the tax skimmed off all that activity — that is where the school's new roof and the clinic's medicine stock come from. When the economy hums, rights stop being paper promises and become data in the phone and supper on the stove.

The violating column, equally visible. The boss paying under what living costs. The site with no hard hats. And the cost that slips past every till: the brickworks whose smoke the valley breathes, whose ash washes into the stream the next village drinks from. Catch the trick — the bricks' PRICE includes none of the coughing; somebody pays that cost, just never at the counter. Economists call it an externality: a bill delivered to people who never signed the deal. Ignored long enough, it means today's production quietly consumes tomorrow's — emptied rivers, exhausted soil — the opposite of sustainability, which in plain terms says: do not cook tonight's supper by burning tomorrow's table.

So when the assessment gives you the classic setup — the brickworks that employs the town AND chokes its valley — refuse the one-sided answer. Rule two columns like a bookkeeper: jobs, wages, taxes on the left; lungs, water, tomorrow's resources on the right. Then name the repairs: rules enforced, the polluter billed for the hidden cost, workers' rights defended. Two columns, honestly totalled — that is economic thinking with its conscience switched on.

And the last questions of the lesson are with you right now: promotion and violation from your own area, the sneaky cost named, and the brickworks weighed in both columns. The basic problem now belongs to you — everything else this year is society's set of attempted answers.
