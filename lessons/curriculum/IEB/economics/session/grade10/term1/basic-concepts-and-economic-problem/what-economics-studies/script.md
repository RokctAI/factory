# Part 1 — Expert

Before demand curves, national accounts or business cycles can mean anything, the subject itself must be pinned down. What exactly does Economics study? How is the discipline organised, what methods does it trust, and in what sense is it a science at all? Precise definitions here are the foundation every later topic stands on — and in your final exams, foundations are where careless candidates bleed easy marks. Today: the elements of economic life, the branches of the subject, the working methods of economists, and the honest case for calling Economics a science.

## Subtopic: The Elements — What Economics Is Made Of

Economics is the social science that studies how individuals, businesses and governments use LIMITED resources to satisfy UNLIMITED needs and wants. That single sentence is dense with working parts, so unpack it word by word.

NEEDS are the requirements of survival and basic dignity: food, clean water, shelter, clothing, basic healthcare. WANTS are everything desired beyond that line — the branded sneakers, the gaming console, the bigger television — and the crucial property of wants is that they never run out: satisfy one and the next steps forward to replace it. Set against this endless list stand the RESOURCES, formally the factors of production, and there are exactly four. Natural resources: land and everything nature provides on or under it. Labour: human effort, of muscle and of mind. Capital: the man-made tools of production — machinery, buildings, vehicles, equipment. Entrepreneurship: the organising talent that combines the other three, takes the decisions and shoulders the risk of loss. Each factor collects its own distinct reward, and this pairing is tested relentlessly: natural resources earn rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest, and entrepreneurship earns profit. Four factors, four rewards — learn them as pairs, never as loose lists.

Connecting resources to satisfaction are three PROCESSES. PRODUCTION combines the factors to create goods and services. EXCHANGE trades what has been produced, through markets and money. CONSUMPTION uses the goods and services, killing off wants — temporarily. Production, exchange, consumption: every economy in history, from a village barter circle to a modern industrial state, runs this same three-stage engine.

Hold it there — the questions for this section are with you now: the definition dissected, the four factor-and-reward pairs, and the three processes in their working order.

## Subtopic: The Branches — How the Subject Divides

The subject organises itself into branches, and the first split is the one every future lesson will lean on.

MICROECONOMICS is the study of the individual decision-making unit: a single household stretching its budget, a single firm choosing its price, a single market settling where supply meets demand. Its questions are close-range: why did the price of chicken rise this month? What makes a panel-beating shop take on an extra apprentice? MACROECONOMICS studies the economy taken as one whole: total national output, unemployment across the workforce, inflation as a rise in the general level of prices, national income. Its questions are wide-angle: is the country growing? Why are prices everywhere climbing? The reliable sorting test: one shop's bread price is micro; the cost of living for the nation is macro.

Beyond that great divide sit the named working branches. ECONOMIC THEORY constructs the laws and models that explain behaviour. APPLIED ECONOMICS carries theory into particular fields — labour economics, agricultural economics, transport economics. ECONOMIC HISTORY traces how economies developed over time, from subsistence and barter to money and industry. DEVELOPMENT ECONOMICS concentrates on raising the welfare of poorer economies — a branch with obvious weight in Southern Africa. And cutting across all of them runs the distinction between POSITIVE and NORMATIVE economics. A positive statement describes what IS and can be checked against evidence: the economy grew by nought comma nine percent last year. A normative statement declares what OUGHT to be: the fuel levy should be scrapped. The first is testable; the second is a value judgement, however well argued. Sorting statements across that line is a classic assessment task — practise it until it is reflex.

Break here for this section's questions, which are with you now — micro separated from macro, the working branches matched to their jobs, and positive sorted from normative.

## Subtopic: The Methods — How Economists Work

Economics earns the name science through its METHOD — the same disciplined loop the natural sciences use, adapted for studying society.

First, OBSERVATION: economists watch the actual economy and record it as data — prices, employment, output, spending. Second, HYPOTHESIS: a candidate explanation is proposed — for example, when the price of a product rises, buyers take less of it. Third, TESTING: the hypothesis is confronted with evidence; since no one can rerun a whole country under laboratory control, the economist's laboratory is statistics and history. Fourth, LAW or THEORY: an explanation that survives repeated testing earns the title of a law — the law of demand — but always wearing its guard clause, CETERIS PARIBUS: all other things remaining equal. The clause matters because in a real society everything shifts simultaneously; a law isolates one relationship by holding the rest still in thought.

The working tools: DATA, gathered in South Africa by Statistics South Africa and the South African Reserve Bank, and internationally by bodies like the World Bank; INDICES, single numbers that compress thousands of observations — the consumer price index is the famous one; MODELS, deliberate simplifications of reality, like the circular flow diagram arriving later this term — wrong in the details, right in the structure, and useful for exactly that reason; and GRAPHS, the language the subject speaks most fluently.

Then the honest limits. Economics studies people, and people learn, adapt, and change behaviour in response to predictions about them. So economic laws are strong tendencies rather than iron certainties, forecasts carry error margins, and public disagreement between economists is the normal sound of the method working on difficult material. When an answer asks for the limitations of the method, state them plainly — that honesty is rewarded.

Pause for this section's questions, with you now: the four-step loop in order, the tools named with examples, ceteris paribus explained, and the limits stated without embarrassment.

## Subtopic: Economics Among the Sciences

Where does Economics sit in the family of knowledge? The classification must be argued, not merely announced.

Economics is a SOCIAL science: its raw material is human behaviour — choosing, trading, cooperating, competing. It shares that territory with sociology, psychology and political science, and it inherits their shared difficulty: the subject matter has free will. Yet within the social family, Economics is the most heavily QUANTITATIVE member. It measures everything it can reach — prices, quantities, growth rates, indices — and it builds formal mathematical models. That habit gives it a strong family resemblance to the natural sciences: hypotheses proposed, data collected, predictions tested, laws formulated.

Now run the contrast with a natural science such as chemistry. The chemist controls laboratory conditions and repeats the experiment at will; the resulting laws hold without exception — the same reaction under the same conditions gives the same result, every time. The economist can almost never experiment on an entire economy, cannot freeze society while one variable moves, and finds regularities that hold usually rather than always. The defensible classification is therefore this: Economics is a social science that applies scientific method — empirical, quantitative, model-building — to subject matter that will never behave like a molecule.

Why does the classification matter beyond the mark it earns? Because membership of the sciences means economic claims must answer to EVIDENCE, not to volume or authority. When a public figure promises that some policy will create jobs, the economist's reflex is to ask for the data, the mechanism and the comparison. That reflex — test the claim before believing it — is the most valuable export this subject offers every citizen who studies it.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: argue the classification in both directions — what Economics shares with the natural sciences, what it can never share, and why admitting the difference strengthens rather than weakens the subject.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ground from your own street — because you have been practising economics since your first week of pocket money; today we simply put names to what you already do.

## Subtopic: The Science of Not Enough

The entire subject fits in one line: everyone wants more than there is — so now what?

Test it on yourself at the tuckshop with sixty rand. The wants queue up instantly: the pie, the cold drink, airtime, saving toward the sneakers. Sixty rand cannot cover the queue — and here is the sting — the moment one want is fed, another shuffles forward to take its place. That self-refilling queue is what economists mean by unlimited wants. The sixty rand — and standing behind it, all the world's land, workers, machines and risk-takers — is what they mean by limited resources. Economics is the study of that permanent gap and of every arrangement human beings have invented for living inside it.

Every economy, from your pocket to the whole country, runs the same three-step engine across the gap. Somebody MAKES: production. Your aunt who runs a weekend car wash combines the yard she uses, the cousins she pays to wash, the pressure hose she bought, and her own nerve in setting the whole thing up — those are the four factors, working in an ordinary driveway. People SWAP: exchange — clean cars traded for fifty-rand notes. People USE: consumption — the customer drives off happy, and by next Saturday the dust has restocked his wants. Make, swap, use: the grand vocabulary of production, exchange and consumption describes a driveway perfectly.

And each contributor collects a different kind of pay. The yard's owner collects rent. The cousins collect wages. Money tied up in the hose earns interest. And your aunt, who carried the risk that nobody would pitch up, keeps the profit. Four contributions, four rewards — visible any Saturday morning.

Quick check before we push on — the questions on the not-enough problem are reaching you right now: wants against resources, the three-step engine, and who earns which reward at the car wash.

## Subtopic: Close-Up Lens, Drone Shot

Economics looks at the world through two camera settings, and once you can name them you will never mix them up again.

The CLOSE-UP lens frames one decision-maker at a time. Why did the salon put braids up by thirty rand? Why does the corner shop stock more cold drinks in December? Why did one firm hire two extra drivers? That is microeconomics — the economics of somebody in particular, of a single market small enough to stand inside.

The DRONE SHOT lifts the camera until the entire country fills the frame. Not one salon's price, but all prices rising together — that is inflation. Not one person hunting for work, but millions — the unemployment rate. Not one shop's till, but the value of everything the nation produced this year. That is macroeconomics — the economics of everybody at once.

The test that never fails: can you point at it from your gate? One taxi fare — micro. The cost of living — macro. One bakery expanding — micro. National economic growth — macro.

Add one more sorting skill while the camera is out: some sentences MEASURE and some sentences WISH. Bus fare went up by two rand — measurable, checkable, positive economics. Bus fare is a disgrace — a judgement, defensible but not testable, normative economics. Neither kind of sentence is wrong; the damage comes from confusing them — one person quoting measurements, the other voicing values, both convinced they are arguing about facts. Economics teaches you to label the sentence before you fight about it.

Your questions for this part are up now: frame by frame and sentence by sentence — close-up or drone, measurement or wish.

## Subtopic: Testing Like a Scientist, Watching People Like a Neighbour

Final piece: what makes this a science — and what kind of science it can honestly claim to be.

You already use the method. You notice the tuckshop queue is longest on Mondays — observation. You guess why: nobody packs lunch after a weekend — hypothesis. You watch the next three Mondays to check — testing. The pattern holds — you now own a small, tested law of school life. Notice, guess, test, keep what survives: that loop is the whole scientific method, and economists simply run it on prices, jobs and spending instead of tuckshop queues.

Economists bolt on one phrase you should learn to love: CETERIS PARIBUS — if nothing else changes. It is needed because real life never changes one thing at a time. Prices move AND payday lands AND a new shop opens across the road — so which caused what? The phrase lets a law pull out one thread from the tangle: IF only the price rises, people buy less. That is not cheating; it is how clear thinking handles a knotted world — one thread at a time.

And the honest difference from physics: water never boils differently because it overheard the weather forecast. People do exactly that. Families stock up before a petrol price increase precisely because the increase was announced; savers tighten up the moment retrenchment rumours start. Economics studies subjects who talk back and act on predictions about themselves. So its laws are strong tendencies, its forecasts come with humility, and when two economists disagree on the radio, you are hearing a young science wrestling the hardest material there is: us.

That is your subject: the science of not-enough, run on people. Everything ahead this year — markets, money, cycles, flows — is that one problem, studied with that one method.

And the last questions of the lesson are with you right now: run the loop on something you have noticed on your own street, deploy ceteris paribus correctly, and explain in your own words why a science of people must stay humble. That is Economics, defined.
