# Part 1 — Expert

The circular flow taught us that an economy can swell and shrink; this lesson studies the pattern those movements trace over the years: BUSINESS CYCLES, the recurring rise and fall of economic activity around its long-term growth path. The full toolkit for the topic: the four phases of the cycle described with their signature conditions, the indicators that reveal which phase we are in, the four movements hidden inside any economic time series, the exogenous and endogenous explanations for why cycles happen, and their very unequal effects. South Africa's own cycle, formally dated by the Reserve Bank, keeps every part of the story concrete.

## Subtopic: The Phenomenon — Four Phases

Chart a country's real production across decades and two patterns emerge together: a long upward TREND — the economy's growth path over generations — and a WAVE that lifts above and dips below that path, over and over, never on schedule. The wave is the business cycle, and its anatomy carries four names.

The UPSWING, or expansion: output climbing, firms hiring, incomes fattening, spending flowing back into yet more production — injections outrunning leakages, round after round. The PEAK: the upper turning point — activity at full stretch; factories near capacity, vacancies everywhere, but prices starting to strain upward and scarce skills commanding premiums; for the moment, there is no higher to go. The DOWNSWING — contraction, and when deep and long, recession: output sliding, retrenchment notices, incomes and spending both shrinking — the contracting spiral the circular flow predicted. And the TROUGH: the lower turning point — activity at its weakest, but quietly stocked with the ingredients of recovery: costs down, borrowing usually cheaper, worn-out equipment queueing for replacement — until the turn comes.

Two points of discipline. First, the cycle repeats without a timetable: phases differ in length and depth every time, so "cycle" promises a recurring shape, never clockwork. Second, the working definition of a recession that practice uses: two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP — memorise it word for word; it is quoted, not paraphrased.

## Subtopic: Indicators — the Economy's Instruments

Nobody sees the cycle directly; it is read off INDICATORS — measurable series that move with economic activity — and the classification that matters is by TIMING.

LEADING indicators turn BEFORE the economy does: they are the warnings. Building plans approved, new vehicle sales, fresh orders placed with factories, share prices, business confidence surveys — every one records a decision made today about activity tomorrow, which is exactly why they bend first. COINCIDENT indicators move WITH the cycle and confirm the present: current production, retail sales, this month's employment. LAGGING indicators turn only AFTER the economy has turned, certifying what already happened: unemployment is the textbook case — firms retrench late and rehire late — along with inventories relative to sales and outstanding credit. Keep the sequence straight, because questions test it directly: leading warns, coincident confirms the present, lagging certifies the past.

In South Africa the RESERVE BANK does this professionally: it compiles composite leading, coincident and lagging indicators — each a bundle of many series merged into one line — and it officially DATES the national cycle, announcing well after the fact when an upswing or downswing began and ended. Our history shows long, grinding downswings and hard-won upswings, moved by commodity prices, drought, global demand and domestic confidence — and the composite leading indicator is one of the most closely watched numbers in the financial press for exactly the reason you now understand: it bends before the economy bends.

## Subtopic: Decomposing the Time Series

A plotted economic series — monthly output, sales, employment — is never one movement; it is FOUR movements superimposed, and the skill is naming and separating them.

The TREND: the direction over decades — for most economies gently upward, powered by population growth, capital accumulation and technology. The CYCLICAL movement: the business cycle itself — waves of several years' length rolling around the trend; this topic's subject. The SEASONAL movement: patterns that repeat inside every single year because the calendar drives them — retail surging each December, farm output tracking the harvest, coastal tourism tracking the holidays. So predictable is this rhythm that statisticians strip it out and publish "seasonally adjusted" figures, precisely so the cycle underneath becomes visible. And IRREGULAR movements: one-off shocks with no pattern at all — a flood, a port strike, a pandemic month — noise that decomposition sets to one side.

Why bother separating them? Because raw numbers deceive. Every December, retail leaps — read that leap as an upswing and you have mistaken the season for the cycle. A flood year's collapse in a province's output is irregular, not cyclical. The trained habit: before announcing "the economy is turning," ask WHICH of the four movements you are actually looking at — trend, cycle, season or shock. Scenario questions hand you exactly these confusions to untangle, and the four names, correctly applied, are the marks.

## Subtopic: Causes and Effects

Why do the waves come at all? The explanations split by where the push originates.

EXOGENOUS causes start OUTSIDE the economic machine: drought — brutal for a farming economy; wars and political shocks; a global pandemic; and, for South Africa above all, swings in WORLD COMMODITY PRICES — when iron ore and platinum prices slide on world markets, our mines, exports and incomes slide with them, through no decision taken here. In these theories the economy is a calm pond, disturbed by stones thrown from outside.

ENDOGENOUS causes grow INSIDE the machine's own workings: waves of optimism and pessimism that feed on themselves — confident firms invest, the hiring spreads confidence further, until expectations overshoot and snap; credit that expands easily in good times — banks lending freely, spending swelling — then contracts sharply the moment caution returns; and the mechanism you already own from the circular flow: injections and leakages chasing each other, overshooting and undershooting the balance point. Real-world cycles are usually both at once: an outside stone landing in water that was already sloshing.

The EFFECTS give the topic its weight, and they are never evenly shared. In the downswing: unemployment climbs, with retrenchments landing first and hardest on the newest and least skilled; incomes and profits shrink; government tax revenue falls at the precise moment more households need support; poverty and inequality deepen; and postponed investment quietly weakens the NEXT upswing before it starts. In the upswing: jobs, incomes and revenue recover — though late in the boom, prices and interest rates push upward. Knowing the phase is practical intelligence: for a household weighing new debt, a firm timing its expansion, a government drafting a budget — and for any learner reading the news with an economist's eye.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again, from the main road — because anyone who has lived through a flush year and a lean year in the same town has already FELT a business cycle. Today it gets its proper names.

## Subtopic: The Economy Breathes

Call up a year you remember when things flowed: the rank humming, shops putting up "help wanted" signs, everyone's cousin suddenly employed, month-end crowds spilling off the pavement. Now a year when everything pinched: quiet tills, shortened shifts, "come back next year, maybe." Same town, same people — different YEARS. That difference, repeating down the decades, is the business cycle: the economy breathing in and breathing out.

The breath has four moments, and you have lived through all of them. Breathing IN — the upswing: month by month a little more work, a little more spending, each feeding the other — more customers means more hiring means more customers. FULL LUNGS — the peak: everything at full stretch; jobs easy to find, but prices creeping and every resource strained — full is full. Breathing OUT — the downswing: orders thin, shifts shrink, retrenchments begin, and every cut wallet spends less at the next shop — bad news forwarding itself down the street. EMPTY — the trough: the hardest months; yet look at what gathers there unnoticed: prices low, loans cheaper, worn-out things needing replacement — and one morning, the orders start again.

Two street truths to pocket. The breath never runs on a timetable — no one can name the month the turn arrives. And the official name for a serious breathing-out: two quarters in a row of shrinking production — a recession. When the news anchor says the word, that definition is the one being used.

## Subtopic: Headlights, Speedometer, Rearview Mirror

How do you tell which phase you are in — before it is obvious to everyone? The way a driver reads the road: three instruments, facing three directions.

HEADLIGHTS shine ahead — the leading indicators. Building plans approved: somebody intends to build next year. New bakkies ordered: somebody is confident about next season's business. Share prices, confidence surveys — all of them record decisions about the FUTURE taken today, so they bend before the economy does. When the headlights dim — plans shelved, orders cancelled — the turn is coming, even while today still feels fine.

The SPEEDOMETER reads this exact moment — the coincident indicators: this month's production, this month's retail sales, who is employed right now. It tells you where you actually are, not where you are heading.

The REARVIEW MIRROR shows the road already travelled — the lagging indicators. Unemployment is the famous one: employers retrench only after the bad months have piled up, and rehire only after the good ones have proved themselves — so joblessness peaks AFTER the trough has passed. Strange but useful: when the mirror looks darkest, the worst may already be behind you.

And who watches all three dials for the whole country? The Reserve Bank. It merges dozens of series into official leading, coincident and lagging composites, and — much later — stamps the dates: the downswing began here; the upswing began there. So when a bulletin reports "the composite leading indicator declined for a third straight month," you can translate it precisely: the nation's headlights, dimming.

## Subtopic: Why the Wave Comes — and Who Gets Wet

Final stretch: what pushes the breath in and out — and who feels the splash hardest.

Some pushes arrive from OUTSIDE the economy's own machinery. A drought: no harvest, no farm wages, no spending in the farming towns. A distant war lifting the oil price at every pump here. A pandemic closing the world's doors. And South Africa's particular weather system: WORLD PRICES for what we mine — when iron ore or platinum drops on markets we never see, the mine towns of the Northern Cape and the platinum belt feel it within months, and nobody local did a thing wrong. Outside stones, landing in our pond.

Other pushes are brewed INSIDE the machine. Mood is a genuine economic force: confident firms invest and hire, and the hiring spreads the confidence — until everyone has overbuilt and the mood snaps like a branch. Credit swells the wave the same way: banks lend generously in the sunshine and call in umbrellas when the rain starts — pumping the peak higher and cutting the trough deeper. Most real waves are both at once: an outside stone hitting water that was already rocking.

And who gets wet? Never everyone equally. The downswing reaches the shop floor long before the boardroom: retrenchments start with the last hired and least trained; the township feels the quiet months before the office park does. Government is squeezed from both ends simultaneously — tax money drying up exactly as more families queue for help. And a deep trough robs the future too: the machines not bought and the skills not trained in the lean years hobble the next flush ones.

So the cycle is not a line on a chart — it is the shape of ordinary lives across time, and reading it is self-defence: settle debts near the peak, hold steady near the trough, and never mistake a December jump or a drought's dip for the tide itself.
