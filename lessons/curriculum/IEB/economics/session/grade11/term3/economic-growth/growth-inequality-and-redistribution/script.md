# Part 1 — Expert

Two questions sit at the centre of this session, and no economy escapes either: how do we produce more, and who ends up with what is produced? The first question is economic growth. The second is the distribution of income and wealth, measured with the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, and answered in policy through redistribution. South Africa happens to be the world's most extreme worked example of all of it, which makes our own numbers the best study material you will ever get.

## Subtopic: Economic Growth — Meaning, Measurement and South Africa's Record

Define it precisely: economic growth is a sustained increase in a country's production of goods and services, measured as the percentage change in real gross domestic product from one year to the next. Two corrections inside that definition do all the analytical work, and questions test both.

Correction one: real, not nominal. Nominal GDP is measured at this year's prices, so it rises whenever prices rise, even if not a single extra loaf, litre or lesson was produced. Real GDP holds prices constant, so only genuine extra output moves the number. Test yourself: nominal GDP up seven percent in a year of six percent inflation means real growth of roughly one percent. Whenever a headline quotes growth, your first reflex must be to ask whether inflation has been stripped out.

Correction two: per capita. A country's total output can rise while the population rises faster, in which case the average person is producing and receiving less than before. Real GDP per capita — real output divided by the number of people — is the version of the growth number that connects to living standards. The rule in one line: average lives only improve when output grows faster than the population sharing it.

Apply both corrections to South Africa and you get the defining fact of our recent economic history. Across the last decade, real GDP growth has averaged only about one percent a year — held down by power cuts, broken freight logistics and investor uncertainty — while the population has grown at roughly one and a half percent. Output grew; output per person fell. Slowly, year after year, the average South African has been getting poorer, and no honest discussion of our economy can start anywhere else. It also explains the urgency of the growth targets in the National Development Plan: compounding is merciless and marvellous at once — an economy creeping at one percent takes about seventy years to double, while one running at five percent doubles in roughly fourteen.

Why chase growth at all? Because growth is the funding source for everything else: employment comes from expanding production; the tax base that pays for teachers, nurses and grants expands with output; and a growing economy can lift the poor without first taking from someone else. Growth has blind spots — the entire second half of this lesson is about them — but it has no substitute.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two corrections applied, the decade's two numbers compared, and the doubling arithmetic used.

## Subtopic: Measuring Inequality — the Lorenz Curve and the Gini Coefficient

Growth tells you the size of the total; it says nothing about the shares. For that, economists draw a picture — the Lorenz curve — and we will construct it in words, slowly enough that you can redraw it from this narration alone.

Start with a square. Along the bottom, arrange the entire population from poorest to richest and measure them cumulatively in percentages — the poorest ten percent, the poorest thirty, the poorest sixty, all the way to one hundred. Up the side, measure the cumulative percentage of the nation's income those people receive between them. Now draw the reference line: under perfectly equal sharing, any thirty percent of the people would receive exactly thirty percent of the income, any eighty percent would receive eighty. Plot that and you get the straight diagonal from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner — the line of perfect equality.

The country's actual sharing traces a different path. It must begin at the bottom-left corner — nobody holds nothing — and it must end at the top-right corner — everybody together holds everything. Between the corners, though, it droops beneath the diagonal, because the early, poorer part of the population holds far less than its proportional share. Take the South African reading that should live in your memory: the poorest sixty percent of the population receive only about ten percent of total income. On the diagram, that is a point sixty along the bottom but only ten up the side — a curve hanging far below the diagonal's sixty. The rule of the picture: the deeper the droop, the worse the inequality.

The Gini coefficient compresses the droop into one number. Measure the area trapped between the diagonal and the actual curve; divide it by the whole area under the diagonal. If sharing is perfectly equal, the trapped area is zero and the Gini is zero. If one person receives literally everything, the curve runs flat along the floor and up the far wall, the trapped area fills the entire triangle, and the Gini is one. Real countries span the space between: the Nordic economies cluster around zero comma two five to zero comma three, while South Africa measures about zero comma six three — the most unequal major economy that statisticians have ever reliably recorded.

Finish with a distinction many answers blur: income versus wealth. Income is a flow — what arrives during a year. Wealth is a stock — everything owned, accumulated across years and inherited across generations. Wealth is always distributed more unequally than income, and South Africa is the extreme case again: the top tenth is estimated to hold over eighty percent of all wealth, a concentration built during generations in which the law itself barred most South Africans from owning land, firms and professional qualifications. A stock like that cannot be evened out by any single year's earnings.

Work this section's questions now: axes described cumulatively, the sixty-ten point placed, the Gini stated as a ratio of areas with both endpoints, and flow kept apart from stock.

## Subtopic: Redistribution — the Methods Governments Actually Use

When a society judges the market's distribution unacceptable, government redistributes — using the budget and the law to move income and opportunity down the queue. South Africa operates one of the largest redistribution systems of any middle-income country, and you need its methods organised, named and illustrated.

First method: progressive taxation. The definition turns on percentages, not amounts. A tax is progressive when higher earners surrender a larger share of their income — not merely more rands, which even a flat tax would collect. South Africa's personal income tax steps upward through brackets, starting at eighteen percent and reaching forty-five percent on the top slice of the highest incomes, with a small group of top earners supplying most of the revenue. Contrast the regressive case: a uniform tax on spending, like VAT, absorbs a larger fraction of a poor household's budget than a rich one's — which is precisely why the basket of staples, maize meal and brown bread among them, carries no VAT.

Second method: cash transfers. The social grant system reaches around twenty-eight million people every month — the older persons grant above two thousand rand, the child support grant above five hundred, disability and foster care grants, and the social relief of distress grant beneath them all. No other instrument moves purchasing power to the poorest households as directly, and research consistently finds the grants cutting extreme poverty and childhood hunger.

Third method: redistribution in kind — giving services rather than money. Free basic water and electricity allocations, no-fee schools, the school nutrition programme, subsidised public clinics, state-built housing. Every service delivered free is a rand the household keeps, which makes in-kind provision income by another route.

Fourth method: reshaping the labour market and the ownership of assets. The national minimum wage places a floor beneath the working poor. Employment equity and broad-based empowerment policy push opportunity and ownership outward; land reform addresses the land stock directly; and sustained spending on schooling in townships and rural districts targets the most powerful redistribution of all — moving skills, which set what a person can earn for the rest of their life.

Then argue the tension, because one-sided answers score poorly. Redistribution spends what growth earns: push tax rates too far and investment can relocate, shrinking the base everything depends on. Yet leaving a Gini of zero comma six three untouched also strangles growth, because people without nutrition, education or transport can neither produce nor buy. The mature conclusion: growth and redistribution are complements — growth pays for the sharing, and sharing, by building skills and widening demand, feeds the growth.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: four methods with examples, progressive defined through percentages, and the tension argued in both directions.

## Subtopic: Achieving Growth, the Constraints in the Way, and Standard of Living

Last piece of the expert half: where growth actually comes from, why South Africa's is stuck, and what standard of living really measures.

Growth has only two ultimate sources: more inputs, and more output per input. More capital — machines, plants, roads, ports — bought with savings, whether our own or foreign investors'. More capable labour — schooling, training, nutrition, health. Better methods — technology, innovation, and the discipline of trade, because exporting forces firms up to world standards and gives them the world as a customer. And wrapped around all of these, an investment climate worth the risk: dependable electricity and transport, personal safety, predictable policy, clean administration.

Now audit South Africa against that list, item by item, because every national constraint is one of the growth sources with a padlock on it. Electricity and logistics: years of load shedding and a freight rail system moving far below capacity have capped how much the economy can physically produce. Skills: schools release too few learners with the mathematics, technical and digital capabilities firms are hiring for — so vacancies sit unfilled while unemployment stands above thirty percent, a mismatch rather than a shortage of people. Savings and investment: South Africans save too little to finance the country's investment needs, leaving us reliant on foreign capital — the very capital that policy uncertainty, corruption and crime chase away. Learn the framing sentence: each constraint is a growth method with a padlock on it. It converts a memorised list into an essay structure.

Finally, standard of living. Real GDP per capita is the standard gauge, but list its blind spots and you understand why it cannot stand alone. It is silent on distribution — the average can rise while most people fall behind. It omits unpaid household labour and undercounts informal work. It records the output of a polluting factory as pure gain and the damage as nothing. And it measures money, never health or learning. Hence the broader gauges, led by the Human Development Index, which sets income alongside life expectancy and education. South Africa's HDI position sits well below its income position — we produce like an upper-middle country and live shorter, less schooled lives than that production predicts — and that gap between growth and development is exactly where this term's next topic begins.

The final questions of this part are with you now: sources of growth matched to their South African padlocks, and GDP per capita's blind spots set against the HDI.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Time to shrink the whole topic to the size of things you can actually see: a potjie at a family braai, and a queue of one hundred people holding one hundred coins.

## Subtopic: The Pot and the People — Growth Done Honestly

Let the economy be the potjie at your family's year-end braai. Growth means exactly one thing: this year's pot has more food in it than last year's pot. Not fancier plates, not higher prices — more actual food.

Two tricks can make the pot look bigger than it is, and honest measurement exists to catch both.

Trick one: the price trick. Suppose the pot holds precisely the same amount as last year, but everything that went into it cost more rands. Measured in money, the pot looks bigger; measured in food, nothing changed. Economists catch this by valuing this year's pot at last year's prices — real growth — so that rising price tags cannot masquerade as rising output. Hear a claim like growth of seven percent in a year of six percent inflation, and translate instantly: about one percent more actual food.

Trick two: the headcount trick. Now suppose the pot genuinely grew by two percent — but this year's braai has three percent more people around it. The pot is bigger and every plate is smaller. Dividing the pot by the number of eaters gives the per-person number — per capita — and only that number tells you whether the average person at the braai is doing better.

Hold South Africa up to the braai. For roughly ten years, our pot has grown about one percent a year while the crowd around it has grown about one and a half percent. Bigger pot; smaller plates; the average person slowly worse off. If you master a single fact from this lesson, make it that one — you can now explain the national mood at any dinner table using nothing but a pot and a headcount.

So why does everyone from unions to bankers demand a bigger pot? Because the pot is where everything else is ladled from. Jobs exist because production expands. The taxes that build schools and clinics and pay grants are scooped from the pot before the plates are filled. Without growth, helping one plate means emptying another — possible, and sometimes right, but always limited. With growth, plates can fill together.

Your questions for this section are with you now — every scenario hides either the price trick or the headcount trick, and your job is to name which.

## Subtopic: The Queue of One Hundred — Seeing Inequality Without a Graph

Now for the sharing — and instead of a graph, a scene.

Shrink South Africa to one hundred people and line them up across a school field, emptiest pockets on the left, fullest on the right. Shrink the year's entire income to one hundred coins, held somewhere along that line. Your task: walk the queue from the left, counting coins as you pass each person.

If income were shared perfectly equally, the walk would be dull: one coin per person, every person. Twenty people in, twenty coins; seventy people in, seventy coins. Draw that steady count and you get a straight diagonal climb — the picture economists label perfect equality.

Now walk the queue as it actually stands. You pass the first twenty people — a fifth of the whole country — and your count has reached barely two or three coins. You keep walking. At person sixty, well past the halfway mark, the count has crawled to only about ten. Then everything changes at the far end: the final ten people in the queue hold roughly sixty-five of the hundred coins between them. Your count barely moves for sixty paces and then avalanches in the last ten. Sketch that walk — the long crawl, the late avalanche — and you have drawn the Lorenz curve, drooping far beneath the straight diagonal. The droop is the inequality.

One number captures the droop: the Gini coefficient — the gap between the steady walk and the real walk, taken as a fraction of the largest gap possible. Gini of zero: no gap, every step one coin, perfect equality. Gini of one: the largest gap possible, a single person clutching all hundred coins. South Africa's walk scores about zero comma six three — the deepest droop of any major economy on Earth.

And one more layer before the questions. The coins you counted are income — this year's flow. But look at what the queue owns rather than earns — houses, land, shares, inherited savings — and you are looking at wealth, the stock. The wealth queue is even more lopsided than the income queue, because stocks accumulate across generations, and within living memory the law reserved land, businesses and qualifications for a minority. When you count the wealth queue, you are counting history.

Take this section's questions now — read the count at twenty, at sixty and at the final ten, and say what the droop and its number mean.

## Subtopic: Moving Coins Along the Queue — and Growing the Pot at the Same Time

A queue that lopsided invites the obvious question: can coins be moved? They can, and South Africa moves them on an enormous scale, in four distinct ways.

Way one: collect proportionally more from the fullest pockets. Personal income tax climbs in steps — the higher your income, the higher the rate on the top slice, ending at forty-five cents in the rand. That staircase is what progressive means: a bigger share from the rich, not merely more rands. Watch for the reverse trap, though: a flat spending tax like VAT quietly takes its biggest bite from the emptiest pockets, which is exactly why maize meal and brown bread are sold VAT-free.

Way two: place coins straight into the emptiest pockets. Social grants land in around twenty-eight million hands every month — over two thousand rand for pensioners, over five hundred per child, plus disability and distress grants. Nothing else the state does reaches hungry households faster, and the evidence says it genuinely reduces extreme poverty.

Way three: hand over goods and services instead of coins. The free basic water and electricity allocation, the no-fee school, the feeding scheme at break time, the subsidised clinic, the state-built house. A rand the household never has to spend arrives just as surely as a rand paid in cash.

Way four: change the size of tomorrow's coins. A minimum wage lifts the floor under the working poor; ownership and land policies shift the assets themselves; and money spent making township and rural schools genuinely good shifts the deepest thing of all — skills, the machine that mints a person's future income.

Close with the tension, argued honestly in both directions. Every coin moved is first ladled from the pot, so moving coins depends on the pot growing — tax the cooks too brutally and they stop cooking: investment leaves and takes the jobs along. But it cuts the other way too: a queue where sixty people share ten coins cannot buy much, learn much or produce much, so extreme inequality starves the very pot it depends on. The answer worth writing is never growth or sharing — it is that each one funds and feeds the other, and the whole craft of policy is keeping both moving at once.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: the four ways named with examples, the staircase explained in percentages, and the pot-and-queue tension argued both ways.
