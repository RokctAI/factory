**Bianca:** The class has queued up some good ones before we close. First: if raising the repo rate hurts families with bonds and can cost jobs, why is the Reserve Bank allowed to do it without asking anyone?

**Tutor:** That question is really about why independence exists, and the answer is one of the great lessons of economic history. Inflation control requires decisions that hurt now and pay off later — exactly the kind of decision elected politicians struggle to take, because the pain arrives before the next election and the benefit after it. Countries that let governments steer interest rates kept discovering the same pattern: rates held too low for too long to keep voters happy, inflation ratcheting upward, and then a far more brutal correction later. So the modern design hands the instrument to an independent central bank with a public mandate — in our case, written into the Constitution: protect the value of the currency, pursued through the three-to-six percent band. The Bank is not unaccountable — it must explain every decision publicly, its committee's reasoning is published, and its mandate comes from the democratic order itself. What independence removes is not accountability but short-term political convenience. And notice the deeper point for your essays: the design admits that inflation is so damaging, especially to the poor, who hold cash and cannot hedge, that we deliberately place its cure beyond the reach of the election cycle. That is not anti-democratic; it is democracy protecting its own future purchasing power.

**Bianca:** Second: the transmission chain has five links, and some of the class are worried about blanking on the order. Is there a way to rebuild it under pressure instead of just memorising it?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is better than memorising: rebuild it by asking who pays whom. Start at the top of the money system and follow the cost downhill. Who borrows from the Reserve Bank? The commercial banks — so a repo rise hits their funding cost first; that is link one. What do banks do with a cost increase? What every business does — pass it to customers, by raising prime; link two. Who borrows at rates built on prime? Every bondholder, car buyer and card user in the country — their repayments rise; link three. What does a household do when repayments eat more of the month? It spends less elsewhere, and firms facing dearer finance shelve their plans; link four. And what happens to prices when the whole country spends less at once? Sellers lose the power to keep raising them — demand pressure fades, inflation drifts back toward the band; link five. Notice you never memorised anything: you followed the money downhill from the central bank to the shop shelf, and the order generated itself. Practise the rebuild twice and you will be able to produce the chain forwards, backwards, or from any link a question hands you — which matters, because questions love starting you in the middle.

**Bianca:** Third: why do we even need fiscal policy if the repo rate already controls spending? It sounds like two tools doing one job.

**Tutor:** They overlap on one job — managing total demand — but each does things the other cannot, and the differences are the substance of every comparison question. The repo rate is fast and economy-wide: the committee meets every second month, the rate moves that day, and it touches every borrower at once. But that reach is also its weakness — it cannot distinguish. It lands on the reckless borrower and the careful family alike, and it can only push the whole economy harder or softer. Fiscal policy is the opposite creature. It is slow — one Budget a year, political approval required — but it can aim: a duty placed on luxury goods and not on bread, spending routed to one province's clinics, a grant lifted for the poorest households specifically. Fiscal policy can also do distribution, which the repo rate cannot attempt: no interest rate decision can feed a hungry child or build a school. And there is a third reason both exist: they check each other. When the Bank tightens while the treasury spends freely, the policies collide and borrowers pay extra for the contradiction — which is why the mature answer to almost any policy scenario ends by asking whether the two hands are pulling together. One wheel, two taps, and an economy that needs all three working in the same direction.

**Bianca:** Last one: in a data-response question with a headline and a quote, how do we avoid getting lost in the story and losing the economics?

**Tutor:** Use the five-move routine as your reading lens, not just your writing plan. Whatever the article says — a family cancelling a holiday, a business owner complaining about instalments — your first pass extracts exactly five facts. Problem: is the scenario inflation above the band, or an economy stalling? The headline usually answers this. Driver: which institution is acting — the Reserve Bank or the minister? Underline the name; if the passage puts the wrong hand on an instrument, that error is itself worth marks. Instrument: repo rate, spending, or taxation — nothing else qualifies. Direction: is the move adding spending power or withdrawing it — expansionary or contractionary? And transmission: which links of the chain does the story illustrate? A quote about a rising bond instalment is link three wearing human clothes; a shop owner saying customers have stopped following price increases is link five. Now write your answer by walking the five moves in order, quoting the story as evidence at each step. The real skill here is translation: the article speaks in kitchens and instalments, you answer in drivers, instruments and directions, and the marks sit exactly at the join. Do that twice in practice and data questions become the most predictable questions you will ever face — because every story is the same chain in different clothes.
