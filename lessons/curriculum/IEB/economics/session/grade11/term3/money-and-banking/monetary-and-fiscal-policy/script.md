# Part 1 — Expert

Every economy has two control panels, and this session teaches you to operate both without ever confusing them: monetary policy, in the hands of the South African Reserve Bank, and fiscal policy, in the hands of the government through the Minister of Finance. We will run one scenario through the whole lesson — the one the news serves up every few years: inflation has broken above the target band. Which lever moves, whose hand moves it, how the effect travels from a boardroom decision to the till at your local shop, and what the finance minister could do instead.

## Subtopic: Two Policies, Two Drivers

Everything in this topic rests on one separation, so build it carefully before anything else — because attributing an instrument to the wrong driver sinks an entire answer no matter how good the rest of it is.

Monetary policy belongs to the South African Reserve Bank — the SARB, our central bank. Its territory is the money side of the economy: what borrowing costs, and how much money and credit circulate through the banking system. Its flagship instrument is the repo rate. Two facts about the SARB carry marks on their own. It is independent — no minister, no party, no president instructs it. And its mandate, written into the Constitution, is to protect the value of the currency, which it pursues in practice by holding inflation inside a target band of three to six percent.

Fiscal policy belongs to the government itself, and its public face is the Minister of Finance delivering the annual Budget. Its territory is the state's own money: what government spends — salaries, grants, infrastructure — and what government collects — taxes. Spending and taxation are its two great instruments.

Here is the sorting question that never fails: whose hand is on the lever? Repo rate — the Reserve Bank's hand — monetary. Income tax, VAT, grant increases, a new hospital budget — the minister's hand, through the Budget — fiscal. Ask the question about every instrument you are shown, every time, before you write a word.

Add the two direction labels and the toolkit is complete. Policy that adds spending power to the economy — cheaper borrowing, bigger spending, lower taxes — is expansionary. Policy that deliberately withdraws spending power to cool things down is contractionary, also called restrictive. Classification questions live on these two words.

The questions for this section are with you now: each policy matched to its driver and instruments, the SARB's mandate and band stated exactly, and the two direction labels applied.

## Subtopic: The SARB's Move — the Repo Rate

Set the scenario running: consumer inflation has pushed past six percent, above the band. The Reserve Bank must act. What does it reach for?

It raises the repo rate. Definition first, precisely: the repo rate is the interest rate the SARB charges the commercial banks — Standard Bank, Absa, FNB, Nedbank, Capitec — when they borrow from it. It is the wholesale price of money in South Africa, the rate underneath every other rate.

Now trace the transmission mechanism — the chain of cause and effect that carries a single decision at the Reserve Bank into every household budget in the country. Five links, each one causing the next.

Link one: with the repo rate up, the commercial banks' own funding from the SARB costs them more.

Link two: the banks recover that cost from their customers by lifting the prime lending rate — the benchmark rate on their loans. When repo moves, prime follows almost at once.

Link three: every credit agreement priced off prime becomes dearer. The monthly bond instalment climbs. Vehicle finance climbs. Credit cards, store accounts and overdrafts climb. New borrowing suddenly looks expensive, and existing borrowers find their month shorter than their money.

Link four: households and firms respond by holding back. The kitchen renovation is shelved; the delivery business decides not to finance a third van, because the repayments would swallow what the van was meant to earn.

Link five: with borrowing and buying slowing everywhere at once, aggregate demand — total spending in the economy — cools. Less money competes for the same goods, sellers lose the room to keep pushing prices up, and inflation eases back toward the band.

That sequence is contractionary monetary policy in action. And say the uncomfortable part out loud, because complete answers always do: the same rate rise that tames prices also slows production and can cost jobs while it works. The Reserve Bank is not waving a wand; it is administering a medicine with side effects, and it knows it.

Work this section's questions before the fiscal side opens: the repo rate defined word-perfectly, the five links recited in order, and the side effects acknowledged.

## Subtopic: The Minister's Toolbox — Two Fiscal Instruments

Turn now to the government's own control panel. The scenario calls for two fiscal instruments, and there are exactly two to give.

Instrument one: government spending. The state pays hundreds of thousands of salaries, funds the grant system, and builds roads, schools and clinics — a river of spending flowing into the economy every month. Against inflation, the minister can narrow the river: slow the growth of budgets, delay new projects, freeze non-essential posts. Less state money entering the economy means less total demand pressing on prices. Spending restraint to cool the economy is contractionary fiscal policy.

Instrument two: taxation. The Budget sets personal income tax, company tax, VAT and the excise duties. Against inflation, the minister can raise them: heavier income tax shrinks take-home pay, and a higher VAT rate makes every purchase dearer, so households simply cannot spend as before. Less money in circulation, less pressure on prices. Tax increases are likewise contractionary.

Both instruments carry a reverse gear. When the economy is in recession, the minister can widen the river — accelerate public works, lift grants — and lighten taxes, leaving more in every pocket. Both moves inject demand: expansionary fiscal policy.

Now classify the full scenario, the way structured questions require. The SARB raising the repo rate: contractionary monetary policy. The treasury restraining spending: contractionary fiscal policy. The treasury raising taxes: contractionary fiscal policy. Three levers, two hands, one direction — all leaning against inflation together.

And train your eye for the planted error: a statement like "the Minister of Finance raised the repo rate to curb inflation" is defective before any discussion begins. The repo rate is not the minister's lever — it belongs to the independent Reserve Bank. Naming the wrong hand on the instrument is the entire point of such a question, and catching it is the entire answer.

This section's questions are in front of you now — the two instruments named, each classified in both directions, and the wrong-hand error spotted instantly.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Two Policies — and Answering Like an Economist

Set the two policies side by side, because the richest questions in this topic are comparisons.

Compare them on speed. Monetary policy moves quickly: the Monetary Policy Committee sits every second month, and the repo rate changes the day the decision is announced. Fiscal policy moves on a political calendar: the Budget arrives once a year, and significant shifts in spending or tax need approval and legislation.

Compare them on precision. The repo rate is economy-wide and undiscriminating — it lands on every borrower in the country, the prudent along with the reckless. Fiscal instruments can be pointed: an excise duty on luxury imports, a spending programme routed to one province or one industry. Compress the contrast into a sentence you can deploy anywhere: monetary policy is fast but blunt; fiscal policy is slow but can be aimed.

Compare them on who carries the weight. A repo increase reaches indebted households and leveraged firms first — the bondholder feels it within a month, the debt-free tenant barely notices. Tax rises land on whoever the tax defines; spending cuts land on whoever the programmes served. No policy is painless, and naming who carries the pain is what separates an economist's answer from a slogan.

Finally, understand that the two hands can cooperate or collide. Inflation surrenders fastest when both pull the same way — the Reserve Bank tightening while the treasury restrains its own spending. But if the Bank tightens while government spends freely, the treasury pumps in the demand the Bank is trying to drain, and the Bank is forced to raise rates further than it otherwise would — with borrowers paying for the contradiction.

Close the expert half with the five-move routine for any policy question you will ever face. Move one: identify the problem — inflation above the band, or an economy in recession. Move two: identify the driver — Reserve Bank or Minister of Finance. Move three: identify the instrument — repo rate, spending, or taxation. Move four: give the direction and the label — expansionary or contractionary. Move five: walk the transmission chain link by link until you reach spending and prices. Run those five moves and no scenario, however dressed up, is new to you.

The final questions of this part are with you now: speed against precision, the weight-bearers of each instrument, and the five-move routine executed on an unseen scenario.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Let us land all of this in one household in Soshanguve — a bond on the house, a car on finance, groceries climbing — and watch both policies walk through the front door.

## Subtopic: Two Drivers, Two Different Steering Wheels

Two very different institutions are steering this economy, and nearly every mark in the topic is earned simply by never confusing them.

Driver one is the South African Reserve Bank. Picture it as the setter of one crucial price: the price of borrowing money in South Africa. It employs no teachers, builds no clinics and pays no grants. Its single obsession is what your rand is worth — in practice, keeping inflation inside the three-to-six percent band — and its steering wheel is a single number, the repo rate. One more thing makes it unusual: it is independent. No politician can instruct it to move the rate before an election. That whole package is monetary policy.

Driver two is the government, and you meet it every February when the Minister of Finance opens the Budget. This is the country run like a household account: money out — teachers' and nurses' pay, social grants, roads, housing, clinics; money in — income tax, VAT, company tax, the levies on fuel. That package is fiscal policy.

One question sorts every instrument ever put in front of you: whose hand is on it? The repo rate — the Reserve Bank's hand — monetary. A VAT change, a grant increase, a school building programme — the minister's hand — fiscal. Make the question a reflex, because plenty of test sentences are built precisely to punish those who skip it.

Then pocket the two labels. Picture the economy as a fire you are managing. Anything that adds wood — more state spending, lighter taxes, cheaper loans — is expansionary. Anything that damps the flames — spending restraint, heavier taxes, dearer loans — is contractionary. For any policy move, ask one thing: wood on, or damped down?

Your questions for this section are with you now — find the hand on each lever, and say whether it feeds the fire or damps it.

## Subtopic: How One Number in Pretoria Reaches Your Street

The scenario: prices are running, inflation is above six percent, and the Reserve Bank raises the repo rate. Follow the decision from the announcement to the Soshanguve kitchen table, step by step.

Step one: the repo rate is what the Reserve Bank charges Standard Bank, Absa, FNB, Nedbank and Capitec when they borrow from it. The moment it rises, the banks' own money got more expensive.

Step two: banks are not charities — they pass the increase on by raising prime, the benchmark rate they charge their customers. Repo up on Thursday, prime up by Friday.

Step three: every debt in the house now costs more. The bond instalment jumps. The car repayment jumps. The store account and the credit card charge more each month. The neighbour who was about to sign for a loan looks at the new numbers and steps back.

Step four: the household and the businesses around it pull in their spending. The new fridge waits for next year. The woman running the catering business decides against financing a second bakkie — at the new rate, the bakkie would work for the bank, not for her.

Step five: multiply that pulling-in across every street in the country, and the total money chasing goods shrinks. Shopkeepers who had been lifting prices every second month discover their customers will no longer follow, and the price rises slow. Inflation drifts back down toward the band.

That is contractionary monetary policy told as a story — and tell the ending honestly. The medicine works by making the whole economy spend less, so output slows and some jobs go while it takes effect. The family with the bond hurts months before the family without one feels anything. The Reserve Bank knows all this, which is why every rate decision is a weighing of pains, not a free win.

Take the questions for this section now, and before you do, recite the chain once in your own words — repo, prime, repayments, spending, prices — because each step causes the next, and a caused story is harder to forget than a memorised list.

## Subtopic: The Budget's Two Taps — Fast Tool, Slow Tool

The Minister of Finance cannot move the repo rate — that wheel belongs to the Reserve Bank. What the minister holds instead are the two taps on the national budget.

Tap one: spending. The state is the largest single spender in the country, so easing that tap closed — slower hiring, postponed projects, tighter departmental budgets — withdraws demand and lets prices settle. Opening it in a recession — public employment programmes, higher grants, building projects — pours demand back in and warms the economy up.

Tap two: taxation. Turn it up — more income tax, higher VAT — and month-end pockets are simply shallower, so spending cools and price pressure fades. Turn it down and pockets deepen, spending returns, and a limping economy gets its push.

Assemble the scenario and classify every move. Reserve Bank raises the repo rate: contractionary monetary. Treasury eases the spending tap closed: contractionary fiscal. Treasury turns the tax tap up: contractionary fiscal. Two hands, three levers, one direction — everything damping the fire together.

Keep your eye trained for the broken sentence: "the Minister of Finance raises the repo rate to fight inflation." Stop right there — the minister holds the taps, not the wheel. The repo rate belongs to the Reserve Bank, and pointing out the wrong hand is the complete answer to that question.

Then hold the master comparison. Monetary policy is fast but blunt: the committee meets every second month and the rate moves same-day — but it lands on every borrower alike, the careful family and the careless company. Fiscal policy is slow but aimed: one Budget a year, needing political sign-off — but it can place a duty on luxury goods specifically, or steer spending to one province, one programme, one town's clinic.

And remember that the wheel and the taps can work together or cancel each other. Inflation falls fastest when the Bank tightens while the treasury restrains itself. If the treasury keeps pouring while the Bank is damping, the Bank has to damp harder — and it is ordinary borrowers who pay the difference in higher instalments.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: the two taps, the two hands, and fast-but-blunt against slow-but-aimed. Hold these and every policy scenario in Grade 11 and Grade 12 opens the same way.
