# Part 1 — Expert

This session shifts the question from how much an economy makes to what that making does for the people inside it: economic development. We will pull development apart from growth, learn the instruments that measure it, test the six classic characteristics of developing countries against South Africa one by one, examine the strategies countries choose when they set out to develop — with South Africa's own sequence of national plans as the running case — and end with a source of development thinking that deserves far more respect than it historically received: Indigenous Knowledge Systems.

## Subtopic: Development versus Growth — and How Development Is Measured

Begin with a distinction that carries an entire essay. Economic growth answers a counting question: did real GDP rise this year? Economic development answers a living question: did ordinary lives get better — longer, healthier, better schooled, less poor, less unequal? Most of the time the two move together, but the interesting cases are the ones where they part company. Picture a state sitting on natural gas: production and export earnings can climb for a decade while half its children never finish primary school and its clinics stand empty — growth with development left behind. Now picture a low-income country that spends carefully on vaccines, teachers and clean water: life expectancy and literacy can climb well past what its modest income would predict — development pulled ahead of growth. Whenever a question hands you a country, your first instinct should be to test the output numbers and the life numbers separately.

Because rands and dollars alone cannot capture better lives, the world's headline measure is the Human Development Index. The HDI blends three dimensions into a single score between zero and one: income, taken as gross national income per person adjusted for local buying power; health, taken as life expectancy at birth; and education, taken as the years of schooling children can expect and adults actually received. And here is the South African result you must carry into every comparison: our income places us in the world's upper-middle band, yet our HDI position sits well below our income position, because South Africans die younger and learn less than a country producing this much should allow. One country, two rankings, and the gap between them is the development deficit itself.

Beneath the index sit the poverty measures. Absolute poverty is a fixed floor: the World Bank's extreme poverty line of a few US dollars a day, or South Africa's food poverty line — the rand amount that buys minimum daily nutrition, a floor roughly a quarter of our population still lives below. Relative poverty is a moving standard: being far beneath what your own society considers a decent life. And multidimensional poverty counts deprivation directly — households missing adequate schooling, water, sanitation, housing and work all at once — because a family can sit just above the money line while lacking almost everything the money line is supposed to buy.

Keep the three instruments in their lanes: growth counts production, the HDI scores the quality of lives, and poverty lines count the people underneath the floor.

The questions for this section are with you now: growth and development separated with an example of each pulling apart, the HDI's three dimensions, and the meaning of South Africa's two rankings.

## Subtopic: The Six Signs — Common Characteristics of Developing Countries

No two developing countries are alike, yet they share a family resemblance the syllabus captures in six characteristics. Treat them as a diagnostic instrument, not a memory list — and for every one, ask how well South Africa actually matches, because we match some cleanly, some only partly, and the partial matches are where the best answers are written.

First: a low standard of living. Low average income, and beneath the average, hunger, overcrowded housing and stretched budgets. South Africa complicates this immediately: our average income is respectable by world standards, but the distribution around it is among the most unequal ever measured, so millions live in absolute poverty inside an economy whose average suggests comfort. Our problem is less the size of the average than the violence of the spread.

Second: low productivity. Each hour of work yields little, because workers lack equipment, training and reliable health. A seamstress hand-stitching school uniforms produces in a week what a small factory manages in a morning — not through any fault of effort, but through the absence of machines and training. And low productivity locks its own door: little output means low pay, low pay means no savings, no savings means no machines, and no machines keep productivity low. That loop is the first of the vicious circles of poverty.

Third: rapid population growth and a heavy dependency burden. Young, fast-growing populations mean every earner supports a queue of dependants — school-going children, job-seeking cousins, ageing parents. Income that development would want saved and invested is rightly spent on tonight's supper instead. South Africa grows at about one and a half percent a year — slower than much of the continent, but in our recent low-growth years still faster than output, which means production per person has been slipping backwards.

Fourth: unemployment and underemployment on a large scale. Too few formal jobs, and beneath them a layer of people surviving on work far smaller than their abilities. On this characteristic South Africa is not merely typical — we are an extreme case, with an official unemployment rate above thirty percent and an expanded rate above forty once discouraged work-seekers are counted. Few countries anywhere record numbers like these.

Fifth: dependence on the primary sector and on raw exports. Many developing economies earn their foreign income from unprocessed minerals and crops whose world prices they do not set and cannot predict. South Africa is a partial fit with a precise shape: our production is dominated by services, yet our export earnings still lean heavily on platinum, gold, coal and iron ore — so the commodity-price rollercoaster still runs straight through our trade accounts.

Sixth: deficient infrastructure and incomplete markets. Roads not built, electricity that fails, freight rail that underdelivers, and credit markets that never reach the poor. South Africa holds world-class infrastructure and broken infrastructure in the same borders, which is its own kind of development diagnosis.

The method to practise: take any country a question offers, run the six signs across it, and declare each one a clean fit, a miss, or a partial fit you can explain. That declared judgement is what separates analysis from recall.

Work this section's questions now: the six characteristics, the mechanism inside each, and South Africa tested honestly against at least three of them.

## Subtopic: Development Strategies — and South Africa's Succession of Plans

Suppose a country decides to develop deliberately. Its choices cluster around three great questions, and South Africa's own plans since 1994 show the questions being answered in real time.

Question one: who leads — state or market? A state-led strategy plans, builds and owns: public investment, state enterprises, directed credit. A market-led strategy liberalises: free prices, open trade, private investment doing the pulling. History's development successes almost all blended the two, but the blend is argued over in every legislature on Earth, and fiercely in ours.

Question two: which way does the economy face — inward or outward? Inward-looking development walls off home industries behind tariffs so domestic manufacturing can grow up protected — import substitution. Outward-looking development pushes industries into export markets where world-class competition forces them to world standards. The fastest development stories of the last century — the East Asian exporters — chose the outward door, and their success reshaped the argument everywhere.

Question three: what is the engine — physical capital or human capital? Big-push thinking pours resources into dams, ports, industrial corridors and power stations. Human-capital thinking insists the binding constraint is people: educate, feed and heal the population and the rest becomes possible. The record is blunt on one point — no nation has ever developed while leaving its people sick and unschooled, so the human investment cannot be postponed, whatever else is built.

Now South Africa's sequence, told as three plans answering three different emergencies. The Reconstruction and Development Programme arrived with democracy in 1994 and put basic needs first: houses, running water, electrification and clinics delivered directly to communities apartheid had deliberately starved of them. In 1996 came GEAR — Growth, Employment and Redistribution — a stabilisation turn that disciplined public debt and inflation, betting that sound finances would draw the investment that would create the jobs; the finances did steady, but the jobs never arrived in the numbers promised. And from 2012 the umbrella has been the National Development Plan, the NDP 2030, targeting the elimination of income poverty and unemployment down to single digits by 2030, built on infrastructure, education and a capable, professional state. Around these plans run the instruments you should name in any answer: the Expanded Public Works Programme offering temporary work, special economic zones recruiting industrial investment, and the social grant system holding up the income floor for millions.

Then give the evaluation that earns respect for its honesty: services, houses and grants were genuinely delivered at scale; the growth and employment targets have been missed again and again, as electricity shortages, skills gaps and weak implementation kept dragging on every plan. Write this sentence somewhere permanent: a plan is only a strategy — development is a delivery record.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: the three strategic choices, the three plans in order with the emergency each answered, and one honest evaluative judgement.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Knowledge Systems — Development from Within

The last source of development thinking is also the oldest: Indigenous Knowledge Systems, IKS — the knowledge, practices and institutions communities have built across generations and transmitted through culture rather than through laboratories and journals. Two things are asked of you: what IKS contributes to development, and why and how it must be protected.

Contributions first, and insist on concreteness. Indigenous finance: the stokvel, a rotating savings circle where members pay in monthly and each member in turn receives the whole pot, moves tens of billions of rands through South Africa every year among people the formal banks historically would not serve; burial societies extend the same logic into insurance for millions. That is a functioning financial system built on trust instead of buildings. Indigenous agriculture: sorghum and millet that shrug off drought, intercropping that protects the soil, and communal work parties — the tradition of neighbours ploughing and harvesting together — that solve labour shortages without a cent of credit; as the climate dries, this knowledge is becoming more valuable, not less. Indigenous medicine: the majority of South Africans consult traditional healers, whose practice is now recognised in law, and indigenous plants hold genuine pharmaceutical value. Indigenous governance: customary institutions that manage communal land and settle resource disputes in places where formal institutions barely reach.

Why does this belong in a strategy lesson? Because development that ignores the institutions people already trust has a habit of failing. A savings product designed against the stokvel loses to the stokvel; a health programme that sneers at healers loses the patients who trust them; a drought plan that overlooks sorghum irrigates the wrong crop. Building on existing institutions is cheaper, faster and more likely to be adopted than bulldozing them.

Protection, through the case you must be able to narrate. For generations the San used the hoodia plant to hold off hunger on long hunting journeys. That knowledge was taken and patented for commercial appetite-suppressant products without the San's consent and without payment — the practice called biopiracy, profiting from indigenous knowledge with neither permission nor compensation. Only after years of dispute did a benefit-sharing agreement finally recognise the San as the holders of that knowledge. Rooibos supplies the better ending: after a long campaign, the industry now pays a negotiated share to the Khoi and San communities recognised as the tea's traditional knowledge holders. The policy response you should name: South Africa's indigenous knowledge legislation, compulsory benefit-sharing, and the recording of traditional knowledge so no patent office can pretend it was newly invented.

For essays, hold the balanced position: IKS is neither folklore to be displayed nor a substitute for modern science — it is a development asset, to be built upon where it works and defended in law while it earns.

The final questions of this part are with you now: IKS defined, two contributions argued with real examples, and the hoodia story told as the argument for protection.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now let us bring all of it down to the size of one family, one teenager, one neighbourhood — because that is where development either shows up or does not.

## Subtopic: Taller or Better? — What Development Actually Asks

Think about a boy in Grade 8 who shoots up ten centimetres in a year. His height is easy to measure — stand him against the wall, make the pencil mark. But the mark tells you nothing about the questions that matter: is he sleeping, is he eating properly, is he passing, is he okay? A person can get taller without getting better. So can a country.

Growth is the pencil mark: the economy produced more this year than last, full stop. Development is everything the pencil mark cannot see: are people living longer, can the children read, does the tap run, is there a working clinic within reach, is there a real chance of a job at the end of school?

Watch the difference arrive in one town. A mine outside town reopens: trucks, wages, tills ringing — the pencil mark jumps. But if the town's school still crams sixty learners to a class, if the clinic still opens twice a week, if the water still comes by tanker — the town grew and did not develop. Now run it the other way. A mobile clinic starts visiting weekly, the library gets computers, a bursary programme lands at the high school. Even before anyone's payslip changes, lives got better — that is development moving first, growth still to follow.

Because no single money number can see all of this, the world scores countries on three things at once and calls it the Human Development Index: money, meaning income per person; health, meaning how long people live; and learning, meaning how long children stay in school. One score, between zero and one.

And South Africa's scorecard fits in one uncomfortable line: on money we sit in the world's upper-middle; on the full score we sit lower, because we do not live as long or learn as much as our income says we should. A country richer than it is healthy or educated. Take that line into your final exams — it is the whole growth-versus-development argument compressed to a sentence.

Your questions for this section are with you now — each one describes something changing in a town, and asks: did the pencil mark move, did life get better, or both?

## Subtopic: The Six Signs, Walked Down One Street

Six characteristics of developing countries can feel like furniture to memorise. So stop memorising and start walking — one street, six scenes.

Scene one: month-end arrives and the food must stretch — samp twice this week, school shoes resoled instead of replaced. That is a low standard of living. And always the South African sting in the tail: fifteen minutes away there is a suburb where none of this is true, because our deepest problem is the gap, not just the average.

Scene two: a seamstress at her kitchen table, hand-stitching uniforms beautifully and slowly. She is skilled and she works hard — what she lacks is a machine. Each hour produces little, so each hour pays little, so she can never save enough to buy the machine that would change everything. That is low productivity, running in its own locked circle.

Scene three: one payslip and one pension walk into a house of eight — school-going children, a cousin looking for work, a grandmother whose grant anchors the whole household. Economists call it the dependency burden: every rand is spoken for tonight, so no rand is left to build tomorrow.

Scene four: outside the internet café, matriculants printing the same CV for the fortieth time. Work that is missing, or too small for the people holding certificates. South Africa's version of this scene is the harshest on Earth — three in ten unemployed on the strict count, four in ten when you count those who have stopped looking.

Scene five: the coal trucks thunder past the street all day, headed for the port — and in the shop, the tools and appliances on the shelf are all imported. Selling the raw thing cheap, buying back the made thing dear, at prices set on other continents. When the commodity price dips, this street feels it first.

Scene six: the substation trips again, the spaza's fridge dies again, and the bank declines the loan again. Broken basics — infrastructure and credit that keep letting go of your hand just as you pull.

Six scenes, one street, and you can rebuild the whole textbook list from memory of the walk.

Take this section's questions now — each shows you a single scene and asks which of the six signs is standing in front of you.

## Subtopic: Plans on Paper, Roots in the Ground

How does that street actually develop? Help arrives from two directions — plans from above, and roots from below — and South Africa is the textbook for both.

The plans, as one story in three chapters. After 1994, the RDP went straight at the basics: houses built, taps connected, wires strung, clinics opened — development handed directly to streets that had been deliberately skipped. Then GEAR, from 1996, turned to the country's books: debt and inflation brought under control, on the bet that stable money would attract the investors who would hire the street. The books steadied; the hiring never matched the promise. Since 2012 the master plan has been the NDP 2030 — end income poverty, push unemployment to single digits, rebuild infrastructure and schooling. Working alongside it, the everyday machinery: public employment programmes offering temporary work, special economic zones courting factories, and grants holding the floor under millions. The report card, and say it plainly because honesty scores: the basics and the floor were largely delivered; growth and jobs were missed, year after year, with power cuts, skills shortages and weak delivery doing the tripping.

But the street never sat waiting. Long before any plan, the stokvel was already running: a circle of neighbours, each paying in every month, each taking a turn to carry home the full pot — fees paid, stock bought, funerals covered with dignity. Tens of billions of rands move through these circles every year, among people the banks once refused to see. The garden at number twelve grows sorghum, because the grandmothers knew which crops survive a dry year long before any forecast said so. The healer's door opens for patients the clinic queue cannot reach. This is Indigenous Knowledge Systems — IKS — and it is not nostalgia; it is infrastructure built out of trust, tested across generations.

The smart move is to join the two directions: banks that build products riding on stokvel logic instead of against it; health systems that work alongside healers; drought planning that plants what the grandmothers planted. And the law must stand guard over the roots. When the San's knowledge of the hoodia plant was patented for profit without consent — the theft called biopiracy — it took years of struggle before a benefit-sharing agreement acknowledged the true owners; the rooibos communities fought a similar fight and now earn their recognised share. Knowledge is property, and some of it belongs to the street.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: the three plans in order with one honest verdict, the stokvel explained as indigenous finance, and the hoodia case argued as the reason protection is not optional.
