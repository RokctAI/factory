**Bianca:** The class has questions before we wrap. First: the HDI squashes three completely different things — money, health, schooling — into one number. Doesn't that hide more than it shows?

**Tutor:** It hides plenty, and knowing exactly what it hides is worth marks. Any average conceals its ingredients: two countries can share an HDI score while one is rich and sickly and the other poor and healthy — the blend flattens the difference. The index also says nothing about distribution: a country can post a decent score while its top tenth lives one kind of life and its bottom half another, which is precisely South Africa's situation. So why use it at all? Because the alternative — judging countries by income alone — hides even more. Income-only rankings once let resource-rich states with empty schools look like success stories. The HDI's contribution is the forced marriage: no country can score well without delivering on health and education, whatever its export earnings. The bulletproof way to handle it in an answer is to use the index and then interrogate it: quote the score, then say what it cannot show — distribution, inequality, deprivation below the average — and reach for the poverty measures to fill the gap. A measure plus its limitations is analysis; a measure alone is trivia.

**Bianca:** Second one: if South Africa breaks half the six characteristics — upper-middle income, service-dominated economy — why do we still call ourselves a developing country at all?

**Tutor:** Because the classification tracks the lives, not the averages, and the six signs are designed to be run one at a time rather than totalled. Take the inventory honestly. On the averages we look almost developed: upper-middle income, sophisticated banks, services dominating production. But run the individual signs and the developing-country diagnosis returns. Unemployment: not merely present but world-extreme, above thirty percent official. Living standards: a quarter of the population below the food poverty line, inside the most unequal income distribution ever reliably measured. Infrastructure: world-class in one suburb, failing twenty minutes away. Exports: still leaning on unprocessed minerals despite the modern service economy. What South Africa really is, is a dual economy — a developed economy and a developing economy sharing one border and one currency — and that duality is the answer the question wants. Never argue we are simply one or the other; argue sign by sign, show which side of the dual economy each sign describes, and conclude that the developing-country label holds because development is judged at the level of lives, and millions of South African lives are still lived below the floor.

**Bianca:** Third: everyone can recite RDP, GEAR, NDP by now — but if the plans keep missing their growth and jobs targets, what's the point of studying them?

**Tutor:** Because the misses teach more economics than the plans do. Each plan is a hypothesis about how development works, and each result is the test. The RDP hypothesised that development is delivery: build the houses, connect the taps, and lives improve directly. Result: largely confirmed for services — the delivery happened and mattered — but delivery alone created few jobs. GEAR hypothesised that development follows stability: discipline debt and inflation and investment will arrive carrying employment. Result: half confirmed — stability arrived, the private investment surge did not, so the jobs did not either. The NDP hypothesised that the missing ingredient is capability: skilled people, working infrastructure, a professional state. Result so far: the diagnosis is widely accepted while the delivery keeps being tripped by the very weaknesses it diagnosed — power shortages, skills gaps, implementation failure. Now you can answer the real question, which is never merely to list the plans: it is to explain why plans with sensible economics kept underdelivering. Answer: because a strategy on paper still has to pass through electricity, skills and state capacity to reach the street — and that is why the lesson insists a plan is only a strategy while development is a delivery record.

**Bianca:** Last one from the class: for the essay, is it enough to say IKS is culturally important, or does it need harder economics than that?

**Tutor:** It needs harder economics, and the good news is the hard economics is easy to supply. Make three moves. Move one, scale: stokvels and burial societies rotate and pool tens of billions of rands every year — savings mobilisation and insurance, two core financial-system functions, performed at national scale for people the formal system excluded. Any institution doing that volume of financial work is an economic institution, full stop. Move two, efficiency: development interventions succeed only if adopted, and adoption follows trust. A savings product that fights stokvel logic loses customers; a health campaign that dismisses healers loses patients; a drought strategy that ignores sorghum wastes water on the wrong crop. Building on trusted institutions lowers the cost of every rand of development spending — that is an efficiency argument, not a sentimental one. Move three, property: hoodia and rooibos established that traditional knowledge is an asset capable of earning income for its holders, and that without legal protection the asset gets taken — biopiracy is theft of capital, in the strict sense. Scale, efficiency, property rights: write your IKS paragraphs on those three rails and the essay reads as economics from the first line.
