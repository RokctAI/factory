**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: when a question asks for causes of globalisation, half of us write technology and half write policy. Do we need both, and how do we know how deep to go?

**Tutor:** You need both, always — and the depth is decided by the mark allocation's oldest rule: a cause is only complete when it reaches its consequence. Think of each cause as a circuit that must close. Containerisation: a uniform steel box is packed once and craned from ship to rail to road without opening, which collapsed freight costs, which made it profitable to manufacture oceans away from customers. Three links, circuit closed. Undersea cables: information crosses the planet in under a second, which lets a firm supervise factories on three continents in real time, which makes the global value chain workable. Then announce the switch of families in one sentence — technology made it possible, policy made it permitted — and close two more circuits on the other side: tariffs negotiated down over decades into the WTO in 1995, so goods cross borders cheaply; exchange controls dismantled, so capital crosses at a keystroke; and the closed giants — China from 1978, the Soviet bloc after 1989, our own economy after 1994 — rejoining the market with billions of workers and buyers. Two families, two or three closed circuits each. A list of nouns is a broken circuit: the marker sees the wire but no current. And here is the practical bonus — closing circuits is faster than listing, because you stop scratching for an eighth noun and spend the time on the word 'which'.

**Bianca:** Next: someone in class said their family's clothing shop closed because of cheap imports, so for them globalisation is simply theft. How do we write about the losses without either dismissing that or turning the essay into a rant?

**Tutor:** By giving that family's story its full economic weight — and then giving the rest of the evidence the same respect. The mistake is not feeling strongly; the mistake is presenting half a ledger. Open the losses honestly: when tariffs fell, imported clothing and footwear arrived below local cost, factories across KwaZulu-Natal and the Cape Flats went quiet, tens of thousands of jobs vanished, and the towns carrying those industries have never fully recovered. That story is true, painful and examinable — cite it with confidence. But the same ledger contains the other page: vehicle plants at Kariega and Rosslyn exporting to the world and sustaining over a hundred thousand jobs; prices in every trolley far below what a sealed economy could offer, which functions as a raise for every wage earner including the poorest; and globally, hundreds of millions lifted out of extreme poverty by export-led growth. Now let the evidence write your conclusion instead of your anger: globalisation grew the total and moved the shares — the gains landed widely and thinly, the losses landed narrowly and brutally, on streets like your classmate's. That asymmetry is the exact reason serious economists support cushioning policy: retraining, safety nets, and time-limited protection while industries rebuild. Written that way, the family's story becomes your strongest analytical asset — proof that you understand where the shares moved — rather than a substitute for analysis. Feeling plus full ledger equals evaluation marks; feeling alone equals sympathy and half the credit.

**Bianca:** Third: the chocolate bar makes the divide sound permanent — the North finishes, the South stays a farm. Is there actual evidence the South can climb out, or is that just hopeful talk?

**Tutor:** There is hard evidence in both directions, and the strongest answers hold both. Evidence of climbing: the largest escape from poverty in recorded history happened inside this topic — China entered the world market as a supplier of cheap assembly and now designs, brands and finishes at the top of value chains; South Korea travelled from raw exports to semiconductors within two generations; India exports software services, which is pure finishing with no farm attached. The centre of the world economy has measurably shifted southward, and Southern institutions — BRICS and its development bank, with South Africa a member — now exist to press the advantage. So the treadmill is not destiny. But now the discipline: the escape routes all ran through the same gate — moving up the value chain, from selling raw to selling made. The economies still standing on the treadmill, many of them African, are precisely those still exporting minerals and crops and importing machines; for them the three mechanisms — sagging terms of trade, dollar debt, weak voice — grind on exactly as described. That is why the African Continental Free Trade Area matters analytically and not just politically: a continental market for African manufactures is a gate wide enough for economies our size to walk through, which no single small home market could offer. So the examinable formulation: the divide is escapable but not self-escaping — every economy that crossed it industrialised deliberately, and the ones that wait for the map to move on its own stay exactly where they are.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the essay on South Africa's position in the global economy, what is the strongest structure — and what single mistake costs the most marks?

**Tutor:** Structure it as position, evidence, tension, resolution — four movements, one page each in your plan. Position: South Africa is a highly open economy — exports plus imports well over half of GDP — sitting on the southern side of the divide with Northern-grade finance inside it; that paradox is your thesis, and stating a thesis at all already separates you from most of the room. Evidence: two links with their purposes — the African free trade area as the market for our manufactures, preferential Northern access for fruit, wine and vehicles, BRICS for Southern alliance, WTO as the floor; then two lived exposures — petrol tracking world oil, the platinum belt tracking world vehicle demand. Tension: the two-handed debate, argued with real cases — integration built Kariega, and openness gutted the clothing towns; protection shelters poultry and steel jobs, and every tariff quietly taxes each family's trolley. Resolution: the conditional conclusion — deeper integration paired with skills, infrastructure and safety nets, plus protection only where it is temporary and tied to a plan for competitiveness. Now the costly mistake: writing the whole essay about the world in general and mentioning South Africa in one closing sentence. The question grants its highest marks to specificity — named agreements, named industries, named towns — because specificity is the only proof that you studied the economy you actually live in. Four movements, South African names in every one of them. That is the full-marks shape.
