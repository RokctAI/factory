# Part 1 — Expert

This session takes on globalisation: the definition with teeth, the forces that produced it, the consequences it hands out — generously and cruelly at once — and the old fracture it has failed to close: the divide between the global North and the global South. South Africa lives inside every paragraph of this topic as an open economy on the southern side of the fracture, so the evidence stays close to home, and so do the marks.

## Subtopic: What Globalisation Is — and the Forces That Caused It

Globalisation is the process through which the world's economies grow ever more interconnected and interdependent — goods, services, capital, workers, information and ideas crossing borders in rising volumes, until national economies operate as components of a single world system. Apply the practical test: a harvest failure, an interest rate decision or a factory closure on one continent now moves prices, jobs and incomes on another within weeks, not years. When that test passes, you are looking at globalisation.

Its causes sort into two families, and a complete answer holds both: technology made integration possible, and policy made it permitted.

Technology first. Transport was transformed by standardisation: the shipping container — one uniform steel box craned from vessel to rail to road without ever being opened — cut the cost of ocean freight so far that distance nearly vanished from the price of goods. Communication was transformed by fibre and satellite: undersea cables now carry a design file, a purchase order or a payment across the planet in under a second. Computing closed the loop, letting one firm supervise factories, stock levels and cash across a dozen countries in real time. Put the three together and a company can carve production into stages and station each stage wherever it is performed best and cheapest — the global value chain, the defining production structure of our era.

Policy second. From the late 1940s governments negotiated import tariffs downward round after round, a process that culminated in the World Trade Organisation in 1995 as referee for most of world trade. Exchange controls were dismantled, freeing finance to cross borders at a keystroke. And the great closed economies walked back into the market: China opening from 1978, the former Soviet bloc after 1989, and South Africa itself re-entering world trade and investment after 1994 — billions of new workers and customers joining one system. Multinational corporations stitched the whole fabric together, producing and selling in dozens of jurisdictions simultaneously.

Keep the sentence that organises every causes question: containers, cables and computers made it possible; trade rounds, financial liberalisation and reopening economies made it allowed.

The questions for this section are with you now: globalisation defined with its practical test, and each cause filed under technology or policy.

## Subtopic: The Consequences — a Two-Sided Scorecard

The consequences of globalisation are authentically double-edged, and strong answers keep score on both sides rather than cheering for one.

The gains side. Specialisation raises efficiency: each country concentrates where it produces best, and consumers everywhere collect the dividend in lower prices and wider choice — the clothing, electronics and groceries on a South African shelf would cost dramatically more in a sealed economy. Capital flows toward opportunity: foreign direct investment delivers factories, technology and skills to economies short of savings — South Africa's vehicle plants at Kariega, East London and Rosslyn build for world markets and sustain over one hundred thousand jobs across the assembly lines and component firms. Knowledge diffuses faster: mobile money, solar technology and modern medicines reached the South at unprecedented speed. And at the largest scale, export-led growth carried hundreds of millions of people — most dramatically in Asia — out of extreme poverty within two generations.

The losses side. Open competition destroys as it creates: when clothing tariffs came down, imports priced below local production hollowed out South African textile and footwear centres — factories in KwaZulu-Natal and the Western Cape closed, and tens of thousands of jobs vanished from towns that had been built around them. Instability travels the same wires as investment: the 2008 financial crisis began in American housing finance, yet within a year South African mines and factories were shedding workers because our customers' economies had stopped ordering — contagion, the fever of an interconnected system. Bargaining power tilts toward whatever can move: multinationals can book profits in low-tax jurisdictions and play governments off against each other, thinning the tax base, while workers — rooted in place — lose leverage; economists call the dynamic a race to the bottom. And the winnings have been spread unevenly, between nations and inside them: most open economies, ours included, have watched internal inequality widen through the globalisation decades.

The verdict that earns the evaluation marks: globalisation enlarges the world's total income while redistributing opportunity toward the efficient and the mobile — so the intelligent policy question is never simply for or against, but how to harvest the gains while re-skilling and shielding the people on whom the losses fall.

Work this section's questions now: two gains and two losses argued with South African evidence, contagion traced through 2008, and the verdict compressed into one sentence.

## Subtopic: The North/South Divide

Beneath the scorecard lies the deep structure. The North/South divide names the persistent gap between the developed economies — the global North: North America, Western Europe, Japan and other high-income economies — and the developing economies of the global South: most of Africa, Latin America and large parts of Asia. Treat the labels as economic shorthand rather than cartography — New Zealand sits deep in the southern ocean and squarely in the economic North.

The gap appears in every serious statistic: the North holds a minority of the world's population but the majority of its income, capital, patents and research; the South holds most of humanity, most of its poverty and its youngest populations. But statistics only describe the divide — mechanisms explain it, and explanations earn the marks.

Mechanism one: the terms of trade. Much of the South still exports primary products — minerals, crops, fuels — and imports manufactured goods and services. Primary prices lurch unpredictably, and across long periods a ton of raw exports has tended to purchase fewer and fewer manufactured imports. An economy that sells raw and buys made walks a treadmill: it must export more each year merely to afford the same equipment.

Mechanism two: debt. Southern governments borrow largely in foreign currency. When world interest rates climb or the local currency weakens, the repayment burden swells in local terms, and budgets for clinics, schools and infrastructure are squeezed to keep creditors paid — a cycle African economies have endured repeatedly, and several are enduring now.

Mechanism three: voice. The rulebooks of world trade and finance are drafted inside institutions — the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, the WTO — where influence and voting weight have historically leaned North. One quiet example with loud consequences: import tariffs in rich markets have long escalated with processing — low on raw cocoa, higher on chocolate — nudging the South to keep shipping its resources unfinished.

Yet the divide is not frozen. The rise of China, India and Brazil has dragged the world's economic centre of gravity southward; the BRICS grouping — South Africa among its members — campaigns for a stronger Southern voice; and the African Continental Free Trade Area is building the South's own internal market. The honest summary: the divide is real and deep, narrowing sharply in parts of Asia while persisting, and sometimes widening, across much of Africa.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: the divide defined as economics rather than geography, all three mechanisms explained, and one force reshaping the divide named with evidence.

## Subtopic: South Africa Inside the Global Economy

Close where essay questions close: South Africa's own position, argued with numbers and names.

Start with openness. South Africa's exports plus imports come to well over half of GDP, placing us among the more open of the world's sizeable economies — and the exposure is lived daily. The petrol price moves with world oil and the rand; bread tracks world wheat; the platinum belt's payrolls rise and fall with global vehicle demand. When the world economy slows, South African mining towns feel it before the news reports it.

Know the architecture of our links. The African Continental Free Trade Area is the strategic prize: the continent buys our manufactured goods — vehicles, machinery, processed food — rather than only our ores, so it is the market in which South Africa trades as an industrial economy. Preferential arrangements keep our fruit, wine and vehicles flowing into American and European markets. BRICS ties us to the major Southern economies and their New Development Bank. And WTO membership sets the baseline rules beneath everything.

Now the two-sided experience, told straight. Openness built a vehicle industry that exports to the world, carries our citrus and wine into Northern supermarkets, and supplies the foreign capital that funds our chronic savings shortfall. The same openness let cheap imports gut clothing and footwear towns, whips the rand around on every shift in global sentiment, and leaves export earnings hostage to commodity prices set elsewhere. South Africa contains the whole divide within its own borders: a Northern-grade financial sector around the Johannesburg exchange, and a Southern-style dependence on raw exports in the trade accounts.

For essays, run the policy debate with both hands. Hand one: integrate deeper — chase export markets, recruit investment, use the African market to industrialise. Hand two: protect strategically — tariffs and local-content requirements to hold jobs while industries rebuild, as current policy attempts with clothing, poultry and steel. The mature conclusion echoes the scorecard: openness grows the pie and moves the slices, so integration must arrive together with the skills, infrastructure and safety nets that let ordinary South Africans survive the losses and collect the gains.

The final questions of this part are with you now: openness evidenced with a figure, one agreement matched to its purpose, and the two-handed debate argued through to a conclusion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic through two things you can touch: the takkies on your feet, and a bar of chocolate.

## Subtopic: The World in Your Pocket

Start with your phone, and read it the way a detective reads a passport. The design was drawn in America or Korea. The processor chip was etched in Taiwan. The screen glass was melted in Korea or Japan. The battery holds cobalt from the DRC and lithium that may have left Zimbabwe. Assembly happened in China, Vietnam or, increasingly, India. And last night's software update reached you through an undersea cable that comes ashore at Melkbosstrand on the West Coast. Six countries, minimum, folded into one object in your pocket — and your takkies could tell the same story: designed on one continent, stitched on a second, shipped through a third, sold to you on a fourth. That is globalisation, worn daily.

How did the world arrange itself like this? Two doors had to swing open.

Door one: it had to become possible. The shipping container did the heavy lifting — a standard steel box, packed once, craned from ship to train to truck without ever being opened, until moving a pair of takkies across an ocean cost less than the laces. Undersea cables and the internet did the light lifting — designs, orders and payments crossing the world in a blink. Once goods move cheaply and information moves instantly, a firm can slice production like bread: design here, components there, stitching somewhere else, every slice placed where it is done best and cheapest.

Door two: it had to become permitted. For decades governments taxed imports hard at the border and forbade money to leave. Then, agreement by agreement, the tariffs came down; a referee for world trade — the World Trade Organisation — took the whistle; money was unshackled; and the giant locked-away economies, China first among them, walked back into the world's market. South Africa's own door reopened after 1994, when the world's shops and investors returned.

Technology opened what was possible; policy opened what was allowed. Every causes question is asking for both doors — name them and walk through each.

Your questions for this section are with you now — file each cause behind its door: could-move, or allowed-to-move.

## Subtopic: One Shop Now — Who Won and Who Lost on Our Streets

When the planet becomes a single shop, two true South African stories unfold at the same time. Carry both.

Story one, the winning street: Kariega. A global carmaker built a plant there to serve the world, not just us — hatchbacks roll off that line into European showrooms, and the plant anchors component factories, logistics firms and tens of thousands of pay packets across the industry. Add the quiet win in every trolley: because the shop is global, clothes, phones and food cost far less than a sealed-off South Africa could make them for. Cheap imports work like a pay rise nobody announces — the same wage simply buys more.

Story two, the losing street: the footwear and clothing towns. When tariffs dropped, imported shoes and shirts arrived at prices no local factory could meet. Machines fell silent in Pietermaritzburg's footwear works and in clothing factories across KwaZulu-Natal and the Cape Flats; tens of thousands of jobs went, and the towns built on those wages have never fully healed. Say it honestly: the world got cheaper shirts, and particular South African streets paid the bill.

And one consequence needs no factory at all: the fever that rides the wires. In 2008, American banks collapsed over housing loans South Africa never touched — yet within a year our smelters and assembly lines were cutting shifts, because the countries that buy from us had stopped buying. Around a million South African jobs disappeared in that downturn. Connected means exposed: one shop, one flu.

So when someone demands "is globalisation good or bad?", refuse the playground version and answer like an economist: it grows the total and moves the shares. The world's pot gets bigger; the gains flow to the efficient, the skilled and the mobile; the losses land on whoever competes head-to-head with the world's cheapest producer. Policy's job is to spread the first and cushion the second — skills, safety nets, and sometimes temporary protection while an industry finds its feet.

Take this section's questions now — each hands you a winner or a loser from these streets, and asks for the mechanism that chose them.

## Subtopic: Top of the Map, Bottom of the Map

Shade the world map by income and the pattern is instant: the rich economies cluster across the top — North America, Europe, Japan — while most of the poor world sits below — Africa, Latin America, much of Asia. Economists call it the North/South divide, and it is shorthand about wealth, not latitude: New Zealand floats in the far south of the map and the comfortable north of the money.

Why won't the gap close? Follow one chocolate bar. The cocoa grows in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire — the South. Farmers sell the raw beans for a few rand's worth of cents. The roasting, the recipe, the brand, the wrapper and the shelf all happen in Europe — where the finished slab sells for thirty rand. The South grew the essential ingredient and kept a sliver; the North did the finishing and kept the rest. Selling raw and buying made is the engine of the divide: the money lives in the finishing, and the finishing lives up north. Worse, bean prices swing wildly, and over the decades a sack of beans has bought fewer and fewer machines in return.

Now load two more weights onto the southern side. Debt: Southern countries borrow in dollars, so when interest rates rise in the North or the home currency slips, repayments balloon until hospitals and schools are trimmed to pay the creditors. Voice: the rules of world trade and money were drafted in rooms where the North held the votes — and you can read it in the tariff schedules, which stay low on raw beans and climb on finished chocolate, quietly instructing the South to stay a farm.

But the map is not still. China and India have lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty and dragged the world's economic centre southward. BRICS — with South Africa inside — insists on a Southern seat at the table. And Africa's continental free trade area is the boldest reply of all: let Africa roast, stitch, assemble and sell to Africa, so the finishing — and the profit — stays on this side of the map.

South Africa stands with a foot on each side: Johannesburg finance that matches the North, an export bill that still leans on ores like the South. Which foot carries our future depends on whether we learn to finish what we dig and grow.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: the chocolate bar traced from cents to rands, the three southern weights named, and the forces redrawing the map argued with one example each.
