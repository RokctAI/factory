# Part 1 — Expert

This session puts the environment on an economist's balance sheet: a stock of wealth that markets treat as free, and therefore spend as if it were worthless. We work out exactly why unregulated markets run the environment down, sort the instruments governments use to stop them, pin down what sustainability actually means, and then measure the stakes for South Africa — a country that pollutes heavily, suffers early, and is attempting one of the most closely watched energy transitions on Earth.

## Subtopic: Why Markets Fail the Environment

Begin with the diagnosis, because vague talk about greed earns nothing in an essay. Environmental deterioration has a technical cause, and the cause is a market failure called an externality.

An externality is a cost or a benefit that falls on somebody who never agreed to the transaction. Consider a tannery on the banks of a river. The leather buyer pays the tannery; the tannery pays for hides and chemicals and wages. Every rand inside the deal is accounted for. But the chemical effluent slides downstream to the irrigation farmers and the households drawing water below the plant, and their losses — sick crops, treatment costs, illness — appear in nobody's books. Because those costs never reach the tannery's price, leather is sold too cheaply, and a too-cheap product is bought and produced in too great a quantity. Read that chain carefully: pollution is not the price system misfiring. It is the price system working flawlessly on numbers that are wrong.

The second failure concerns ownership. Many environmental assets are common resources — open to all, owned by none. Grazing land held by a whole district, an aquifer under many farms, the fish along an open coastline, the atmosphere itself. Each individual user captures the whole gain from taking a little more, while the damage to the shared stock is split among everyone. So every user, behaving perfectly rationally, takes a little more — and the stock collapses. Economists call this the tragedy of the commons, and its logic is merciless: whatever belongs to everybody is protected by nobody.

The third failure is time. Markets aggregate the wishes of people alive and spending now. A child who will need clean groundwater in the year 2070 casts no vote in any market today. Damage that matures over decades — a warming atmosphere, a salinised soil, an exhausted fishery — is systematically underweighted by decisions judged on this year's returns.

South Africa supplies a full case file. The Vaal River system has repeatedly been fouled by failing sewage works — a cost dumped on downstream users by upstream operators who never pay it. Communal rangelands in parts of Limpopo and the Eastern Cape carry more animals than they can regrow, textbook commons pressure. Poachers have stripped abalone from the Western Cape coast because the resource is open and enforcement thin. And because our electricity is generated overwhelmingly from coal, South Africans emit more carbon per person than the citizens of most far richer countries.

The questions for this section are with you now: the externality traced through the tannery, the commons logic reconstructed step by step, and each South African case matched to the failure it illustrates.

## Subtopic: Protecting the Environment — the Policy Toolbox

If the disease is wrong prices, missing owners and absent future voices, the medicine follows directly: correct the prices, impose the rules, create the owners, or bind the nations. Governments work with four families of instruments, and a complete answer names all four with examples.

Family one is command and control — direct regulation. The state legislates limits and prosecutes breaches: emission ceilings for smokestacks, water-quality standards, compulsory environmental impact assessments before a mine or estate may break ground, closed fishing seasons, minimum quotas, and protected areas where extraction is simply forbidden. South Africa builds this family on a constitutional foundation: everyone holds the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or well-being, and a body of environmental law gives that right operational force. The strength of regulation is certainty — a legal ceiling is a ceiling. Its weakness is enforcement: a standard without inspectors is a press release.

Family two is the market-based family — instruments that make the polluter pay. An environmental tax attaches the missing price: South Africa's carbon tax charges large emitters for every ton of greenhouse gas, one of the first such taxes in the developing world; the plastic bag levy and the tyre levy apply the identical logic to everyday purchases. Notice the elegance of the design. The externality was a real cost carrying a price of zero; the tax replaces the zero with a number, so the polluter finally confronts the true cost of production, and cutting pollution converts from charity into savings. The mirror image is the subsidy for clean alternatives — rebates on rooftop solar, support for renewable generation. Between tax and rule sits the tradable permit scheme: government caps total emissions, issues permits up to the cap, and lets firms trade them, so the firms that can abate cheaply do the abating and sell their spare permits to those that cannot — the cap is honoured at the lowest cost to the economy.

Family three creates owners. Where the commons dies of ownerlessness, assign the resource to someone with a stake in its future: fishing rights vested in a named coastal community, or land-restitution parks where a community owns the land inside a game reserve and earns the tourism revenue from it. An owner conserves because next decade's value already belongs to her.

Family four is international agreement, because greenhouse gases and migrating fish ignore borders. A country acting alone bears the full cost and captures a sliver of the benefit, so the temptation is to let others act — the free-rider problem. Treaties such as the Paris Agreement, which South Africa has signed, commit countries to targets simultaneously so that nobody is cutting alone.

For the evaluation marks: regulation is certain but administration-hungry; taxes recruit self-interest but fall hardest on poor households unless the revenue is recycled back to them; permits find the cheapest reductions but require honest monitoring; treaties have global reach and weak teeth. Sound policy layers the families rather than choosing one.

Work this section's questions now: the four families each named with a South African instrument, the carbon tax defended through the externality argument, and one strength paired honestly with one weakness.

## Subtopic: Sustainability — Living Off the Interest, Not the Capital

Now the concept that organises the entire topic. The definition worth memorising word for word comes from the Brundtland Commission: sustainable development meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Translate that into balance-sheet language. Nature is capital — a productive stock that can pay out forever if the stock itself is left intact. A herd breeds; a forest regrows; a catchment refills; sunlight arrives daily without invoice. Sustainable use means living off the interest: harvesting no faster than the stock replaces itself. Deterioration is spending the capital — netting fish faster than they spawn, cropping soil fertility without restoring it, burning in one century the fossil carbon that accumulated over three hundred million years. The account still shows withdrawals working — until it belongs, empty, to your grandchildren.

The resource distinction sharpens the point. Renewable resources — fish, timber, water, soil, grazing — regenerate, but only below a critical harvest rate. Below the threshold, the stock is immortal; above it, the stock spirals down and a renewable behaves, in practice, like a non-renewable. Non-renewable resources — coal, iron ore, gold, platinum, chrome — regenerate on no timescale that matters to humans. For them, sustainability cannot mean preservation of the stock; it means converting the once-off proceeds into durable assets — educated people, infrastructure, diversified industry — so that when the orebody is finished, the wealth is not.

Sustainability stands on three pillars, and essay questions love the trio: environmental — keep the natural stocks alive; economic — keep production, income and employment running; social — distribute the costs and benefits justly. Remove any pillar and the structure fails: an economy that ruins its water ruins itself; a conservation scheme that impoverishes its neighbouring village will be undermined by that village; growth whose pollution is inhaled mainly by the poor is not development but cost-shifting.

South Africa's sternest test of the three pillars is the just transition. Most of our electricity is coal-fired; whole municipal economies in Mpumalanga are built on coal wages; yet both the climate and our export customers demand decarbonisation. Move too slowly and we absorb worsening droughts and floods while our goods pay carbon penalties at foreign borders; move carelessly and we strand tens of thousands of workers and their towns. A just transition means building clean energy at speed while retraining workers and reindustrialising the coal regions — all three pillars carried at once.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: the Brundtland definition quoted exactly, interest-versus-capital applied to one renewable and one non-renewable, and the three pillars stress-tested against the just transition.

## Subtopic: The Global and Local Impact on South Africa

Finish with impacts, because they have moved out of the textbooks and into the news bulletins.

The global mechanism takes one tight paragraph. Combustion of fossil fuels releases greenhouse gases; the gases trap outgoing heat; the planet has already warmed by more than one degree above pre-industrial temperature; and a warmer atmosphere loads the dice for extremes — deeper droughts, more violent rainfall, rising seas. Now add the moral geometry that shapes every climate negotiation: the industrialised North accumulated its wealth through two centuries of emissions, while the South — with the least fiscal room and the most fragile agriculture — absorbs the earliest and heaviest damage. Africa contributes only a few percent of global emissions and carries a disproportionate share of the consequences.

South Africa's own evidence list is recent and specific. Water: we are a structurally water-scarce country with rainfall far below the world average, and the southern and eastern Cape have lived through multi-year droughts that pushed metropolitan dam systems toward failure and forced emergency restrictions on households and industry. Floods: in April 2022, extreme rainfall over KwaZulu-Natal killed more than four hundred people and swept away homes, bridges and factory stock worth billions of rands. Agriculture: the El Niño drought of 2015 and 2016 cut the maize harvest so severely that South Africa, normally a maize exporter, had to import grain — and food price spikes always strike hardest at poor households, who spend the largest share of income on food. Heat: rising temperatures punish outdoor labourers and residents of dense informal settlements first.

Then there is the trade exposure. Our export customers in the North are introducing carbon border charges — levies on the emissions embodied in imported goods. Steel, aluminium and other products made with coal-fired electricity will pay a climate penalty at the customs post, which converts our coal dependence into a creeping tariff on our entire export basket.

Against all this, know the response file: South Africa's emission-reduction commitments under the Paris Agreement; the carbon tax; renewable-energy auctions that have procured wind and solar power at some of the lowest prices recorded anywhere; and the Just Energy Transition Partnership, under which wealthy countries pledged roughly eight and a half billion dollars to help finance our exit from coal — a global experiment in whether climate justice can be financed in practice. The closing sentence for an essay: South Africa is exposed three times over — as a heavy polluter, as an early victim, and as a coal-dependent exporter — which is why sustainability here is not sentiment but solvency.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the warming mechanism written as a chain, two South African impacts cited with dates or numbers, and the triple exposure compressed into a single sentence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic through a smelter's ash, a shared field, and a spaza shop's stock money.

## Subtopic: The Smoke Nobody Pays For

Picture a scrap-metal smelter on the edge of a township. It buys old car bodies and burglar bars, melts them down, and sells shiny new steel rods to builders. The builders pay the smelter; the smelter pays for scrap, electricity and wages. Inside that deal, everyone is satisfied. But every afternoon the furnace ash drifts across the fence line and settles on the washing lines, the vegetable patches and the school netball court. Children cough. Washing must be done twice. Spinach comes up grey. The neighbourhood pays, every single day — and the neighbourhood never signed the deal.

That unposted bill is what economists call an externality: a cost carried by outsiders to a transaction. And it sets a trap. Because the smelter never pays for the coughing and the ruined washing, its steel rods are priced too low — and cheap rods sell fast, so the furnace runs longer, and the ash falls thicker. The market is not broken; the market is doing exactly what its prices tell it to do. The prices are simply lying.

Second scene: a communal grazing field outside the same town. It belongs to everyone, which is to say it belongs to no one. Each livestock owner thinks it through honestly: if I put one more cow on the field, the whole cow is mine — the thinner grass is a problem shared by forty families. Forty owners, each individually sensible, each add one more cow. Within a few seasons the field is dust. Nobody was stupid and nobody was wicked; the arithmetic of the unowned did the damage. That is the tragedy of the commons — what belongs to everybody is protected by nobody — and it is the same arithmetic emptying open fishing waters and drawing shared boreholes down.

Third scene: notice who never appears in either story. The girl who will need that field, and that borehole, in the year 2070. She buys nothing today, so today's market gives her needs a price of exactly zero — unless somebody writes her into the sums deliberately.

Now enlarge the picture until it covers the country: coal smoke over the power-station belt, sewage in the Vaal, stripped abalone reefs, some of the highest per-person emissions in the developing world. Same three stories, national scale.

Your questions for this section are with you now — each one shows you a deal, and asks who is paying without having signed, and why that makes the market produce too much.

## Subtopic: Putting a Price on the Smoke

If the sickness is bills that never arrive, the cures write themselves: deliver the bill, set the limits, appoint an owner, or sign with the neighbours.

Cure one: limits with consequences. The law tells the smelter how much it may emit, full stop; no new mine or mall proceeds without an environmental study; each fishing community gets a quota and a season. Our constitution promises everyone an environment not harmful to their health, and the environmental laws hang off that promise. But be honest in your evaluation: a limit is only as real as the inspector who checks it. An unenforced rule is a polite notice.

Cure two: deliver the bill — the economist's favourite. The missing invoice can be written by the state as a tax. You have paid one yourself: the plastic bag levy exists so that a bag is no longer free, and a thing that costs something gets thought about. The carbon tax scales the same idea up to the giants — every ton of greenhouse gas now has a price on it, so a firm that cuts its emissions is no longer doing charity; it is cutting costs. Feel the reversal there: the same self-interest that caused the smoke is now hired to remove it. And the tool works in reverse as a reward — rebates for rooftop solar and support for clean power pay people to choose cleanly.

Cure three: appoint an owner. The dust-bowl field can be saved by giving it a keeper with a future: a grazing committee that fences camps and rotates the herds, a coastal village that owns its abalone rights outright, a community that holds title to reserve land and banks the tourism income. Ownership flips the arithmetic — one more cow now eats your own next year, so you stop at the field's limit, not past it.

Cure four: sign with the neighbours. Smoke drifts over borders, so one clean country in a dirty world pays much and fixes little. That is why countries sign together — South Africa's signature is on the Paris Agreement — promising targets side by side so nobody carries the load alone or hides behind the rest.

None of the four cures is complete on its own: rules need policing, taxes pinch poor households unless the money flows back to them, ownership must be allocated fairly, and treaties rely on promise-keeping. Real protection stacks all four.

Take this section's questions now — each names a cure, and asks which broken piece of the market it mends.

## Subtopic: Eating the Seed Mealies

Farming households have carried this topic's deepest rule for generations: eat the harvest, never the seed. However hard the winter bites, the planting bag stays shut — because a family that eats its seed mealies eats next year's entire crop in one season.

That rule, written large, is sustainability. The famous definition — quote it exactly — says we must meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Nature is the seed bag. Fish spawn, forests regrow, catchments refill: draw on them more slowly than they renew and they feed your family forever; draw faster and the renewable collapses like a mine that has run out. And coal, gold and platinum never regrow at all — each ton is spent exactly once, so the only honest use of that once-off money is to build things that outlast the mine: schooling, railways, industries, towns with second acts. Think of a spaza owner who starts eating the stock money instead of the profit — the shelves thin, the shop dies. A country can do that to itself in slow motion.

Sustainability keeps three books balanced at once: nature's book, the economy's book, and the fairness book. Blow any one of them and the other two follow. Poison the rivers and the farms and factories on them close. Fence off a reserve while the village beside it goes hungry, and the fence will not survive the village. Get rich on smoke the poor must breathe, and you have not developed — you have merely posted the bill downstairs.

South Africa is running this exact test in real time. The southern Cape watched its dams sink toward empty through a multi-year drought. In 2022, KwaZulu-Natal buried more than four hundred flood victims and lost billions of rands of homes and roads in a single week of rain. The 2016 drought forced a maize-exporting country to import its staple. And nearly all our electricity still comes from coal — which feeds the same warming that is flooding and drying us, and which our customers abroad have started taxing at their borders. Dirty power is quietly becoming a surcharge on everything South Africa sells.

So the country is attempting the hardest balancing act in the syllabus: shutting down the coal era without shutting down the coal towns — clean plants built fast, workers retrained, Mpumalanga given a second economy — with richer nations contributing billions because their smoke came first. Whether it works will be decided by, among others, the people writing this exam.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: seed versus harvest applied to a fishery and a platinum mine, the three books tested on a real dilemma, and the drought, the floods and the maize imports cited as proof that this topic has left the realm of theory.
