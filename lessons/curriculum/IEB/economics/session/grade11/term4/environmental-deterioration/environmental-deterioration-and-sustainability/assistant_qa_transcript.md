**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the smelter's ash is really just 'prices that lie', couldn't the neighbours simply sue, and let the courts fix it without any economics?

**Tutor:** Courts help, and in a perfect world of cheap lawyers they might even be enough — that insight has a distinguished history in economics. If the township could easily prove the harm, identify the polluter, and enforce a judgment, the smelter would face the cost of its ash through damages, and the missing price would be restored through the courtroom instead of the tax office. But now list what that requires: hundreds of affected households organising together, scientific proof linking this ash to these coughs, legal fees that dwarf a poor community's means, and a defendant with assets worth suing. Each requirement is a wall, and pollution cases fail on every one of them routinely — many small victims with individually small damages facing one well-lawyered firm is the exact situation courts handle worst. That is why economists treat litigation as a complement, not a substitute: taxes and standards work automatically, before the harm, without requiring victims to fight; the law of delict mops up afterwards where it can. For an evaluation answer, frame it as transaction costs: when bargaining or suing is cheap, private parties can fix externalities themselves; when transaction costs are high — many victims, hard proof, deep pockets on one side — policy instruments must do the pricing instead. That one framing sentence turns a legal detour into economics marks.

**Bianca:** Next one: the class keeps hearing that the carbon tax just makes electricity more expensive for ordinary families while the big polluters carry on. Is that true, and how should we argue it?

**Tutor:** Argue it in three honest moves, because both halves of the complaint contain truth. Move one — state the purpose: the tax exists to change behaviour, not to raise revenue. A ton of emissions used to cost nothing; now it costs something, so every boardroom in the country has a new line item that shrinks when they clean up. That is the externality being priced, and it is the whole point. Move two — concede the equity problem: any tax that touches energy is regressive, because poor households spend a far bigger slice of income on electricity and transport than rich ones do. If the tax filters into the electricity price, the family cooking on a two-plate stove feels it proportionally hardest, and pretending otherwise loses you credibility and marks. Move three — give the design answer: the problem is not the instrument but where the money goes. Recycle the revenue — fund free basic electricity, subsidise public transport, top up grants — and the poor are compensated while the price signal keeps pushing firms toward clean production. Economists call that a recycled or revenue-neutral design. As for the big polluters carrying on: in the tax's early phases, generous allowances did soften it for the largest emitters, which is a fair criticism of the rate, not of the logic — the remedy is a stronger price, not no price. Purpose, equity concession, design fix. Three moves, full marks.

**Bianca:** Third: sustainability keeps sounding like a rich country's hobby. With our unemployment, shouldn't South Africa pollute first and clean up later, the way the North did?

**Tutor:** That argument deserves respect, because the North genuinely did industrialise on smoke, and telling poor countries to stay clean while staying poor would be an injustice stacked on an injustice. But walk the pollute-first strategy forward and it collapses on three facts of our own situation. Fact one: the damage no longer waits politely for us to get rich. The KwaZulu-Natal floods took over four hundred lives and billions of rands in a week; the 2016 drought turned a maize exporter into a maize importer; a water-scarce country cannot grow an economy on rivers it has poisoned. Deterioration is not tomorrow's bill — it is already in this year's budget. Fact two: the world has changed the rules of trade. The North polluted into an atmosphere nobody priced and sold into markets nobody policed; we would be polluting into carbon border charges, where goods made with dirty electricity pay penalties at the customer's port. Pollute-first is now a strategy for pricing yourself out of your own export markets. Fact three: the clean path has become the cheap path — our renewable auctions have delivered some of the lowest electricity prices ever recorded, and building that capacity is itself an employment programme. What survives of the argument is the justice claim, and it has won real money: the Just Energy Transition Partnership exists precisely because the North's historical smoke created an obligation to help finance the South's clean path. So the answer is not pollute first — it is: clean path, but the North helps pay. That is the position South Africa argues in every negotiation, and it is the position that earns the evaluation marks.

**Bianca:** Last one: essays on this topic always ask us to 'discuss measures to protect the environment', and everyone writes the same list. How do we stand out?

**Tutor:** Stop writing a list and start writing a repair manual — organise by what is broken, and attach each tool to its breakage. Open with one diagnostic sentence: markets damage the environment because costs go unpriced, resources go unowned, and nations free-ride on each other. That sentence is your essay's spine, and most answers in the pile will not have one. Then four short paragraphs, each with the identical internal rhythm — name the tool, state its mechanism, land a South African example, close with a one-line evaluation. Regulation: direct limits — emission ceilings, impact assessments, quotas; certain in principle, only as strong as its inspectors in practice. Market instruments: put the missing price back — the carbon tax and the bag levy make pollution cost something, subsidies reward clean choices, tradable permits find the cheapest cuts; efficient, but regressive unless revenue is recycled. Property rights: give the commons an owner — community fishing rights, grazing committees, restitution parks earning tourism income; owners guard future value, but the allocation must be fair. Treaties: Paris — because emissions ignore borders; wide reach, weak enforcement. Then the closing sentence that separates the top answers: no single family repairs all three failures, so effective protection stacks them — rules for the worst harms, prices for the rest, owners for the commons, treaties for the atmosphere. Diagnostic spine, four rhythmic paragraphs, stacking conclusion. Practise it once tonight and it will hold under any time pressure.
