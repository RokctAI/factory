# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the first of the year's two revision sweeps — the systems, factors, national accounts and market machinery of the early terms, together with the two great policy levers, rebuilt into four structures you can carry into any exam room: economic systems and the four factors; measuring and mapping the economy; elasticity, costs and revenue; and connected markets under the two policy steering wheels. Nothing here is new; everything here is sharpened.

## Subtopic: Economic Systems and the Four Factors

Every economy must answer three questions — WHAT to produce, HOW to produce it, and FOR WHOM — and economic systems are distinguished by WHO gives the answers. In the FREE MARKET economy, prices answer: millions of purchases signal what people want, profit pulls resources toward it, and the state stays out of the kitchen. Strengths: efficiency, innovation, variety. Weaknesses: inequality, public goods left unbuilt, externalities left unpriced. In the CENTRALLY PLANNED economy, officials answer: planners fix outputs and prices from above. On paper: fairness and jobs for all. In practice: without price signals, gluts pile up beside shortages, effort goes unrewarded, and innovation stalls. Every actual economy is MIXED — markets allocating most goods while the state taxes, regulates and provides — and South Africa's mixture carries an extra label you must use: DUALISTIC. A first-world financial and industrial economy operates alongside a vast informal and subsistence economy — two economies, one flag.

Whichever system runs, it runs on the four factors of production, and each factor collects its own reward. NATURAL RESOURCES — everything nature supplies — earn RENT. LABOUR — human effort of hand and mind — earns WAGES, and its quality is human capital, built through education, training and health care. CAPITAL — produced means of production: machinery, buildings, tools — earns INTEREST; remember that capital is not money but what money buys for production. ENTREPRENEURSHIP — the factor that assembles the other three, carries the risk and takes the decisions — earns PROFIT, and profit is the RESIDUAL: what remains after rent, wages and interest are settled, which some years is nothing and some years is less than nothing. That residual position is the entire justification for profit — it is the price of bearing uncertainty.

The South African layer that lifts an answer: access decides everything. A licence, a serviced stand, a tender, a loan — these determine who actually gets to combine the factors, which is why local economic development, empowerment procurement and small-business finance sit inside this topic rather than beside it.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the systems separated by who answers the three questions, the dualistic label defended, and each factor bound to its reward with profit argued as the residual.

## Subtopic: Measuring and Mapping the Economy — Aggregates, Sectors and Infrastructure

One economy, three tape measures, one reading. The PRODUCTION method totals the value added by every industry — gross value added — then adds taxes on products and subtracts subsidies to reach GDP at market prices. The INCOME method totals the factor rewards: wages plus rent plus interest plus profit. The EXPENDITURE method totals final spending: C plus I plus G plus X minus M. Each component carries its own precision marks. C is household consumption, the biggest block. G is government CONSUMPTION — the salaries of nurses, teachers and police — not grant transfers. I is gross fixed capital formation — today's spending that builds tomorrow's capacity: plants, roads, machinery. X minus M adjusts for the border, because imports meet local demand without local production. And GNI reroutes income to where it belongs: GDP plus what residents earn abroad minus what foreigners earn here.

Now the map behind the measurements. Three SECTORS: PRIMARY draws from nature — farming, fishing, mining; SECONDARY transforms — manufacturing, construction, utilities; TERTIARY serves — retail, transport, finance, education, government. South Africa's structural arc matters: the primary share of GDP has shrunk over the decades, manufacturing has drifted sideways — the pattern economists call premature deindustrialisation — and services now generate roughly two-thirds of GDP. Sectors chain together: iron ore becomes steel becomes a bridge; sunflowers become oil becomes the shelf — so a failure upstream travels downstream with interest.

Beneath the whole map lies INFRASTRUCTURE — electricity, transport, water, communications — the skeleton every sector hangs on. When the skeleton fails, the body limps: the load-shedding years are the national demonstration, with production interrupted, small firms bankrupted, and investors priced away by uncertainty. And infrastructure is also a gate at household level: the family with power, water, transport and signal can work, study and sell; the family without them is locked outside the economy before breakfast.

The questions for this section are with you now: three methods reconciled to one GDP, each expenditure component classified without slips, the sectoral arc told in the right direction, and infrastructure argued as engine and gate at once.

## Subtopic: Markets at Work — Elasticity, Costs and Revenue

PRICE ELASTICITY OF DEMAND measures how sharply quantity demanded reacts to a price change: percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in price. Read the result in five bands: ELASTIC, above one — quantity reacts hard; INELASTIC, below one — quantity barely flinches; UNITARY at exactly one; and the two textbook extremes, perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic. For the curves in words: elastic demand draws flatter — a small price rise chases away much quantity; inelastic demand stands steeper — price climbs, quantity holds. The DETERMINANTS tell you which band you are in: substitutes first and always, necessity versus luxury, the slice of income the item claims, and time — elasticity grows as buyers discover alternatives.

The payoff arrives through TOTAL REVENUE — price times quantity. Facing INELASTIC demand, a price rise fattens revenue: the few customers lost are outweighed by the higher price on everyone who stays — think staple foods, fuel, prepaid electricity. Facing ELASTIC demand, a price rise drains revenue — and a price CUT can fill the till instead, because volume floods back faster than the price fell. One line carries the whole logic: elasticity tells you which direction revenue moves when price moves.

Now the firm's own ledger. FIXED costs stand still whatever output does — the rent is owed on a day of zero sales; VARIABLE costs climb with every unit made. From the totals come AVERAGE cost per unit and MARGINAL cost — the cost of the NEXT unit, which is the only number a production decision actually consults. A small firm in a crowded market is a PRICE TAKER: the market hands it a selling price, so each extra unit brings in exactly that price — marginal revenue equals price. The profit rule follows: keep expanding while marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost, and stop where MARGINAL REVENUE EQUALS MARGINAL COST — before that output every unit adds to profit; past it every unit subtracts. In any cost table, verify by reading the MR and MC columns row against row, and report profit as total revenue minus total cost at the chosen output.

Stop for this section's questions now — the elasticity number computed and banded, determinants applied, the revenue payoff argued in both directions, and the stopping rule located and verified in a table.

## Subtopic: Connected Markets and the Two Policies

Markets converse through RELATIVE prices, because buyers compare before they buy. SUBSTITUTES rival each other: margarine against butter — when butter's price jumps, the DEMAND curve for margarine shifts right. COMPLEMENTS travel together: braai stands and charcoal — when meat prices surge, the demand for charcoal eases left. Write the mechanism, never just the label: a price change in one market SHIFTS the demand curve in the other.

PRODUCT markets and FACTOR markets close into a loop: the demand for labour is DERIVED from the demand for products — nobody hires a welder except because somebody, somewhere, is buying what welding builds. When imported furniture displaces local furniture, the workshop's carpenters receive the product market's verdict in the factor market. And structure the markets by counting sellers: perfect competition's many, monopolistic competition's many-but-differentiated, oligopoly's dominant few, monopoly's one — from the street of spaza shops to the single national electricity grid.

Above the whole web stand two steering wheels. MONETARY policy is held by the South African Reserve Bank, whose primary mandate is price stability, targeted as inflation between three and six percent. Its lever is the REPO RATE — the rate at which commercial banks borrow from the central bank. Raise it and the chain tightens link by link: banks lift prime, credit and bond repayments grow heavier, households and firms cut borrowing and spending, demand cools, inflation subsides — with slower growth as the toll. Lower it and the chain runs loose in reverse. FISCAL policy is held by the elected government through the annual budget: SPENDING injects demand and builds directly; TAXATION withdraws demand and redistributes; the gap between them is the deficit, financed by borrowing that stacks up as public debt — whose interest bill quietly elbows schools and clinics out of future budgets.

Compare the two as questions demand: different drivers — an independent central bank versus an elected treasury; different levers — one interest rate versus a thousand budget lines; different speeds — the repo adjusts in an afternoon, the budget turns annually; different targets — inflation on one side, growth, jobs and redistribution on the other. The complete answer ends on the mix: two hands, one economy, best when pulling together.

The final questions of this part are with you now: substitutes and complements argued through curve shifts, the derived-demand loop closed, the four structures counted out, and the two policies compared driver by driver.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery through an ordinary Saturday: a street of food stalls, a school tuckshop, and a supermarket till slip.

## Subtopic: Three Kitchens and Four Pay Packets

Walk down a street of three kitchens. Kitchen one: the cooks watch the queue. Whatever sells out by nine gets doubled by ten; whatever sits gets scrapped. Nobody instructs anyone — the queue instructs everyone. That is the free market: quick, inventive, merciless — and whoever cannot pay stands outside the queue entirely. Kitchen two: no cook may choose. A menu arrives from a distant office — so many loaves, so much soup, prices fixed. That is the planned economy: noble on paper, but the office cannot see the queue, so Tuesday drowns in soup while Thursday runs out of bread. Kitchen three: the queue decides the menu, but an inspector checks the fridge, the taxman takes a share, and a soup pot at the door feeds those who cannot pay. That is the mixed economy — which is every real economy on Earth. South Africa adds its own seasoning: we run a polished restaurant strip and a paraffin-stove economy in the same country at the same time. In an answer, both words: mixed AND dualistic.

Whichever kitchen wins, four contributors queue for pay at closing time. The ground the stall stands on — nature's input — collects RENT. The hands that kneaded and fried — labour — collect WAGES, and trained hands collect more, which is the entire argument for staying in school. The fryer, the gas bottle, the counter — capital, things made in order to make other things — collect INTEREST. And the owner, who rented the ground, hired the hands and bought the fryer with her savings? She collects PROFIT — strictly whatever is LEFT after the other three envelopes are filled. Some Saturdays her envelope is fat; some Saturdays it is empty; a bad month, it is negative. Profit is the only envelope that can come up empty, and that is precisely why it exists: it pays her for carrying the risk no one else would.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are with you right now: three kitchens told apart by who reads the queue, and four envelopes matched to four contributions, with profit as the leftover.

## Subtopic: Rubber Bands, Planks and the Next Plate

Some prices you can sidestep; others you must swallow. Your favourite chips go up two rand — you shrug, grab the rival brand or the popcorn next to it, and life continues: your demand stretched like a RUBBER BAND. The taxi fare to school goes up two rand — you grumble, you pay, you ride: that demand is a PLANK, and planks do not bend. The stretch has a formula: percentage change in quantity over percentage change in price. Rubber bands score above one — elastic. Planks score below one — inelastic. What builds a plank? No substitute, a necessity, a small bite of the budget. What builds a rubber band? A shelf full of rivals and the time to try them.

Why should any seller care? Because the stretch forecasts the till. Push up the price of a PLANK and the till swells — the riders still ride. Push up the price of a RUBBER BAND and the till shrinks — customers scatter faster than the higher price can compensate; with rubber bands it is the price CUT that packs the stall. One question — how far do they bounce? — settles every revenue problem this topic can ask.

Now run your own vetkoek stall and decide how many to fry. Two costs live in your book: the stand fee, owed even if you fry nothing — fixed; and the flour, oil and mince that leave with every vetkoek — variable. The going price is fifteen rand, and the market is far too big for your stall to move it — you take the price as given. So put every decision to one interrogation: the NEXT vetkoek, and only the next one. While the next vetkoek costs less than fifteen rand to make, fry it — it adds profit. The morning the next one starts costing MORE than fifteen — the oil overworked, the helper on overtime — stop frying. That is the entire theory of the firm in one sentence: produce until the next unit earns exactly what it costs. Marginal revenue equals marginal cost, said in vetkoek.

Take this section's questions now — rubber bands and planks diagnosed, the till forecast before the price moves, and the next vetkoek interrogated all the way to the stopping point.

## Subtopic: The Till Slip and the Two Drivers

Measuring a country is reading its till slip. Total everything produced and sold — final goods only, or the flour gets counted once alone and again inside the vetkoek. The slip can be added three ways, and all three must land on the same figure: add the value each business ADDED at its own step; add everything the factors EARNED — every wage, rent, interest payment and profit; or add everything buyers SPENT — household trolleys, firms' new machines, government's nurses and teachers, plus what foreigners spent on our exports, minus what we spent on theirs. Three adding routes, one economy, one total.

The slip also sorts into three departments. Out of the earth: farms, boats, mines — primary. Through the works: bakeries, factories, building sites — secondary. Across the counter: shops, taxis, banks, classrooms — tertiary. Our slip has been reorganising itself for decades: the counter department has grown to about two-thirds of the total while the works department thinned. And every line on the slip leans on the wiring behind the wall — power, roads, water, signal. Let the power fail and watch the whole slip shorten; this country has run that experiment live, year after year, and the results are not disputed.

Finally, meet the two drivers steering the economy's speed. Driver one sits at the Reserve Bank holding a single pedal: the REPO RATE. Press it down — costlier borrowing — and within weeks every bond, car instalment and store account weighs more; spending cools; prices settle. Ease off and credit cheapens, tills ring louder. This driver's contract is one sentence long: hold inflation between three and six percent. Driver two sits at the Treasury holding the budget: choosing what to SPEND on clinics, grants and roads, and what to TAX from payslips, purchases and profits. Spend beyond the tax take and the difference is borrowed — and the interest on the borrowing quietly eats a growing slice of every future budget. The repo pedal answers in an afternoon; the budget ship turns once a year. Quick pedal, slow ship, one economy between them — and the favourite comparison question is simply: which driver, which lever, which speed, which target?

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the till slip added three ways, the three departments with their long shift, and the two drivers matched to pedal, ship, speed and target. Carry the structures in with you — the questions open to them like locks you already hold keys for.
