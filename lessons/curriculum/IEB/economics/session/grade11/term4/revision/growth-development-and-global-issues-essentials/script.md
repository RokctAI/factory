# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the second of the year's two revision sweeps: the material of terms three and four rebuilt into the four structures that carry the long questions — growth, inequality and redistribution; development and the developing world; globalisation and the North/South divide; and the environment as the market's blind spot. At this level the questions stop paying for definitions alone and start paying for arguments, so every section here closes the full loop: mechanism, evidence, evaluation.

## Subtopic: Growth, Inequality and Redistribution

ECONOMIC GROWTH is the increase in a country's REAL production — real GDP — between one period and the next. Two adjustments are compulsory before any claim of growth survives scrutiny: strip out inflation, because a rise in prices is not a rise in output; and divide by population, because production growing slower than the number of people means shrinking average shares. South Africa's record anchors the topic: growth has crawled for more than a decade, frequently behind population growth, so income per person has flatlined — the starting fact of every constraints essay.

Inequality must be measured before it can be argued. The LORENZ CURVE, narrated for the ear: arrange the entire population from poorest to richest along the horizontal; accumulate their share of total income up the vertical. Perfect equality traces the straight diagonal — any twenty percent of people holding exactly twenty percent of income. Reality bows below that line, and the deeper the bow, the harsher the inequality. The GINI COEFFICIENT compresses the bow into a single number: zero for perfect equality, one for a single person holding everything. South Africa's Gini sits around nought comma six five — among the highest figures ever recorded anywhere — and that number should appear, placed and interpreted, in any inequality answer you write.

REDISTRIBUTION operates through instruments, each of which must be named with its mechanism. PROGRESSIVE TAXATION: the percentage taken rises with income, so the fortunate fund the rest. SOCIAL GRANTS: old-age pensions, the child support grant and disability payments flowing into millions of households monthly — the state's most direct antipoverty machine. FREE BASIC SERVICES: allocations of water and electricity to poor households. The NATIONAL MINIMUM WAGE: a floor under the price of labour. LAND REFORM and empowerment policy: moving productive assets, not just income. And each instrument carries its evaluation in the same breath: grants relieve poverty today but lean on a narrow tax base; the wage floor reaches only the employed; transferred assets create wealth only when finance, skills and markets travel with them.

Complete the structure with the growth constraints: an unreliable electricity supply, a shortage of skills standing beside mass unemployment, low savings and investment rates, crime and policy uncertainty deterring capital, and a public debt whose interest bill now competes with schools and hospitals for every budget rand. The sentence that ties the topic closed: redistribution needs growth to pay for it, and growth needs redistribution to legitimise it — the pot must grow while the shares move.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: real per capita growth defined with both adjustments, the Lorenz curve narrated and the Gini placed, instruments matched to mechanisms, and constraints named with their consequences.

## Subtopic: Development and the Developing World

Growth counts production; DEVELOPMENT audits lives. Development is the wider process in which rising income arrives together with longer lives, better schooling, expanding freedom and real opportunity — and the distinction cuts both ways in questions: growth without development, where GDP climbs while living standards stall, and development goals that GDP never sees. The HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX makes the distinction operational by averaging three dimensions — life expectancy, education, and income per person — which is why a middle-income country that schools and doctors its people well can outrank a richer neighbour that does not.

DEVELOPING COUNTRIES share a portrait the syllabus expects you to paint: low income per head; rapid population growth and young age structures; high unemployment and underemployment; export baskets dominated by PRIMARY products — minerals and crops; DUALISM — a modern urban economy running beside subsistence livelihoods; and deficits in infrastructure, health care and education that keep human capital thin. Test South Africa against the portrait honestly: we match it in dualism, unemployment and raw exports, and break it with deep capital markets, a sophisticated banking system and research universities. Arguing both sides of that fit is precisely the evaluation skill the long questions reward.

DEVELOPMENT STRATEGIES group into families. Growth-first strategies trust the expanding pot to reach everyone eventually. Redistribution-with-growth engineers the reaching deliberately. Basic-needs strategies begin with water, food, clinics and classrooms. Export-led strategies climb through world markets. South Africa's policy history walks through the families in order: the RDP led with basic needs; GEAR tightened the finances in pursuit of stability and growth; AsgiSA attacked specific bottlenecks; and the NATIONAL DEVELOPMENT PLAN, the standing framework, targets poverty and inequality through infrastructure, education and employment. The recurring verdict — and markers reward you for delivering it with evidence rather than cynicism — is strong diagnosis, weak delivery: the distance between the document and the ground remains South Africa's development gap.

INDIGENOUS KNOWLEDGE SYSTEMS complete the picture: development grown from within — inherited knowledge of crops, healing, grazing rotation and communal organisation. It costs little, fits local conditions exactly, and carries what imported blueprints never do: ownership. Development done to people fails quietly; development done with people compounds.

The questions for this section are with you now: growth split from development through the HDI, the developing-country portrait drawn and tested against South Africa both ways, the strategy families sorted, and the plans sequenced by name.

## Subtopic: Globalisation and the North/South Divide

GLOBALISATION is the deepening interconnection of the world's economies — goods, capital, people and information crossing borders until national economies function as parts of one system. Its causes arrive in two families, and complete answers deliver both. TECHNOLOGY made it possible: the standardised container collapsing freight costs; undersea cables and the internet making information instant; computing coordinating operations across continents — together enabling the GLOBAL VALUE CHAIN, production sliced into stages and stationed wherever each is done cheapest. POLICY made it permitted: tariffs negotiated downward for decades into the World Trade Organisation; exchange controls dismantled so finance moves freely; and the closed economies — China from 1978, the former Soviet bloc, South Africa after 1994 — rejoining the world market.

The consequences demand a two-sided scorecard. Gains: specialisation delivering cheaper goods to every consumer; foreign investment building world-scale industry — our vehicle plants sustain over one hundred thousand jobs; technology spreading at speed; and export-led growth lifting hundreds of millions from poverty, mostly in Asia. Losses: import competition destroying exposed local industries — South Africa's clothing and footwear towns lost tens of thousands of jobs when tariffs fell; CONTAGION — the 2008 collapse began in foreign housing finance and was cutting South African shifts within a year; mobile capital outbargaining rooted workers and squeezed tax bases; and gains distributed unevenly between and within countries. The verdict formula: globalisation grows the total while moving the shares — policy's task is to capture the gains and cushion the losses.

The NORTH/SOUTH DIVIDE is the structure beneath: the developed North holding most of the world's income, research and finance; the developing South holding most of its people and poverty — economic shorthand, not geography. Master the three sustaining mechanisms: the TERMS-OF-TRADE treadmill — raw exports whose prices swing and sag against the manufactured imports they must buy; DEBT denominated in foreign currency, so Northern interest rates squeeze Southern social spending; and VOICE — rules drafted in institutions where votes tilted North, visible in tariffs that climb with processing and so discourage Southern industrialisation. Then the counterforces: Asia's transformation, BRICS — South Africa a member — demanding a Southern seat, and the African Continental Free Trade Area assembling the South's own market. South Africa straddles the line: Northern-calibre finance in Johannesburg, Southern-pattern raw exports in the trade accounts.

Stop for this section's questions now — both cause families, the scorecard argued with South African evidence, the three divide mechanisms, and one counterforce with an example.

## Subtopic: Market Failure and the Environment

The environment is where market bookkeeping breaks down, and each break has a name. An EXTERNALITY is a cost or benefit landing outside the transaction: the factory and its customer settle their invoice while the smoke settles on the neighbourhood — a real cost priced at zero, so the product sells too cheap and society produces too much of it. PUBLIC GOODS and COMMON RESOURCES extend the failure: clean air excludes no one, so no one pays for it; the shared river, the communal grazing, the open fishery belong to everyone — and what belongs to everyone is protected by no one, because each user pockets the whole gain of one more unit while the loss spreads across all. Unpriced and unowned, nature is overdrawn: that is the market failure at the core of every environmental question.

The POLICY TOOLBOX answers failure by failure. COMMAND AND CONTROL: legislation, standards, protected areas and environmental impact assessments — direct and certain, but blunt and expensive to police. MARKET INSTRUMENTS restore the missing price: pollution taxes — the CARBON TAX on large emitters is the standing South African example — make the polluter carry the social cost; tradable permits cap total pollution and let firms trade shares so the cheapest reductions happen first; subsidies reward clean substitutes like rooftop solar. PROPERTY RIGHTS and community management give the commons an owner with a future to protect. Environmental education and international agreements close the set, because emissions hold no passport.

SUSTAINABILITY is the topic's organising idea: meeting the needs of the present WITHOUT compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own — in one image, living off nature's INTEREST while leaving the CAPITAL intact. Three pillars must stand together — economic, social and environmental — and any strategy that abandons one eventually loses all three.

South Africa's exposure is concrete: a water-scarce country warming faster than the global average; droughts that turned a maize exporter into an importer and floods that killed hundreds and destroyed billions of rands of infrastructure in KwaZulu-Natal; an electricity system built on coal while export customers prepare carbon border charges; acid mine drainage leaking from closed mines into living rivers; and millions of livelihoods resting directly on soil, rain and fisheries. The closing argument: the green transition is not a tax on growth — it is the entry fee for exports, water and food security in the decades you will work through.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the externality run through to too-cheap-too-much, the commons logic told, each tool matched to its failure, and sustainability defined on its three pillars.

## Subtopic: The Pot and the Queue of One Hundred

Put the economy on a table as one loaf of bread. GROWTH means next year's loaf is bigger than this year's — but check two tricks before celebrating. Trick one: a loaf that costs more is not a bigger loaf; scrape off the price froth first. Trick two: count the eaters; a loaf three percent bigger shared by three percent more people has improved nobody's slice. That double-check is our own last decade in miniature: the loaf crept, the table filled, the slices stood still.

Now watch how the loaf is shared. Line up one hundred people from thinnest slice to thickest, and stroll down the line keeping a running total of bread. In a perfectly fair country your total would rise steadily — half the bread by person fifty. In ours, you pass person sixty still holding barely a tenth of the loaf, while the final handful at the end of the line hold roughly half of it between them. Your stroll traced the Lorenz curve; the deeper it sags under the fair diagonal, the worse the sharing. And the sag scored as a number from zero to one is the Gini coefficient — ours hovers near nought comma six five, about the deepest sag any country has ever measured.

So the state moves bread back along the line, and you must know its five hands. Hand one: taxes that cut thicker slices from thicker plates. Hand two: grants — pension and child-support money that millions of households actually cook with each month. Hand three: free basic water and electricity where plates are thinnest. Hand four: a legal floor under wages. Hand five: moving the ovens themselves — land and ownership reform. Every hand works and every hand has a blister: grants need a growing loaf behind them; the wage floor helps only those with a job; an oven handed over without flour, skills or a market bakes nothing yet.

And the closing truth needs both arms: growing the loaf with frozen shares starves the front of the line; moving shares of a shrinking loaf runs out for everyone. Grow it and share it — an essay arguing only one arm is half an essay.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this section are with you right now: the two tricks caught, the stroll down the line, the sag scored, and five hands with their blisters.

## Subtopic: Taller or Better — and the One-World Shop

A teenager can shoot up ten centimetres and still fail the fitness test. Height is not health. Countries work the same way: GROWTH is the height — more produced this year than last. DEVELOPMENT is the full medical: are people living longer, learning more, choosing more freely? The world's medical chart, the Human Development Index, checks exactly three vitals — health, schooling, income — which is why a country of modest income that vaccinates and educates can rank above a richer one that does not.

What does the developing world look like on the chart? Six symptoms you could spot on a single drive: thin wallets; young faces everywhere; hands with nothing to do; wealth leaving raw — ore trucks and crop trailers heading for the port; a glass skyline visible from a tin-roof settlement — two economies in one windscreen; and clinics, classrooms and taps that run out before the queue does. Our country presents some symptoms and defies others — world-class banks beside village taps that fail — and stating BOTH is what an honest diagnosis sounds like.

How do countries get better? Four treatment plans: grow first and trust the wealth to trickle; grow and share by design; start from basic needs — water, food, clinics, schools — because sick, unschooled nations cannot grow; or sell your way up through exports. South Africa has filed its plans in initials — RDP for basics, GEAR for stable finances, AsgiSA for bottlenecks, NDP for the long horizon — and the chart keeps repeating one note: excellent diagnosis, thin delivery. And one treatment needs no imports at all: what communities already know — which seed survives which soil, which plant heals, how shared land gets rested — knowledge that is cheap, fitted and owned by its users.

Then step into the one-world shop. Your phone carries six countries' fingerprints — design, chip, glass, battery minerals, assembly, and the undersea cable that delivers its updates. Containers and cables made that shop possible; trade agreements unlocked its doors. The shop hands out gifts — cheaper goods, vehicle-plant jobs, millions lifted from poverty — and hands out bills: sewing and shoe machines silenced by imports, a foreign crash reaching our payslips within a year. And study the shop's floor plan: the profitable counters — roasting, branding, finishing — stand in the world's North, while the South delivers raw stock to the back door for cents. The way up is finishing what we dig and grow, and Africa's new free trade area is that idea signed into policy.

Take this section's questions now — height against medical, six symptoms on one drive, the initials in order, and the one-world shop's gifts and bills with South African evidence.

## Subtopic: The Smoke, the River and the Seed Mealies

Three short stories hold the entire environmental topic.

Story one: the smoke nobody pays for. A workshop galvanises steel on the edge of town; buyers pay for the shiny sheets, and the acrid fumes drift over the school next door — burning throats that appear on no invoice anywhere. Because the fumes cost the workshop nothing, the sheets are priced too low and the orders keep coming: the market is not misbehaving, it is obeying prices that leave out the truth. The repair is honest bookkeeping: make the polluter pay — a charge per ton of emissions, the way the carbon tax bills the country's biggest emitters — and watch clean technology stop looking expensive the moment dirty technology carries its real price.

Story two: the river nobody owns. Every household waters cattle there, every net fishes there, and each family reasons the same way: my extra bucket, my extra catch, is fully mine, while the falling river is everybody's problem. Multiply that perfectly sensible arithmetic by every family, every day — and the river dies. What belongs to everyone is protected by no one. The repairs all install a guardian: enforceable rules and seasons, a community trust that owns the water and its fish, or capped, tradable catch shares — anything that writes the river's limit into each user's own sums.

Story three: the seed mealies. In a hungry winter the planting bag whispers from the shelf — and the family that cooks it cancels next year's harvest entirely. Sustainability is the refusal to eat the seed: live off what nature regrows — the interest — and never off the breeding stock that does the regrowing — the capital. Nations run the same pantry: fish taken faster than they spawn, aquifers pumped past the rains' refill, coal burned into a warming sky. Our pantry carries the strain already: the drought that made a maize exporter import its staple, floods that buried hundreds and broke billions of rands of roads and homes, and a coal-fired grid facing customers who now charge for carbon at their borders.

Hold the three stories and you hold the topic: bill the smoke, guard the river, save the seed. The economy is not the environment's enemy — it is the only cook the pantry has, and its one non-negotiable lesson is the difference between dinner and seed.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each story matched to its failure and its repair, sustainability stated in a single sentence, and the three pillars standing together. Argue with mechanisms, close with evaluation — that is the entire wishlist of anyone marking you, and by now it is simply how you think.
