# Part 1 — Expert

This session takes the demand and supply machinery you already command and aims it at a wider truth: no market lives alone. A price change in one market drags demand around in another; what happens at the tills decides what happens to jobs; and the number of sellers inside a market decides how its prices get made in the first place. Three relationships — substitutes and complements, product markets and factor markets, and the four market structures — and every one of them is a dependable source of exam marks.

## Subtopic: Relative Prices — the Signal Buyers Actually Read

Begin with a distinction that unlocks the whole lesson: the difference between a price and a relative price.

The money price of rice might be thirty rand a kilogram. But no shopper weighs that number in a vacuum. The silent question every buyer asks is: thirty rand compared to what? Compared to maize meal at fifteen rand a kilogram, rice costs double — and that comparison, the price of one good expressed in terms of another, is the relative price. It is the signal that actually steers the trolley.

Prove it with a thought experiment. Imagine every price in the economy doubled overnight — rice to sixty, maize meal to thirty — and your income doubled with them. Every money price changed; the relative price did not — rice still costs twice what maize meal costs — and a rational shopper's split between them would not move either. Now suppose instead that only rice doubles while maize meal holds at fifteen. Maize meal's own tag never moved, yet maize meal just became relatively cheaper, and shoppers will lean towards it.

That is the deep point: markets coordinate behaviour through relative prices. When economists call prices signals, they mean relative prices — a good turning relatively cheaper pulls buyers in and warns the suppliers of its rival; a good turning relatively dearer pushes buyers away and invites producers to supply more of it.

Keep one piece of Grade 10 housekeeping razor-sharp, because everything below rests on it. A change in the price of the good itself moves you along its demand curve. A change in anything else — including the price of a related good — shifts the entire demand curve to a new position. Along versus shift: it gets tested relentlessly.

The questions for this section are with you now: relative price computed and interpreted, the doubling thought experiment reasoned through, and movement along a curve kept separate from a shift of the curve.

## Subtopic: Substitutes and Complements — and Their Graphs in Words

Now the first relationship between markets: related goods. Two goods are substitutes when they answer the same want, so one can stand in for the other — rice and maize meal, butter and margarine, bus trips and train trips. Two goods are complements when they are used together, so each needs the other — a smart TV and a streaming subscription, a printer and its ink, a car and its tyres.

Substitutes first, with the graph painted in words as we go. Draw the market for margarine: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal, the demand curve sloping down from top left to bottom right. Now let the price of butter — the substitute — leap after a dairy shortage, say from sixty rand to eighty-five rand a brick. Shoppers at the fridge compare relative prices and swing towards margarine. In the margarine market this is not a movement along the curve — margarine's own price has not changed. It is a rightward shift of the entire margarine demand curve: at every possible margarine price, more margarine is now wanted than before. The new curve sits to the right of the old one, and the market settles at a new equilibrium with a higher margarine price and a larger margarine quantity. The rule in one breath: a rise in the price of a substitute shifts demand for the other good to the right.

Now complements, same axes, new market: streaming subscriptions. Let the price of smart TVs fall as cheaper models flood in. TVs and streaming are used together, so cheaper TVs mean more screens in more homes, and every new screen wants something to play. The demand curve for subscriptions shifts to the right — at every subscription price, more are demanded. Run it in reverse for the version questions love: if the price of mobile data rises, streaming on the move gets dearer to enjoy, and the demand curve for subscriptions shifts to the left. The rule: a rise in the price of a complement shifts demand for its partner to the left; a fall shifts it to the right.

Notice the tidy symmetry worth two easy marks: for substitutes, the related good's price and the demand for our good move in the same direction; for complements, they move in opposite directions.

Work this section's questions now: substitutes and complements identified, each shift described in words with its direction, and the symmetry rule applied to a pair you have never seen before.

## Subtopic: Product Markets and Factor Markets — the Loop That Connects Shops to Jobs

The second relationship is larger: whole categories of markets feeding one another. Product markets are where goods and services are sold — the shops, the online stores, the service counters. Factor markets are where the factors of production are hired — the labour market where wages are settled, the capital market where loans are priced, the property market where rent is negotiated.

The bridge between them is one of the most examinable ideas in Grade 11: derived demand. Nobody hires an electrician out of affection for wiring; the demand for electricians is derived from the demand for working lights and powered homes. Nobody demands truck drivers except because goods must reach buyers. Demand in a factor market is always borrowed from demand in some product market.

Run the loop forward with a South African case. Suppose households across the Western Cape rush to install rooftop solar after years of power cuts. In the product market, installation prices firm up and solar firms book out for months. That surge pours straight into the factor markets: firms demand more electricians, more panels and inverters, more finance. Wages for accredited installers get bid upward; import orders for panels climb; banks price a wave of home-improvement loans. The product-market boom became factor-market demand.

The loop runs backward too. Those higher wages and pricier components are production costs, and rising costs shift supply curves in product markets to the left — nudging installation prices higher still, or cooling the boom. And recall the circular flow from Term 1: the wages paid in factor markets become household income, which returns as spending in product markets. Tills and pay packets are not two separate worlds; they are two halves of one loop, and a shock to either half travels the full circle.

For exam purposes, own both directions in a sentence each. Forward: stronger product demand raises derived demand for factors, lifting wages and factor prices. Backward: higher factor prices raise costs, shifting product supply leftward and lifting product prices. Drill each direction until it takes you five seconds.

Attend to this section's questions before the final piece: derived demand defined and illustrated, the forward loop traced from solar roofs to electricians, and the backward loop traced from wages to prices.

## Subtopic: The Four Market Structures — a First Map

The third relationship asks a different question: inside a single market, how many sellers are there, and how much grip does each have on the price? The answer files every market in the economy under one of four structures. Grade 12 will spend a year on their graphs; today you master the map.

Structure one: perfect competition. Very many small sellers, identical products, buyers and sellers fully informed, and no barriers to entry — newcomers walk in freely. No single seller can bend the price; each one is a price taker. Fresh-produce markets come close: one cabbage farmer among hundreds sells at the going price or not at all.

Structure two: monopolistic competition. Still many sellers and easy entry, but now the products are differentiated — similar, never identical. Every coffee shop, takeaway and hair salon offers its own version, so each seller holds a small degree of price power built on taste, loyalty and location — but rivals stand close, and the power stays small.

Structure three: oligopoly. A few large sellers dominate and entry is hard — think of South Africa's big supermarket groups, its mobile networks, or its domestic airlines. The defining feature is interdependence: each firm must predict its rivals' reactions before touching a price, which is why oligopoly prices often sit still while the fighting moves to advertising, loyalty programmes and specials. The standing temptation is collusion — agreeing not to compete — which is illegal and policed by the Competition Commission; the bid-rigging uncovered among construction firms around the twenty-ten stadium projects remains a famous South African example.

Structure four: monopoly. One seller, a product with no close substitute, and entry blocked — by law, by licence, by ownership of a resource, or by the sheer cost of duplicating a network. The freight-rail network is the standard local example: no rival is going to lay a second set of tracks across the country. A monopolist is a price maker: it can set price by choosing how much to supply, though even a monopolist is disciplined by what buyers can afford — and by the regulator.

The classification line worth memorising: perfect competition is the only perfect market; the other three are imperfect markets, because their sellers hold some power over price. And the two questions that file any market in seconds: how many sellers, and how hard is the door to enter?

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four structures ranked by seller numbers and entry, each matched to a South African example, and perfect separated from imperfect with the reason stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three relationships from ground level: a supermarket shelf, a rooftop full of solar panels, and the difference between a farmers' market and the pipes under your street.

## Subtopic: The Shelf Decides — Rice, Maize Meal and the Compared Price

Stand in the staple-foods aisle with two hundred rand and eavesdrop on your own mind.

You never ask "is rice expensive?" floating free. You ask "is rice expensive compared to maize meal?" If rice is thirty rand a kilo and maize meal is fifteen, the maize meal is the value play. That compared price — one good measured against the other — is what economists call the relative price, and it is the only price your feet actually obey.

Test it. If every tag in the shop doubled tonight, and your pay doubled too, tomorrow's shopping would feel surreal, but your basket would land the same — nothing became cheaper compared to anything else. But if only rice doubles while maize meal sits still, maize meal suddenly reads like a bargain even though its tag never moved. Cheaper compared-to is what pulls the trolley.

Now name the two kinds of teammates goods can be. Rice and maize meal do the same job — filling the pot with starch — so they are substitutes: one can step in for the other. Bread and polony do their job together — one school sandwich needs both — so they are complements: partners, not rivals.

And here is how one market whispers to another. A poor harvest shoves maize meal up to twenty-five rand. In the rice aisle something odd happens: rice's own price has not moved, yet more shoppers reach for rice this week than last. Picture rice's demand curve — price up the side, quantity along the bottom, sloping down. It does not slide along itself; the entire curve steps to the right, because at every rice price there are now more willing buyers. Rival's price up, my demand up — the substitute signature.

Partners pull the other way. If bread turns pricey, fewer sandwiches get made, and polony sales sag even though polony's tag never changed — polony's whole demand curve steps to the left. Partner's price up, my demand down.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for each pair, ask first: same job, or job done together? Rivals move together; partners move apart.

## Subtopic: Every Job Is Hiding Inside Something Somebody Buys

Here is a question that sounds philosophical but is pure economics: why does an electrician have a job?

Not because the world loves wiring. An electrician has a job because somebody, somewhere, wants the lights to stay on. The want for working power comes first; the want for the electrician is borrowed from it. Economists say the demand for labour is derived — second-hand demand, handed down from the product to the people and materials that deliver it. Every job you can name is hiding inside something somebody buys: the delivery driver inside your online order, the seamstress inside a school uniform, the miner inside the battery of a phone sold on another continent.

Once you see that, you can watch tills and jobs tug each other like two children holding hands. Suppose the Western Cape goes on a solar spree. First the product market heats: installers book out for months and installation prices firm up. Then — automatically — the factor markets heat too: roofs need accredited electricians, so their wages get bid up; systems need panels and inverters, so import orders surge; households need finance, so banks write more loans. Nobody organised that second wave. Demand for sunshine on tap became demand for hands, boxes and loans all on its own.

But the hands pull back. Those higher wages and pricier inverters are costs to the installers, and higher costs make firms less eager to supply at any given price — which nudges installation prices up further. And there is a third pull, the one from the circular flow: the electrician's fatter pay packet goes home and becomes spending at the shops, warming product markets all over town.

So when the news says "solar is booming" or "a factory is closing", you now hear it properly: product markets and factor markets are two halves of one loop. Whatever happens to the things people buy happens next to the people who make them.

Take this section's questions now — each one asks you to find the product hiding behind a job, or the jobs hiding behind a product.

## Subtopic: Count the Sellers — From the Market Stalls to the Water Pipes

Last relationship: how the number of sellers in a market decides who gets to write the price. Walk through four South African scenes, counting sellers as you go.

Scene one: a Saturday farmers' market with forty spinach sellers, all selling the same spinach. Try asking five rand more than the stall beside you and watch your customers drift three steps sideways. Many sellers, identical goods, nothing stopping newcomers from setting up a table — nobody controls the price; everybody takes it. That is perfect competition, and it is the only market we call perfect.

Scene two: the hair salons along the main road. Many sellers again, and opening another is easy — but each one is a little different: this one's fades are famous, that one takes walk-ins, the other has the comfortable chairs. Each salon can charge a little more than its neighbours on the strength of that difference, but only a little. Many sellers, similar-but-not-identical services: monopolistic competition.

Scene three: the airlines on the domestic routes. Count the sellers: a handful. Launching a new airline swallows billions, so challengers rarely appear. And watch how the few behave — before one touches a fare, it studies how the others will answer, each watching the rest like players around a poker table. Few sellers, hard entry, everyone watching everyone: oligopoly. The dangerous shortcut is agreeing not to compete — collusion — which is illegal, and the Competition Commission exists precisely to catch it.

Scene four: the water pipes under your street. One seller. No substitute you can switch to, and no rival will ever dig a second network past your gate. One seller, blocked entry: monopoly — the price maker, which is exactly why monopolies get watched and regulated.

Four scenes, one ladder: as sellers grow fewer and the entry door grows heavier, price power grows — from taking the price, to nudging it, to watching rivals, to making it. Every market you will ever meet stands on one rung of that ladder.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: count the sellers, test the entry door, place the market on its rung — and keep rivals-move-together, partners-move-apart in your pocket for the first two.
