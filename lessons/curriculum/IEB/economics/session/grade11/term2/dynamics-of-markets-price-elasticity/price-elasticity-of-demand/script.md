# Part 1 — Expert

This session upgrades the law of demand from a direction into a measurement: price elasticity of demand. The law promises that when price rises, quantity demanded falls. Elasticity says by how much — and that "how much" is the difference between a spaza that prices boldly and one that prices itself out of business, the reason the Budget leans on cigarettes and fuel rather than on maize meal, and a dependable source of marks in your final exams.

## Subtopic: The Elasticity Number — Definition and Calculation

Price elasticity of demand measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a change in the good's own price. In words, the formula reads: elasticity equals the percentage change in quantity demanded, divided by the percentage change in price. Both changes expressed as percentages — that is what lets us compare a bus ticket fairly against a bag of oranges or a luxury watch.

Master the percentage-change step before anything else, because that is where most marks go to die. Percentage change equals the change, divided by the original value, times one hundred. The original value — the number things started at — always sits on the bottom.

Now a complete worked example, taken slowly. A commuter bus company raises its monthly pass from five hundred rand to five hundred and seventy-five rand. Monthly passengers slip from two thousand to one thousand eight hundred and eighty. Step one, the price change: seventy-five rand on an original five hundred is seventy-five over five hundred times one hundred — a fifteen percent increase. Step two, the quantity change: one hundred and twenty fewer passengers on an original two thousand is one hundred and twenty over two thousand times one hundred — a six percent decrease. Step three, divide: six percent over fifteen percent gives zero comma four. The passengers barely budged: a hefty price push produced a modest quantity response.

One convention before we interpret. Demand slopes downward, so one of the two changes is always negative — strictly the answer is minus zero comma four. Economists routinely drop the minus sign and work with the absolute value, because what matters is the size of the response — and the polished answer says so explicitly rather than quietly deleting the sign.

A second example for contrast. A neighbourhood car wash raises its full wash from sixty rand to seventy-five rand — a twenty-five percent increase. Weekly washes tumble from two hundred and forty to one hundred and forty-four — a forty percent decrease. Forty over twenty-five: elasticity one comma six. A price push of similar spirit, but the customers scattered.

Two services, two numbers, one scale: zero comma four against one comma six. The rest of this lesson teaches you to read that scale fluently.

## Subtopic: Reading the Number — Five Categories and Their Graphs in Words

The elasticity scale pivots on the number one, and every demand curve you will ever meet lands in one of five zones.

Elasticity above one: elastic demand. Buyers respond more than proportionally — our car wash at one comma six. These are goods and services people can postpone, replace or do themselves. Picture the graph: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal; an elastic demand curve runs relatively flat, because a small vertical dip in price launches a long horizontal stride in quantity.

Elasticity below one: inelastic demand. Buyers respond less than proportionally — our bus pass at zero comma four. Goods people feel they cannot do without. The curve stands relatively steep: even a tall vertical jump in price wins only a short horizontal shuffle in quantity.

Elasticity exactly one: unitary elasticity. A ten percent price rise brings precisely a ten percent quantity fall — the boundary case, and, as the revenue section will prove, the point where total revenue holds perfectly still.

Then the two textbook extremes. Perfectly inelastic demand, elasticity of zero: quantity refuses to move at any price. The curve is a vertical line rising straight off the quantity axis — the identical quantity bought whatever the price. Insulin for a diabetic patient comes close. Perfectly elastic demand, elasticity of infinity: at one particular price buyers absorb any amount offered, but nudge the price up one cent and quantity collapses to nothing. The curve is a horizontal line lying flat at that price — and you have already met it as the demand line facing one small maize farmer delivering to the silo in a perfectly competitive market.

Now a warning that earns marks: slope and elasticity are related but not identical. Elasticity changes as you travel along a straight demand curve — elastic near the top where prices are high and quantities small, inelastic near the bottom. So never declare "this curve is elastic" from its shape alone; say "relatively elastic", or better, calculate. Steep-suggests-inelastic and flat-suggests-elastic is a sketching habit, not a theorem.

## Subtopic: What Makes Demand Elastic or Inelastic — the Determinants

Why does bus demand hold firm at zero comma four while car-wash demand bolts at one comma six? Five determinants settle it, and questions return to them constantly.

First, and towering over the rest: the availability of substitutes. Where close alternatives wait nearby, buyers slip away from a price rise easily, so demand is elastic — one brand of instant noodles among six on the shelf is hyper-elastic. Where nothing else does the job — a chronic medicine with no generic equivalent — demand is inelastic. Almost every other determinant is this one wearing a disguise.

Second: necessity versus luxury. Necessities — maize meal, the commute to work, electricity for the fridge — keep being bought through price rises: inelastic. Luxuries — restaurant sushi, imported sneakers, a professional car wash when a bucket waits at home — get postponed or dropped: elastic.

Third: the share of income the good claims. Goods that nibble the budget — a box of candles, a packet of salt — are not worth re-planning a life over: inelastic. Goods that swallow a serious slice — rent, school fees, a vehicle — force a response: elastic.

Fourth: time. In the month after an electricity tariff hike, households mostly grit their teeth and pay — inelastic in the short run. Give them two years and geysers go onto timers, LED bulbs replace old ones, and solar panels sprout on roofs: demand grows more elastic as adjustment time lengthens.

Fifth: habit and addiction. Goods that grip their users — cigarettes, alcohol, arguably mobile data — keep their buyers through price rise after price rise: inelastic. Which is exactly why the Budget returns to so-called sin taxes year after year: most smokers keep smoking, so the revenue keeps arriving.

Worth one more line: how broadly the good is defined. Protein as a whole is deeply inelastic — households must eat — but one particular brand of tinned fish is highly elastic. The narrower the definition, the more substitutes surround it, the higher the elasticity climbs.

For essay answers, never just list these — deploy them in pairs: name the determinant, then bolt the good in the question onto it with a because-sentence.

## Subtopic: Elasticity and Total Revenue — the Payoff

Here is why firms and the Treasury care about this number. Total revenue is price times quantity, and a price change drags those two in opposite directions: lift the price and each sale earns more, but there are fewer sales. Which force wins? Elasticity referees.

Run the bus numbers. Before: five hundred rand times two thousand passengers — one million rand a month. After the increase: five hundred and seventy-five rand times one thousand eight hundred and eighty — one million and eighty-one thousand rand. Revenue climbed by eighty-one thousand rand. With inelastic demand, the passengers who stay pay so much more that they outweigh the few who leave: price up, revenue up.

Now the car wash. Before: sixty rand times two hundred and forty washes — fourteen thousand four hundred rand a week. After: seventy-five rand times one hundred and forty-four — ten thousand eight hundred rand. Revenue shrank by three thousand six hundred rand. With elastic demand, the customers who flee outweigh the richer price on each wash: price up, revenue down.

The rulebook, three lines worth engraving. When demand is inelastic, price and total revenue travel in the same direction — so a price rise grows revenue. When demand is elastic, price and total revenue travel in opposite directions — so a price cut grows revenue. When elasticity sits exactly at one, small price changes leave revenue untouched — revenue is at its summit.

This rulebook explains pricing everywhere you look. Prepaid electricity tariffs climb because household demand is inelastic. Airlines slash off-peak fares because leisure travel is elastic — the cheap seats fill faster than the discount hurts. The Minister of Finance taxes cigarettes, alcohol and fuel because inelastic demand means the tax collects reliably instead of killing the market — while the same logic explains why VAT stays off staple foods: taxing the necessities of low-income households would squeeze hardest exactly where nobody can cut back.

One closing caution: revenue is not profit. More sales also cost more to supply, and the cost-and-revenue machinery from earlier this term handles that side. Elasticity answers the revenue question — and that is precisely the question it gets asked with.

## Subtopic: The Shrug Test — Ouch Alone Is Not the Question

Here is the day-one mistake nearly everyone makes: assuming elasticity asks "does the price rise hurt?" It does not. It asks: when the price rises, what do buyers actually do?

Watch two price rises in the same neighbourhood. Prepaid electricity goes up. Everyone groans, the group chats catch fire — and almost every household loads the same units next week, because the fridge and the lights are not optional. Loud ouch, tiny change in behaviour. Now the corner shop lifts the price of energy drinks. Far less noise — but hands quietly reach for the cheaper brand, or for water, and sales sag fast. Quiet ouch, big change in behaviour.

Economists ignore the groaning and measure the movement. Demand that barely shifts when price rises is inelastic — like a stone: push it and it sits there. Demand that shifts a lot is elastic — like a feather: the same push and it sails across the room. Electricity is a stone. Energy-drink brands are feathers.

To turn it into a number, compare two percentages: by what percent did the price move, and by what percent did the buying move? Divide the buying-change by the price-change. An answer below one means buyers moved less than the price did — stone, inelastic. Above one — feather, elastic. Exactly one is the balance point.

Test it. Price up fifteen percent, buying down six percent: six over fifteen is zero comma four — a proper stone. Price up twenty-five percent, buying down forty percent: forty over twenty-five is one comma six — a feather in a breeze. And one habit guards the whole calculation: percentage change always divides by the original number, the number things started at.

## Subtopic: Why Maize Meal Is a Stone and Ice Lollies Are Feathers

Why do goods split this way? Ask four everyday questions about any good, and its elasticity explains itself.

Question one: is there a way out? This is the big one. When one brand of ice lolly gets pricey, the way out is right there in the freezer — another brand, or frozen juice made at home. Ways out everywhere: feather. When the price of maize meal rises and pap is what fills the pot every evening — no real way out: stone. Nearly everything else circles back to this question of substitutes.

Question two: must-have or nice-to-have? Maize meal, the trip to work, electricity for the fridge — must-haves stay bought when prices climb: stone. Ice lollies, takeaways, a new phone cover — nice-to-haves wait for another day: feather.

Question three: how big a bite of the budget? A box of matches doubling in price steals a rand — nobody re-plans the month: stone. School transport going up ten percent moves real money — every family responds somehow: the bigger the bite, the bigger the reaction.

Question four: how much time have buyers had? The month a tariff jumps, everyone simply pays — stone for now. Two years later, timers sit on geysers and panels sit on roofs — the feather emerges slowly. Time turns stones into feathers.

And one sneaky extra: habits hold people in place. Cigarettes keep selling through every price rise because the addiction blocks the way out from the inside. That is exactly why tobacco and alcohol duties rise in the Budget year after year — the buyers stay, so the money comes.

Run any good through the four questions — way out, must-have, budget bite, time — and you can call stone or feather before your calculator wakes up. That prediction, written in because-sentences, is precisely what essay questions reward.

## Subtopic: The Shopkeeper's Dilemma — When Raising the Price Loses You Money

Now the payoff, through one week at Zanele's spaza near the school. She sells maize meal and ice lollies, and she wants more revenue — more total money through the tin. Price times quantity. Her instinct says: raise prices. Her instinct is exactly half right.

Monday, she lifts the maize meal price ten percent. Customers mutter — but pap is supper and the way out does not exist: a stone. Almost nobody stops buying. She now earns ten percent more on nearly every bag: the tin fills faster. For stone goods, price up means revenue up.

Thursday, feeling brave, she lifts the ice lollies from ten rand to twelve. Ice lollies are a treat with ways out in every direction — the other brand, the garage shop, cold water from the tap: a feather. Sales drop from three hundred a week to two hundred and ten. Count the tin: it was ten rand times three hundred — three thousand rand. Now twelve rand times two hundred and ten — two thousand five hundred and twenty. She raised the price and lost four hundred and eighty rand. For feather goods, price up means revenue down — and, run backwards, a price cut on a feather good can grow revenue, because the rush of new buyers outweighs the discount on each one.

That is the entire rulebook in one shop. Stone: push price up, revenue follows. Feather: pull price down, revenue follows. Balance point at one: revenue stands still.

Once the rulebook is yours, you see it operating everywhere. Prepaid electricity priced firmly because households must keep the lights on. Winter ranges slashed at end of season because fashion is pure feather. The Budget taxing cigarettes, alcohol and fuel — stone, stone, stone — because the buyers stay and the revenue lands, and the mirror-image logic keeping VAT off staple foods: taxing the poor's stones would press hardest exactly where nobody can let go.

One final honesty check: the tin holds revenue, not profit — costs still wait offstage, and earlier this term you built their machinery.
