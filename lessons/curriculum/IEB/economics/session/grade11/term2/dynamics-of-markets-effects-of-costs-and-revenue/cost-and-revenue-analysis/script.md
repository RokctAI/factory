# Part 1 — Expert

This session assembles the toolkit that every market-structure discussion in Grade 12 will stand on: cost and revenue analysis. We begin with nothing but a table of output and total cost, tease out the average and marginal cost hiding inside it, construct the revenue side at a market price of R40, and then settle the single question that keeps every business owner awake: how many units should I make if I want the largest possible profit?

## Subtopic: The Cost Table and the Three Cost Concepts

Here is the cost schedule for our small firm, for output levels zero up to six units. Total cost: at zero units, R24. At one unit, R44. At two units, R58. At three units, R70. At four units, R88. At five units, R120. At six units, R166.

Pause on the very first row, because it carries a lesson all by itself. The firm has produced nothing at all, and yet R24 has already been spent. Why? Because some bills do not care whether production happens — the workshop rent, the insurance premium, the alarm-company contract. That R24 at zero output is the fixed cost, and it sits buried inside every total further down the column. Everything above R24 in any later row is variable cost — the spending that only happens because units are being made: materials, casual labour, the electricity that drives the machines.

From the totals we derive two working measures. Average cost is total cost divided by output — what each unit costs when the whole bill is shared out. Row by row: one unit gives 44 divided by 1, which is R44. Two units: 58 over 2 is R29. Three units: 70 over 3 is about R23,33. Four units: 88 over 4 is R22. Five units: 120 over 5 is R24. Six units: 166 over 6 is about R27,67. Track the pattern: average cost slides down, touches bottom at four units, then turns upward — the classic U-shape. Early on, the R24 of fixed cost is being shared among more and more units, thinning the slice each unit carries; later, the firm pushes past its comfortable capacity and costs bite back.

Marginal cost is a different creature, and it will run this whole lesson: the extra cost of making one more unit. You uncover it by subtracting neighbouring totals. From zero to one unit: 44 minus 24 is R20. One to two: 58 minus 44 is R14. Two to three: 70 minus 58 is R12. Three to four: 88 minus 70 is R18. Four to five: 120 minus 88 is R32. Five to six: 166 minus 120 is R46. Marginal cost sags at first, then climbs hard.

The classic error is using division where subtraction belongs. Average answers "what has each unit cost so far?" — so you divide. Marginal answers "what did the newest unit add?" — so you subtract. Lock in the right verb for each and most of the topic's traps lose their teeth.

The questions for this section are with you now: fixed cost read straight off the zero-output row, average cost found by division, and marginal cost found by subtracting neighbouring totals.

## Subtopic: Building the Revenue Side at a Market Price of R40

Now for the income column. Our firm sells into a competitive market at the going price of R40 per unit. It is a price taker — no decision it makes can budge that R40.

Total revenue is simply price multiplied by quantity. Zero units: R0. One unit: R40. Two: R80. Three: R120. Four: R160. Five: R200. Six: R240. The column climbs in identical steps of R40.

Average revenue is total revenue divided by output. One unit: 40 over 1 is R40. Two units: 80 over 2 is R40. Three units: 120 over 3 — R40 once more. Notice what is happening: average revenue simply *is* the price. It cannot be anything else — the money earned per unit sold is exactly the price each unit sold for.

Marginal revenue is the extra income from selling one additional unit — found by subtracting neighbouring totals, the same move we used on the cost side. From one unit to two: 80 minus 40 is R40. Two to three: 120 minus 80 is R40. Every single step: R40. For a price-taking firm, marginal revenue equals the price, because each extra unit sells at the unchanged R40 without disturbing the market in the slightest.

So the whole revenue side is wonderfully tidy: total revenue rises by R40 per unit, while average revenue and marginal revenue lie flat together at R40. Drawn on a graph, that flat line at R40 is the demand line the firm faces — horizontal, the fingerprint of perfect competition. In Grade 12 you will meet the monopolist, whose marginal revenue slides downhill — but the *method* you are building today transfers without a single change.

Attend to this section's questions before we make the big decision: total revenue at each output, average revenue recognised as nothing but the price, and marginal revenue locked flat at R40 for a price taker.

## Subtopic: Finding the Profit-Maximising Output

Time to decide. The firm chases maximum profit, and profit is total revenue minus total cost. Start with the blunt instrument — compute profit at every single output — because seeing the full landscape earns your trust for the sharper method that follows.

Zero units: 0 minus 24 — a loss of R24. One unit: 40 minus 44 — a loss of R4. Two units: 80 minus 58 — profit R22. Three units: 120 minus 70 — profit R50. Four units: 160 minus 88 — profit R72. Five units: 200 minus 120 — profit R80. Six units: 240 minus 166 — profit R74.

Read the profit column top to bottom: minus 24, minus 4, 22, 50, 72, 80, 74. It rises, crests at five units with R80, and then slips. Answer: the profit-maximising output is five units.

But the economist has a faster, deeper route — the one exam answers are built from. Reason unit by unit, at the margin. Each additional unit brings in marginal revenue of R40. Each additional unit costs its own marginal cost. The rule: make every unit whose marginal revenue covers its marginal cost, and refuse the first unit where marginal cost climbs above marginal revenue.

March through the table. Unit one: earns R40, costs R20 — make it, R20 to the good. Unit two: R40 against R14 — make it, R26 up. Unit three: R40 against R12 — make it. Unit four: R40 against R18 — make it. Unit five: R40 against R32 — still R8 ahead — make it. Unit six: R40 against R46 — this unit *wipes out* R6 of profit. Turn it down.

Halt at five — exactly where the brute-force column peaked. The rule "produce until marginal revenue equals marginal cost" — MR equals MC — delivered the answer without a single profit calculation. That is why the rule carries the marks: it proves you understand *why* five wins, not merely that it does.

This section's questions are in front of you now — the profit column read properly, the unit-by-unit march completed, and the sixth unit rejected with a reason instead of a feeling.

## Subtopic: Verifying, Graphing and the Habits That Earn Marks

Verify from both directions, the way a strong exam answer always does. At five units, did the last unit earn its place? Marginal cost R32, marginal revenue R40 — yes, it added R8 of profit. Would a sixth unit pay its way? Marginal cost R46 against R40 — no, it would burn R6 of profit. Confirmed from both sides: five units. And the profit there: total revenue R200 minus total cost R120 gives R80 — exactly what the profit column showed. Two independent methods, one answer — that is when you trust it.

Understand precisely *why* unit six is refused even though six units still deliver R74 of profit. R74 is genuine profit — but it is R6 *below* R80. Maximising profit means capturing every unit that pays for itself and not one unit beyond. The test is never "is the firm still profitable overall?"; the test is "does the *next unit* pay?"

When a graph is asked for, put output on the horizontal axis and rands on the vertical. Marginal cost draws its hook shape — falling from 20 through 14 to 12, then rising through 18, 32 and 46. Marginal revenue lies flat at R40. They cross between five and six units, marking the profit-maximising zone — and the answer is read as the last whole unit before marginal cost breaks through the flat line: five.

Your self-check routine for any cost-revenue table, in five steps. One: total cost at zero output must equal fixed cost. Two: average is total divided by quantity — *divide*; marginal is the change between neighbouring totals — *subtract*. Three: at a given market price, average revenue and marginal revenue both equal that price. Four: find where marginal cost cuts up through marginal revenue and stop just before it. Five: confirm with the profit column when time allows.

Own this table now. Next year, perfect markets, monopolies and oligopolies will all be analysed through these same six columns — only the revenue side changes shape. The method stays yours permanently.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the answer defended from both directions, the marginal cost hook drawn against the flat revenue line, and the five-step self-check run on an unfamiliar table.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same six columns, but standing behind a boerewors-roll stand at a Saturday flea market — a skottel, a cooler box, and one honest question asked again and again.

## Subtopic: Costs That Sleep and Costs That Work

Picture the stand. Before the first roll touches the grill, money has already walked out of your pocket: the stall fee for the day, the gazebo hire, the gas bottle for the skottel. Those amounts are owed whether you sell out by noon or stand in the rain selling nothing. In our firm's table, that sleeping money is R24 — check the row where output is zero yet total cost reads R24. Nothing made, R24 gone. Those are the fixed costs.

Then come the costs that only wake up when you start cooking: rolls, wors, onions, tomato relish, and the friend you pay to help when the queue stretches. Sell more, spend more. Those are the variable costs, and they are why the total climbs as output climbs — from R24 at zero, through R44, R58, R70, R88 and R120, to R166 at six units.

Now hold two questions apart, because the whole topic balances on the gap between them.

Question one: "what is each one costing me on average?" You answer by sharing the full bill across everything made — you divide. At three units, total cost is R70, so R70 shared across three is about R23,33 each. At four units, R88 over four is R22 each. At five, R120 over five is R24. See how it dips and then lifts again — and the reason is homely: the sleeping R24 gets carved into more and more slices, so each roll carries a thinner sliver of it — until the pace strains you, the helper works overtime and the gas runs hot, and the numbers push back up.

Question two is a different question entirely: "what did the last one cost me?" Not the average — only the newest one, alone. You answer by subtracting: this row's total minus the previous row's. From three units to four, total cost moves from R70 to R88, so the fourth unit cost R18 on its own. The fifth: R120 minus R88 is R32. The sixth: R166 minus R120 is R46. That is marginal cost, dipping early and then climbing steeply as the stand gets stretched thin.

Two questions, two verbs. Average speaks about everything made so far — divide. Marginal speaks about the latest one only — subtract. Swap the verbs and every number downstream is poisoned.

Your questions for this section are with you now — before touching the calculator, decide whether each one asks about everything so far or only about the latest unit, and the verb chooses itself.

## Subtopic: Forty Rand a Roll, Every Single Roll

The income side is the easy side, and there is a real reason it is easy.

Our stand sells at the price the whole market has settled on: R40 a roll. It is one small stand among a row of them, so charging R55 would empty the queue and charging R30 would be charity — the price is simply what the market pays. Economists call the stand a price taker.

That makes the takings pure multiplication. One roll, R40. Two, R80. Three, R120. Four, R160. Five, R200. Six, R240. Total revenue, stepping up evenly.

Ask our two questions on this side of the table. What does each roll bring in on average? Divide: R120 over three rolls is R40. R200 over five is R40. Always R40 — and it could never be otherwise, because income per roll is nothing but the price of a roll. Average revenue is the price.

And what did the latest roll bring in? Subtract: R80 minus R40 is R40; R120 minus R80 is R40. Again R40, at every step. Marginal revenue is the price too, because one extra roll sold at this stand does not drag the market price down for anyone.

Turn the money side into a picture. Rolls along the bottom, rands up the side. Total revenue is a straight line rising steadily out of the corner, gaining R40 with every roll. Average revenue and marginal revenue lie together as a single flat line at the R40 mark, level from end to end. That flat line is the tell-tale sign of a small firm in a competitive market — and next year, when you meet the monopoly that must cut its price to sell more, and its marginal revenue line tilts downhill, today's flat line will feel like a luxury.

Take this section's questions now — and make sure you can explain, in your own words, why both R40 lines lie flat before moving on.

## Subtopic: Is the Next One Worth Making?

Here comes the decision — one you already make in daily life without noticing.

The blunt route first: profit at every level, money in minus money out. Zero rolls: nothing in, R24 out — a loss of R24. One roll: R40 in, R44 out — a loss of R4. Two: R80 against R58 — R22 profit. Three: R120 against R70 — R50. Four: R160 against R88 — R72. Five: R200 against R120 — R80. Six: R240 against R166 — R74. Profit climbs, peaks at five rolls with R80, then sags.

Now the smarter route — really just how you weigh one more taxi trip or one more slice of cake. Interrogate each roll on its own: is this one worth making? Every roll brings in R40. Each roll costs what it costs.

Roll one brings R40 and costs R20 — make it, R20 up. Roll two brings R40 and costs R14 — make it. Roll three brings R40 and costs R12 — make it. Roll four brings R40 and costs R18 — make it. Roll five brings R40 and costs R32 — still R8 up, so make it. Roll six brings R40 and costs R46 — that roll takes R6 straight out of your pocket. Refuse it.

Stop at five — precisely where the blunt route peaked, and you never touched the profit column. Economists compress it into "produce until marginal revenue equals marginal cost", but underneath it is only this: keep going while the next one pays, and stop the moment it does not.

Watch the trap, because this is where good answers become excellent ones. At six rolls the stand still banks R74 — real, positive money. But R74 is R6 short of R80. "Am I making money?" is the wrong question. "Does the next one make money?" is the right one.

Picture this side as well. Rolls along the bottom, rands up the side. The marginal cost line is not straight: it slides from R20 down to R12 by the third roll, then bends sharply upward through R18, R32 and R46 — a lopsided hook, low in the middle, steep on the right. The marginal revenue line lies flat across it at R40. They cross between the fifth and sixth rolls, and the answer is the last whole roll before the cost line punches up through R40 — the fifth.

And here come the final questions of the lesson: divide or subtract, flat lines or hooked ones, and the single question a firm asks of every next unit. Master these and next year's market structures will only ever redraw the revenue side — the method never changes.
