**Bianca:** Class questions before we wrap up! The one I got most often today: our figures showed inventories FALLING by sixty billion, and it was subtracted. But surely selling stock is good for the economy — why does it count against GNE?

**Tutor:** Because GNE is trying to measure spending on what the economy produced THIS year, and goods sold off a shelf were produced in an earlier year. Picture a furniture shop that starts January with two hundred couches in the storeroom and ends December with one hundred and fifty. Customers bought couches all year — that spending is in C at full value. But fifty of those couches were not made this year; they were made last year and merely stored. If we counted the customers' full spending and made no correction, this year's production would look bigger than it was. Subtracting the inventory rundown removes exactly those fifty old couches from the total. The mirror case works too: when firms build stock UP, workers and machines genuinely produced goods this year that no customer has bought yet, so the accounts add the increase — the firm is treated as having bought its own output. So the sign is not a moral judgement about whether destocking is good or bad for business. It is bookkeeping that keeps one question honest: how much was produced in these twelve months? Rising stock, add. Falling stock, subtract. Say it that way and you will never lose the sign again.

**Bianca:** Next one: a learner's cousin works in Dubai and sends money home every month. Someone claimed those remittances are why GNI differs from GDP. Is that right?

**Tutor:** It is close enough to be dangerous, so let us make it precise. The bridge between GDP and GNI is PRIMARY income — payment for supplying factors of production across borders. The cousin's monthly SALARY, earned by a South African resident working abroad, is primary income received, and it genuinely pulls GNI up relative to GDP — as do dividends a Johannesburg pension fund earns on foreign shares, or interest on money lent abroad. Flowing the other way is the primary income paid out: the dividends and interest earned inside South Africa by foreign-owned banks, mines and factories. In our figures, one hundred and ten billion came in, two hundred and ninety billion went out, and the net minus one hundred and eighty billion is why GNI landed at six thousand five hundred and twenty. Now the caution. If the cousin simply GIFTS part of an already-earned salary to the family — and especially if the cousin has lived in Dubai long enough to count as a resident there — that gift is a transfer between households, not payment for a factor of production, and transfers sit in a different part of the accounts entirely. So the accurate sentence is: cross-border wages, interest, dividends and rent move GNI; gifts and family support do not. The learner's cousin might contribute to both, but only through the salary channel does the money touch GNI.

**Bianca:** This one caused a proper argument: if government paying a teacher counts in G, why does government paying a grant not count? Both are just government paying a person.

**Tutor:** The argument dissolves the moment you ask what government RECEIVED, because the accounts record purchases, not payments. When the state pays a teacher, it receives something real in exchange: a month of teaching, delivered to classrooms, and that service is part of national output — it was produced, so it must be counted, and G is where it lands. When the state pays a grant, it receives nothing in exchange. No lesson was taught, no pothole filled, no service rendered. Money moved from taxpayers, through the treasury, to a household — purchasing power changed pockets while production stood still. Recording it as G would claim the economy produced something it did not. But watch what happens next, because the grant does not disappear: the household spends it on bread, paraffin and school shoes, and those are real goods that real firms produced this year, counted in C at that moment. One payment, one appearance in the accounts — just later, and in a different letter. The test you can apply to any government payment in three seconds: name the good or service the state got in return. Can you name it? Then G. Silence? Then it is a transfer, and it waits for the household to spend it.

**Bianca:** Last question, and it is the cry for help: four aggregates, three conversions, one panicking learner. What is the survival method?

**Tutor:** Anchor yourself to one home base and three signposted roads out of it. Home base is GDP at market prices, six thousand seven hundred, because every other aggregate is one trip away from it. Road one is the TRADE road, connecting GDP to GNE: take away net exports and you are back at what residents spent, six thousand six hundred and forty. Road two is the PRICE-TAG road, connecting GDP to GVA: strip off the seven hundred and thirty of product taxes, restore the thirty of subsidies, and you are at the maker's price, six thousand. Road three is the OWNERSHIP road, connecting GDP to GNI: settle the primary income account with the rest of the world, minus one hundred and eighty net, and you are at what residents keep, six thousand five hundred and twenty. Each road answers exactly one question — who bought it, whose price, whose pocket — and no road ever mixes with another. When a question lands, do not reach for a memorised definition; ask which road it is driving on. And build the checking habit into your working: whichever direction you travel, add your answer back the other way and confirm you return to six thousand seven hundred. That reverse trip costs one line and catches nearly every arithmetic slip before it costs you.
