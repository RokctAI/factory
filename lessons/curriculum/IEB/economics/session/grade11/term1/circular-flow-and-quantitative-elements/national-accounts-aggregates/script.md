# Part 1 — Expert

In Grade 10 the circular flow was a diagram: money leaving households, arriving at firms, and coming back as income. In Grade 11 the diagram gets a price on every arrow. This session constructs the expenditure side of the national accounts piece by piece — household consumption C, government consumption G, and gross fixed capital formation I — and then chains together the three headline aggregates: gross value added, gross national expenditure and gross national income, with every conversion worked in full so you can travel the chain in either direction.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Measure One Economy

Begin with the identity on which the whole topic rests. Whatever the economy produces in a year gets sold; whatever gets sold, somebody paid for; and every rand that was paid became income in somebody's hands. So there are three doors into the same room. Count PRODUCTION, count EXPENDITURE, or count INCOME — walk through any door and you must arrive at the same total. That is why the national accounts publish gross domestic product by three separate methods, and why the small mismatch between them is printed openly as a residual instead of being quietly smoothed away.

The production method sums VALUE ADDED: each firm's output minus the intermediate goods and services it bought from other firms. The subtraction is not optional. The steel a car factory buys already contains the income earned at the steel mill; add the full value of the car AND the full value of the steel and you have counted the steel twice. The expenditure method sums all spending on FINAL goods and services. The income method sums the factor incomes — compensation of employees and operating surplus — with consumption of fixed capital added back to lift the net figure to a gross one.

Three word-pairs must be at your fingertips. FINAL versus INTERMEDIATE: it is the buyer's purpose that decides, not the product. Sugar delivered to a cold-drink bottler is intermediate; the same sugar in a family's trolley is final. GROSS versus NET: gross totals still contain consumption of fixed capital — the year's wearing-out of machines, vehicles and buildings — while net totals have removed it, and only net tells you whether productive capacity genuinely grew. DOMESTIC versus NATIONAL: domestic asks WHERE the production happened, inside South Africa's borders regardless of who owns the factory; national asks WHO earns the income, South African residents regardless of where their assets sit.

The questions for this section are with you now — you will be shown a measurement and asked which method it belongs to, and asked to defend the exclusion of intermediate goods in one clean sentence.

## Subtopic: Household Consumption (C) and Government Consumption (G)

FINAL CONSUMPTION EXPENDITURE BY HOUSEHOLDS, the symbol C, is what households spend on goods and services to satisfy their wants directly. It is the giant of the expenditure side — roughly sixty cents of every rand of domestic spending — which is why economists watch monthly retail sales the way a doctor watches a pulse.

You must know its four classes. DURABLE goods survive three years or more of use: cars, lounge suites, washing machines, laptops. SEMI-DURABLE goods wear out sooner: school uniforms, running shoes, curtains, cellphone covers. NON-DURABLE goods are consumed almost at once: maize meal, cooking oil, petrol, prepaid electricity, medicine. SERVICES vanish in the moment of delivery: a haircut, a bus ticket, a data bundle, a term's school fees, an insurance premium. The economics of the split: durable and semi-durable purchases can be delayed, so they are the first casualties when incomes tighten and the first to surge when confidence returns, while non-durables barely move — a household postpones the washing machine, never the maize meal.

What drives C? Disposable income first and always; then household wealth, the interest rate and the price of credit, expectations about jobs and prices, the size and age profile of the population, and the taxes that shrink take-home pay.

FINAL CONSUMPTION EXPENDITURE BY GENERAL GOVERNMENT, the symbol G, is the day-to-day spending of national, provincial and local government on goods and services: the salaries of teachers, doctors, magistrates and traffic officers, plus the textbooks, vaccines, diesel and electricity consumed while public services are delivered.

Two items are forever wrongly stuffed into G, and you must be able to evict both. First, TRANSFER PAYMENTS — social grants, student bursaries, subsidies to households. Government hands over money and receives no good or service in return, so nothing was bought and nothing belongs in G. The rand is counted once, later, inside C, when the receiving household spends it at the shop; recording it in G as well would count the same rand twice. Second, government CAPITAL expenditure — a new library, a new water treatment works, a new commuter rail line. That is the creation of an asset, not current consumption, and it is recorded under I.

Take the questions on this section now — expect to sort real payments into C, G, or neither, and to name the two exclusions without prompting.

## Subtopic: Gross Fixed Capital Formation (I)

GROSS FIXED CAPITAL FORMATION is spending on FIXED capital: assets that will serve in production repeatedly for longer than a year. Learn it in three layers — what it is, what it contains, and why it matters.

The contents: dwellings and other buildings; construction works — highways, railways, bridges, dams, harbours, transmission lines; machinery and equipment; vehicles used in production; computers, software and network infrastructure; and cultivated biological assets — a macadamia orchard, a wine farm's vines, a dairy herd — because a tree or a cow that yields output year after year is working capital equipment in living form. Two boundary cases matter. Buying an existing building or a used machine from someone else adds nothing to the nation's stock of assets — the asset merely changed owners — so it is not capital formation. And the CHANGE IN INVENTORIES — the rise or fall in stocks of inputs and unsold goods — is part of capital formation but is not FIXED, so the accounts show it on its own separate line.

Who carries out the investing? Private business enterprises do the largest share; public corporations — the state-owned electricity, rail, port and water utilities — do the heavy infrastructure; and general government builds the schools, clinics, roads and reservoirs.

Now put figures on gross versus net. Let gross fixed capital formation for the year be one thousand billion rand, while consumption of fixed capital — the depreciation of everything already standing — is six hundred and twenty billion. Net investment is one thousand minus six hundred and twenty: three hundred and eighty billion rand. Only that portion enlarges the economy's productive capacity; the other six hundred and twenty billion merely stood still, replacing what crumbled. An economy whose gross investment slips below its depreciation is eating its own machines.

Why I outranks the other components: it expands productive capacity — the outward march of the production possibility curve from Grade 10; it arms each worker with better tools, lifting productivity; it employs people twice, once in the building and again in the operating; and every firm that uses the new road or the new power line produces more cheaply. Measured against gross domestic product of six thousand seven hundred billion rand, our one thousand billion of investment is about fourteen comma nine per cent — less than half of the roughly thirty per cent of GDP that the National Development Plan holds up as the level South Africa needs to grow quickly.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — definition, composition, and the gross-to-net subtraction, done without a pause.

## Subtopic: GVA, GNE and GNI — the Chain of Conversions

Now bolt the aggregates together. All figures in rand billions.

GROSS NATIONAL EXPENDITURE, GNE, is everything residents spend on final goods and services: C plus G plus I plus the change in inventories. Our economy has C of four thousand two hundred, G of one thousand five hundred, I of one thousand — and inventories that FELL by sixty, because firms sold off stock from their shelves faster than they replaced it. So: four thousand two hundred plus one thousand five hundred is five thousand seven hundred; plus one thousand is six thousand seven hundred; minus sixty is six thousand six hundred and forty billion rand. Note the sign — a rundown of inventories subtracts. Some publications label this identical total gross domestic expenditure, GDE; the two names carry one meaning, so follow whichever label the question uses.

GNE is not yet GDP, because part of residents' spending bought foreign goods, and foreigners bought part of ours. Add exports of two thousand one hundred, subtract imports of two thousand and forty: net exports are plus sixty, and GDP at market prices is six thousand six hundred and forty plus sixty — six thousand seven hundred billion rand. This time the country produced slightly more than its residents spent, and the surplus left as net exports.

GROSS VALUE ADDED, GVA, reaches the same economy from the production side, valued at BASIC prices — what producers actually receive before taxes on products are stacked on top. To convert GVA into GDP at market prices, add taxes on products such as VAT and the fuel levy, and subtract subsidies on products. GVA of six thousand, plus taxes on products of seven hundred and thirty, minus subsidies of thirty, gives six thousand seven hundred — GDP again. Run it backwards just as confidently: six thousand seven hundred minus seven hundred and thirty plus thirty returns six thousand billion rand.

GROSS NATIONAL INCOME, GNI, swaps the question from where to who. Add the primary income South African residents earn from the rest of the world — wages, interest, dividends, rent — and subtract the primary income paid out to non-residents. Received one hundred and ten, paid two hundred and ninety: net primary income is minus one hundred and eighty, so GNI is six thousand seven hundred minus one hundred and eighty — six thousand five hundred and twenty billion rand. South Africa's GNI habitually sits below its GDP, because a meaningful slice of the profits made by firms operating here flows out as dividends and interest to shareholders abroad.

The last questions of this part are with you now — run the chain forwards and backwards, and say in plain words what each of the three conversions is correcting for.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same aggregates, no jargon — built out of a spaza shop's shelf, a bakery's oven and a family's monthly budget, because you have been doing national accounting all your life without the labels.

## Subtopic: The Country's Till Slip

Imagine every purchase made in South Africa in a whole year printing onto one endless till slip. The national accounts are nothing grander than that slip, sorted into piles.

Pile one, towering over the rest, is the households: airtime, taxi fare to school, a bag of oranges, rent, a new washing machine, a pair of takkies. That pile is C, and it is roughly sixty cents of every rand on the slip. Sort inside the pile by lifespan. The washing machine will still be spinning in five years — durable. The takkies will last a season or two — semi-durable. The oranges and the prepaid electricity are gone within days — non-durable. And the taxi ride? The moment you climb out, there is nothing to show for it but having arrived — that is a service. One pattern worth carrying into any data you ever read: in hard times the washing machine gets postponed and the oranges do not, so appliance showrooms go quiet months before food shops do.

Pile two is the state doing its ordinary shopping: the teacher's salary, the clinic's vaccines, the diesel in the ambulance. That is G.

And now the classic mistake. Your gogo's pension grant is NOT on the government pile. Government bought nothing when it paid her — it moved money to her. The moment she buys bread and tea, those purchases land on the household pile, counted once, exactly where they belong. Put the grant in G as well and you have invented spending that never happened. Same warning for the new clinic being built down the road: bricks and cranes are not day-to-day shopping. Building something meant to serve for decades belongs on the next pile.

Questions on the till slip are coming to you now — sort a handful of everyday items into C or G, and make sure the grant does not sneak onto the wrong pile.

## Subtopic: Spending That Builds Tomorrow

Pile three. A township bakery spends one hundred and fifty thousand rand on flour, sixty thousand on packaging, and five hundred thousand on a new industrial oven. One of those purchases is a different kind of thing. Which?

The oven. The flour becomes bread and is gone by Friday; the packaging leaves with the customers. The oven will still be baking in ten years. Anything bought to be USED in production for more than a year — rather than used up — is capital, and the whole country's total of such purchases is gross fixed capital formation, the letter I.

So what sits on this pile nationally? Factories and houses, highways and railway lines, dams and harbours, wind turbines and substations, delivery vans, laptops and fibre cables — and orchards and dairy herds, because a macadamia tree is really a slow machine that manufactures nuts for thirty years.

Two fine-print warnings. If the bakery had bought a used oven from a closing-down café, the country would gain no oven at all — the same oven simply changed kitchens — so second-hand purchases are not capital formation. And respect the word GROSS. The bakery's old oven lost, say, one hundred and eighty thousand rand of its remaining life this year through wear. Spend five hundred thousand on the new one and the bakery's true gain in baking capacity is only three hundred and twenty thousand. Scale that up: the country invests one thousand billion gross, its existing machines and roads wear down by six hundred and twenty billion, so genuine new capacity is three hundred and eighty billion. Invest less than the wear and tear, and a nation is quietly baking with a dying oven.

And here is why this pile is the special one: it is the only spending that makes NEXT year's till slip longer. You drew this in Grade 10 — the production possibility curve shifting outward. This pile is the shove.

Your questions on this pile are up now — pick out the capital item from a list, and turn a gross figure into a net one.

## Subtopic: Three Totals, One Country

Three big abbreviations left, and each one is just a total plus one honest correction.

GNE — gross national expenditure — is the whole till slip added up. Households four thousand two hundred, government one thousand five hundred, the building-things pile one thousand, and then minus sixty, because shops ran their storerooms down this year and selling old stock is not new spending. Total: six thousand six hundred and forty billion rand. That is what the people of this country SPENT.

But the slip is not the same as the factory floor. Foreigners bought two thousand one hundred billion of our goods, and we bought two thousand and forty billion of theirs. Add ours out, take theirs off: six thousand seven hundred billion — what the country actually PRODUCED. This year production beat spending by sixty billion, and that surplus sailed out through the harbours as net exports. A country can also do the opposite — spend more than it makes — and then the gap arrives by ship as a trade deficit.

GVA — gross value added — is the same six thousand seven hundred seen from the producer's side of the counter, before the tax till rings. Producers actually created six thousand billion of value; then seven hundred and thirty billion of VAT and fuel levies were stacked on top and thirty billion of subsidies were peeled off, landing on six thousand seven hundred. Same goods, two price tags — the maker's price and the shelf price.

GNI — gross national income — asks the sharpest question of the three: of everything produced here, how much do South Africans KEEP? Residents earned one hundred and ten billion abroad, while two hundred and ninety billion of profit and interest made here was paid out to foreign owners. Net, one hundred and eighty billion walks out, leaving six thousand five hundred and twenty billion. That gap is the standing rent on all the foreign capital that built mines, malls and cell towers here — capital the country used, and now pays for.

Here come the final questions of the lesson — three totals, three corrections: trade, the tax price tag, and ownership. Master those and every national accounts headline you ever read will unfold into plain language.
