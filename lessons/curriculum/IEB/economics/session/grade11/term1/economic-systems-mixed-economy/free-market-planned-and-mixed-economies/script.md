# Part 1 — Expert

Strip any economy down to its frame and you find the same three decisions being made, somehow, every day: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. An ECONOMIC SYSTEM is the machinery a society builds to make those decisions. This session examines three machines — the free market, the centrally planned economy, and the mixed or dualistic economy — tests each against the three questions, and ends by locating South Africa exactly: mixed by design, dualistic by history.

## Subtopic: The Three Questions and the Test They Set

The three questions grow straight out of scarcity, the Grade 10 foundation: wants without limit, resources with hard limits, so choosing is compulsory.

WHAT should be produced, and how much of each thing? More clinics means fewer stadiums; the resources cannot do both at full scale. HOW should it be produced? With many workers and simple tools, or few workers and expensive machines — a question with real weight in a country where millions are looking for work. FOR WHOM is it produced? Once the goods exist, something must decide who receives them: money, need, merit, or connections.

Beneath every system sits one master variable: who owns and controls the MEANS OF PRODUCTION — the farms, mines, factories, machinery and networks with which all output is made. Ownership is not a technicality; whoever owns the means of production holds the right to decide, and the right to decide is precisely what answers the three questions.

From that root, learn the comparison checklist you will use across all three systems: ownership of the means of production; the co-ordinating mechanism — prices discovered in markets, or targets set in plans; the role of profit; the strength of competition; the breadth of consumer choice; and the size of the state's role.

One distinction guards against a frequent error: an economic system is not a political system. Economies are classified by ownership and co-ordination; political orders by how power is won and held. They often arrive in pairs historically, but they are separate axes, and keeping them separate marks you as precise.

Take this section's questions now — the three questions stated exactly, and ownership of the means of production identified as the dividing line between systems.

## Subtopic: The Free Market Economy

The FREE MARKET system — capitalism — is built on private ownership of the means of production. Its defining features: legally protected private property; freedom of enterprise and of choice; profit as the engine of effort; rivalry among many buyers and sellers; and a state kept small — a referee for contracts, courts and defence, not a player.

What co-ordinates it is the PRICE MECHANISM, and the skill is to narrate it as a process, not to name-drop it. Nobody issues instructions. Suppose households suddenly want more of a good: demand rises, so its price climbs; the higher price fattens profit; fatter profit pulls new producers and resources into the industry; supply grows and the price eases back. In one motion the price has broadcast information — this is scarce — and delivered incentive — making this pays. That double duty is why the process earned the name the invisible hand.

Apply the three questions. WHAT: consumers decide through spending — consumer sovereignty, every rand a ballot. HOW: producers decide, hunting the least-cost mix of labour and machinery. FOR WHOM: income decides — output flows to purchasing power.

Strengths: ruthless efficiency, since producers who waste resources make losses and vanish; innovation, since new products win profit; wide choice; and self-adjustment — the system responds to changed wants overnight, with no committee involved.

Weaknesses, each stated as a working mechanism. INEQUALITY: because "for whom" is settled by ability to pay, a person born with no assets and no schooling receives almost nothing, and the outcome can be efficient and unjust simultaneously. PUBLIC GOODS: nobody can be excluded from a lighthouse or a police patrol, so nobody pays voluntarily, so the pure market barely supplies them. EXTERNALITIES: a smelter that fills the neighbourhood's lungs pays nothing for the harm, so its costs are understated and its output is too large. MARKET POWER: winners absorb rivals, competition thins, and the surviving monopoly raises prices on captive customers. INSTABILITY: markets left alone swing through booms and busts.

The questions for this section are with you now — narrate the price mechanism step by step, and give the free market's answers to all three questions.

## Subtopic: The Centrally Planned Economy

The CENTRALLY PLANNED system — also labelled command, socialist or communist — is built on state ownership of the means of production. Its features: farms, mines and factories held collectively; a central planning authority issuing output targets, typically in multi-year plans; prices set administratively instead of emerging from exchange; production aimed at use rather than profit; and jobs and basic provision guaranteed by the state.

Here one body answers all three questions. WHAT: the planners decide, according to their reading of social need. HOW: the plan decides, assigning each enterprise its quota of workers, machines and materials. FOR WHOM: the state decides, in principle by need, delivering through set wages, rations and free or heavily subsidised housing, schooling and health care.

State the strengths fairly, because they are real. Income and wealth can be spread far more evenly. Unemployment can in principle vanish, since the state employs everyone. Basic needs can be guaranteed to every citizen regardless of earnings. The nation's resources can be swung rapidly behind one goal — the reason planned economies have industrialised at remarkable speed. And nothing is spent on competing brands and duplicated advertising.

Now the weaknesses, again as mechanisms. Deepest is the INFORMATION problem: the wants, costs and local conditions of millions of people are facts no central office can collect, while market prices summarise them automatically and daily — so every plan is built on partial knowledge and is therefore partly wrong. The INCENTIVE problem follows: without profit to win or failure to fear, effort, quality and invention go unrewarded. Then SHORTAGES AND SURPLUSES: administered prices cannot move, so the error signals never fire — queues form for the under-produced good while the over-produced one stacks up in warehouses. Consumer choice shrinks to the plan's menu. And the planning bureaucracy itself consumes resources and corrects its mistakes slowly.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — planned answers versus market answers, question by question, plus one strength and one weakness each carried by its mechanism.

## Subtopic: The Mixed and Dualistic Economy — South Africa

Pure systems exist only in textbooks. Every functioning economy is MIXED: private enterprise and market prices operating alongside state ownership, regulation and provision.

In the mixed economy the three questions get shared answers. WHAT: consumers lead through markets, and government fills the market's blind spots — public goods like roads, courts and policing, and merit goods like basic schooling and clinics that people under-buy when left alone. HOW: firms chase least cost, but inside a fence of rules — labour law, environmental standards, competition law, safety codes. FOR WHOM: the market deals the first hand through earned income, and redistribution then re-deals part of it — progressive income tax, social grants, free basic water and electricity, no-fee schools, public health care.

South Africa is a textbook case. The Constitution protects property and simultaneously entrenches socio-economic rights. Private firms produce most output, while state-owned enterprises run electricity, rail, harbours, broadcasting and post. Regulators referee the markets — the Competition Commission, the National Credit Regulator, and the sector authorities for energy and communications. And the redistribution arm is heavy: progressive taxation finances one of the developing world's largest grant systems.

But a second word is required for South Africa, and Grade 11 must wield it: DUALISTIC. Two economies share one border. The FIRST economy is formal, modern, capital-intensive and plugged into world markets — listed corporations, deep mines, first-rate banks, commercial agriculture, high-skill services. The SECOND economy is informal and survivalist — hawking at taxi ranks, spaza retail, backyard services, subsistence plots, piece work — marked by tiny capital per worker, no written contracts, no formal credit and low productivity.

Do not stop at describing the dualism; explain where it came from, because the causes are structural. Generations of land dispossession and the migrant labour system stripped and concentrated ownership. Apartheid spatial planning settled the majority far from workplaces, so reaching a job devours a big share of a small wage. Schooling was engineered to be unequal, so the certificates the first economy demands were withheld from the second. Add the collateral wall — banks lend against assets, and the assetless cannot borrow — and the two economies reproduce themselves. The country is simultaneously sophisticated and poor, and nearly every policy fight in South Africa, from land reform to township enterprise funding to commuter rail, is at bottom a fight about bridging the two.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the mixed economy's divided answers, the South African institutions behind them, and dualism explained through its causes, not just its symptoms.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now run the whole topic through one Saturday sports tournament at a community hall, where the same three questions get answered twice before lunchtime.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Run the Kitchen

Picture the tournament: eight teams, a crowd of families, and everybody hungry. Somebody has to answer three questions before noon. What food gets made? How — many hands or one big pot? And who gets fed?

Answer one lives at the food stalls along the fence. Nobody organised them. The amagwinya stand sells out before the first whistle; the fruit-cup stand barely moves. Nobody calls a meeting about it — next Saturday there are two amagwinya stands and the fruit cups are half price. The sold-out table sent the message and the pricing did the planning. Spectators with cash eat lekker; the kid without coins just smells the oil. That is the free market: prices talk, profit motivates, and your pocket decides your plate.

Answer two lives in the organisers' tent. The committee decided weeks ago: one giant pot of curry and rice, funded from entry fees, one ladle per person, every player fed, no money asked. Nobody plays hungry — a genuine achievement. But the committee guessed the menu, and if half the players cannot stand curry, they eat curry anyway, every tournament, because no till exists to overrule the committee. That is central planning: fairer plates, blinder cooking.

Answer three is what the tournament actually does: the free meal tent for every player AND the stalls along the fence for everyone else. Both at once. That is a mixed economy — and that, in one sports ground, is South Africa.

Questions on the three kitchens are coming to you now — match each kitchen to its system, and name who decides the menu in each.

## Subtopic: What the Till Knows and the Memo Doesn't

Stay at the tournament, because the deepest idea in this topic is lying on the amagwinya table.

A price is a message. Sold out before the first whistle says, without anyone writing it: make more of these. Fruit cups going warm in the sun says the opposite. Now scale up: every till, every stall, every shop in the country is sending these little messages all day, automatically, free of charge — millions of them, each one a fact about what people want and what is running short.

The organisers' tent gets no messages. The committee must guess what hundreds of mouths want — and a national planning office must guess for millions, product by product, month after month. Some guesses land. Many do not, and here is the trap: when the guess is wrong, the price cannot flag it, because the office fixed the price in advance. So the country queues for the thing the plan under-made while the thing it over-made fills sheds. Not stupidity — just the plain impossibility of one office knowing what a million tills know.

But turn the coin over, and be fair. The till is a superb listener with one deafness: it hears only money. The kid at the fence with empty pockets wants lunch as much as anyone — and transmits nothing. No coins, no signal, no amagwinya cooked for him. The market is not cruel by intention; it is simply unable to detect a want that carries no rand. And that one sentence is why every country on earth pitches a free meal tent somewhere beside its stalls.

Your questions on messages and memos are up now — what a price can do that a plan cannot, and what a plan will do that a price never will.

## Subtopic: Two Economies in One Country

The last idea is the one that explains the country you actually live in.

Monday morning, two workplaces. In Rosebank, a fintech office: standing desks, medical aid, payslips, a pension fund, and a bank that phones to offer credit. Twenty kilometres away at a Tembisa taxi rank, a woman sells fruit and vegetables from a crate: no payslip, no contract, no medical aid, and a total business capital of a few hundred rand restocked daily. If the crate is stolen, the business is over.

One country, one currency, one constitution — two economies. That is dualism: a formal, modern economy and an informal, survivalist one, running in parallel.

The lazy explanation is that one worked harder than the other, and the evidence dismantles it. Trace the actual walls between the crate and the office. Ownership: land and assets were confiscated and concentrated over generations, so one side inherited capital and the other inherited nothing to pledge. Distance: people were settled far from the workplaces, so the taxi eats the wage before the month ends. Schooling: the qualifications the office requires were deliberately kept scarce on one side. Credit: banks lend against collateral, so the woman with no assets cannot borrow the stock money that would let her grow — the wall guards itself. Four walls, all structural, none about effort.

And now the mixed economy makes sense as a decision rather than a compromise. The market builds the fintech office and should. Grants, no-fee schools, free basic services, land reform, township enterprise finance and commuter transport are the state's attempts to cut doors through the four walls. Whether the doors are working is what every budget speech and every election is really arguing about — and now you can name the argument.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — mixed and dualistic, held apart and used together. Master the pair and you can classify any economy on earth in three sentences, and explain your own in five.
