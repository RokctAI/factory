# Part 1 — Expert

In Grade 10 the four factors of production were introduced and left as a list. This year the list comes alive: what defines each factor, which income each one earns, what determines the size of that income, and how the four payments together account for every rand of value a business creates. Then the lens widens to South Africa itself — to the question of who can actually get their hands on these factors, and to the planning, participation and procurement machinery designed to open that access.

## Subtopic: The Four Factors and the Four Rewards

Four factors, four rewards — and the matching must be word-perfect.

NATURAL RESOURCES — the traditional name is land — are everything nature supplies to production: soil, mineral deposits, water, fish stocks, forests, climate, and even the radio spectrum carrying this lesson. Their reward is RENT. LABOUR is human effort in production, of muscle and of mind; its reward is WAGES and SALARIES. CAPITAL is the produced means of production — machines, buildings, tools, vehicles: goods that were themselves made, whose purpose is to make further goods; its reward is INTEREST. ENTREPRENEURSHIP is the factor that assembles the other three: it initiates, organises, chooses, innovates, and shoulders the risk nobody will insure. Its reward is PROFIT.

Two precision points eliminate the standard mistakes before they start. First, capital in economics means REAL capital — the oven, not the overdraft. Money is financial capital, a claim that can be exchanged for real capital; a nation gets richer by accumulating machines and infrastructure, not by multiplying banknotes. Second, labour is a service, not a person: the employer hires the day's effort, while the human being delivering it keeps every one of their rights.

Tie this back to the Grade 10 circular flow: households OWN the four factors and offer them in the FACTOR MARKET; firms hire them there; and rent, wages, interest and profit stream back to households as factor income. That loop is why national income and national product are a single quantity viewed from two ends.

Watch for the deliberately mismatched pair, because it is a favourite trick. Interest pays capital, never entrepreneurship; profit pays entrepreneurship, never capital. And dividends are no factor payment at all — they are profit already earned, being handed on to shareholders.

Take this section's questions now, and answer with the full pairing: the factor, its reward, and the market where the payment happens.

## Subtopic: Natural Resources and Labour Under the Microscope

Each factor has a personality, and the personality explains the pay.

NATURAL RESOURCES show four defining characteristics. They are not produced by anyone. They are unevenly scattered — a country either holds chrome and platinum under its soil or it does not. They are geographically stuck: an iron-ore deposit cannot relocate to the coast, which is why railway towns spring up around ore bodies rather than the other way round. And their total supply is fixed — no price, however high, can call more coastline or platinum reef into existence. That fixed supply is the key to the payment: with supply unable to respond, RENT is set almost entirely by demand. A corner trading site beside a busy Soweto intersection earns fierce rent not because anyone spent money making it, but because many traders want exactly that spot and no one can produce a second one. Classify resources further into RENEWABLE — fisheries, forests, water — and NON-RENEWABLE — coal, iron ore, platinum, gone once used. South Africa's endowment is lopsided: superb minerals, scarce water, and only a modest fraction of truly high-potential arable land. And by law the minerals belong to the nation: under the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act, mineral rights vest in the state, not in whoever owns the farm above the reef.

LABOUR has its own defining traits. It is INSEPARABLE from the worker — the effort arrives with the person attached, which is why labour law exists and steel law does not. It is PERISHABLE: today's unworked hours are gone forever, not stored for later — the precise reason unemployment is a permanent loss rather than a delay. It is HETEROGENEOUS: an electrician and an actuary are not substitutes. And it moves between jobs and towns only with friction.

Separate quantity from quality. QUANTITY is the labour force — the employed plus the unemployed who are actively searching. QUALITY is human capital — schooling, training, health and experience — and quality is what drives PRODUCTIVITY, output per worker per hour. Wages emerge from demand and supply, but three South African forces shape the result: collective bargaining by unions and bargaining councils, the national minimum wage as the legal floor, and productivity as the ceiling any employer can sustain. And always name the structural riddle: mass unemployment standing next to unfilled high-skill vacancies — a MISMATCH between the skills on offer and the skills in demand, which is a different disease from a simple shortage of jobs.

Work through this section's questions now — give a factor's characteristics, then use one of them to explain exactly how its reward gets determined.

## Subtopic: Capital, Entrepreneurship and the Residual

CAPITAL is born from restraint. Building a machine requires pulling resources away from making consumer goods — the very movement along the Grade 10 production possibility curve: less consumption now, so the whole curve can push outward later. Saving releases the resources; investment turns them into capital.

FIXED capital serves round after round — factories, machines, vehicles. WORKING capital is consumed in a single round — raw materials, stock on the shelf, the cash that meets the wage bill. Capital also decays: depreciation, called consumption of fixed capital in the national accounts. Gross investment is all spending on new capital; NET investment is gross minus depreciation, and only the net portion adds capacity. Capital's reward is INTEREST — the price of the funds that bought it, moved by the repo rate, the riskiness of the borrower, and the length of the loan.

ENTREPRENEURSHIP is the conductor: it decides the product, hires the other factors, innovates, and absorbs the one risk no policy will cover — that the customers never arrive. Its reward, PROFIT, is a RESIDUAL. Rent, wages and interest are promised in contracts and paid regardless of how the year went; profit is whatever is left over, and the leftover can be a loss. That is the deep logic of ownership: the person guaranteed nothing is the person who commands everything.

Now do the arithmetic. A furniture factory in Pietermaritzburg, one year. Sales — the value of output — R6 000 000. Bought-in timber, fabric, electricity and transport — R2 400 000. VALUE ADDED equals output minus intermediate purchases: 6 000 000 minus 2 400 000 gives R3 600 000 — the pot this business actually created, and the only pot its factors can be paid from.

Distribute it. Wages and salaries R2 250 000; rent on the factory site R330 000; interest on the equipment loans R270 000; consumption of fixed capital R270 000. Those four sum to R3 120 000. Profit takes the remainder: 3 600 000 minus 3 120 000 equals R480 000. Now add all five slices back together: R3 600 000 exactly — the built-in check that your split is right.

Read the proportions: labour takes 62,5 per cent of value added, rent about nine per cent, interest 7,5 per cent, depreciation 7,5 per cent, profit roughly thirteen per cent. Two remarks complete the analysis. Depreciation is nobody's income — it is machinery being used up, reserved so the saws and presses can one day be replaced; strip it out and net value added is R3 330 000. And the R2 400 000 of timber and electricity never enters the share-out, because it is intermediate consumption — it already delivered wages and profits inside the sawmill and the power utility. Paying it out twice is the double count the whole national accounting system is built to block.

The questions on this section are with you now — compute the value added, divide the pot, and explain why the residual has to be the residual.

## Subtopic: Local Economic Planning, Access and Empowerment Procurement

Factors of production are never abstract — they sit in actual towns, and the syllabus follows them home.

LOCAL ECONOMIC PLANNING rests on a constitutional foundation: promoting social and economic development is an object of local government. Every municipality must adopt an INTEGRATED DEVELOPMENT PLAN — the IDP — and revisit it every year, with local economic development written inside it. Strip away the format and the IDP asks a pure factor question: what does this municipality actually hold — irrigable land, a mineral deposit, a harbour, a rail junction, a reservoir of skills — and how can those factors be combined HERE, instead of shipping them out raw for other places to capture the value? Beneficiation plants, agro-processing and tourism corridors are all answers to that one question.

ACCESSIBILITY OF MARGINALISED GROUPS identifies who stands outside the factor markets: women, young people, people with disabilities, rural households, informal traders. The barriers must be named with precision, because vague sympathy explains nothing. Land — without registered title or secure tenure, there is nothing to pledge, so there is no collateral. Finance — lenders price risk, and no collateral means no loan, so the venture dies unborn. Information and skills — knowing that a tender exists, and being able to compile a compliant bid, are resources in their own right. Space and transport — apartheid settlement geography left households far from workplaces, so a heavy slice of a small wage is burnt reaching the job. Networks — contracts travel along existing relationships, and newcomers break in slowly. The remedies pair off against the barriers: development finance through institutions such as the Small Enterprise Finance Agency and the National Youth Development Agency, tenure reform, business incubators, learnerships, and investment in public transport.

COMMUNITY PARTICIPATION is built into the IDP by law, through ward committees and representative forums. Defend it in economic language, not moral language: residents hold the local knowledge that makes a scarce budget land where need binds tightest, and infrastructure a community chose is looked after, while infrastructure imposed on it is stripped.

EMPOWERMENT AND PROCUREMENT completes the chain. Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment is scored on a scorecard covering ownership, management control, skills development, enterprise and supplier development, and socio-economic development; the total sets a firm's B-BBEE level, and the level shapes the work it can win. Preferential procurement supplies the enforcement: under the Preferential Procurement Policy Framework Act, state tenders are scored on points — the great majority for price, a fixed remainder for defined goals such as transformation — the 80/20 and 90/10 formulas, with 90/10 reserved for larger contracts. The economics underneath: the state is the biggest single customer in the country, so steering a share of its buying toward previously excluded suppliers steers DEMAND — and demand is the force that converts idle factors into factor incomes.

The last questions of this part are in front of you now — planning, barriers, participation and procurement, each argued through its named mechanism rather than a warm sentiment.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again, from a two-chair hair salon in a converted container, where every factor on the syllabus is close enough to touch.

## Subtopic: The Salon, the Stylist, the Clippers and the Owner

One container salon at the edge of a busy shopping strip. Everything the syllabus calls a factor of production is inside or underneath it.

Start with what nature provided: the ground the container stands on, the water running into the basins. Nobody manufactured land or groundwater — they were there. Whoever owns them collects RENT.

Then the people: two stylists cutting, braiding and colouring all day. Skill, hands, concentration. They earn WAGES.

Then the made things that do the earning: the clippers, the dryers, the chairs, the geyser, the card machine. Every one of them was built by somebody, and none of them is for enjoyment — they exist to produce haircuts. That is CAPITAL, and the loan that bought them charges INTEREST.

And finally the person who looked at that busy strip two years ago and thought "people here need a salon" — who borrowed, signed for the container, hires the stylists and lies awake when the geyser bursts. That is the ENTREPRENEUR, and the entrepreneur's payment is PROFIT.

Two classic confusions, both now dodgeable. Money is not capital: the R150 000 loan in the bank account is not the factor — the clippers and chairs it bought are. And profit is not a salary: the stylists' pay is fixed in advance; the owner's profit is only whatever is still breathing at month-end.

Say the four pairs out loud once — ground, stylist, clippers, owner — then go to this section's questions and match them without blinking.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Day's Takings

Now follow the money, because "remuneration" is nothing more than a month's takings being divided.

In a month the salon rings up R45 000. Immediately, part of that was never truly the salon's money: R13 500 went out for shampoo, relaxer, colour, electricity and airtime — all bought from other businesses. Subtract it. What remains, R31 500, is the value this salon itself ADDED. That is value added, and it is the only pot the factors can be paid out of.

Divide the pot. The two stylists: R15 000. Rent for the site: R4 500. The interest slice of the equipment loan: R2 500. Money set aside because the clippers and dryers are a month closer to giving up: R3 500. Add those: 15 000 plus 4 500 is 19 500; plus 2 500 is 22 000; plus 3 500 is 25 500. The owner keeps the rest: R31 500 minus R25 500 — R6 000.

Stare at the order of that queue, because the order is the entire lesson. Everyone else stood FIRST in line, at amounts agreed before the month began; the owner stood LAST, at an amount nobody ever promised. Let the geyser burst in a quiet month and the leftover goes negative — the stylists still collect their R15 000, and the owner absorbs the hole alone. Bearing that hole is precisely why the owner gets to make the decisions.

One more habit: that R13 500 of shampoo and electricity already paid somebody's wage at the warehouse and somebody's profit at the utility. Count it again inside the salon and the same rand is counted twice — the exact error national accounting exists to prevent.

Questions on the money split are coming to you now — run the subtraction yourself, find the leftover, and explain why the leftover belongs to the person who was promised nothing.

## Subtopic: Whose Town Is It — Planning, Doors and Tenders

The final piece asks the biggest question: who actually gets a chance to own, hire or borrow any of this?

Every municipality writes a plan — the IDP — and beneath the formatting the plan asks one honest question: what does this town actually HAVE, and how do we earn a living from it here? A valley with perfect citrus and no juicing plant railcars its oranges away and buys the juice back at ten times the price. Local economic planning is the fight to keep that value in the valley.

Now the doors. Imagine three people, each with the same solid plan for a catering business. The first owns a house with a title deed, so the bank holds the deed and advances the loan. The second has no deed, but heard early about a hospital catering tender and got a bid in on time. The third has neither deed nor news, lives ninety minutes and two taxis from town, and only learnt of the tender after it closed. One idea, three fates — and the idea's quality decided none of it. That is accessibility of marginalised groups compressed into one story: no collateral, no loan; no information, no tender; no transport, no time. It is also why the ward meeting is worth attending: the plan gets written with or without you, and the paving, the clinic and the market stalls go where the plan points.

Then meet the largest customer in the republic: the state. It buys desks, cement, school meals, uniforms and security services at a scale no private buyer approaches. When it advertises a tender, the bids are scored — most of the points for price, and a fixed handful for empowerment goals such as ownership, skills training and developing small suppliers. That is B-BBEE and preferential procurement in motion, and the logic is not charity. Demand is what turns idle factors into incomes, and this is the country's biggest stream of demand being aimed at people the old order aimed it away from.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — plans, doors and tenders. Hold the whole picture: four factors, four rewards, one value-added pot, and a live national argument about who gets within reach of the pot at all.
