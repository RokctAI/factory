**Bianca:** Time for the class questions! The first one had half the group arguing at break: a wine estate grows its own grapes, makes its own wine in its own cellar, and runs a restaurant and tasting room on the farm. Which sector is it in? Surely it cannot be all three?

**Tutor:** It can be all three, and the estate is a perfect one-property tour of the whole topic, because the rule is to classify the activity, never the business. Walk the property. In the vineyard, workers are growing and picking grapes — harvesting directly from nature, so primary. In the cellar, those grapes are being crushed, fermented and bottled — a raw material transformed into a manufactured product, so secondary. In the restaurant and tasting room, meals and experiences are being served to visitors — services, so tertiary. The national accounts treat the estate exactly that way: each activity is recorded in its own sector even though one owner and one gate cover them all. So when a question describes a business, underline the verb. Growing, catching, digging — primary. Crushing, bottling, building — secondary. Serving, selling, transporting, advising — tertiary. The verb in the sentence, not the name over the gate, is what you are being asked to classify, and once you work by verbs, businesses that straddle sectors stop being scary and start being easy marks.

**Bianca:** Next question: if mining is only about six percent of GDP, why did the whole economy seem to shake last year every time the news mentioned mineral trains and ports? Six percent sounds too small to matter that much.

**Tutor:** Because a sector's GDP share measures its size, not its grip, and mining grips the economy in three places that the six percent number never shows. Grip one is foreign currency: minerals and metals still make up a very large slice of South Africa's export earnings, and those earnings pay for the imported fuel, medicines, machinery and electronics that every other sector uses daily. Choke the mineral trains and you choke the country's ability to pay for imports — that is why the freight crisis registered nationally. Grip two is linkages: a mine spends continuously on explosives, steel, engineering, transport, catering, banking and insurance, so mining output drags production out of the secondary and tertiary sectors behind it; economists call these backward linkages, and they multiply the sector's true footprint. Grip three is place: in towns across the North West, Limpopo and Mpumalanga, a single shaft or smelter is effectively the local economy, so nationally modest can still be locally total. The polished formulation for an essay holds both truths at once: services dominate South Africa's production, while mining retains an influence far beyond its GDP share through export earnings, linkages and regional employment. Never let one truth erase the other.

**Bianca:** This one comes from the debate club, apparently: someone argued that since load shedding hurt private businesses, the state should simply hand the whole electricity system to private companies and the problem is solved. Is that the economics answer?

**Tutor:** The economics answer is more careful than either slogan, and careful is what earns credit. Start by granting the evidence: years of generation failures and freight bottlenecks imposed real, measurable costs, and no honest analysis excuses that. But move from evidence to structure. A transmission grid, a national rail network, a city's water pipes — these are natural monopolies: building a second, rival grid alongside the first would waste billions, so one network there will always be. And a monopoly needs discipline whatever its owner. An unregulated private monopoly can extract high prices just as surely as a badly governed public one can decay; ownership alone guarantees nothing. What actually changed in South Africa is more precise and worth describing accurately: the shared networks remain, while competition is being introduced where it is feasible — independent producers now generate power and sell it into the grid, and private train operators are being allowed onto freight corridors. So structure the essay in three moves: the failures demonstrate the cost of under-investment and weak governance; the workable remedy combines regulated shared networks with private participation and competition at the edges; and the test of any arrangement, public, private or mixed, is a single question — does it deliver reliable service at low cost? Argue tests, not slogans.

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is the practical cry for help: essay answers about infrastructure and opportunity keep coming back with "develop your point further" written on them. What does a fully developed version of that point look like?

**Tutor:** Underdeveloped answers assert; developed answers trace. "Infrastructure helps the economy" is an assertion — true, vague, and worth very little. The developed version walks one person down one chain until the economics is visible. Build it in three moves. Move one, the achievement, with a number attached: about ninety percent of households now have electricity, one of the fastest service roll-outs any middle-income country has managed. Move two, the gap, with a place attached: access remains thinnest in the rural former homelands and in informal settlements, and mobile data prices keep the poorest largely offline. Move three, the chain — this is the part that earns the credit: take a specific person and follow the missing service into their productive life. A dressmaker without reliable power cannot promise delivery dates, loses her regular customers to a bigger firm, and her income falls — the missing kilowatt became a missing livelihood. Or a learner without electricity revises by candlelight, underperforms in the finals, and enters the job market a step behind. End every chain with the mechanism named: the missing service raised that person's cost of participating in the economy, so unequal access became unequal opportunity. One complete chain, a number, a place, and the mechanism sentence — that is what fully developed looks like.
