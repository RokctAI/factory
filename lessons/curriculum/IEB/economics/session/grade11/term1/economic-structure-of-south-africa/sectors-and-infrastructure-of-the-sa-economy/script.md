# Part 1 — Expert

This session draws the map of how South Africa actually earns its living: the three sectors into which every act of production falls, the century of change that rearranged their weights, and the shared installations — grids, railways, harbours, pipes and towers — that every sector stands on. By the end you will classify any business you are shown, describe the country's structure with defensible numbers, and build the argument that access to infrastructure and services determines who is able to take part in the economy in the first place.

## Subtopic: Three Sectors, One Economy

Sort all production by one test: how far the activity stands from nature.

The primary sector harvests directly from nature. Digging manganese in the Northern Cape, cutting sugar cane on the KwaZulu-Natal coast, harvesting rooibos in the Cederberg, netting pilchards out of Saldanha Bay — wherever the raw material is lifted straight from the earth, the sea or the field, the activity is primary.

The secondary sector reshapes raw materials into manufactured goods. Car assembly plants in East London and Kariega, a sugar refinery boiling cane into white sugar, a cement works in Lichtenburg, a furniture factory in Durban — and all of construction, from RDP houses to office towers. Manufacturing is the core of this sector.

The tertiary sector delivers services instead of objects. An insurance office in Umhlanga, a taxi operator on the Soweto routes, a tour guide on Table Mountain, a teacher in front of a class, a software developer in Stellenbosch writing banking apps. You cannot stack a service on a shelf, yet it is production in full: it satisfies wants and it generates income.

Now anchor the picture with the South African proportions, in figures worth memorising in round form. Services are the colossus, supplying roughly two thirds of gross domestic product, with finance and business services the largest single contributor, followed by trade, transport and government. The secondary sector supplies about one fifth, of which manufacturing is around thirteen percent of GDP. The primary sector, despite its place in the national imagination, is the smallest: mining sits near six percent and agriculture near three percent of GDP.

And immediately attach the caution that separates careful answers from careless ones: a sector's share of GDP and its share of employment answer different questions. Agriculture's three percent of GDP understates its role as an employer, particularly in rural districts where it is often the only employer. Small in rands can still be large in livelihoods, and you must keep the two scoreboards apart.

The questions for this section are with you now: activities to sort into their sectors, and the three GDP shares to arrange from largest to smallest.

## Subtopic: How the Sectors Link and How the Structure Has Shifted

The sectors are not three separate economies; they are stations on one line. Track a single cup of rooibos tea. A farmer in the Cederberg grows and cuts the bush — primary. A processing plant ferments, dries and packs the leaves into boxes — secondary. A distributor trucks the boxes to shops, a retailer sells them, a bank clears the card payment and an advertising agency designed the label — tertiary, four times over. Every product you own has ridden a line like this, and value is added at every station. Interdependence is the technical word, and it cuts both ways: a drought that withers the rooibos harvest will, months later, idle the packing line and thin out the shop's shelf.

Now add the dimension of time, because the South African structure has been rebuilt twice. A hundred years ago the economy rested on shafts and fields — the primary sector ruled. From the 1920s to the 1970s, factories multiplied behind protective tariffs and cheap coal-fired power, until the secondary sector became the engine. From the 1980s onward the centre of gravity slid to services — banking, retail, telecommunications, tourism, government. This ordered journey, primary to secondary to tertiary, is called structural transformation, and nearly every maturing economy makes it.

South Africa's journey, however, has a defect you must be able to name. Manufacturing peaked above twenty percent of GDP in the early 1990s and has slid to roughly thirteen percent today. Economists call the pattern premature deindustrialisation: the factory era began to fade before it had employed the great numbers of workers leaving farms and mines. The loss stings because manufacturing is historically the great absorber of semi-skilled labour — precisely the kind of employment an economy with unemployment above thirty percent is starving for. Services do hire, but a large share of service jobs demand certificates and degrees that excluded workers have not yet had the chance to earn.

A complete description of South Africa's structure therefore makes three moves: tertiary dominance stated with its share, the historical walk from primary through secondary to tertiary, and the early fading of the secondary sector flagged as the structural weakness.

Take this section's questions now: the tea's journey reassembled station by station, the three eras placed in order, and premature deindustrialisation defined with the reason it matters.

## Subtopic: Infrastructure — the Economy's Skeleton

Infrastructure is the shared, large-scale, long-lived equipment that all production depends on. Nobody desires a substation for its own sake the way they desire shoes or data — yet switch it off and every producer in the province feels it within the hour.

Learn the two-way split, because questions turn on it. Economic infrastructure serves production directly: generation plants and the transmission grid, the road and rail networks, the harbours at Durban, Richards Bay, Ngqura, Saldanha and Cape Town, airports, dams and bulk water schemes, and the fibre and cellular networks. Social infrastructure serves people directly and production indirectly: schools, clinics and hospitals, housing and sanitation. The indirect route is real economics — a child who learns and a worker who is treated at a clinic return to the economy more productive, so a hospital raises output the slow way.

On paper South Africa is unusually well endowed for a middle-income country: Africa's densest railway network, deep-water ports on two oceans, a grid designed decades ago to sell some of the world's cheapest electricity, and mobile coverage reaching nearly every settlement. The prize good infrastructure delivers is competitiveness — one shared asset that cuts costs for every firm simultaneously.

The honest continuation is that the endowment has been under strain. Electricity tells it most sharply: years of load shedding — rotating scheduled blackouts that reached their worst in 2023, when cuts ran on most days of the year — pushed firms into buying diesel generators and rooftop solar, lifting their costs while cutting their output. The freight system repeats the story in steel: mineral exporters have watched foreign buyers go elsewhere, not for lack of ore but because locomotives, lines and port cranes could not shift the tonnage. Estimates put the growth lost to these bottlenecks at percentage points per year.

Carry this sentence into any essay: a failure of shared infrastructure behaves like a tax levied on every producer at the same moment — it raises costs across all three sectors at once, which is exactly why restoring it is described as the cheapest growth plan the country possesses.

This section's questions are ready for you: the economic-social split applied, the strengths and the bottlenecks both named, and the everyone-taxed-at-once logic used on a fresh example.

## Subtopic: Service Provisioning, Access and Economic Opportunity

The last layer of the map is human: which households actually receive services, and what receiving them does to a person's chances.

Begin with the genuine achievement. Since 1994 the reach of basic services has expanded enormously: about ninety percent of households now have an electricity connection, nearly ninety percent have piped water in the home, the yard or close by, and cellular signal covers almost the entire population. Quote those figures with confidence — few middle-income countries have rolled services out at that pace.

Then puncture the average, because averages flatter. The remaining gaps cluster exactly where incomes are lowest: the rural districts of the former homelands, and informal settlements on metro edges. A village household in the Eastern Cape may have a light bulb yet still queue at a distant communal tap; a family in an informal settlement outside Tshwane may reach a standpipe but have no flush sanitation and no tarred road for the ambulance. Above the household level sits the digital divide: for most South Africans the internet arrives only through mobile data, and data priced per gigabyte weighs heaviest on the smallest incomes — the poorest users pay the most, relative to earnings, for every hour online.

Why is this an economics topic rather than only a social one? Because each service is a gate into economic participation. Without electricity, a seamstress cannot run her overlocker and a matric learner cannot revise after sunset. Without affordable transport, a vacancy thirty kilometres away is fictional — taxi fare can swallow so much of a low wage that working produces almost nothing. Without data, job adverts, price comparisons and low-cost banking all happen out of sight. The compact summary: weak service access raises the price of participating in the economy, and it raises that price most steeply for the poor — which is the mechanism by which gaps in pipes and cables copy inequality into the next generation.

Structure the argument in three steps whenever you are asked for it: the achievement with a number, the gap with a place, and the mechanism — access turns infrastructure into opportunity, so unequal access is unequal opportunity.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the delivery figures, the location of the gaps, and the full access-to-opportunity mechanism written out in sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same map, redrawn on one Saturday morning: a market stall, a cup of tea, and the wires and pipes that quietly decide whether any of it happens.

## Subtopic: From the Ground, Through the Factory, Over the Counter

Take this morning's cup of rooibos and run the film backwards.

The rooibos bush grew on a farm in the Cederberg — somebody planted it, tended it, cut it. That is the primary sector: collecting straight from nature. Farms, mines, trawlers, plantations. If the work happens where nature holds the raw material, call it primary.

But nobody drinks a bush. A plant chopped, fermented and dried the leaves, then sealed them into teabags inside a printed box. That is the secondary sector: factories converting raw material into products. Making, building, processing — if the job turns stuff into goods, call it secondary.

And the box still had to reach your kitchen. The truck that hauled it, the supermarket that shelved it, the card machine that took the payment, the electricity bill the shop pays the municipality — all tertiary: services. Nothing you can hold in your hand, but remove them and the tea never leaves the farm.

Now the fact that surprises almost everyone about South Africa. Say "our economy" and most people picture headgear and gold dust. Yet of every hundred rand the country produces, roughly sixty-five rand come from services, about twenty from factories and building sites, and less than ten from mines and farms combined. South Africa is a services economy that happens to own famous mines — not a mining economy with some shops attached.

One trap before the questions: a small rand share is not the same as a small importance. Farming contributes about three rand in a hundred, yet it fills the nation's plates and employs people in districts where no other work exists. The money scoreboard and the jobs scoreboard are different games.

Your questions for this section are with you now — for every business named, ask the one question that never fails: does it take from nature, make into goods, or serve people?

## Subtopic: One Street, All Three Sectors Holding Hands

Stand at a Saturday market and watch the sectors pass a baton.

The boerewors-roll seller's story starts on a Karoo farm where the cattle grazed — primary. The abattoir, the spice works and the bakery that turned beef into wors and flour into rolls — secondary. The seller grilling at her stand, the gas supplier who delivered her bottle, the payment app on her phone — tertiary, three services deep before you take a bite. One roll, all three sectors, and none of them able to deliver it alone. Let foot-and-mouth disease close the cattle auctions, and weeks later the wors price climbs and her roll shrinks or costs more. Trouble at any station rides the line to the end.

Now run South Africa's history like a film on fast-forward. In your great-grandparents' time, the country worked underground and in the fields — the mines-and-farms era. Then came the factory decades: steel mills, clothing factories, car plants filling whole towns. And within your own lifetime, the services era: malls, banks, fibre, call centres, government offices. Primary, then secondary, then tertiary — economies tend to grow up in that order, the way a child crawls, walks and then runs.

But South Africa carries a bruise from that journey, and here it is in plain words: our factories began emptying before enough people had ever worked in them. Textile towns in the Western Cape and KwaZulu-Natal watched their mills close as cheaper imports flooded in. The loss matters because factory work fits people who bring skill and effort but no university degree — the very workers this economy has in the greatest numbers. The service jobs that replaced factory jobs often ask for matric plus a qualification, and that mismatch is one of the reasons willing hands stay unhired.

So the one-sentence portrait of our economy: a large services head, a factory body that shrank too soon, and roots in the ground that are small but impossible to live without.

Take this section's questions now — ride the chain, order the eras, and explain in your own words why the emptying factories left such a mark.

## Subtopic: The Wires and Pipes That Decide Who Gets to Play

Everything so far quietly assumed the lights were on and the taps ran. Infrastructure is the name for that shared equipment — power stations, roads, rails, harbours, dams, pipes, towers — plus the people-serving kind: schools, clinics, houses.

Feel its power through one power cut on one trading corner. The barbershop's clippers die mid-cut. The butchery's fridges start warming towards spoilage. The internet café goes black and the schoolboy printing a CV is turned away. The tuck shop's card machine drops and cash-less customers walk. Nobody on that corner became less skilled at four o'clock — one shared failure taxed every one of them in the same minute. Now multiply that corner by every corner in the country, on most days of the year, and you have roughly the electricity story of 2023. That is why economists insist that repairing power, rail and ports is the cheapest growth plan available: mend one shared thing and thousands of businesses exhale together.

Then ask who the pipes actually reach. About ninety households in every hundred have electricity, and nearly ninety have piped water in or near the home — a delivery record thirty years in the making and worth saying proudly. But step into a deep rural village or an informal settlement and the averages dissolve: the tap is communal and far, the toilet is shared, and mobile data is the most expensive line in the family budget.

And here is the economics under all of it: services are the ticket at the gate of the economy. No electricity, no sewing machine running and no studying after dark. No affordable transport, and the distant job pays nothing once the taxi has been paid. No data, and the vacancies are advertised in a room you cannot enter. Where access is unequal, opportunity is unequal — the gap in the pipe becomes a gap in a person's future.

And here come the last questions of the lesson: one on the power-cut corner, one on the access numbers, and one asking you to trace, in your own words, how a missing pipe turns into a missing chance.
