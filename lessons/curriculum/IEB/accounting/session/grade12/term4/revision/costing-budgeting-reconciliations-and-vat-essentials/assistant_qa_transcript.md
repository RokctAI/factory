**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: at Marang the work-in-progress opened and closed at the same R45 000 and just cancelled. What is the adjustment actually doing when the balances differ, and which direction trips people?

**Tutor:** The adjustment converts what the factory SPENT this year into what the factory FINISHED this year, and those are different populations of tracksuits. Total cost of production — the R1 200 000 — is everything poured into the factory during the year: materials issued, machinists paid, overheads absorbed. But some of that money is standing on the floor inside half-made garments at year-end, and some of this year's finished output was already half-made when the year began, paid for out of LAST year's spending. So the funnel does a swap: add the opening work-in-progress, because those half-made units were completed and delivered into this year's finished pile; subtract the closing work-in-progress, because that spending has not produced a finished unit yet. When opening exceeds closing, the floor released value and finished production exceeds the year's spending; when closing exceeds opening, the floor swallowed value and finished production falls short. The direction that trips candidates is subtracting the OPENING balance — it feels like old stock that should come off, the way raw materials work — but work-in-progress runs the opposite way to the raw materials note precisely because it sits below total cost of production, not above it. The self-check is the unit cost: divide by the fifteen thousand FINISHED units and ask whether the answer is sane against the selling price. If your R80 came out at R120 against a R160 selling price with healthy profits reported, your adjustment probably ran backwards. And always label the final line cost of production of finished goods — the label itself is routinely a mark, because it proves you know which population you have costed.

**Mandy:** Next: the unit cost at Marang is R80, but the contribution working used R110 of variable cost, not R80. Why does break-even refuse to use the unit cost we just calculated so carefully?

**Tutor:** Because the R80 is a blend, and break-even only works with costs that behave — costs whose total moves in a straight line with units. Look inside the R80: it contains the variable manufacturing cost per tracksuit, which genuinely repeats with every unit, and a slice of the R345 000 fixed factory overheads, which was divided by this year's fifteen thousand units to get there. That fixed slice is an average, not a behaviour: make ten thousand units next year and the same overheads spread to R34,50 each; make twenty thousand and it thins to R17,25. A number that changes whenever volume changes cannot predict what happens at a different volume — which is exactly what break-even asks. So the model separates the families: the R110 of truly variable cost per unit — materials, labour, the selling costs that travel with each sale — stands against the R420 000 of fixed costs waiting in the numerator. If you build contribution from the R80 unit cost, you count part of the fixed costs twice: once inside the contribution figure, once in the numerator — and your break-even point lands somewhere meaningless, usually flatteringly low. That is the cardinal error the topic punishes hardest. The discipline is one question asked of every cost before it enters the contribution: if the factory made one more unit, would this total rise? Materials, yes — variable. Factory rent, no — fixed, into the numerator. Keep the families apart and both the R80 and the R110 are correct — they are just answering different questions: the R80 tells you what a tracksuit absorbed this year; the R110 tells you what the next tracksuit will cause.

**Mandy:** Third: depreciation keeps getting banned from the cash budget, but we DID budget for the equipment purchase itself. Is that not the same money appearing after all?

**Tutor:** It is the same asset but never the same month, and the two documents are deliberately built not to overlap. Follow the equipment through both. The cash budget records the purchase once, in full, in the month the money moves: equipment bought in June for R60 000 cash is a June payment line of R60 000 — brutal and simple, which is why the budget is where you spot the overdraft coming and move the purchase or arrange the loan in advance. The projected income statement never shows that R60 000 at all; instead it shows depreciation, the systematic slicing of the cost over the asset's life — say R12 000 a year — matched against the income the equipment helps earn. Two clocks, one asset: cash feels the whole blow on day one; profit feels a thin slice every year for five years. Put depreciation into the cash budget and you charge the business twice for the same machine — once when the money left, and again every month in phantom instalments no bank account ever sees. The same one-question test rescues every sorting mark: did money move THIS month? Depreciation, never — banned. Bad debts, never — the cash simply fails to arrive, which the collection schedule already handled by collecting only ninety-seven percent. A loan received, yes — in, even though it is not income. Provisional tax, an interim dividend, the equipment itself — yes, in their months. And the interpretation habit that finishes the question: after sorting, read the budget for trouble. A projected overdraft in the equipment month is not a fact to report; it is a decision to make — delay, lease, or pre-arrange finance — and the mark is for making it.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the VAT settlement the drawings were R6 000 excluding VAT but the bad debt was R2 300 including VAT, and the two adjustments ran in opposite directions. How do we keep the readings and the directions straight under pressure?

**Tutor:** Two separate decisions per nudge — first the arithmetic reading, then the direction — and each has its own logic, so never let one decide the other. The reading first. Excluding VAT means the tax is not inside the number yet, so the VAT is a clean fifteen percent on top: fifteen percent of the R6 000 drawings is R900. Including VAT means the tax is already baked in, so the number is one hundred and fifteen parts and the VAT is fifteen of them: fifteen over one hundred and fifteen of R2 300 is R300. Divide an inclusive figure by simple fifteen percent and you claim tax that was never charged — R345 instead of R300 — and the reading mark dies quietly. Now the direction, and here you ask one question: what did SARS originally allow or receive, and has reality changed? The drawings: when the business bought that stock, it claimed input tax back, on the promise the stock would be resold and generate output tax. The owner ate the promise — the goods left the business without a sale — so the input claim must be returned: the amount owed to SARS goes UP by R900. The bad debt: when the sale happened, the business paid output tax to SARS on the full R2 300 as if the money would arrive. It never will, so SARS holds tax on income that does not exist, and the vendor claims it back: owed goes DOWN by R300. Notice the deep symmetry — both adjustments simply make the VAT account match reality, because VAT is a tax on transactions that actually complete. And that is why the trust sentence belongs in every ethics answer on this topic: the R1,80 in every R13,80 sale was never the business's money; correcting the settlement honestly is not generosity, it is returning what is held in trust.
