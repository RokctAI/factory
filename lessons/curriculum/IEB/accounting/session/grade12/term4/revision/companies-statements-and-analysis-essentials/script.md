# Part 1 — Expert

Term four is revision territory, and your final exams reward the student who can rebuild the whole company story from memory: income statement, notes, balance sheet, cash flow, and then the analysis that interrogates all of it. One company carries this whole revision — Kiepersol Limited, seven hundred and thirty thousand ordinary shares in issue — and every figure will be computed out loud, because in the examination the marks sit on the workings, not on the memory of a format. The plan is the story's own plan: profit first, then equity, then cash, then judgement.

## Subtopic: The Income Statement Chain Rebuilt

The income statement is a chain, and every link is a mark. Say the chain before touching any number: sales, minus cost of sales, gives gross profit; add other income, subtract operating expenses, gives operating profit; add interest income, subtract interest expense, gives net profit before tax; subtract income tax, and net profit after tax remains. Six links, learned as a spine.

Now Kiepersol's year. Sales R4 500 000, cost of sales R3 000 000 — gross profit R1 500 000, and notice the mark-up check: profit of 1 500 000 on cost of 3 000 000 is exactly fifty percent on cost, so if the information states a fifty percent mark-up, the figures agree. Other income of R60 000 lifts the total to R1 560 000. Operating expenses of R760 000 — this is where the adjustments live: depreciation, the audit fees still owed, directors' fees, trading stock deficit after the count — leaving operating profit of R800 000. Interest income R20 000, so profit before interest expense is R820 000. Interest expense R120 000 — a R1 000 000 loan at twelve percent, and a good question will make you find one of those three numbers from the other two. Net profit before tax: R700 000.

Then the line students lose softest marks on: income tax at twenty-seven percent of R700 000 is R189 000, leaving net profit after tax of R511 000. Twenty-seven percent is the company rate — not the VAT fifteen, not a personal rate — and the tax calculation is a gift mark that must not be donated back.

Hold R511 000 in mind. Divided by seven hundred and thirty thousand shares it will become earnings per share of 70 cents, and half of the analysis hangs on it. This section's questions are with you now: the chain in order, the mark-up check, and the tax line done at the correct rate.

## Subtopic: Share Capital, Buy-backs and Retained Income

Shares have no par value — one account, ordinary share capital, credited with the full proceeds of every issue. Kiepersol began the year with six hundred thousand shares that had raised R2 244 000. On the first day of this financial year the directors issued one hundred and thirty thousand new shares at the market-related price of R5,20 — R676 000 straight into share capital. The note now reads, line by line: six hundred thousand shares at the beginning, R2 244 000; one hundred and thirty thousand shares issued at R5,20, R676 000; seven hundred and thirty thousand shares at the end, R2 920 000. Average issue price: R2 920 000 divided by seven hundred and thirty thousand — R4,00. That average is the key that unlocks every buy-back question.

The buy-back drill, exactly as you should rehearse it. Suppose a company with the same R4,00 average repurchases thirty thousand shares at R4,60 — above the average, as buy-backs usually are. Three lines, spoken in order. Ordinary share capital decreases by thirty thousand times R4,00 — R120 000. Retained income decreases by thirty thousand times the 60 cents difference — R18 000. Bank decreases by the full thirty thousand times R4,60 — R138 000. Check it: 120 000 plus 18 000 equals 138 000. The logic earns the marks: the capital account gives back the average it holds, and retained income surrenders the premium, because that premium is income the company had retained on those very shares.

Now Kiepersol's retained income note, the appropriation story in four lines. Opening balance R350 000. Add net profit after tax, R511 000 — the link from the income statement. Subtract dividends of R328 500: an interim of 20 cents on seven hundred and thirty thousand shares, R146 000, already paid, plus a final of 25 cents, R182 500, declared but unpaid, so it doubles as a current liability. Closing balance: 350 000 plus 511 000 minus 328 500 — R532 500. Shareholders' equity therefore stands at R2 920 000 plus R532 500 — R3 452 500. Questions on this section are ready now: the share capital note built, the three buy-back lines with their check, and the retained income note closed at R532 500.

## Subtopic: Cash Flow Signposts and the Three Workings

The cash flow statement answers one question the income statement cannot: where did the money actually go? Three sections, spoken as a map. Operating activities: cash generated from operations, then interest paid, tax paid, dividends paid. Investing activities: fixed assets bought and sold — and at this level disposals are handled at carrying value, no profit or loss complication. Financing activities: shares issued, shares repurchased, loans raised or repaid.

The same three workings decide this topic every time, so drill them as subtractions.

Tax PAID is not the tax charge. Kiepersol's charge is R189 000, but SARS was owed R12 000 at the start of the year and R18 000 at the end. Paid equals charge plus opening owed minus closing owed: 189 000 plus 12 000 minus 18 000 — R183 000. Logic, not formula: the year began owing 12 000 and that was settled; of this year's charge, 18 000 remains unpaid.

Dividends PAID follows the same shape with the current liability called shareholders for dividends. Last year's final dividend of R130 000 sat in that account at the start and was paid during this year; this year's interim of R146 000 was paid in cash; this year's final of R182 500 is declared, unpaid, and waiting in the closing balance sheet. Paid: 130 000 plus 146 000 — R276 000.

Financing tells Kiepersol's strategy in two lines: proceeds of shares issued, R676 000 in; the loan unchanged at R1 000 000. A reader sees the company funding itself with new equity rather than new debt — and writing one sentence of that kind, reading the statement rather than reciting it, is where the interpretation marks sit. The questions for this section are with you now: paid versus charged, the dividend timing, and disposals at carrying value.

## Subtopic: The Indicators That Decide the Marks

Analysis is quote, compare, judge — three sentences per indicator, every time. Run Kiepersol through the four decisive comparisons.

Liquidity first. Current assets R840 000 — inventories R360 000, debtors R290 000, cash R190 000 — against current liabilities of R400 000: creditors R199 500, SARS R18 000, shareholders for dividends R182 500. Current ratio 2,1 to 1; acid-test, stripping the stock, 1,2 to 1. Both healthy, and the operating checks agree: stock turns eight times a year — cost of sales R3 000 000 over average stock R375 000 — and debtors on credit sales of R2 900 000 pay in about 36,5 days, just past thirty-day terms and worth one sentence of caution.

Returns second. Return on shareholders' equity: R511 000 over average equity of R3 023 250 — 16,9 percent, towering over the roughly eight percent a fixed deposit pays, so shareholders are well paid for their risk. Return on total capital employed: profit before tax plus interest, R820 000, over average equity plus the loan, R4 023 250 — 20,4 percent.

Gearing third, and here is the argument you must write out in full: the whole pot earns 20,4 percent while the loan costs twelve. Every borrowed rand earns about eight cents more than it costs, so gearing is positive and, with debt-equity at a light R1 000 000 against R3 452 500, Kiepersol could afford to borrow for expansion — always adding the caution that interest is owed in bad years too.

Value last. Net asset value: equity of R3 452 500 over seven hundred and thirty thousand shares — about 473 cents. The shares trade at 540 cents, a premium of 67 cents, which is the market paying for a future the balance sheet cannot record. Earnings per share 70 cents; dividends per share 45 cents; pay-out about 64 percent — generous to income-seekers, leaving about a third of earnings to fund growth. Final questions of this part are up now: the two liquidity ratios, the gearing argument stated in full, and price against net asset value read in the same units.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same company, new clothes: a family pizzeria that became a company, seven hundred and thirty thousand slices of ownership spread across a big family — because every statement in the company story is just an honest answer to a question a family member would ask at the kitchen table.

## Subtopic: The Pizzeria's Year on One Page

The pizzeria sold R4 500 000 of pizzas this year. The flour, cheese and oven-fire behind those pizzas cost R3 000 000, so the cooking itself made R1 500 000 — every rand of cost cooked into a rand and a half of price. Renting out the storeroom behind the kitchen brought in R60 000 more. Then the running of the place — wages, insurance, the delivery scooters wearing out, the accountant who checks the books — took R760 000. A little interest arrived on savings, R20 000, and the bank took R120 000 for the loan it gave the pizzeria. What was left before the tax collector called: R700 000.

The tax collector takes a fixed slice of company profit — twenty-seven cents in every rand — R189 000. The family's kept profit for the year: R511 000. That single number now does everything. Spread across seven hundred and thirty thousand slices, each slice earned 70 cents. Of that, 45 cents was actually paid out to the family during the year — some at mid-year, some promised at year-end — and the remaining 25 cents per slice stayed in the pizzeria, quietly making every slice worth more. Profit earned, profit paid, profit kept: one page, three fates. Questions on this piece are with you now: the cost of the pizzas against their price, the tax collector's fixed slice, and what each slice earned against what it was paid.

## Subtopic: Following the Cash, Not the Profit

Grandmother asks the awkward question: if the pizzeria made R511 000, why did the bank balance not grow by R511 000? Because profit is a story about the YEAR, and cash is a story about the TILL — and the two run on different clocks.

Three clocks, in fact. The tax clock: the pizzeria owed the tax collector a bit from last year, R12 000, and settled it; of this year's R189 000 slice, R18 000 will only be paid next year. So the cash that actually left for tax was R183 000 — charge plus old debt minus new debt. The dividend clock: the R130 000 promised to the family at the end of LAST year was paid early THIS year, and this year's mid-year payout of R146 000 left in cash too — R276 000 out — while this year's year-end promise of R182 500 has not left the till yet. The growth clock: the family sold one hundred and thirty thousand new slices to cousins for R676 000 cash, money that came IN without a single pizza being cooked.

So the bank balance moves for reasons profit never mentions, and profit moves for reasons the bank never sees — the scooters wearing out cost profit every year but take no cash at all. A family that watches only profit gets surprised at the till; a family that watches only the till forgets the scooters are dying. Watch both. Your questions are ready now: why paid differs from charged, which promise is still in the till, and what wears out without spending a cent.

## Subtopic: Three Questions Before You Buy a Slice

A cousin from Cape Town wants to buy slices. Three questions settle it, and they are the whole analysis section in disguise.

One: what is a slice honestly worth in the books? Everything the pizzeria owns, minus everything it owes, comes to R3 452 500. Divide by seven hundred and thirty thousand slices: about 473 cents each. But slices actually trade between cousins at 540 cents. The 67 cents extra is not foolishness; it is the price of the pizzeria's future — the loyal customers, the corner location, next year's pizzas — none of which the books are allowed to record.

Two: does the family's money work harder in the pizzeria than in the bank? The pizzeria earned 16,9 cents on every rand of family money; the bank pays about eight for doing nothing. Nearly nine extra cents per rand is the reward for risk, and it is generous.

Three: is the borrowed money helping or hurting? The whole pot of long-term money — family plus bank loan — earned 20,4 cents per rand this year, while the loan costs twelve. Every borrowed rand earns more than it eats, so the loan is working FOR the family; if the pizzeria's earning power ever slid below twelve, that same loan would start working against them, because the bank collects in bad years too.

Worth in the books against price between cousins; the pizzeria against the bank; earnings against the cost of debt. Three comparisons, each ending in a judgement — and that is the exact three-sentence habit the examination pays for, on every indicator, every time. The last questions of the lesson are with you now.
