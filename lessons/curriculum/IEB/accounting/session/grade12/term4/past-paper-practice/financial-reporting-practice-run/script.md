# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a full practice set on financial reporting and evaluation — one hundred and fifty marks of company accounting, written for this session and walked task by task the way a marker reads it. The tasks cover the ground your final exams will demand: statements, notes, cash flow, analysis, audit and governance. One company, Amatola Limited, carries the recording work; a two-company comparison carries the analysis. Every step is spoken with the marks I have allocated to it, because pacing an examination is mark arithmetic: effort spent in proportion to the marks on offer, and the workings always shown, since the method mark survives even when the answer dies.

## Subtopic: Warm-Up — Concepts and the Asset Disposal

A practice set should open gently: four concept marks, one each, matching statements to terms. Drill the pairs that recur. The going-concern assumption: statements are prepared expecting the business to continue — one mark. Prudence: never overstate income or assets — one mark. Materiality: information that would change a user's decision must be shown separately — one mark. IFRS: the international standards listed companies follow — one mark. Four marks in four sentences; bank them fast.

Then the warm-up working: an asset disposal, eight marks in this set. A delivery vehicle, cost R240 000, accumulated depreciation of R118 000 at the start of the year, is sold eight months into the year. Step one, current depreciation: fifteen percent on cost is R36 000 a year, but only eight months have run — R24 000. Two marks: one for the rate on cost, one for the time apportionment, and the time fraction is where wrong answers are harvested. Step two, total accumulated depreciation at sale: 118 000 plus 24 000 — R142 000, one mark. Step three, carrying value: 240 000 minus 142 000 — R98 000, one mark. In this task the vehicle is sold at carrying value, so the selling price is R98 000, and the remaining marks sit in the ledger presentation: cost out of the asset account, accumulated depreciation closed off, bank in. Eight marks, no surprises, provided the eight-twelfths reflex fires.

## Subtopic: The Income Statement Task

The big recording task — thirty-five marks in this set. Amatola Limited, year-end results needing adjustments, and the marker follows your adjustments one by one.

Rent income first. The account shows R91 000 received, but the tenant has paid fourteen months. Divide by fourteen — R6 500 a month — so the year's true rent income is R78 000, and R13 000 is income received in advance, a current liability. Two marks: the fourteen-month insight, the R13 000 deferral.

Insurance: R21 600 covers twelve months, but four of those months belong to next year. Prepaid expense: R7 200, current asset; this year's expense drops to R14 400. Two marks, and the direction matters — students who ADD the prepayment lose both.

Audit fees: the auditors have completed the work, the fee of R42 000 is unpaid and unrecorded. Accrue it: expense up, accrued expenses up. One mark, pure vigilance.

Depreciation: vehicles R96 000, equipment R34 000 — R130 000 total, three or four marks of rate-times-base workings, each asset class its own mark.

Interest on loan: the loan of R800 000 at nine percent should cost R72 000, but only R66 000 was recorded. Accrue R6 000 more. Two marks: one for the nine-percent expectation, one for the shortfall.

Now the two lines every set of company results ends with. Net profit before tax: R840 000 after all adjustments. Income tax at twenty-seven percent: R226 800 — one mark, and it must be twenty-seven, the company rate. Net profit after tax: R613 200 — one mark, carried forward mercilessly into the retained income note, which is why an error here bleeds; but the marking principle of carrying forward means your FOLLOW-ON work still earns marks if the method stands. Show every working.

## Subtopic: The Share Capital and Retained Income Notes

Two notes, eighteen marks in this set, and they tell the year's ownership story.

Ordinary share capital. Opening: six hundred thousand shares that raised R2 400 000 — average R4,00. During the year, one hundred and fifty thousand new shares at R5,50: R825 000 — one mark for the multiplication, one for adding it in. Subtotal: seven hundred and fifty thousand shares, R3 225 000, new average R4,30 — one mark, and say the average aloud because the buy-back depends on it. Then the directors repurchase sixty thousand shares at R4,90. The note shows the capital portion only: sixty thousand times the R4,30 average — R258 000 out. Two marks. Closing: six hundred and ninety thousand shares, R2 967 000 — one mark for the balance.

Retained income, five lines. Opening balance R380 000 — given. Add net profit after tax: R613 200, carried from the income statement — one mark, follow-on protected. Subtract the buy-back premium: sixty thousand shares times the 60 cents paid above average — R36 000 — two marks, and this is the line that separates prepared candidates, because the premium lives HERE, not in share capital. Subtract dividends: interim of 25 cents on seven hundred and fifty thousand shares, R187 500; final of 40 cents on six hundred and ninety thousand shares — after the buy-back — R276 000; total R463 500 — three marks, one per calculation, one for the share counts being different, which is precisely the trap. Closing balance: 380 000 plus 613 200 minus 36 000 minus 463 500 — R493 700, one mark. Shareholders' equity now reads R2 967 000 plus R493 700 — R3 460 700.

## Subtopic: The Balance Sheet Task

The statement of financial position, twenty-five marks here, is an exercise in filing under pressure. Speak the skeleton: non-current assets, current assets; then equity, non-current liabilities, current liabilities — and the two totals must agree.

Amatola's equity side is already built: R3 460 700. Non-current liabilities: the loan, R800 000 — but always check the information for the portion repayable within twelve months, which shifts to current. Current liabilities, and here the adjustments come home to roost: trade creditors R212 300; accrued expenses R48 000 — the audit fee and the loan interest from the income statement task; income received in advance R13 000 — the tenant's extra two months; SARS for income tax R16 800 — the charge of R226 800 less provisional payments of R210 000; shareholders for dividends R276 000 — the final dividend, declared but unpaid. Total current liabilities: R566 100. Each correctly placed item is a mark, and each is a mark BECAUSE it was an adjustment — the task tests whether your adjustments landed on both sides of the accounting equation.

Total equity and liabilities: 3 460 700 plus 800 000 plus 566 100 — R4 826 800. The asset side must meet it: fixed assets at carrying value R3 905 000; inventories R395 000; trade and other receivables R336 800, which includes the prepaid insurance of R7 200; cash R190 000. Total: R4 826 800. Balance. If it does not balance, do not go hunting — rule it off, move on, and let the follow-on marks protect you; the balancing figure itself is rarely worth more than one mark, and the hunt costs marks elsewhere.

## Subtopic: The Cash Flow Statement Task

Cash flow, twenty marks in this set, is three workings and a map. The marker awards the workings even when the final statement stumbles, so speak them as small ledgers.

Tax paid. Charge R226 800; owed to SARS at the start R11 200; owed at the end R16 800. Paid: 226 800 plus 11 200 minus 16 800 — R221 200. Three marks: opening, closing, direction.

Dividends paid. The interim of R187 500 left in cash this year; last year's final of R230 000, sitting in the opening shareholders for dividends, was paid this year too. Total: R417 500. This year's final of R276 000 stays behind as a liability. Three marks, all direction marks.

Fixed assets purchased. Opening carrying value R3 560 000, plus purchases, minus depreciation R130 000, minus the disposal at carrying value R98 000, equals closing R3 905 000. Solve: purchases R573 000. Four marks, and the equation form is the safest route — the marker can see every component.

Financing section: proceeds of the share issue, R825 000 in; buy-back of shares, R294 000 out — the FULL price paid, capital plus premium, because cash left in full. Two marks, and the buy-back at full price catches everyone who forgets that the R36 000 premium was a retained income story, not a cash story. Interpretation closes the task: this company raised equity, returned some to shareholders, and invested R573 000 in capacity — one sentence, one mark, easy money.

## Subtopic: The Analysis and Interpretation Task

Thirty marks, and the formulas are given to you — so the marks are for choosing indicators and arguing, not remembering. Two companies, same industry. Baobab Limited: debt-equity 0,6 to 1; return on total capital employed 16,2 percent; return on equity 18,1 percent; current ratio 1,3 to 1; share price 920 cents against net asset value of 700 cents. Karee Limited: debt-equity 0,2 to 1; return on total capital 10,4 percent; return on equity 9,8 percent; current ratio 4,1 to 1; price 480 cents against net asset value of 530 cents. Loans cost twelve percent.

Gearing, six marks here. Baobab borrows at twelve and earns 16,2 — positive gearing, each borrowed rand clears 4,2 cents, which is why its return on equity beats its return on capital. Quote both figures, compare to twelve, judge: Baobab's gearing is justified though risk rises with the 0,6 ratio. Karee earns 10,4 against the same twelve — borrowing would destroy value, so its timid 0,2 gearing is wise, not weak. Marks fall one per quoted figure, one per comparison, one per judgement.

Liquidity, four marks: Baobab at 1,3 is tight but workable; Karee at 4,1 is not safe, it is lazy — money sleeping in stock and debtors that could fund expansion or dividends. The counter-intuitive judgement is the mark.

The market's verdict, four marks: Baobab trades 220 cents above its net asset value — confidence in its future; Karee trades 50 cents BELOW — the market doubts its assets will earn their keep, though a bidder might smell a bargain. Same units, cents against cents, always.

Then the shareholder question: which company would you invest in? There is no wrong company — there are only argued and unargued answers. Two indicators quoted, one risk acknowledged, a conclusion: full marks.

## Subtopic: The Audit Report and Governance Task

The set closes with the published-report task — ten marks of reading, not arithmetic.

The audit report first. Know the three opinions you must command as verdicts: unqualified — the statements fairly present, in all material respects; qualified — fairly present EXCEPT FOR stated matters; disclaimer or adverse — the auditor cannot, or will not, vouch for them. One mark each when defined, two when applied to a scenario. Know the addressee: the report speaks to the SHAREHOLDERS, because the auditors work for the owners, not the directors they are checking. Know what the audit fee in the income statement implies: an external, independent examination was bought — and if the same firm also runs the company's bookkeeping, independence is compromised, which is a classic two-mark scenario.

Governance scenarios: a financial director who chairs the audit committee — concentration of power, no independent oversight; directors' remuneration rising while earnings fall — a question for the annual general meeting; a director selling shares before poor results are released — insider trading, illegal, and the ethics marks want the word. The King Code's four demands — responsibility, accountability, fairness, transparency — map onto almost any scenario a question can invent.

Final discipline for the whole set, spoken once: attempt every part, show every working, carry your own figures forward without apology, and spend marks' worth of effort, not affection's worth, on each task. One hundred and fifty marks, walked. That is the practice run.
