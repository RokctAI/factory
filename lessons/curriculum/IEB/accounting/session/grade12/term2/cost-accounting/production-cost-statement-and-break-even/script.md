# Part 1 — Expert

A trader's accounting watches goods arrive at one price and leave at a higher one. A MANUFACTURER's accounting has a harder job: it must follow leather into cut pieces, cut pieces into shoes, and shoes into money — and for that it needs its own vocabulary, its own statement, and its own arithmetic. This lesson assembles the complete grade 12 toolkit: the cost concepts that belong only to manufacturing; the production cost statement with its supporting notes; the abridged income statement that flows out of it; unit costs and the break-even point; and the ethics and internal controls that keep a factory honest. Master this statement properly — it is one of the great set-piece calculations of the year.

## Subtopic: The Language of the Factory

Every manufacturing question starts with a sorting exercise, because factory costs fall into exactly three families.

DIRECT MATERIAL COST — the raw materials that end up inside the product: leather in the shoe, steel in the gate, milk in the cheese. Carriage on raw materials is added to their cost. INDIRECT materials — machine grease, cleaning chemicals, small quantities of adhesive — do not qualify; they belong to overheads.

DIRECT LABOUR COST — the wages of the people whose hands physically build the product: the cutter, the stitcher, the machine operator. The floor supervisor overseeing five teams, the security guard at the gate, the fitter who services the machines — all indirect labour, and again overheads.

FACTORY OVERHEAD COST — every remaining cost the factory needs but cannot attach to a single unit: factory rent, factory water and electricity, depreciation on factory machinery, indirect materials, indirect labour, insurance of the plant. Hold on to the word FACTORY: rent of the showroom is not a factory overhead — it is a selling cost, and keeping factory spending apart from office and selling spending is half the discipline of this topic.

Direct materials plus direct labour carries its own name — PRIME COST — the spending that touches the product directly. Add factory overheads and you reach total MANUFACTURING cost.

Outside the factory gate live the two operating families: ADMINISTRATION COST — office salaries, office rent, stationery, audit fees — and SELLING AND DISTRIBUTION COST — advertising, sales commission, delivery vehicle running costs, bad debts.

And a manufacturer keeps three stocks, never one: RAW MATERIALS still waiting in the storeroom; WORK-IN-PROGRESS — units started but not completed, shoes stitched but not soled; and FINISHED GOODS standing ready for sale. Three stock accounts, three closing balances, and the questions you will face spend their time moving value between them.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: costs sorted into the three factory families plus the two operating ones, prime cost named, and the three stocks told apart.

## Subtopic: The Production Cost Statement

The production cost statement answers a single question: what did it cost to FINISH goods this year? Build it for Khanya Footwear, a school-shoe factory, line by spoken line.

Begin with DIRECT MATERIALS USED, which carries its own note: opening stock of raw materials sixty thousand rand, plus purchases of five hundred and sixty thousand, plus carriage on purchases of twenty-five thousand — materials available, six hundred and forty-five thousand — less closing stock of raw materials, ninety-five thousand. Direct material cost: R550 000. The subtraction is the point: buying leather is not using leather; whatever still lies in the storeroom never entered production.

Add DIRECT LABOUR COST of R350 000. PRIME COST: R900 000.

Add FACTORY OVERHEAD COST, gathered by its own note from indirect materials, indirect labour, factory rent, factory electricity and depreciation on factory machinery: R390 000 altogether. TOTAL MANUFACTURING COST: R1 290 000.

Now the statement's signature move — the work-in-progress adjustment. The year opened with forty thousand rand of half-finished shoes on the floor: last year's spending, completed this year, so ADD it. The year closed with eighty thousand rand still unfinished: this year's spending that produced nothing sellable yet, so SUBTRACT it. COST OF PRODUCTION OF FINISHED GOODS: one million two hundred and ninety thousand, plus forty thousand, less eighty thousand — R1 250 000.

Read the logic underneath the arithmetic: the statement filters a whole year of spending down to the cost of what was actually COMPLETED. Money spent and production finished are two different things, and work-in-progress is exactly the gap between them.

One division before the figures cool: Khanya finished fifty thousand pairs this year. UNIT COST OF PRODUCTION: one million two hundred and fifty thousand divided by fifty thousand — R25 a pair. That single number values the stock, drives the break-even work to come, and lets management ask the only question worth asking: is twenty-five rand good?

Stop for this section's questions now — the materials note with its subtraction, prime cost through total manufacturing cost, the work-in-progress adjustment in both directions, and the unit cost.

## Subtopic: From Factory Floor to Income Statement

The factory's output now enters a trading story, and the manufacturer's income statement takes over precisely where a trader's would say purchases.

FINISHED GOODS first. Opening stock of finished goods: R100 000 — four thousand pairs carried at last year's twenty-five rand. Add this year's cost of production, R1 250 000 — fifty thousand pairs. Available for sale: fifty-four thousand pairs carrying R1 350 000. Closing stock: six thousand pairs at twenty-five rand — R150 000. COST OF SALES: one million three hundred and fifty thousand less one hundred and fifty thousand — R1 200 000, which is forty-eight thousand pairs sold.

SALES: forty-eight thousand pairs at thirty-five rand each — R1 680 000. GROSS PROFIT: one million six hundred and eighty thousand less one million two hundred thousand — R480 000. Test it the way a careful accountant would: gross profit over cost of sales is four hundred and eighty thousand over one million two hundred thousand — exactly forty percent, confirming the mark-up policy.

Then the operating families, each with its note. ADMINISTRATION COST: office salaries, office rent, stationery, audit fees — R150 000. SELLING AND DISTRIBUTION COST: advertising, commission, delivery costs, bad debts — R138 000. NET PROFIT: four hundred and eighty thousand less one hundred and fifty thousand less one hundred and thirty-eight thousand — R192 000.

This is where the classification traps live, so name them out loud. Depreciation splits by USE: factory machinery into factory overheads, office computers into administration, delivery vehicles into selling and distribution. Rent splits by floor space when one building holds both factory and office. Salaries split by role: the production supervisor into overheads, the bookkeeper into administration, the sales team into selling. One kind of expense, three possible homes — the ADDRESS depends on what the spending served, and questions deliberately hand you mixed lists to sort.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — finished goods rolled into cost of sales, the forty percent confirmed, and mixed expenses delivered to their correct notes.

## Subtopic: Break-Even and the Ethics of Making Things

Management's next question: how many units must be sold before the business stops losing money? That is the BREAK-EVEN POINT, and it needs the costs sorted a different way — not by department, but by BEHAVIOUR.

VARIABLE COSTS grow with every unit made: materials, labour paid per unit, sales commission, packaging. FIXED COSTS arrive whatever the volume: rent, insurance, salaries, depreciation. The split matters because every unit sold contributes the gap between its selling price and its own variable cost toward the pile of fixed costs — that gap is the CONTRIBUTION per unit.

Take the standard drill case with given totals. A factory sells its product at thirty-five rand a unit; variable costs run at twenty-three rand a unit; fixed costs for the year are R540 000. Contribution per unit: thirty-five less twenty-three — twelve rand. BREAK-EVEN POINT: fixed costs divided by contribution — five hundred and forty thousand over twelve — forty-five thousand units. Below forty-five thousand units the year runs at a loss; at forty-five thousand it breaks exactly even; every unit beyond adds twelve rand of pure profit. If the factory produced and sold forty-eight thousand units, it sits three thousand units above break-even — a cushion worth stating in every answer, because the interpretation question asks whether the business is safe, not merely where the line falls.

Work the levers of the formula, because good questions always do: push the price up and contribution widens, break-even falls; let materials become dearer and contribution narrows, break-even climbs — at a variable cost of twenty-five rand the contribution shrinks to ten and break-even rises to fifty-four thousand, ABOVE current sales; add fixed costs — a new machine, a bigger building — and the pile to be covered grows. Every management decision moves the line.

Then the ETHICS of making things, which the syllabus names deliberately. Product QUALITY and product AGE: shipping substandard or expired goods — bread past its date, brake pads that crack — trades customer safety for margin. RAW MATERIALS sourced honestly: no illegally harvested timber, no smuggled imports; and choosing LOCAL suppliers strengthens the very economy that buys your product. PRICE FIXING: rivals agreeing on prices is collusion against the consumer and it is illegal. THEFT and FRAUD on the factory floor — leather walking out the gate, hours clocked but not worked, production figures inflated. INTERNAL CONTROLS answer each risk: materials issued only against signed requisitions, wages tied to clock cards, output counted by someone independent of the people who report it, off-cuts and waste tracked — because materials that vanish without record are the factory's version of a stock deficit.

The final questions of this part are with you now: costs re-sorted by behaviour, break-even computed and cushioned, the levers argued, and every ethical risk paired with its control.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole factory again, shrunk to a street corner — because everything a production cost statement says, Ma Zondi's vetkoek stand says before the morning rush is over.

## Subtopic: Ma Zondi's Vetkoek Stand

Ma Zondi fries vetkoek and sells them filled with mince outside the train station. Watch her stand with accounting eyes and the entire factory vocabulary shows up before eight o'clock.

The flour, yeast, oil and mince that BECOME the vetkoek — direct materials. Her nephew Bongani, hired to mix and shape dough all morning — direct labour: the hands that make the product. But the stand needs more than hands and flour: the gas bottle, the monthly stall fee, the scouring pads, the deep oil that fries a hundred batches — nobody on earth can say which vetkoek used which rand of gas. Those are the overheads: real costs, spread across everything, pinned to nothing.

Now add it the factory way. Materials plus Bongani's wages — prime cost, the spending that touches the product. Plus the stand's overheads — the morning's manufacturing cost.

Look around the stand at nine o'clock and find the THREE stocks. In the crate: flour and mince not yet used — raw materials. On the tray: shaped dough waiting its turn in the oil — work-in-progress. In the warmer: golden vetkoek ready to sell — finished goods. Three stages, three values, and money sitting quietly in every one.

And one line Ma Zondi never blurs: the taxi fare to town to fetch serviettes, the counter book she records orders in — that is not the STAND's cost of making anything; that is the cost of running the show. Making costs build the product; office costs run the business; selling costs move the product to the customer. Three addresses, and every rand she spends lives at exactly one of them.

Questions on this piece are with you now: her costs sorted direct, indirect and beyond-the-stand, and the three stocks spotted on the corner.

## Subtopic: What One Vetkoek Costs

Ma Zondi's granddaughter, busy with grade 12, asks the sharpest question of the month: Gogo, what does ONE vetkoek actually cost you? She laughs — but the month's numbers can answer.

This month the stand used seven thousand three hundred rand of ingredients — used, carefully: she bought eight thousand five hundred rand's worth, but one thousand two hundred still sits in the pantry, and the pantry costs nothing to THIS month's vetkoek. Bongani's wages: four thousand two hundred. Overheads — gas, stall fee, odds and ends: three thousand two hundred. Stand total: fourteen thousand seven hundred.

One adjustment, and it is the clever one. On the first of the month a tray of shaped dough stood over from last month — three hundred rand of last month's spending — which this month fried and sold: count it in. On the last day of the month, another tray of dough waits unfried — six hundred rand of this month's spending that has produced nothing sellable yet: leave it out. Finished production for the month: fourteen thousand seven hundred plus three hundred less six hundred — fourteen thousand four hundred rand.

The month finished one thousand two hundred vetkoek. So: fourteen thousand four hundred divided by one thousand two hundred — TWELVE RAND a vetkoek. That number is the whole business in one figure. Selling at eighteen rand, she clears six rand of gross margin on each. If the mince price climbs and the unit cost creeps to fourteen, she sees it the same week — and decides: lift the price, find a cheaper butcher, or accept a thinner margin. A business that never computes its unit cost makes that decision blind, usually by accident, usually too late.

And notice what the granddaughter's question really taught: money SPENT this month and production FINISHED this month are not the same thing. The pantry and the dough tray are where the difference hides. That is the entire production cost statement, standing on a street corner.

Your questions for this part are up now: used against bought, the dough-tray adjustment both ways, and the twelve-rand unit cost put to work.

## Subtopic: How Many Must She Sell Before She Smiles?

Ma Zondi's last lesson is the one that decides whether the stand survives winter: how many vetkoek must she sell in a month simply to cover herself?

Sort her costs by how they BEHAVE, not where they live. Every filled vetkoek she makes eats about ten rand of flour, mince, oil and packet — costs that come and go with each unit: variable. And every month, sell or starve, the stall fee takes four thousand eight hundred rand — the cost that arrives like the calendar: fixed.

Each vetkoek sells for eighteen rand and carries ten rand of its own costs, so each one CONTRIBUTES eight rand toward that fixed pile. Now the only division in the topic: four thousand eight hundred of fixed costs, eight rand of contribution — she must sell SIX HUNDRED vetkoek a month before the stand has paid for itself. Number six hundred and one is the first one that feeds the family.

Prove it, because proving it is how the formula sticks: six hundred sold at eighteen rand is ten thousand eight hundred in; ingredients for six hundred at ten rand is six thousand; stall fee four thousand eight hundred. Ten thousand eight hundred in, ten thousand eight hundred out — zero. Break-even means exactly that: the smile starts at six hundred and one.

Now move her levers, one at a time. Mince prices rise and each vetkoek eats twelve rand: contribution drops to six, and break-even jumps to eight hundred — two hundred extra sales for the same zero. She lifts her price to twenty rand: contribution ten, break-even falls to four hundred and eighty. She considers a second stand at the taxi rank — four thousand eight hundred more in fixed fees — and her break-even doubles to one thousand two hundred: growth is wonderful, but the pile to cover grows before the first extra vetkoek is sold. Every decision she could make moves that one number, which is why managers compute it before deciding, not after.

And the ethics she will not trade: yesterday's vetkoek sold as fresh buys one good week and loses the station crowd forever; agreeing prices with the pap-and-steak seller two tables down is collusion, not friendship; and if the flour starts vanishing when a new helper joins, Ma Zondi counts bags against batches — requisitions and production counts, stand-sized.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the behaviour sort, break-even proved at six hundred, the levers moved one at a time, and the ethics that keep the stand standing. Your final exams will dress this factory in bigger numbers — the machinery underneath is Ma Zondi's, exactly.
