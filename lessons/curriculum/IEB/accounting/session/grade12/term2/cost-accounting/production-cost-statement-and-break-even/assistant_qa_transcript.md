**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the work-in-progress adjustment keeps flipping in my head. Why is OPENING work-in-progress ADDED but CLOSING work-in-progress SUBTRACTED? It feels backwards — surely what you start with is already counted?

**Tutor:** Anchor yourself to what the statement is trying to output, and the directions lock into place. The production cost statement does not measure money spent this year — it measures the cost of goods FINISHED this year. Now interrogate each tray of half-made goods against that goal. The opening work-in-progress — Khanya's R40 000 — is spending from LAST year sitting half-finished on the floor. This year the factory completed those pairs, so their cost belongs to this year's finished output — but that R40 000 appears nowhere in this year's materials, labour or overheads, because it was spent last year. So it must be ADDED, or this year's finished goods are undervalued. The closing work-in-progress — the R80 000 — is the mirror image: spending that IS inside this year's totals, for shoes that did NOT get finished. Leave it in and you claim finished production you have not achieved, so it must be SUBTRACTED. If the flip still threatens you under pressure, run the sanity check with extremes: imagine a factory that spent a million rand and finished nothing — everything still half-made. Total manufacturing cost one million, closing work-in-progress one million, cost of production of finished goods: zero. The formula tells exactly the truthful story. The same in-and-out logic runs the raw materials note — what remains in the storeroom has not been used — and the finished goods calculation — what remains in the warehouse has not been sold. One principle, three stocks: only what LEFT each stage counts for that stage.

***

**Mandy:** Next: fixed costs versus factory overheads — are those the same thing? Rent seems to be both, and I have seen depreciation appear in both lists too.

**Tutor:** They are two different SORTINGS of the same rands, made for two different purposes, and untangling them is a genuinely grade-12 insight. The production cost statement sorts by LOCATION AND FUNCTION: did this cost happen in the factory, the office, or the selling effort? That sorting answers a stewardship question — what did it cost to make the goods? Break-even analysis sorts the very same costs by BEHAVIOUR: does this cost grow with each extra unit, or does it arrive regardless of volume? That sorting answers a decision question — how does profit respond to output? Because the sortings are independent, every combination exists. Factory rent is a factory overhead by location AND a fixed cost by behaviour. Direct materials are a factory cost by location and variable by behaviour. Sales commission lives outside the factory — selling and distribution — yet it is variable, growing with every sale. Office rent is administration and fixed. Even a single account can straddle the line: factory electricity has a fixed service charge plus a usage component that rises with production. So when a question says classify, your first move is to identify WHICH sorting it wants — the statement's three-way function split, or the break-even two-way behaviour split. A student who answers the wrong sorting loses marks while being factually correct, which is the most avoidable loss there is. And the favourite bridge between the two worlds: depreciation on factory machinery is an overhead in the statement and fixed in break-even; depreciation on delivery vehicles is selling and distribution in the statement, and typically fixed as well — location changes, behaviour does not have to.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: Khanya's unit cost is R25 and each pair sells for R35, so every pair seems to make R10. Why does the break-even calculation use a contribution of R12 and not R10? Which number is the real profit per pair?

**Tutor:** Beautiful catch — the gap between those numbers is exactly the difference between the two costing worlds, and seeing it clearly puts you ahead of most of the country. The R25 unit cost is TOTAL production cost per pair: it includes a slice of the factory's FIXED costs — rent, depreciation, supervision — averaged across the 50 000 pairs actually produced. The R10 margin you computed is therefore price minus a figure that already carries fixed costs inside it. Contribution works differently: it is price minus VARIABLE cost only — the R23 of materials, per-unit labour, commission and packaging that each additional pair genuinely causes. The R12 contribution answers the marginal question: if we sell ONE MORE pair, how much extra money appears to help cover the fixed pile? Fixed costs do not grow with that extra pair, so they must stay out of the per-unit figure and sit instead as the lump the contributions must climb over — that is precisely the architecture of the break-even formula. Here is the subtlety worth an essay mark: the R25 unit cost is only true AT one volume. Produce 50 000 pairs and fixed costs spread thin; produce 25 000 and the same fixed rands double up per pair — the average cost rises with no change in efficiency at all. Contribution per pair, by contrast, stays R12 at any volume, which is what makes it the reliable tool for decisions about volume, pricing and extra orders. Both numbers are real; they answer different questions. Average cost tells you what production DID cost; contribution tells you what one more pair WILL bring.

**Mandy:** Last one: the ethics list feels bolted on — quality, expired goods, price fixing, theft. Does any of that actually connect to the calculations we just learned, or is it a separate paragraph to memorise?

**Tutor:** It connects through the numbers themselves — every ethical failure on that list is visible somewhere in the statements you now know how to build, and strong questions are increasingly written that way. Track each one. Product quality: a manager chasing a lower unit cost can substitute cheaper raw materials — the direct materials line falls, unit cost improves, and the break-even looks healthier — while the product quietly becomes dangerous or shoddy. The calculation rewarded the decision; only ethics asks what was traded away. Product age: pushing expired stock out of finished goods avoids a write-off that would raise cost of sales — the honest entry is the write-off, and hiding it overstates profit exactly like the stock manipulation you met in inventory valuation. Price fixing: if competitors agree to hold prices up, the contribution per unit widens and break-even falls — the arithmetic celebrates while the consumer is being robbed; that is why it is illegal, not merely impolite. Theft of materials: watch the materials note — if leather is vanishing, materials USED rises against unchanged production, so unit cost creeps up; a manager who computes unit cost monthly spots the leak that a manager watching only total spending misses. Inflated production figures: overstate the pairs produced and the unit cost falls flatteringly — which is why production counts should come from someone independent of the people whose performance they measure, the factory's own division of duties. So read the ethics list as the shadow side of every formula in the lesson: each number that can be computed can be manipulated, and each manipulation has a control. Write THAT connection into an answer — this failure would appear in this line of the statement, and this control prevents it — and you are answering at the level where the top marks live.
