# Part 1 — Expert

Reconciliation is accounting's habit of never trusting a single version of the truth. The bank keeps one record of your money, you keep another; the supplier keeps one record of your debt, you keep another; the debtors' ledger keeps a hundred small stories, the control account keeps their total. Grade 12 does not just prepare these reconciliations — grade 10 and 11 did that — it INTERPRETS them: what does an old outstanding deposit whisper, what does a supplier's statement dispute prove, what does an aging debtors' book cost? Reconciliation questions are where arithmetic and suspicion work together, and this lesson trains both.

## Subtopic: The Bank Reconciliation, Read for Meaning

The mechanics first, quickly, because they carry the interpretation. Masakhane Traders' bank statement shows a favourable balance of R21 400 at month-end. The business's own bank account in the ledger shows R26 900. Neither is wrong; they are out of step. A deposit of R12 700 made on the last day appears in the books but not yet on the statement — add it to the statement side. Electronic payments of R7 200, released on the last afternoon, have not yet gone through the bank — subtract them. Statement balance 21 400 plus 12 700 less 7 200 — R26 900, agreeing with the books. The reconciliation statement is that bridge, rebuilt every month.

Now the grade 12 skill: read the bridge for TROUBLE. An outstanding deposit is normal for a day or two — the timing gap between the till and the bank. But an outstanding deposit still missing WEEKS later is a red flag with one common explanation: the money was received, recorded, and never banked — someone is using cash and covering the hole with the next receipts, a fraud called ROLLING of cash. The reconciliation is where it becomes visible, which is why the same person must never both handle deposits and prepare the reconciliation.

Other flags with meanings. A payment outstanding for months: possibly a lost or stale instrument — after a long enough delay it must be cancelled and written back. Post-dated instruments received: not yet money, and not yet recordable as receipts. Bank charges and interest appearing only on the statement: they must be brought INTO the books via the bank account before reconciling — the reconciliation statement itself only ever carries genuine timing differences, never items the books simply have not recorded yet.

And read the direction of the balance itself: a persistent overdraft, growing month on month, is the liquidity story told in bank language.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the bridge rebuilt from 21 400 to 26 900, each red flag matched to its meaning, and books-side items separated from statement-side items.

## Subtopic: Reconciling the Creditors' Statement

Every month a supplier sends a STATEMENT: their record of your account. Masakhane's ledger says it owes Siyakha Wholesalers R33 400; Siyakha's statement demands R52 900. A R19 500 argument — and the reconciliation settles it item by item, asking of each difference: whose record is missing what?

Item one: an invoice for R5 800 — goods delivered late in the month, statement has it, ledger does not. The goods are real; the ledger must catch up. ADD R5 800 to the ledger side: 33 400 becomes 39 200.

Item two: Masakhane paid R7 600 by EFT on the twenty-ninth. The statement, drawn on the twenty-sixth, could not have seen it. SUBTRACT from the statement side: 52 900 becomes 45 300.

Item three: a settlement discount of R850 that Siyakha granted but has not yet processed. Subtract from the statement: 44 450.

Item four: Masakhane returned damaged goods and recorded a debit note of R1 750; Siyakha has not yet passed the credit. Subtract from the statement: 42 700.

Item five: an invoice for R3 500 on the statement that belongs to a DIFFERENT customer — Siyakha's posting error. Subtract: 39 200.

Both sides now stand at R39 200 — the true debt. Notice the discipline that got us there: each difference was assigned to exactly one side — the side whose record was incomplete or wrong. Errors in YOUR books are corrected in your books; timing gaps and errors in THEIR records go to the statement's side of the reconciliation. And the corrected ledger figure, R39 200, is what belongs in trade payables.

Interpretation lives here too. A supplier's statement consistently showing charges you never ordered demands scrutiny, not habit. Discounts repeatedly lost because payments went out a day late is money burned by sloppiness. And paying the statement's raw total without reconciling — many businesses do — is how you pay for another customer's goods.

Stop for this section's questions now — the five items assigned to their sides, both columns landing on 39 200, and the cost of not reconciling.

## Subtopic: Control Accounts and the Debtors' Age Analysis

Inside the business, the same two-record logic guards the debtors. The DEBTORS' CONTROL account in the general ledger carries the total owed by all customers; the DEBTORS' LIST totals the individual accounts in the debtors' ledger. Same reality, two routes — so their totals must agree, and every difference is an error with a location. Masakhane's control account shows R96 000; the list totals R94 200. Hunting reveals an invoice for R1 800 posted to the control account via the journals but never entered in the customer's personal account. Correct the list: R96 000 both sides. The pattern generalises: errors in the journals' totals hit the CONTROL account; errors in posting to individual accounts hit the LIST. Locate, correct, agree — and the same machinery runs for creditors.

Now the tool that turns the debtors' book into management information: the AGE ANALYSIS. It sorts each debtor's balance by how long it has been owed. Masakhane's R96 000 book, on 30-day terms: CURRENT — R48 000, which is 50 percent; THIRTY DAYS — R24 000, 25 percent; SIXTY DAYS — R14 400, 15 percent; NINETY DAYS AND OLDER — R9 600, 10 percent.

Read it like a doctor reads a chart. Seventy-five percent of the book is within terms — current plus thirty days. But TWENTY-FIVE percent is overdue, and R9 600 has been owed for three months or more. Old debt is sick debt: the longer a debt ages, the less likely it pays, so the ninety-day column is where bad debts are born — and where the provision for bad debts should be looking. The responses, in escalating order: statements and reminders; interest charged on overdue accounts, if the credit agreement allows; suspension of further credit to the account; and finally attorneys or debt collection, weighing cost against the debt. Prevention beats collection: credit checks before opening accounts, credit limits enforced by the system, and invoices issued promptly — a debtor cannot pay an invoice that arrived late.

And judge the book against the business's own terms: on 30-day terms, a debtors' collection period near thirty days is health; near sixty, the business is an unwilling bank, financing its customers interest-free while possibly paying overdraft rates on its own cash.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the R1 800 located and corrected, the four age columns with their percentages, and the escalating responses in order.

## Subtopic: Ethics, Fraud and the Paper Trail

Every reconciliation in this lesson is also a fraud-detection instrument, and the syllabus names the frauds.

ROLLING CASH — the outstanding deposit that never lands. The cashier takes Monday's takings; when Tuesday's takings arrive, they are banked as Monday's; the hole rolls forward forever, visible only as deposits that are always one beat late. The control: separate the receiving of cash from the banking of it and from the reconciling of it, and investigate ANY deposit outstanding beyond a banking day.

INVOICE FRAUD on creditors — paying statements without reconciling invites fictitious invoices: a supplier, or an insider with a fake supplier, bills for goods never delivered. The control: three-way matching — order, delivery note, invoice — before any payment; the reconciliation catches what slipped through.

WRITING OFF FRIENDLY DEBTS — a clerk with power over the debtors' ledger writes off a friend's healthy debt as bad. The control: write-offs authorised by someone independent of the ledger, and the age analysis reviewed by management — a healthy account jumping straight to written-off leaves a visible scar.

KITING and date games at month-end — moving money between accounts to fatten the visible balance on reporting day. The reconciliation's memory defeats it: items that reverse right after month-end, month after month, form a pattern an auditor reads at a glance.

Behind all four stands one principle: SEGREGATION plus an independent eye. The person who handles the asset must not keep its record; the person who keeps the record must not reconcile it; and someone senior must actually READ the reconciliation — because a reconciliation nobody reviews is a lock nobody turns. Late payment has a price beyond fraud, too: interest charged, discounts lost, and a damaged CREDIT RATING that quietly raises the cost of everything the business buys on credit. Paying on the last day within terms — not before, never after — is not meanness; it is treasury discipline.

The final questions of this part are with you now: each fraud matched to the reconciliation that exposes it, the three-way match, and segregation stated as one principle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson from one week in a household — because everyone already runs three reconciliations without calling them that.

## Subtopic: Why Your Phone and Your Notebook Disagree

You keep a careful notebook of your money. Friday night it says R26 900. Your banking app says R21 400. Nobody has robbed you — the two records are keeping time differently.

Think about what each one knows. You banked R12 700 late this afternoon — YOUR notebook knows; the bank will only show it Monday. You sent two payments totalling R7 200 tonight — your notebook subtracted them the moment you pressed send; they have not landed yet. So take the app's number, add the deposit it has not seen, subtract the payments it has not felt: 21 400 plus 12 700 minus 7 200 — R26 900. The notebook was right; the app was right; the BRIDGE between them is the reconciliation, and you just built one.

Now the part that makes this a grade 12 topic and not a life-skills chat: the bridge is also a lie detector. A deposit that stays on the bridge for one weekend is timing. A deposit still sitting there three weeks later means the money never reached the bank — and in a business, that usually means someone's hand is in the till, covering yesterday's hole with today's takings. The fraud even has a name: rolling the cash. It is invisible in the daily records and OBVIOUS on the bridge — which is exactly why the person who banks the money must never be the person who builds the bridge, and why the owner must actually look at it every month.

Small print your notebook must also learn: bank charges, and interest, appear first on the app's side. Those are not bridge material — the notebook simply has not heard the news yet, so the notebook gets updated first, and only true timing differences ride the bridge.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the bridge built both ways, timing separated from trouble, and the deposit that stayed too long.

## Subtopic: The Supplier's Statement Says You Owe More

Month-end, and Siyakha Wholesalers sends its statement: you owe R52 900. Your own book says R33 400. Before anyone shouts, sit down with both records and go difference by difference — each one belongs to somebody.

Their statement lists an invoice of R5 800 for the goods that arrived on the thirtieth. True delivery — YOUR book is behind. Your book catches up: you owe R39 200 so far. You paid R7 600 on the twenty-ninth; their statement, printed on the twenty-sixth, could not know. Their side comes down. The R850 discount they promised for early settlement is not on the statement yet — their side comes down again. The damaged goods you sent back, R1 750 — they have not processed the credit — down again. And then the gem: an invoice for R3 500 that is not yours at all; someone at Siyakha posted another customer's purchase to your account. Down once more. Their side: 52 900 minus 7 600 minus 850 minus 1 750 minus 3 500 — R39 200. Both records now say the same thing, and THAT is the amount you pay.

Now count what the exercise was worth. Skip the reconciliation and pay the statement, and you pay R52 900 — R13 700 too much, including R3 500 of someone else's shopping. The reconciliation took twenty minutes and earned more than most days at the till. Businesses that pay statements unchecked are donating money to sloppy suppliers — and worse, to anyone who has discovered that they pay unchecked, because fake invoices flow toward businesses that never look.

One habit rule from the whole story: never pay a statement; pay a RECONCILED statement.

Your questions for this part are up now: each difference assigned to its side, both records at 39 200, and the R13 700 saved.

## Subtopic: The List of Who Owes You, and For How Long

Last scene: your side business sells homemade lunches to people at work, on credit. Nine thousand rand is owed to you. Fine — but your notebook has a second question: owed for HOW LONG?

Sort it. R4 500 is this month's — current, healthy. R2 250 is one month old — inside your rules, since you give a month. R1 350 is two months old — overdue. And R900 has been owed for three months or more — the colleague who smiles in the corridor and looks away in the parking lot. That sorted list is an age analysis, and the reading is universal: the older the debt, the sicker it is. Money owed three months rarely arrives whole. In a business, that oldest column is where the accountant points the provision for bad debts — the honest admission that some of it is already lost.

What do you do about the sick columns? Exactly what a business does, in the same order. First the reminder — the statement, the message. Then the price: interest on what is overdue, if your terms said so from the start. Then the tap closes: no more credit for that account until it is settled. And only then the heavy step — collection, if the debt is worth the trouble. But the real cure happened earlier, before the debt was born: decide WHO gets credit — the credit check; decide HOW MUCH — the limit; and record the sale the moment it happens, because a bill delivered late is a bill paid later.

And the mirror image, the last idea of the lesson: somewhere, YOU are a line in a supplier's age analysis. Pay late and you drift into their sixty-day column, and their system quietly reclassifies you: interest, tighter limits, cash up front. That reputation is a credit rating, and it prices everything a business borrows or buys. The whole topic in one sentence: keep two records of every relationship, make them agree monthly, and read the ages — because the record you neglect is the money you lose.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the four ages sorted and totalled, the escalation in order, and the mirror image stated. Reconciliation marks are among the most reliably earnable in your final exams — method, side, agree, interpret.
