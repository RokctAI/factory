# Part 1 — Expert

Ask a shop what its stock is worth and the answer sounds simple — until prices start moving. Buy identical electric fans at forty-two rand in February, forty-five in May, forty-eight in August and fifty in November, sell most of them, and the ones left on the shelf carry no name tags saying which delivery they came from. What are THEY worth? The answer decides cost of sales, gross profit, and the balance sheet in one stroke — and the syllabus offers three defensible answers: specific identification, first-in-first-out, and weighted average. This lesson works all three to the rand on one set of figures, then turns to the discipline around them: validation of stock, the GAAP principles that referee the choice, and the ethics and internal controls that keep the stockroom honest. Inventory valuation appears in your final exams almost without fail, usually with the arithmetic and the ethics stitched together.

## Subtopic: Three Methods, One Shelf of Stock

First the record-keeping frame. Under the PERPETUAL system, stock is a running account: every purchase goes into Trading Stock, every sale moves cost out to Cost of Sales immediately, and the account claims to know the stock figure at any moment. Under the PERIODIC system, purchases pile up in a Purchases account and nobody computes cost of sales until stock is physically counted at year-end: opening stock plus purchases less closing stock equals cost of sales. Either way, the counted stock must be given a VALUE — and that is where the methods part company.

Method one: SPECIFIC IDENTIFICATION. Each item's actual cost is known and tracked — appropriate where items are few, expensive and distinguishable: vehicles by chassis number, jewellery, machinery. A car dealer values each car on the floor at what THAT car cost. Nobody tracks individual boxes of tea this way; the bookkeeping would cost more than the tea.

Method two: FIRST-IN-FIRST-OUT — FIFO. Assume the OLDEST stock is sold first, so the stock remaining on the shelf carries the NEWEST prices. The assumption usually matches physical reality — shops rotate stock so the old sells before it fades on the shelf — but understand precisely: FIFO is an assumption about COSTS for valuation, not a claim about which physical box left first.

Method three: WEIGHTED AVERAGE. Pool the cost of everything available, divide by the units available, and value every unit — sold or remaining — at that average. One price, no history.

And the referee: GAAP. The HISTORICAL COST principle anchors stock at what it cost, not what it will sell for. PRUDENCE steps in when stock is damaged or obsolete — value at the lower of cost and net realisable value, never anticipating profit. And CONSISTENCY: pick a method and keep it, year after year, because comparability dies the moment a business switches methods to suit its story. A change is permitted only for good reason, disclosed openly.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: perpetual against periodic, the three methods matched to the stock they suit, and the three GAAP principles named to their jobs.

## Subtopic: FIFO Worked to the Rand

Radebe Electrical sells one model of desk fan. The year's stock story, spoken slowly: opening stock 250 units that cost forty-two rand each — R10 500. Purchases: May, 500 units at forty-five rand — R22 500; August, 350 units at forty-eight rand — R16 800; November, 150 units at fifty rand — R7 500. Total available: 1 250 units costing R57 300. During the year 950 units were sold at seventy rand each — sales of R66 500 — leaving 300 units on the shelf at year-end.

FIFO values those 300 remaining units at the NEWEST prices, counted backwards from the last delivery. The November delivery supplies the first 150 units at fifty rand — R7 500. Still 150 units to account for, drawn from the August delivery at forty-eight rand — R7 200. Closing stock: seven thousand five hundred plus seven thousand two hundred — R14 700.

Cost of sales follows by subtraction: cost of everything available, R57 300, less what remains, R14 700 — R42 600. Gross profit: sales of R66 500 less R42 600 — R23 900.

Notice what FIFO did in a year of RISING prices: it left the newest, most expensive costs on the balance sheet and sent the oldest, cheapest costs to cost of sales. Consequence one: closing stock sits close to current replacement prices — the balance sheet is up to date. Consequence two: cost of sales is low, so gross profit is HIGH — flattering, and taxed accordingly. Reverse the price trend and every consequence reverses with it. Questions love asking which method shows the higher profit — the answer is always conditional on the direction prices moved, and saying so is the mark.

The layered counting is where marks leak: the 300 units must be built from the last deliveries backwards — 150 at fifty rand first, THEN 150 at forty-eight. Counting forward from the opening stock, or mixing layers, is the classic error.

Stop for this section's questions now — the 300 units layered from the newest deliveries, cost of sales by subtraction, and the rising-price consequences stated.

## Subtopic: Weighted Average Worked to the Rand

Same shelf, same year, second answer. The weighted average method refuses to guess which units remain. Instead: pool everything. Total cost available, R57 300; total units available, 1 250. Average cost per unit: fifty-seven thousand three hundred divided by one thousand two hundred and fifty — R45,84.

Closing stock: 300 units at R45,84 — R13 752. Cost of sales: 950 units sold at R45,84 — R43 548. Check the pair the way a careful accountant would: thirteen thousand seven hundred and fifty-two plus forty-three thousand five hundred and forty-eight is exactly R57 300, the full pool. If your two figures do not rebuild the pool, one of them is wrong.

Gross profit: 66 500 less 43 548 — R22 952.

Now set the two methods side by side, because the comparison IS the understanding. FIFO closing stock R14 700 against weighted average R13 752 — a difference of R948. FIFO gross profit R23 900 against R22 952 — the same R948. Not a coincidence, a law: whatever value sits in closing stock has, by that exact amount, been kept OUT of cost of sales. Value the shelf higher and the year looks more profitable; value it lower and profit shrinks. Stock valuation is profit measurement — the same rands, standing on the shelf or standing in the profit line.

Two cautions. WEIGHTED average, not simple average: never average the four PRICES — forty-two, forty-five, forty-eight and fifty average to R46,25, the wrong answer, because it ignores how many units arrived at each price. Weight by units, always. And carriage on purchases joins the cost of stock — if a delivery costs two rand per unit to transport, that two rand is part of the unit's cost in the pool, not an expense on its own.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the pool averaged by units, the closing-stock-plus-cost-of-sales rebuild, and the R948 explained as one law.

## Subtopic: Validation, Ethics and Control of Stock

Valuation assumed one quiet fact: that 300 units actually exist. VALIDATION is the discipline of checking. A physical stock count at year-end — and cyclical counts through the year — sets the book figure against the floor figure. Radebe's records say 300 fans; the count finds 275. Twenty-five units are MISSING — at the weighted average cost of R45,84, a trading stock deficit of R1 146, written off as an expense. The deficit is not a rounding nuisance; it is information. Theft, breakage, unrecorded sales, or a receiving clerk signing for deliveries that never arrived — the deficit is where every one of those stories first surfaces.

The INTERNAL CONTROLS that keep the number small, each pinned to the risk it answers. Division of duties: the person ordering stock, the person receiving it, the person keeping the records and the person counting must not be one person — a buyer who also receives and records can invent deliveries indefinitely. Documentation: pre-numbered orders, delivery notes checked against invoices, so every unit that entered can be traced. Physical security: locked storerooms, restricted access, cameras at dispatch. Independent counts: counters who are not the storeman, rotating and unannounced. And management review: deficits tracked month by month, because a rising trend is a control failing in real time.

Then the ETHICS the syllabus attaches, sharpened by the valuation methods themselves. Because closing stock value flows straight into profit, stock is a favourite instrument of manipulation: OVERSTATE the count or the value, and profit inflates — flattering managers chasing bonuses, deceiving lenders and shareholders. UNDERSTATE it, and profit shrinks — understating tax owed to SARS. Switching valuation methods in a convenient year is the polite version of the same sin, which is exactly why CONSISTENCY is a GAAP principle and why a method change must be disclosed. An accountant instructed to nudge the stock figure is being instructed to lie in rands — and the professional answer is refusal, whatever the seniority of the voice instructing.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the deficit computed and read as information, controls pinned to risks, and stock manipulation traced from shelf to profit line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from a school snack stand's fridge — because every valuation argument in this lesson happens in miniature the day prices go up at the wholesaler.

## Subtopic: The Fridge That Ate the Profits

Karabo runs a snack stand. Juice boxes cost her eight rand each at the wholesaler in the first term, and she has been selling them at fourteen — six rand profit a box, life is simple. Then the new season's price list arrives, the wholesaler's price climbs to ten rand, and Karabo's fridge becomes a philosophy problem.

She has 35 boxes in the fridge. Some were bought at eight rand, some at ten, and cold juice boxes do not carry receipts. Her cousin asks a simple bookkeeping question: Karabo, what is the stock in your fridge WORTH? And Karabo realises she has three honest options. She could say: I know exactly which box is which — true for her two trays of dated yoghurt tubs with labels on the lids, hopeless for thirty-five identical juice boxes. She could say: I always sell the oldest boxes first — the front row is old stock, so whatever is LEFT must be the newest, the ten-rand boxes. Or she could shrug and say: they cost me eight and ten in some mixture — call every box the average of what the whole batch cost.

Hold onto why the question matters at all. Whatever value Karabo puts on those 35 boxes decides how much profit she made — because boxes in the fridge are cost she has NOT yet used up, and boxes sold are cost she has. The fridge and the profit line share one pot of rands. Value the fridge high, and this year's profit rises with it. Value it low, profit falls. Same fridge, same boxes — the method chooses where the rands stand.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the three honest options named, which stock suits each, and why the fridge's value and the profit are one decision.

## Subtopic: Two Honest Answers to One Question

Run Karabo's numbers, small enough to hold in your head. She started the season with nothing, bought 45 boxes at eight rand — R360 — then 30 boxes at ten rand — R300. Available: 75 boxes that cost R660. She sold 40 boxes at fourteen rand each — R560 in the tin. Thirty-five boxes remain.

Answer one, first-in-first-out. The oldest boxes sold first, so the fridge holds the newest: all 30 of the ten-rand boxes — R300 — plus 5 of the eight-rand boxes — R40. Fridge: R340. Cost of the boxes she sold: R660 less R340 — R320. Profit: R560 less R320 — R240.

Answer two, weighted average. Pool it: R660 across 75 boxes is — careful, weight by boxes, not by prices — R8,80 a box. Fridge: 35 boxes at R8,80 — R308. Cost of sales: R660 less R308 — R352. Profit: R560 less R352 — R208.

Two honest methods, R32 apart. Where did the R32 go? Look at the two fridge values: R340 against R308 — the same R32. Whatever the fridge holds, the profit line released. That is the whole mechanism, in a snack stand.

Now the sentence that earns the interpretation mark, in Karabo's words: prices went UP this season, and the first-in-first-out fridge holds the NEWEST, priciest boxes — so it shows the higher stock value and the higher profit. If the wholesaler had CUT prices, the same method would show the lower profit. The method does not create or destroy a single rand across the years — every box's cost gets charged eventually — it only decides WHICH year carries it.

And the rule Karabo must now live by: pick one answer and keep it. A shopkeeper who values the fridge one way in a good year and another way in a lean year is not doing arithmetic anymore; she is telling stories.

Your questions for this part are up now: both answers computed on the 75 boxes, the R32 traced fridge-to-profit, and the rising-price sentence said properly.

## Subtopic: When the Count and the Book Disagree

One evening Karabo counts the fridge: 32 boxes. Her notebook says 35. Three boxes of profit walked off somewhere — a friend helping herself, a box given away and forgotten, a sale that never reached the notebook. At R8,80 a box, that is about R26 of stock deficit, written off, gone. Three boxes sounds small. Three boxes a week is more than a hundred and fifty boxes a year — weeks of profit, drunk by a ghost.

Scale Karabo up to a company with a warehouse and the ghost gets professional. So business builds fences, and each fence answers a specific trick. Different people order, receive, record and count — because one person doing all four can invent deliveries and drink the difference. Papers with numbers on them — orders, delivery notes, invoices — so every box that ever entered can be traced to a signature. Locks and cameras where the stock lives. Counts done by someone OTHER than the person who keeps the stock, on days nobody announced. And a manager who reads the deficit every month, because a small number growing is a fence failing.

Last, the dark side of the fridge, and it is a favourite grade 12 discussion. Because the fridge's value flows straight into profit, a dishonest owner has two temptations. Puff the fridge UP — count boxes that are not there, value them above cost — and the stand looks more profitable: banks lend, buyers overpay, bonuses get earned on fiction. Squash the fridge DOWN, and profit shrinks — along with the tax owed on it. Both are lies measured in rands, and both are usually caught the same way: by an independent count and a consistent method, which is precisely why the count and the consistency exist.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the deficit priced and scaled, the fences matched to the tricks, and the two temptations with the controls that catch them. Take the layering and the pooling with you — your final exams will hand you a shelf and moving prices, and now you own three honest answers.
