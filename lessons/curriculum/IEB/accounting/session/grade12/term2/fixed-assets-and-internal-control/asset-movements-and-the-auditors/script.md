# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 11 taught the mechanics of fixed assets — buying, depreciating, disposing. Grade 12 changes the question from HOW to SO WHAT: given the fixed asset note in front of you, what is it saying about the business? How old is the equipment, how long will it last, is the company replacing its assets fast enough — and who checks that the assets exist at all? That last question opens the audit content of the syllabus: how internal auditors gather evidence, how they sample, what their reports say, and how their role differs from the external auditor who signs the annual report. Interpretation of asset movements plus the audit process — that pairing turns up together again and again, and this lesson works both sides with one set of figures.

## Subtopic: The Fixed Asset Note and What Moves

The fixed asset note is a reconciliation: it starts with the carrying value at the beginning of the year, tracks every movement, and lands on the carrying value at the end. Speak the movements for Zenzele Couriers' vehicle fleet.

Opening position: vehicles at cost R1 800 000, accumulated depreciation R720 000 — opening carrying value R1 080 000. Movement one: ADDITIONS at cost — new vehicles bought during the year for R500 000. Movement two: DISPOSALS at carrying value — one vehicle sold; its cost was R300 000 and its accumulated depreciation R210 000, so a carrying value of R90 000 left the books. Movement three: DEPRECIATION for the year — R290 000 written off across the fleet. Closing carrying value: one million and eighty thousand, plus five hundred thousand, less ninety thousand, less two hundred and ninety thousand — R1 200 000.

Now verify it the way a careful answer does, through the cost and accumulated depreciation columns separately. Closing cost: one million eight hundred thousand plus additions of five hundred thousand less the disposal's cost of three hundred thousand — R2 000 000. Closing accumulated depreciation: seven hundred and twenty thousand plus the year's two hundred and ninety thousand less the two hundred and ten thousand that left with the disposal — R800 000. And two million less eight hundred thousand is R1 200 000 — the note agrees with itself. Every figure in the note is chained to the others, which is exactly why questions leave one cell blank and ask for it: any missing figure is recoverable from the rest.

One discipline point: additions enter at COST, disposals leave at CARRYING VALUE, and the two never swap. And if the sold vehicle fetched R75 000 against its carrying value of R90 000, the R15 000 loss on disposal belongs to the income statement — the note itself only records that R90 000 of carrying value left.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the note reconciled top to bottom, the two-column verification, and the missing-cell logic.

## Subtopic: Age, Lifespan and the Replacement Question

Now interpretation — three questions the note can answer if you interrogate it.

Question one: HOW OLD are the assets? Take Zenzele's sorting equipment: cost R1 200 000, depreciated straight-line at ten percent per year — an expected LIFESPAN of ten years, R120 000 of depreciation a year. Accumulated depreciation stands at R720 000. Divide: seven hundred and twenty thousand over one hundred and twenty thousand — SIX years of life consumed, on average. Age is accumulated depreciation divided by the annual charge — a formula worth memorising, because it turns two balance-sheet numbers into a story.

Question two: HOW LONG is left? Ten-year lifespan, six years used — four years remain. And now the note becomes management information: equipment with four years of life needs a replacement PLAN — budgeted, financed, scheduled — starting now, not in year ten. A question offering this data expects the recommendation, not just the six.

Question three: is the company REPLACING fast enough? Compare additions against depreciation. Zenzele added R500 000 of vehicles while consuming R290 000 through depreciation — additions comfortably exceed consumption, a fleet being renewed. The opposite signature — depreciation outrunning additions year after year — describes a business quietly eating its own capacity: profits look healthy precisely because little is spent, until the breakdowns arrive. Aging assets bring rising maintenance, downtime, safety risk and obsolete technology, and a student who connects those dots earns the interpretation marks.

Watch the limits of the arithmetic, because strong answers state them: these are AVERAGES across a class — a fleet can hold one new panel van and four wrecks and average out respectable. Fully depreciated assets still in use distort the age calculation. And straight-line rates assume the lifespan guess was right; disposal profits and losses are the running scorecard on that guess.

Stop for this section's questions now — age from the two depreciation figures, remaining life turned into a recommendation, and additions read against depreciation.

## Subtopic: Inside the Internal Audit

Somebody must confirm the assets behind these numbers exist, are used for business, and are guarded. That is INTERNAL AUDIT — employees of the company, reporting to management and the audit committee, checking controls all year round.

Their raw material is AUDIT EVIDENCE, and you must know the kinds: PHYSICAL INSPECTION — go to the yard, find the vehicle by registration, match it to the asset register; DOCUMENTATION — invoices proving cost, licence papers proving ownership, logbooks proving business use; OBSERVATION — watch the dispatch process actually run, not as the manual describes it; ENQUIRY and CONFIRMATION — ask staff, and confirm externally where possible; and RECALCULATION — recompute depreciation and the note's totals. Strong evidence is independent, documented and obtained directly by the auditor; a manager's verbal assurance is the weakest evidence there is.

Checking everything is impossible — Zenzele runs hundreds of assets — so auditors SAMPLE. RANDOM sampling gives every item an equal chance, free of the auditor's habits. SYSTEMATIC sampling takes every tenth item from the register — orderly coverage. STRATIFIED or judgement sampling weights the sample toward risk: the highest-value assets, the portable ones, the depot with last year's discrepancies. In practice auditors combine them: stratify first so the big rands are covered, then randomise within layers. A sample's job is to justify a conclusion about the WHOLE population, so its size and selection must be defensible.

The output is the INTERNAL AUDIT REPORT — typically: scope, what was tested; FINDINGS, the gap between what should happen and what does; RISK, why the gap matters in rands; and RECOMMENDATION, the control that closes it. Finding: six vehicles refuel without logbook entries. Risk: private use of company fuel, unmeasured. Recommendation: fuel cards matched to logbooks, exceptions reported monthly. Findings go to management and the audit committee — internal audit REPORTS weaknesses; fixing them is management's job, and ACCOUNTABLE MANAGEMENT of resources means management answers for what happens next.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the kinds of evidence ranked, the three sampling techniques matched to situations, and a finding written as gap, risk, recommendation.

## Subtopic: The Internal and the External Auditor

Two auditors, two jobs — and this distinction is asked so often that you should carry it ready-made.

The INTERNAL auditor is an EMPLOYEE of the company. Appointed by management or the board; reports to management and the audit committee; works ALL YEAR; and the scope is broad — any control, any process, any efficiency question: stock, cash, assets, procurement, fraud prevention. The purpose is to IMPROVE the business's own controls before problems grow.

The EXTERNAL auditor is INDEPENDENT of the company — a registered auditor in public practice. Appointed by the SHAREHOLDERS at the annual general meeting; reports to the shareholders; arrives principally around year-end; and the scope is focused — the ANNUAL FINANCIAL STATEMENTS, examined for fair presentation, ending in the audit opinion published with the annual report. The purpose is not to improve management but to give OUTSIDERS an independent verdict they can trust.

The two cooperate without merging. External auditors assess internal audit's work — strong internal controls, honestly reported, reduce the external testing needed; weak ones expand it. But the external auditor may never simply adopt internal findings, because internal audit's independence is limited by its own employment: an internal auditor pressing a finding that embarrasses a senior manager is testing the company's governance, and where that pressure fails, the audit committee — and ultimately the external auditor — is the backstop.

Complete the map with the third line of defence: ordinary INTERNAL CONTROLS — the asset register itself, authorisation of purchases, division of duties, physical safeguards — operated by management daily. Controls run continuously; internal audit checks them periodically; external audit verifies the results annually. Three layers, from inside out.

The final questions of this part are with you now: appointment, reporting line, timing, scope and purpose contrasted, and the three layers stacked in order.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson from a bakkie business in the family — because a fixed asset note is just the story of a fleet, and an audit is just a careful relative with a clipboard.

## Subtopic: The Bakkie Owner's Four Numbers

Your uncle hires out bakkies for small removals. Ask him about the fleet and he gives you four numbers without opening a file.

What the fleet cost me altogether: R1 800 000. What the years have used up: R720 000 — that is the wear the books have already charged. So what the books still carry: R1 080 000. And then the year happens to those numbers. He BUYS: R500 000 of new bakkies — the fleet's book value climbs by what they cost. He SELLS: one old bakkie, which the books carried at R90 000 — that amount walks out of the note, and whether he got more or less than ninety for it becomes a separate profit or loss line in the income statement. And the year WEARS the fleet: R290 000 of depreciation. End of story: one thousand and eighty, plus five hundred, minus ninety, minus two hundred and ninety — the fleet's book value ends the year at R1 200 000, in thousands.

That is the entire fixed asset note: begin, buy, sell, wear, end. Five beats. The favourite trick in any question is to hide one beat and ask for it — and because the five are chained, any one can be rebuilt from the other four. Practise reading the chain in both directions until a blank cell feels like an invitation.

One habit protects you from the classic mix-up: things ARRIVE at what they cost; things LEAVE at what the books still carried. Never the other way. The selling price never enters the note at all — it lives in the disposal workings.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the five beats in order, the blank cell rebuilt, and arrivals against departures.

## Subtopic: How Old Is the Fleet, Really?

Now the question your uncle actually loses sleep over: when do the bakkies DIE?

His loading equipment tells the story cleanly. It cost R1 200 000, and he writes off ten percent — R120 000 — each year, because machinery like this lasts about ten years. The wear-account has gathered R720 000. Do the division his accountant does: seven hundred and twenty thousand worn, at one hundred and twenty thousand a year — the equipment is SIX years old on average. Ten-year life, six consumed: four years left.

Four years is not a bookkeeping fact; it is a countdown. New equipment will cost serious money, and a business that waits for year ten to think about it will meet that bill broke. So the countdown becomes a plan: start a replacement fund, or arrange finance, or schedule replacements one machine at a time across the four years. When a question gives you cost, rate and accumulated depreciation, it is really asking: do the division, read the countdown, and SAY what the owner should do about it.

And here is the fleet-wide health check, one comparison: what he ADDED this year against what the year WORE. Added R500 000, wore R290 000 — he is putting capacity in faster than time takes it out; the fleet is getting younger. Flip it — wearing two ninety while adding fifty, year after year — and the fleet ages toward the cliff: breakdowns, repair bills, missed bookings, customers stranded. The cruel part is that the aging years LOOK profitable, because so little is being spent. Plenty of businesses have flattered themselves straight into a dead fleet.

One caution before the questions: these are averages. A fleet of one new bakkie and four wrecks averages out looking fine. Averages open the question; only the asset register — bakkie by bakkie — answers it.

Your questions for this part are up now: the age division, the countdown turned into a plan, and the adding-against-wearing health check.

## Subtopic: The Inside Checker and the Outside Referee

Your uncle's business grew — sixteen bakkies, three depots — and he can no longer see everything. So he hires your cousin Naledi, full-time, to CHECK things. Naledi is internal audit, and watch how she works, because her method is the syllabus.

She does not check every bakkie every week; she SAMPLES. Some days she draws registrations from a hat — random, so no depot can predict her. Some days she works down the register, every fourth vehicle — systematic, so over months everything gets covered. And she always checks the newest, most expensive bakkies, because that is where the big rands sit — sampling by risk. Then she gathers EVIDENCE, and she has a ranking: seeing the bakkie with her own eyes beats the depot manager's assurance; a fuel receipt beats a memory; watching the loading actually happen beats the procedure file. Look, read, watch, ask, recalculate — in roughly that order of trust.

When she finds something — six bakkies refuelling with no logbook entries — she writes it up in three moves: what the rule says against what actually happens; what it could cost in rands; and what would fix it. Then she gives the report to your uncle. Naledi never fixes things herself — she reports; the owner acts. If your uncle shrugs and files it, that is not Naledi failing — that is management failing, and it is management who answers for the fuel that keeps vanishing.

Once a year comes somebody different: an outside accountant nobody in the family employs. She is the external auditor — the referee. Chosen by the people whose MONEY is in the business, reporting to them, not to your uncle. She does not care whether depot two is efficient; she asks one question: do the financial statements fairly show the truth? She will glance at Naledi's files — good inside checking means less outside digging — but she signs her own name on her own opinion, because her value IS her independence.

Keep the full picture: locks, registers and rules working every day; Naledi testing them all year; the referee ruling once a year. Three layers, inside out.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: Naledi's sampling and evidence ranked, her three-move report, and the checker split from the referee. In your final exams this contrast is free marks — as long as you keep appointment, reporting line, timing and purpose straight.
