# Part 1 — Expert

At the close of its financial year a company must produce three documents. The statement of comprehensive income narrates the profit. The statement of financial position freezes the last midnight of the year into a single balanced picture. And the cash flow statement — the genuinely new grade 12 skill — answers the question neither of the others can: what actually happened to the money in the bank? Beneath the three sit the year-end adjustments and final accounts you built in earlier grades, now extended with the company-only entries — income tax, dividends, retained income — and behind them all stands the audit report, the outsider's verdict on whether the whole set deserves trust. One company carries every figure in this lesson: Zamani Limited, a clothing retailer. Follow each number until it lands, because every one of them lands somewhere.

## Subtopic: Year-End Adjustments and the Final Accounts

Nothing about the machinery is new; what is new is what the machinery must now carry. Adjustments exist to enforce MATCHING: this year's statements report this year's income and expenses exactly — accrued expenses brought in, prepaid amounts pushed out, income receivable recognised, income received in advance deferred, depreciation charged, trading stock deficits written off. Watch the PROVISION FOR BAD DEBTS especially: it is adjusted by its MOVEMENT, never rebuilt from scratch. Zamani's debtors of R280 000 justify a provision of R14 000; the account currently shows R17 000; so the adjustment is a DECREASE of R3 000, and only that R3 000 reaches the income statement — as income.

The final accounts then close the year in three steps. The TRADING account sets sales against cost of sales and produces gross profit. The PROFIT AND LOSS account gathers every other income and every operating expense, the interest both earned and incurred, and — the company addition — the year's INCOME TAX, ending on net profit after tax. The APPROPRIATION account distributes that profit: DIVIDENDS out, whatever remains into RETAINED INCOME, the pool of undistributed profit accumulated over the company's entire life.

Speak Zamani's appropriation aloud. Net profit after tax arrives: R423 400. Dividends claim R280 000 — an interim dividend of R130 000 paid in October, a final dividend of R150 000 declared on the last day of the year. That retains R143 400 from this year, which joins the opening retained income of R256 600 to close at R400 000. Each of those numbers will resurface in the statements; part of the skill is recognising them when they do.

And one habit that saves double counting: last year's accruals, prepayments and receivables must be REVERSED at the start of this year. Skip the reversal and the same expense or income is counted in two different years — a favourite trap set inside ledger-account questions.

Take this section's questions now — the provision moved by R3 000, the trading, profit and loss and appropriation accounts in sequence, and the profit traced into retained income.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Comprehensive Income

Assemble the year from the top of the statement of comprehensive income.

Sales, net of debtors' allowances: R4 200 000. Cost of sales: R2 800 000. GROSS PROFIT: R1 400 000. Verify the pricing policy before moving on: gross profit on cost of sales is 1 400 000 over 2 800 000 — a mark-up of 50 percent on cost. Checking that relationship takes ten seconds and confirms the two biggest figures on the page.

Add other operating income of R100 000 — rent income, bad debts recovered, and the R3 000 decrease in the provision for bad debts. Gross operating income: R1 500 000. Deduct operating expenses of R880 000 — salaries and wages, directors' fees, audit fees, sundry running costs, and depreciation of R210 000. OPERATING PROFIT: R620 000.

Add interest income of R20 000: R640 000 before interest expense. Deduct interest expense of R60 000: NET PROFIT BEFORE TAX, R580 000. Deduct income tax at 27 percent — R156 600 — and the statement closes on NET PROFIT AFTER TAX: R423 400, the very figure the appropriation account received. Statement and ledger are one machine viewed from two sides.

Know the three profit levels by name — gross profit, operating profit, net profit — because each measures a different competence: buying and pricing the stock, running the operation, and financing it. And be certain about what never appears among the expenses: DIVIDENDS. A dividend is a distribution of profit to its owners, not a cost of earning it; it belongs in the retained income note, below the line, always. Listing dividends as an expense surrenders easy marks and corrupts every total beneath it.

Stop for this section's questions — the statement recited from R4 200 000 down to R423 400, the 50 percent mark-up confirmed, and dividends kept below the line.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Financial Position and Its Notes

The statement of financial position is the year's closing photograph, and Zamani's balances at R4 100 000. Construct it section by section.

ASSETS. Non-current assets: fixed assets at carrying value — cost less accumulated depreciation, itemised in the fixed asset note — R3 050 000, plus a financial asset, a fixed deposit of R150 000: R3 200 000. Current assets: inventories R420 000, trade and other receivables R280 000, cash and cash equivalents R200 000: R900 000. TOTAL ASSETS: R4 100 000.

Now who claims those assets. SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY: ordinary share capital of R2 900 000 plus retained income of R400 000 — the closing balance the appropriation account just delivered — R3 300 000. NON-CURRENT LIABILITIES: the Dinaledi Bank loan, R400 000, being the portion not repayable within twelve months. CURRENT LIABILITIES: trade and other payables R211 400, SHAREHOLDERS FOR DIVIDENDS R150 000, and SARS income tax R38 600 — together R400 000. Add the claims: 3 300 000 plus 400 000 plus 400 000 is R4 100 000. It balances because it must; if it does not, an error is waiting to be found.

The two company-only current liabilities deserve their own arithmetic. Shareholders for dividends is the final dividend of R150 000, declared on the last day and payable early in the new year. SARS income tax is a running slate: this year's charge was R156 600, provisional payments came to R118 000, leaving R38 600 owing. Swap the relative sizes and the balance crosses the statement to become a current asset — a refund due — so always read the account rather than assuming the side.

The NOTES hold the detail the face summarises. The fixed asset note: cost, accumulated depreciation and the year's movements per asset class. The ordinary share capital note: authorised shares, issued shares, and any issues or buy-backs during the year. The retained income note: opening balance, net profit after tax, interim and final dividends, closing balance. Questions demand the notes as often as the statement itself, so learn what lives in each.

The questions on this section are with you now — the statement balanced at R4 100 000, the SARS slate read in both directions, and each note matched to its contents.

## Subtopic: The Cash Flow Statement and the Audit Report

Profit is an opinion produced by matching; cash is a fact produced by the bank. Companies fail on the fact, not the opinion — so grade 12 adds the CASH FLOW STATEMENT, in three sections.

OPERATING ACTIVITIES. Begin at net profit before tax, R580 000, and reverse the entries that never moved money: add back depreciation of R210 000 and interest expense of R60 000. Cash generated before working capital changes: R850 000. Then the working capital movements. Inventories rose R50 000 — cash out, sitting on shelves. Receivables rose R30 000 — sales recognised, money not yet collected. Payables rose R42 000 — goods received, payment postponed: cash in, for now. Cash generated from operations: R812 000. Now subtract what was genuinely paid: interest paid R60 000; income tax paid R143 000 — last year's closing balance of R25 000 settled, plus this year's provisional payments of R118 000; dividends paid R250 000 — last year's final dividend of R120 000 plus this year's interim of R130 000. This year's final dividend is absent, and must be: declared, not yet paid, no cash moved. Net cash from operating activities: R359 000.

INVESTING ACTIVITIES. Fixed assets purchased: R520 000 out. Fixed deposit increased by R30 000: out. Net cash used in investing: R550 000. Prove the purchases figure the way the ledger would: opening carrying value R2 740 000 plus additions R520 000 less depreciation R210 000 equals the closing R3 050 000 — no disposals this year, so the chain closes cleanly.

FINANCING ACTIVITIES. New shares issued: R240 000 in. Loan repaid: R80 000 out. Net cash from financing: R160 000 in.

Combine: 359 000 in, 550 000 out, 160 000 in — a net DECREASE of R31 000. Opening cash R231 000; closing cash R200 000 — precisely the cash and cash equivalents figure in current assets. The statement must land on the balance sheet's cash figure; when it does, every stride along the way is confirmed.

Finally the AUDIT REPORT — the shortest document in the annual report and the first a careful reader opens. The independent auditor issues one of three opinions. UNQUALIFIED: the statements fairly present the position — the clean opinion. QUALIFIED: fairly presented EXCEPT FOR the listed matters — read the list. DISCLAIMER or ADVERSE: the auditor lacked the evidence to form an opinion, or formed the opinion that the statements do NOT fairly present — the serious end of the scale. The report is addressed to the SHAREHOLDERS: directors prepare the statements, the auditor tests them on the owners' behalf, and the audit fee buys an honest opinion — never a friendly one.

The final questions of this part are ready — the three sections summed to R200 000, tax paid and dividends paid rebuilt from their slates, and the three opinions ranked from clean to alarming.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Here is the same year again, told across a kitchen table — because financial statements are only the formal answers to the three questions every family asks about its own money.

## Subtopic: One Year, Three Photographs

Picture a family that runs a weekend market stall, sitting down in December to talk money.

First question: how did the year GO? That is the statement of comprehensive income — not a photograph at all, really, but the whole year played back at speed: everything sold, what the stock cost, what running the stall cost, the tax taken off, and one figure left at the end. For Zamani Limited the playback ends on R423 400 after tax.

Second question: what do we OWN tonight? That is the statement of financial position — one photograph, taken at midnight on the last day. On one side, everything the company holds: equipment and premises, stock in the storeroom, money customers still owe, cash in the bank. On the other side, everyone with a claim on it: the owners — share capital plus every profit ever kept back — and the debts, long like the Dinaledi Bank loan, short like the suppliers, the final dividend promised to shareholders, and the tax still owed to SARS. Both sides total R4 100 000, because nothing owned is unclaimed.

Third question — the grade 12 question: where is the CASH? Because the family notices something odd: the playback says the year earned over four hundred thousand, yet the cashbox holds LESS than last December. Both statements are true at once. Profit counts a sale the day it happens even if the customer pays in February; it spreads the cost of the gazebo over years of use; it counts a promised dividend nowhere at all. The cashbox counts only money. The cash flow statement is the bridge between the playback and the box — and crossing it properly is most of this lesson.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the playback, the photograph and the bridge, and how a profitable year can still empty the box a little.

## Subtopic: Where Did the Cash Go?

Cross the bridge for Zamani in three strides.

Stride one — the trading itself. Start at profit before tax, R580 000, and un-count what never touched the box. Depreciation reduced profit by R210 000, yet not one rand left the bank for it: add it back. Now read the storeroom and the notebooks. Stock is up R50 000 — that is cash converted into jerseys on a shelf. Customers owe R30 000 more than last year — counted as sales, not yet collected. Suppliers are owed R42 000 more — stock received, payment delayed, which spares cash for the moment. After those, trading truly generated R812 000. Then the unavoidable outflows: interest to the bank, R143 000 of tax to SARS, and the dividends actually PAID this year — listen closely — last year's final dividend plus this year's interim, R250 000 together. The final dividend declared last week is still only a promise. Trading ends the year having put R359 000 into the box.

Stride two — the future. The company spent R520 000 on new equipment and moved another R30 000 into a fixed deposit: R550 000 out. Not lost — planted.

Stride three — the funders. New shares brought in R240 000; the loan was reduced by R80 000. Net R160 000 in.

Now open the box. In 359, out 550, in 160: down R31 000, from R231 000 to R200 000. And R200 000 is exactly the cash figure in the midnight photograph. When your final line meets the balance sheet's cash, every stride before it stands proven.

Then read the story behind the arithmetic. Zamani's trading is strong; the box dipped because the company planted over half a million in its own future, financed partly by new shareholders. The identical R31 000 dip caused by weak trading would tell an entirely different story — and interpretation questions ask you to say which story the numbers tell.

Your questions for this part are up now: depreciation added back, the storeroom and notebooks read for cash, the dividends actually paid, and the box counted down to R200 000.

## Subtopic: The Auditor's Three Verdicts

One question remains: why believe any of it? The directors wrote these statements about their own stewardship — a builder certifying his own house. So the shareholders appoint an outside inspector: the independent auditor.

The auditor tests the records and delivers one of three verdicts. Think of a safety certificate on a house. Verdict one — UNQUALIFIED: the house is sound; the statements fairly present the position. The word misleads everyone at first: unqualified means CLEAN, an opinion with no reservations attached to it. Verdict two — QUALIFIED: the house is sound EXCEPT the roof needs work; fairly presented apart from the matters the auditor lists, and a wise reader studies that list. Verdict three — the alarms: a DISCLAIMER, where the inspector was denied access or evidence and cannot certify anything, or an ADVERSE opinion, where the house is declared unsafe — the statements do not fairly present the position. Nobody should move in on those terms.

Three short points carry the theory marks. The report is addressed to the SHAREHOLDERS — the inspector works for the owners, checking the builders, never the reverse. An audit provides REASONABLE assurance, not certainty — auditors test samples, not every invoice in every file. And FAIRLY PRESENT does not promise correctness to the last cent; it promises a picture a reader can rely on.

Then the thread every company topic returns to: INDEPENDENCE. An auditor who holds shares in the client, socialises with its directors, or depends on this one client for most of their income has a private reason to soften the verdict — and a softened verdict poisons the whole system, because the bank, the small shareholder and the pension fund all trusted that signature.

Behind the signature stands a profession. An external auditor is a registered professional, belonging to a body such as SAICA or SAIPA, and audit work is policed by IRBA, the Independent Regulatory Board for Auditors. A director pressing for a friendlier opinion is asking the auditor to gamble everything: IRBA can fine, suspend or DEREGISTER — struck from the roll, never to sign an audit report again. And the moment an opinion can be bought, notice what is destroyed: the audit stops telling shareholders anything at all, and its entire value evaporates.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now: the three verdicts matched to the house certificate, who receives the report and why, and what independence protects. Keep every one of Zamani's balanced figures close — the next lesson interrogates these very statements with ratios.
