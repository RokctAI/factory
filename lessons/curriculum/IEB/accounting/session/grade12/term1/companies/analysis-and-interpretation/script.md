# Part 1 — Expert

Building the statements was recording; questioning them is accounting's real destination. Analysis and interpretation carries heavy weight in your final exams for one reason: it cannot be memorised, only practised, because every answer must argue from the figures in front of it. The indicator families from grade 11 return dressed for companies, and grade 12 hands the shareholder six instruments of their own: return on shareholders' equity, return on total capital employed, earnings per share, dividends per share, net asset value per share and the pay-out rate. One company supplies every number in this lesson — Bokamoso Limited, six hundred thousand shares in issue — and each indicator gets computed AND interpreted, because the arithmetic earns half the marks and the sentence of judgement after it earns the other half.

## Subtopic: The Grade 11 Toolkit Revisited

Four families of indicators come back from grade 11. Run them on Bokamoso's figures.

PROFITABILITY: gross profit on sales, gross profit on cost of sales, operating profit on sales, net profit on sales — the percentage family that tracks whether margins are holding. Bokamoso's margins are steady this year, so the attention shifts to the families grade 12 leans on hardest.

LIQUIDITY — can the company meet the bills of the coming months? Current assets total R880 000: inventories R380 000, trade receivables R360 000, cash R140 000. Current liabilities: R400 000. CURRENT RATIO: 880 000 against 400 000 — 2,2 to 1. ACID-TEST RATIO: remove the inventories and R500 000 remains against R400 000 — 1,25 to 1. Both healthy: even if not one more item sold, the short-term debts are covered with room to spare.

Ratios alone can flatter, so add the OPERATING CYCLE. STOCK TURNOVER: cost of sales of R2 850 000 over average stock of R380 000 — 7,5 times a year, about forty-nine days on the shelf. DEBTORS' COLLECTION PERIOD: average debtors of R360 000 over credit sales of R4 380 000, times three hundred and sixty-five — exactly 30 days: customers pay on their thirty-day terms, to the day. CREDITORS' PAYMENT PERIOD: average creditors of R400 000 over credit purchases of R2 800 000, times three hundred and sixty-five — about 52 days. The skill is reading the three as one sentence: Bokamoso collects in thirty days and pays in fifty-two, so its suppliers are effectively financing its debtors' book. Favourable — provided the fifty-two days sits within negotiated terms, because a supplier pushed too far answers with cash-on-delivery.

SOLVENCY — will the company survive the long run? Total assets of R4 450 000 against total liabilities of R1 450 000 — about 3,1 to 1. The company could shrink to a third of itself and still cover every debt. Keep liquidity and solvency apart in your answers: one is this month's survival, the other is survival itself.

Take this section's questions now — the two liquidity ratios, the three cycle periods read as one sentence, and liquidity separated from solvency.

## Subtopic: The New Grade 12 Indicators

Now the shareholder's six instruments, each built to answer a question an owner genuinely asks.

RETURN ON SHAREHOLDERS' EQUITY — ROSHE. The question: what did MY money earn? Net profit after tax of R438 000 over AVERAGE shareholders' equity — opening R2 840 000, closing R3 000 000, average R2 920 000 — gives 15 percent. Judge it against the alternative: a fixed deposit pays about 8 percent with almost no risk. Fifteen against eight is a proper reward for the risk of ownership.

RETURN ON TOTAL CAPITAL EMPLOYED — ROTCE. The question: what does the whole long-term pot earn, whoever filled it? Net profit BEFORE tax plus interest on loans — 600 000 plus 120 000, R720 000 — over average capital employed, equity plus non-current loans, R4 000 000 on average. Answer: 18 percent. Before tax and before interest deliberately, because ROTCE measures the business engine itself, prior to the funders sharing out what it produced.

EARNINGS PER SHARE — EPS. Net profit after tax over shares in issue: R438 000 over 600 000 shares — 73 cents. Each share EARNED 73 cents this year, whether or not the cents ever left the company.

DIVIDENDS PER SHARE — DPS. Total dividends over shares in issue: R348 000 over 600 000 — 58 cents: an interim of 25 cents paid mid-year plus a final of 33 cents declared at year-end.

DIVIDEND PAY-OUT RATE. DPS over EPS: 58 over 73 — just under 80 percent, roughly four rand of every five earned. A high pay-out delights shareholders who live on their dividends; a low pay-out funds expansion without new borrowing. Neither is correct in itself — the judgement depends on the company's plans, and writing that dependence down is what earns the interpretation mark.

NET ASSET VALUE PER SHARE — NAV. Total shareholders' equity over shares in issue: R3 000 000 over 600 000 — 500 cents. This is what the BOOKS say one share is worth: recorded assets minus liabilities, divided among the shares.

Stop for this section's questions — all six computed on Bokamoso's figures, and the owner's question behind each one said in words.

## Subtopic: Gearing, Returns and the Share Price

With the computing done, the judgement questions begin — the ones interpretation marks are really paid for.

First: SHOULD BOKAMOSO BORROW MORE? The DEBT-EQUITY ratio measures gearing: non-current loans of R1 050 000 against equity of R3 000 000 — 0,35 to 1, modestly geared. Now the comparison that decides the argument: ROTCE is 18 percent while the loans cost about 11 percent. Every borrowed rand earns eighteen cents in the engine and costs eleven at the bank — POSITIVE gearing, quietly enriching the shareholders. So the case for borrowing to expand is strong — and the counterweight must be stated in the same breath: interest is a fixed claim, payable in the bad years too, so every step up in gearing raises the risk carried by the owners. If the return slid toward eleven, the argument for debt would collapse.

Second: IS THE SHARE PRICE SENSIBLE? Bokamoso trades on the JSE at 620 cents while NAV stands at 500 cents. Buyers pay 120 cents per share ABOVE the book value of the net assets — and that premium is information, not error. The market is pricing what no balance sheet records: expected future profits, the brand, the customer base, the quality of the directors. A share trading well BELOW its NAV carries the opposite message — a market bracing for decline, or an opportunity for a brave buyer — and a well-reasoned answer may defend either reading, provided it reasons. Keep the comparison in matching units: cents against cents, price against NAV.

Third: ARE THE SHAREHOLDERS BEING WELL SERVED? Set out the scorecard. ROSHE of 15 percent against the 8 percent riskless alternative: yes, with a genuine risk premium. EPS of 73 cents against DPS of 58 cents: the dividend is comfortably covered by earnings — no company should pay out what it did not earn. And track EPS THROUGH TIME with one caution: rising EPS on a stable share count is real improvement, but rising EPS just after a share buy-back may be arithmetic flattery — fewer shares dividing the same profit. Naming that trap in an answer is exactly the caution interpretation questions reward.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — gearing argued through eighteen against eleven, the premium to NAV explained, and the scorecard read with its trap.

## Subtopic: Reading a Published Annual Report

The final skill: lift your eyes from a ratio table to the PUBLISHED annual report of a real company, because interpretation questions are set inside exactly that document.

An annual report is more than statements. The DIRECTORS' REPORT is the board's own narrative of the year — operations, dividends declared, who served as directors, significant events after year-end. Read it with respect and suspicion in equal measure: it is the players writing up their own match. The INDEPENDENT AUDITOR'S REPORT is the referee's page — find the opinion paragraph before reading anything else, because unqualified, qualified, disclaimer or adverse determines how much weight the rest of the report can bear. The SUMMARISED financial statements compress the full set for publication — every analysis skill in this lesson transfers to them unchanged. And the GOVERNANCE disclosures: directors' remuneration, the board's committees, and the company's application of the King Code — South Africa's governance framework, demanding that boards act with responsibility, accountability, fairness and transparency.

Then put the toolkit to work on the scenarios this topic loves. AUDIT OPINION FIRST: a qualified opinion on, say, inventory valuation contaminates every ratio built on inventory — compute nothing before checking the referee's page. DIRECTORS' PAY AGAINST PERFORMANCE: remuneration climbing while EPS falls is a governance question, and the annual general meeting is where shareholders must ask it. PRICE AGAINST NAV OVER TIME: a widening discount while directors quietly trade shares raises the sharpest ethics matter in the topic — share-price manipulation and insider trading. Spreading false information to move a price, or trading on knowledge the market does not have, are both illegal, and both appear in ethics questions precisely because the temptation is real.

Close on the discipline that structures every interpretation answer: QUOTE the figure, COMPARE it against something — last year, the norm, a competitor, an interest rate — and JUDGE with a reason. Figure, comparison, judgement. Three sentences, full marks, every time.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the parts of an annual report and the job of each, the King Code's four demands, and the figure-comparison-judgement discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same toolkit again, at a family meeting — because every ratio in this lesson is a question a careful relative asks before trusting a business with the family's savings.

## Subtopic: The Company's Report Card

Uncle Sipho's butchery has become a company, and he wants the family to buy in. Aunt Lerato, who has never trusted a smile in her life, asks for the books and rules up a report card.

First mark: CAN IT PAY THIS MONTH'S BILLS? The butchery holds R880 000 of cash and nearly-cash — meat in the cold room, customers' debts, money in the bank — against R400 000 of bills falling due. More than two rand ready for every rand owed. Strip out the stock entirely — suppose nobody buys another chop — and there is still R1,25 for every rand owed. No embarrassment coming at month-end.

Second mark: HOW FAST DOES THE MONEY TURN? The cold room empties and refills about seven and a half times a year — call it seven weeks a cycle; nothing lies there long enough to spoil. Customers settle in thirty days, exactly on their terms. And the butchery pays its own suppliers in about fifty-two days. Aunt Lerato allows herself half a smile: the suppliers' patience is carrying the customers' slowness — the business banks the gap. Clever, she says, as long as the suppliers agreed to it, because a supplier squeezed past its patience starts demanding cash at the door, and that can choke a business in a season.

Third mark: WOULD A TERRIBLE YEAR SINK IT? Everything the butchery owns is worth about three times everything it owes. A ship with that much hull above the water survives a storm or two.

Now notice the question Aunt Lerato never asked: is the profit BIG? A number on its own told her nothing; big compared to WHAT is her only question. Compare, then judge — that reflex is the entire subject of analysis, and it outranks any single ratio on the card.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the month-end test with and without the stock, the three speeds read together, and why the suppliers' patience has limits.

## Subtopic: Whose Money Works Harder?

Now the family's real question: what will OUR money earn inside this butchery?

The long-term pot is about four million rand — the family's equity plus the bank's loans. Last year the business engine produced R720 000 before anyone was paid out of it: eighteen cents on every rand in the pot. That figure is the engine's honest output — ROTCE — measured before the funders divide it, which is the only fair way to measure an engine.

Split the funders. The BANK's share of the pot costs about 11 percent — its interest is collected in good years and bad, no discussion. The FAMILY takes what the engine leaves after the bank and the tax, and that works out to 15 cents on each of their rand — ROSHE.

Sit with the sum Aunt Lerato writes in the margin: borrow at eleven, earn at eighteen. Every borrowed rand works for the FAMILY — the bank supplies the money, the family pockets the seven-cent difference. That is why sensible debt makes owners richer, and its name is gearing. Then she writes the warning underneath: the bank's interest is owed even when the engine coughs. At eighteen-earning against eleven-costing, borrowing is a servant; if the engine slowed to nine while the bank still wanted eleven, every borrowed rand would start eating the family's supper. Debt is an amplifier — better good years, worse bad ones, always both.

And the test that settles every kitchen-table investment argument: the fixed deposit. The bank pays 8 percent for zero effort and zero fear. The butchery returns 15 — seven extra cents per rand as payment for the family's courage. Worth it, on these figures. But if that 15 ever drifted toward 8, the honest advice at the table would be: take the deposit and sleep soundly.

Your questions for this part are up now: the engine's output against the bank's price, gearing as an amplifier, and the fixed-deposit test applied.

## Subtopic: The Price on the Board and the Value in the Books

One confusion remains, because the cousins have started trading their shares among themselves, and two different numbers about the same share keep being mixed up. Aunt Lerato separates them once and for all.

Number one: what the BOOKS say a share is worth. Everything owned minus everything owed, shared across the six hundred thousand shares: 500 cents each. That is net asset value — the winding-up answer, what a share would collect if the butchery stopped tomorrow and sold everything at its recorded value.

Number two: what a cousin will PAY. The going price is 620 cents — a full 120 cents above the books. Are the cousins being foolish? No. They are not buying the mincer and the cold room; they are buying the butchery's FUTURE — next year's profits, the queue on Saturday mornings, Uncle Sipho's nose for good suppliers. None of that stands on a balance sheet, and all of it commands a price. A share priced above its book value is the market's compliment; a share stuck far below book value is the market's warning — or a bargain for the brave — and either answer can score, provided it argues from the figures.

Then the pair of per-share numbers that describe each share's year. Each share EARNED 73 cents — EPS, its slice of the year's profit. Each share was PAID 58 cents — DPS, the money that actually reached pockets. The missing 15 cents did not evaporate: it stayed behind as retained income, quietly thickening the value of every share. The pay-out rate says it in one number: 58 out of 73, close to eighty percent paid out, the rest kept. Grandmother, living on her dividends, applauds the eighty. The cousin campaigning for a second shop wanted forty. Neither is wrong — they want different futures from the same business, and the directors' pay-out decision is the balancing of exactly those wishes.

And here are the final questions of the lesson, with you now: the books' answer against the traded price, earned against paid per share, and who benefits from a high pay-out. This is the skill your final exams keep rewarding — figure, comparison, judgement, every single time.
