# Part 1 — Expert

In grade 12 the accounting moves past businesses owned by the people who run them and settles on the COMPANY — an entity the law treats as a person of its own, financed by shareholders who may live a thousand kilometres from the till and steered by directors who might hold only a handful of its shares. That single act of separation generates every entry in this lesson: shares sold to the public and later repurchased, tax assessed against the company itself, dividends that exist only once the directors say so, and a cluster of payments — loan interest, directors' fees, audit fees — that each land in a different place in the books. Master these transactions now, because the statements, the cash flow and the ratios later in the term are all built out of them.

## Subtopic: From Owner to Shareholder — the Company Form

Everything begins with INCORPORATION. The founders draft a Memorandum of Incorporation — the MOI, the company's constitution — and register it with the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission under the Companies Act 71 of 2008. On registration a new legal person exists. The company, not its shareholders, owns the delivery vehicles; the company, not its shareholders, owes the suppliers; the company sues and is sued under its own name.

Two consequences follow, and you must be able to state both precisely. LIMITED LIABILITY: the worst outcome a shareholder faces is losing the amount paid for the shares — creditors of a failed company have no claim on a shareholder's personal property. CONTINUITY: because the company's existence is separate from its owners, a shareholder can die, emigrate or sell out, and the company carries on unchanged.

Companies come in two forms. A PUBLIC company carries Limited — Ltd — after its name, may invite the public to buy its shares, and may list on the JSE, where those shares then change hands between investors at market prices. A PRIVATE company — Proprietary Limited, Pty Ltd — may not offer shares to the public and places restrictions on transferring them.

Hold on to the structural idea that powers this whole topic: OWNERSHIP and CONTROL sit in different hands. Shareholders own the company and exercise their power by voting at the annual general meeting; the directors they elect manage the business day to day. Managers spending money that belongs to other people is a risk, so the law supplies a check — a public company must appoint an independent AUDITOR, who examines the records and reports to the shareholders on whether the financial statements fairly present the position.

Finally, the capital vocabulary. AUTHORISED share capital is the ceiling written into the MOI — the greatest number of shares the company may ever issue. ISSUED share capital is what has actually been sold, and it may never exceed the authorised figure. South African shares carry NO PAR VALUE: there is no printed face value, so every issue is simply recorded at the price it actually raised.

Take this section's questions now — they cover the two consequences of separate legal personality, Ltd against Pty Ltd, the split between owning and managing, and authorised against issued capital.

## Subtopic: Issuing Shares and Buying Them Back

One company runs through this whole lesson: Naledi Limited, a building-supplies retailer. Its MOI authorises two million ordinary shares. On the first day of the financial year it has 600 000 shares in issue, which raised R3 000 000 in total — an average of R5,00 per share.

Start with the SHARE ISSUE, the simplest entry here. On 1 September the directors issue 200 000 new shares at R8,00 each to fund a second branch. Cash received: 200 000 times R8,00, which is R1 600 000. The entry: debit Bank, credit Ordinary Share Capital, R1 600 000. Because the shares have no par value there is no premium account — the entire issue price lands in Ordinary Share Capital. Naledi Limited now has 800 000 shares in issue and share capital of R4 600 000. Immediately recompute the AVERAGE issue price: R4 600 000 divided by 800 000 gives R5,75 per share. Write that average down every time the balance changes, because the next transaction cannot be done without it.

The SHARE BUY-BACK is where marks are won and lost. On 28 February, the last day of the year, Naledi Limited repurchases 40 000 shares from a retiring shareholder at R9,00 each — a payment of R360 000. The rule has two limbs. Ordinary Share Capital is debited with the AVERAGE issue price of the repurchased shares: 40 000 times R5,75 is R230 000. The EXCESS paid above the average is debited to RETAINED INCOME: R360 000 less R230 000 is R130 000. Credit Bank with the full R360 000. Why the split? The capital account only ever received R5,75 per share on average, so that is all it can surrender; the extra R3,25 per share is past profit paid out to the departing shareholder.

Close the position: 760 000 shares remain in issue, and share capital stands at R4 600 000 less R230 000 — R4 370 000. Repurchased shares are CANCELLED on the spot. They are not stored for resale, they carry no votes, and they will never earn another cent of dividend.

The questions on this section are with you now — the issue at full issue price, the recalculated average of R5,75, and the R360 000 split between share capital and retained income.

## Subtopic: Income Tax and Dividends in the Ledger

Because a company is its own person, it is taxed as its own person. It does not wait for year-end either: PROVISIONAL TAX is paid in advance, usually in two instalments, each recorded as debit SARS — Income Tax, credit Bank. Treat the SARS account as a running score between the company and the revenue service.

Naledi Limited paid a first provisional instalment of R160 000 in August and a second of R200 000 in February — R360 000 in total. When the books close, net profit before tax comes to R1 500 000, and at the company rate of 27 percent the income tax for the year is R405 000. Record the charge: debit Income Tax, credit SARS — Income Tax, R405 000. Now read the account the way you would read any account: R360 000 of debits against a R405 000 credit leaves a CREDIT balance of R45 000 — a current liability, SARS income tax, still to be paid over. Reverse the sizes — provisional payments larger than the final charge — and the balance flips to a debit: a current ASSET, a refund owed to the company. Never assume the direction; compute it, because questions are built on that flip.

DIVIDENDS turn on one word: DECLARED. A shareholder has no right to demand a dividend — it exists only once the directors declare it, and it is expressed in CENTS PER SHARE on the shares in issue at the declaration date. Naledi Limited declared and paid an INTERIM dividend of 30 cents per share in October, when 800 000 shares were in issue after the September issue: 800 000 times 30 cents is R240 000 — debit Dividends on Ordinary Shares, credit Bank. The FINAL dividend of 50 cents per share was declared on 28 February, moments after the buy-back cut the register to 760 000 shares: 760 000 times 50 cents is R380 000. Declared on the last day means unpaid at year-end, so the credit goes to SHAREHOLDERS FOR DIVIDENDS — a current liability settled early in the new year. Total dividends for the year: R620 000. The classic error is computing the final dividend on 800 000 shares; count the shares in issue on each declaration date, every time.

Pause for this section's questions — provisional payments against the final charge, the R45 000 read off the SARS account, and two dividends on two different share counts.

## Subtopic: Loans, Directors' Fees and Audit Fees

Three further transactions complete the toolkit.

First, a LOAN WITH CAPITALISED INTEREST. Naledi Limited owes Kopano Bank R800 000 at 10 percent per annum, and the contract capitalises the interest — instead of being paid across separately, the interest is ADDED to the loan balance. The year's interest is 10 percent of R800 000, which is R80 000: debit Interest on Loan, credit Loan. Instalments of R180 000 were paid during the year: debit Loan, credit Bank. Trace it through: R800 000 plus R80 000 less R180 000 leaves R700 000 owing at year-end. Two traps live here. Candidates subtract the interest, or leave it out entirely — but capitalised interest makes the loan grow before the payments pull it down. And the interest remains a full expense on the income statement even though no separate interest payment ever left the bank account.

Second, DIRECTORS' FEES — the price of management. Directors are paid fees for the work of running the company, and those fees are an OPERATING EXPENSE, deducted in arriving at net profit. Do not let them drift anywhere near dividends. Fees pay for labour; dividends reward ownership; a director who also holds shares collects both, but through two separate doors, and each door leads to a different section of the books.

Third, AUDIT FEES — the price of the check on management. The independent auditor's charge for examining the records is likewise an operating expense. Attach the governance point: the auditor is appointed by, and reports to, the SHAREHOLDERS — not the directors — because the entire purpose of the audit is to test the directors' stewardship on the owners' behalf. An auditor who accepts lavish hospitality from the directors, or whose fee depends on giving a clean opinion, has surrendered the independence that gives the report its value.

Tie the topic together with RETAINED INCOME: the profit the company keeps after tax and dividends, rolled up year after year — the account the buy-back reached into, and the bridge to the financial statements in the next lesson.

The last questions of this part are ready for you — the loan traced to R700 000, fees kept apart from dividends, and the auditor's independence explained.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same content, told the way it actually happens — through a spaza on a corner that grew until one owner's savings could no longer feed it.

## Subtopic: The Spaza That Became a Company

Naledi's spaza started with airtime, bread and paraffin. It grew into a building-supplies yard — cement, timber, roof sheets — and now the contractors are asking her to open in three more towns. The bank has lent what it will lend. Her own money is in the business already. So she takes the step every growing business eventually faces: she registers a company and sells SLICES of it to other people.

Each slice is a share. Buy one and you own a piece of the yard — a claim on its profits and a vote once a year — without ever stacking a single bag of cement. And the law now draws a hard line: the COMPANY owns the stock and the bakkies, the COMPANY owes the cement supplier. If everything collapses, a shareholder loses the price of the slices and nothing else. Nobody arrives at their door for the furniture. That guarantee — limited liability — is the entire reason strangers will hand money to a business they will never run.

Strangers want the rules in writing, so the company registers its rulebook, the Memorandum of Incorporation. The rulebook fixes the largest number of slices that may ever exist — the authorised share capital. The slices actually sold so far are the issued share capital. Think of a loaf: authorised is the most slices the loaf may ever be cut into; issued is how many have already been cut and handed over the counter.

Who runs the yard from Monday to Saturday? Not the shareholders — most will never visit it. They elect DIRECTORS to manage on their behalf. And that is the tension the whole topic keeps returning to: the people spending the money do not own it. Hold that sentence — it explains the AGM, the audit, and half the theory questions you will ever meet.

Questions on this piece are with you now: what a share buys you, what limited liability really protects, the loaf against the slices already cut, and why owners and managers are different people.

## Subtopic: Selling Slices of the Business

Now walk Naledi Limited's money through the year in counter language.

Over the years the company sold 600 000 slices for R3 000 000 altogether — R5,00 a slice on average. This year the new branches need funding, so it cuts 200 000 fresh slices and sells them at R8,00 each. Why more than R5,00? Because the yard is bigger and busier than when the first slices were sold — a piece of a stronger business simply costs more. In comes R1 600 000; the capital account now holds R4 600 000 for 800 000 slices, and the new average is R5,75 a slice.

Then a founding shareholder retires and wants his money out. The company buys his 40 000 slices back at R9,00 each — R360 000 leaves the bank. Slow down here, because this is the entry that catches everyone. The capital account never received R9,00 for those slices; across all its slices it received R5,75 on average. So the capital account returns exactly what it held — 40 000 times R5,75, which is R230 000 — and the remaining R130 000 comes out of the profits the company has saved up over the years: retained income. One cheque, two sources. A refund of what the slices contributed, topped up with a parting share of old profits.

And the slices that came back? Shredded. Cancelled. The company is now divided into 760 000 slices instead of 800 000 — so every remaining slice quietly became a slightly larger fraction of the whole yard. That, in one sentence, is why companies bother buying back their own shares.

Your questions for this part are up: why fresh slices sell above the old average, how the R360 000 splits in the books, and what happens to a slice the company buys back.

## Subtopic: The Company's Own Bills

A person in the eyes of the law gets bills in its own name.

The first bill is TAX. When the yard was just Naledi's, she paid the tax herself. Naledi Limited pays its own, at 27 percent of profit — and SARS collects on account, in advance, twice a year, based on the company's best guesses. The guesses added up to R360 000. When the real profit was counted, the tax came to R405 000. So the year closes with R45 000 still owed to SARS — a debt on the balance sheet. Guess too generously instead and the debt swaps sides: SARS owes the company a refund. It is one account, kept like the credit book behind the counter — paid on one side, owed on the other, and the balance can face either way.

The second is not truly a bill: DIVIDENDS. Nobody owes a shareholder anything until the directors DECLARE a dividend, priced in cents per slice. In October, with 800 000 slices out there, Naledi Limited paid 30 cents a slice — R240 000. On the last day of the year it declared 50 cents more, but by then the buy-back had trimmed the company to 760 000 slices — R380 000, promised but not yet paid, sitting as a debt to shareholders until the money goes out in the new year. Count the slices on the day of each declaration. Every single time.

Third, work gets paid as work. The directors earn directors' fees for managing; the auditor earns an audit fee for checking the books on the shareholders' behalf; both are ordinary running expenses of the yard. A director who also owns slices gets paid through two doors — fees for the managing, dividends for the owning — and keeping those doors separate is one of the easiest marks in the topic to protect.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the SARS score read in both directions, two dividends on two share counts, and fees held apart from dividends. Lock these transactions in — every statement you build for the rest of the term rests on them.
