**Mandy:** Time for the class questions! First one: in the cash flow statement we added interest expense back to the profit, and then a few lines later we subtracted interest paid — the same R60 000. Why put it back just to take it out again? It looks like a step that cancels itself.

**Tutor:** It looks circular until you see what each step is doing, and then it becomes one of the most honest parts of the statement. The starting point, net profit before tax, is a MATCHING number — it already contains the interest expense the year incurred. But the operating section wants to show two things separately: first, how much cash the pure trading engine generated, and second, what the company then actually paid out of that cash. So the add-back strips interest out of the profit figure to leave clean trading, and the later line reports the interest genuinely paid, as its own visible item. In Zamani's case the two amounts happen to be equal — R60 000 incurred, R60 000 paid — so the net effect is nil and the exercise feels pointless. But suppose the company had incurred R60 000 of interest and only paid R45 000 by year-end, with R15 000 accrued. Then the add-back is still R60 000, while interest PAID shows R45 000 — and the statement correctly reports the smaller cash outflow. The two lines only cancel when incurred equals paid; the format exists precisely for the years when they do not. The same logic runs the tax line: the charge was R156 600, but the cash that left for tax was R143 000, and the cash flow statement reports the R143 000, because it deals in facts about the bank, not opinions about matching.

***

**Mandy:** Next question: the balance sheet says the company is worth R3 300 000 to its shareholders. If somebody offered to buy the whole of Zamani Limited tomorrow, is R3 300 000 the price?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly not, and the reason teaches you what a balance sheet is and is not. The R3 300 000 is the ACCOUNTING equity: assets as recorded, minus liabilities as recorded. Those recorded figures obey specific rules that deliberately look backwards. Fixed assets sit at carrying value — cost less accumulated depreciation — not at what the shop and its fittings would fetch today. Nothing on the statement captures the things a buyer actually pays extra for: the location, the regular customers, the brand the business has built, the trained staff — what the business world calls goodwill. A thriving retailer usually sells for more than its equity; a struggling one, holding stock nobody wants at the recorded value, might sell for less. So read the balance sheet as a disciplined, verifiable record of what went in and what is owed — a floor for negotiation, not a price tag. The price of the whole company is set the same way the price of one share is set: by what a willing buyer will pay for the future profits. Keep the two ideas in separate pockets — recorded value and market value — because the analysis lesson will make you compare them deliberately, and candidates who blur them argue nonsense with great confidence.

***

**Mandy:** Third one, and it is the question half the class wrote down: why is this year's final dividend of R150 000 in the balance sheet AND in the retained income note, but not in the cash flow statement? It feels like the statements disagree with each other.

**Tutor:** They agree perfectly — they are just answering different questions about the same event, and dating is everything. On the last day of the year the directors declared a final dividend of R150 000. Ask the balance sheet's question: does the company owe anything tonight? Yes — declaration created a legal debt, so Shareholders for Dividends R150 000 sits under current liabilities. Ask the retained income note's question: how much of this year's profit was given to shareholders? R280 000 — the interim of R130 000 and the final of R150 000 — because appropriation follows declaration, not payment. Now ask the cash flow statement's question: did money leave the bank for this? Not yet — the payment run happens in the new year — so this year's final dividend cannot appear among dividends PAID. What does appear there is R250 000: LAST year's final dividend of R120 000, which was paid early this year, plus this year's interim of R130 000. Next year the pattern rolls forward: the R150 000 will show up in next year's cash flow statement when it is actually paid. So the three documents form a relay — the note records the decision, the balance sheet records the debt, and the cash flow statement records the settlement, one year later. Once you see the relay, the apparent disagreement becomes the proof that each statement is doing its own job properly.

***

**Mandy:** Last question: if an adverse opinion is such a disaster, why would directors ever publish statements that earn one? Surely they would just fix whatever the auditor found first?

**Tutor:** In most years that is exactly what happens, and it is worth understanding the quiet negotiation behind every clean opinion. During the audit, the auditor raises the problems found — a stock figure that cannot be supported, a debt treated as an asset — and the directors usually adjust the statements, because a qualified or adverse opinion costs them dearly: banks reprice loans, shareholders revolt, the share price falls. So the system corrects most errors before publication, which is precisely the point of having it. The opinions you asked about appear when that negotiation fails. Sometimes the directors refuse to adjust — perhaps because the honest figures would reveal a breached loan condition or an unlawful payment — and the auditor, whose signature is on the line with IRBA watching, will not bend. Then the adverse opinion is published, and it is the auditor doing exactly the job the shareholders paid for: shouting a warning the directors tried to muffle. A disclaimer is subtly different — it says the auditor was PREVENTED from gathering evidence, records missing or access denied, and could not form a view at all. Readers treat both as serious alarm signals, and sometimes the alarm itself forces the change: directors resign, statements are re-prepared, regulators arrive. So the answer is: they would rather fix it, and usually do — the harsh opinions exist for the minority of cases where management would rather hide the truth than repair it, and that is when the audit proves its whole value.
