# Part 1 — Expert

Every company is an exercise in trusting strangers. Shareholders post their savings into a business run by directors most of them will never meet, and then rely on those directors to spend carefully, report truthfully, and share the profit fairly. Corporate governance, the King Code, professional ethics, and the three layers of audit all exist for one purpose: to turn that trust from a gamble into a system. The topic is tested from two directions — theory questions about who holds which power and answers to whom, and scenario questions where something has already gone wrong and you must name the broken principle and prescribe the fix. Both skills get built here on one company: Masakhane Limited.

## Subtopic: Owners, Directors and the Board

The subject begins at the split that makes it necessary: the people who OWN a company are not the people who RUN it.

The SHAREHOLDERS own Masakhane Limited. Their capital bought the shares; their risk stops at what they paid — the company's debts are never their personal debts. But owning is not managing, and a shareholder's real power lives at the ANNUAL GENERAL MEETING: attend and vote, one vote per share; ELECT the directors, and remove the ones who fail; APPOINT the independent auditor and approve the audit fee; receive the annual report and pull it apart; put questions directly to the board; and approve the major decisions the law reserves for owners. Between meetings a shareholder receives dividends only when the directors declare them — a declared reward, never a guaranteed one.

The DIRECTORS manage the company on the shareholders' BEHALF, and that word carries legal force. A director owes a FIDUCIARY DUTY: act in good faith, in the best interests of the company, never for private advantage, and declare any personal interest touching a company decision. Their responsibilities form a list worth knowing cold: run the operations; safeguard the assets through proper internal control; prepare financial statements that fairly present the results — the directors' report says precisely this; declare only dividends the company can afford; and account to the shareholders at the annual general meeting.

The board itself has anatomy. EXECUTIVE directors work inside the company every day — the chief executive, the financial director. NON-EXECUTIVE directors come from outside to provide oversight, and INDEPENDENT non-executive directors carry no other financial connection to the company at all — which is exactly what makes their judgement worth having. Sound governance also splits the top: the chairperson — ideally an independent non-executive — leads the BOARD, while the chief executive leads the COMPANY. Fuse the two jobs in one person and you concentrate the very power governance exists to divide.

The questions for this section are with you now: the shareholder's powers against the director's duties, the fiduciary idea, and the three kinds of director on a board.

## Subtopic: The King Code and Its Four Demands

People spending other people's money need a rulebook, and for South African boards that rulebook is the KING CODE — now in its fourth edition, King IV. It is not legislation; it is a code of good practice, and the Johannesburg Stock Exchange requires its listed companies to apply the Code and explain in their annual reports how they have applied it. Its centre is ethical and effective leadership, expressed as four demands on every board.

RESPONSIBILITY: take charge of the company's assets and direction — set the strategy, manage the risks, and repair what breaks instead of concealing it. ACCOUNTABILITY: be answerable, to the shareholders first, for every decision taken; the working test is that in a well-governed company no one spends money without answering to somebody for it. FAIRNESS: weigh the legitimate interests of ALL who hold a stake — the small shareholder as well as the controlling one, employees, creditors, customers, the surrounding community — not merely the loudest voice at the table. TRANSPARENCY: report openly, the poor results with the good, so that an outsider can see the company's true position.

The Code then converts words into structures. An AUDIT COMMITTEE of independent non-executive directors oversees the financial reporting, receives what the internal auditors find, and stands guard over the external auditor's independence. A REMUNERATION COMMITTEE sets the directors' pay — so that directors never set their own — and transparency then puts that pay in the annual report, director by director, where shareholders can measure it against performance.

The skill this topic demands is diagnosis. A board conceals a bad half-year from its shareholders: name transparency, with accountability alongside. Directors award themselves bonuses while creditors go unpaid: fairness, and responsibility with it. Treat the four demands as diagnostic instruments, not vocabulary to recite.

Pause for this section's questions, with you now: the Code's legal status, the four demands, and the two committees matched to their work.

## Subtopic: Professional Ethics and the Code of Conduct

Governance binds the board as a body; ETHICS binds each professional personally. Accountants in South Africa belong to professional bodies — the institutes of chartered and professional accountants — and membership carries a CODE OF PROFESSIONAL CONDUCT resting on five principles. INTEGRITY: straightforward and honest, always — no adjusted figure is small enough to be innocent. OBJECTIVITY: no bias, conflict of interest or pressure may override professional judgement. PROFESSIONAL COMPETENCE AND DUE CARE: keep the skills current and do the work thoroughly. CONFIDENTIALITY: information gained through work is neither used nor leaked — though confidentiality never becomes a shield for illegality. PROFESSIONAL BEHAVIOUR: obey the law, and avoid anything that discredits the profession.

Test the principles against Masakhane's year. The financial director is told to move January's sales into December so the year looks stronger — integrity refuses, because statements carrying next year's sales no longer fairly present this year. A director's brother owns a transport firm bidding for Masakhane's delivery contract — a CONFLICT OF INTEREST. The ethical route is procedural, not personal: declare the connection, withdraw from the decision, and leave the remaining directors to choose on merit. Concealing the link and steering the contract breaches governance even if the brother's quote was the cheapest, because the decision was never protected from the bias.

The sharpest ethics content concerns shares. A director learns — before the market does — that Masakhane has won a contract that will transform its profits, and quietly buys shares while the price still reflects the old story. That is INSIDER TRADING: dealing on information the market lacks, illegal under South African financial markets law, and it works identically in the other direction, selling ahead of unpublished bad news. Its twin offence is SHARE-PRICE MANIPULATION: spreading false information or arranging artificial trades to push a price where it should not go. Both steal from the outsiders that published statements exist to protect. In a scenario answer, do three things: name the offence, state why it is wrong, and give the consequence — dismissal, criminal prosecution, and for a professional, expulsion by their body.

This section's questions are ready for you now: the five principles, the conflict-of-interest procedure, and insider trading distinguished from manipulation.

## Subtopic: Internal Control, Internal Audit and the Independent Audit

Trust still needs verification, and a company verifies itself in three layers.

Layer one: INTERNAL CONTROL — the daily machinery management runs to protect assets and information. DIVISION OF DUTIES: the person who orders never pays, and the person who pays never records. AUTHORISATION: nothing moves without an approved signature. DOCUMENTATION: every transaction leaves a paper trail. PHYSICAL SAFEGUARDS: safes, passwords, access control. RECONCILIATION: records checked against reality — bank statements, stock counts — at regular intervals. Controls operate every working day, and responsibility for their existence sits with the directors, nowhere else.

Layer two: INTERNAL AUDIT — the company's own employees testing those controls throughout the year. Their governance meaning lies in the reporting line: internal audit delivers its findings to the AUDIT COMMITTEE, not merely to the managers whose work was tested — because a finding that embarrasses a manager, reported only to that manager, has a way of vanishing. Internal audit recommends; management stays accountable for acting on the recommendation.

Layer three: the INDEPENDENT AUDIT. The external auditor is a registered auditor in public practice, appointed by the SHAREHOLDERS at the annual general meeting, reporting to those shareholders, publishing an opinion on whether the annual financial statements fairly present the position. The entire value of that opinion rests on INDEPENDENCE — which is why the auditor may hold no shares in the client, why lucrative side-consulting for the same client endangers objectivity, and why the audit committee exists partly to guard that independence and to recommend the appointment honestly.

Now hold the whole chain in one image. Management operates the controls and answers to the board. The board answers to the shareholders. Internal audit tests the controls for the audit committee. The independent auditor verifies the statements for the shareholders. Every arrow in the diagram points toward the owners — accountability, drawn as a picture.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five control measures, the reporting lines, and the three layers in their order.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same machinery again, but at street level — because company governance is a neighbourhood sports club's committee with more zeros on the statement.

## Subtopic: The Club and Its Committee

Sondela Netball Club has a hundred and fifty members. Each paid a joining fee, and that pooled money bought the posts, the match dresses, the storeroom and its lock. The members OWN the club. But a hundred and fifty people cannot run one club, so once a year they gather and ELECT a committee of seven to run it for them. That gathering is the club's annual general meeting, and the election is the members' true power: a committee that runs the club into the ground is voted out at the next one.

Watch carefully what the members keep and what they give away. They keep the votes; the right to a full report-back once a year; the right to stand up and ask the treasurer exactly where June's gate money went; and the right to choose an OUTSIDE person — connected to nobody on the committee — to check the books each year. They give away the daily running: fixtures, kit orders, the float tin, the bank card. That is the shareholder-director split in miniature: owners keep the power to appoint, question and remove; the committee receives the power to manage.

And the committee holds that power on the members' BEHALF. The treasurer's rand is not the treasurer's — every cent belongs to a hundred and fifty people who trusted her with it. The day a committee member starts treating club money as her own money, or club decisions as personal favours to distribute, the deal the election created is broken. A company writes that deal into law and calls it fiduciary duty; the club calls it not robbing your own team.

Questions on this piece are with you now: what the members keep, what the committee receives, and whose money sits in the treasurer's tin.

## Subtopic: House Rules That Keep the Money Clean

The King Code's four demands are Sondela's house rules with formal names.

RESPONSIBILITY: the committee looks after what the club owns — dresses signed in and out after every match, gate money banked the same evening, the storeroom's broken hinge fixed before the rain gets to the kit. A committee that lets club property decay has already failed, even before a cent goes missing. ACCOUNTABILITY: at the yearly meeting the treasurer accounts for every rand — in, out, and why. Not a highlights reel: everything. The test is one question — does anyone in this club spend money and answer to nobody? If the answer is yes, the rule is broken. FAIRNESS: the committee serves all hundred and fifty members — the under-14 side's dresses matter as much as the first team's, and the quietest member's fee bought as much of the storeroom as the chairperson's did. TRANSPARENCY: the books stay open. A bad season is reported as a bad season. A committee that shares only good news is hiding something, and hiding is the seed of every scandal you will ever be asked to diagnose.

Now the scenario this topic returns to again and again. The treasurer, Zanele, orders the new match dresses from her sister's shop — no other quotes, no comparison, decided alone. Diagnose it: Zanele held a personal interest and never declared it; objectivity and fairness died together, and a reasonable price cannot resurrect them, because the decision was never protected. Then repair it, step by step: declare the family connection; step out of the kit decision completely; let the rest of the committee collect three quotes; minute the choice and the reasons. Handled that way, the same conflict resolves cleanly — and her sister's shop may still win, honestly this time.

Your questions for this part are up now: the four house rules with a club example for each, and the Zanele scenario diagnosed and repaired.

## Subtopic: Who Checks the Checkers?

Electing a committee you trust does not mean nobody counts the money.

First, the everyday habits — internal control wearing club colours. Two signatures on the club account, so no single person can move money alone. Gate takings counted by one member, written up by a second, banked by a third — the counter never the recorder. A receipt book for every cent that arrives; a folder of till slips for every cent that leaves. The habits run every match day, and they protect the honest as much as they catch the dishonest: when nothing is missing, the treasurer can PROVE nothing is missing.

Second, the club asks Karabo — sharp-eyed, and deliberately not on the committee — to check things through the year: count the dresses against the register, follow one Saturday's gate money from the notebook to the deposit slip. When she finds a gap, she reports to the club's oversight group, never privately to the treasurer she is testing — a finding delivered only to the person it embarrasses tends to evaporate. Karabo is internal audit: inside the club, working all year, testing the habits.

Third, once a year the MEMBERS — never the committee — appoint an outside accountant with no ties to anyone in the club to examine the books and tell the members whether the treasurer's report fairly shows the truth. That is the independent audit, and its power lies entirely in the appointment: chosen by the owners, reporting to the owners, checking the people in charge.

Finish with the method that answers every scenario question in this topic — three moves. One: STATE the problem from the facts — the treasurer's sister supplied the kit without quotes. Two: NAME the principle or duty broken — an undeclared conflict of interest; fairness and transparency breached. Three: RECOMMEND the remedy that closes the gap — interests declared, quotes compared, decisions minuted, duties divided. Problem, principle, remedy — in that order, every time, and the marks follow. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the club's controls, Karabo against the outside accountant, and the three-move method itself.
