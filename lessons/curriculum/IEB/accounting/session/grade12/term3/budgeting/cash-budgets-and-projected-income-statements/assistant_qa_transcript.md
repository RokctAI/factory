**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the collection schedule keeps tripping me on WHICH month's percentage applies to WHICH sales. Is there a layout that stops the layers from scrambling?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is worth drilling until it becomes a reflex. Draw the months as columns and the SALES as rows, then interrogate each row: where does this month's money land? Take August's credit sales of R90 000 on the 25-55-15 pattern. Twenty-five percent lands in August itself, fifty-five percent lands in September, fifteen percent in October, and five percent lands nowhere, ever. Do that for every sales row and any question answers itself: read DOWN the column of the month you were asked about. September's column collects three entries — twenty-five percent of September's own sales, fifty-five percent of August's, fifteen percent of July's — and the discipline of rows-are-sales, columns-are-cash prevents the classic scramble of applying fifty-five percent to the wrong month. Two refinements protect the remaining marks. First, direction of time: the same-month percentage always attaches to the NEWEST sales and the smallest percentage to the OLDEST — if your biggest layer is flowing from the oldest month, you have reversed the pattern. Second, a completeness check: each sales row's percentages must account for one hundred percent — twenty-five plus fifty-five plus fifteen plus five — and the five percent must appear NOWHERE in the schedule, because bad debts never become cash. Questions often probe exactly that, asking how much of July's sales is still expected after September, or what bad debts the pattern implies across a year's sales; both fall out instantly if your table is built by rows. And when the question flips to creditors, the same table works with one row per month's purchases and usually a single payment column — shifted a month, at ninety-six percent if a settlement discount applies.

**Mandy:** Next: why does the course keep insisting the cash budget and the projected income statement are different documents? If both describe the same future month, surely a profitable month must also be a cash-positive month, more or less?

**Tutor:** The whole reason budgeting exists as a topic is that MORE OR LESS fails exactly when it matters most. The two documents can and regularly do point in opposite directions in the same month, and each disagreement describes a real business situation. Take profitable-but-cash-starved, the dangerous quadrant. Dube Cycles sells R150 000 in a month at healthy margins — the projected income statement glows. But three-quarters of those sales are on credit and collect over the following two months, while the suppliers, the wages and the scooter deposit all demand cash NOW: the cash budget for the same month shows a deep shortfall. Businesses die in precisely that month — profitable on paper, unable to pay the wages that keep the doors open — and it is the growth months, when rising sales pump ever more money into debtors and stock, that are the most treacherous. That is why the scenario of expanding sales alongside tightening cash is not a paradox but the expected pattern. Now the reverse quadrant: cash-rich but unprofitable. A month in which a loan lands, or old debtors finally pay, or the owner contributes capital, can show a fat closing balance while trading itself runs at a loss — the cash budget smiles while the income statement warns. Read only the bank balance and you miss the rot. So the two documents are two instruments on one dashboard: profitability is the engine's health, cash is the fuel line, and either can fail independently. The boundary questions — depreciation, loans, capital purchases, drawings — are really testing whether you understand WHY the instruments differ, which is the difference between placing items correctly by rule and placing them correctly by understanding.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: if the 5 percent never pays, why sell to those customers at all? Someone said the business should only sell for cash and the whole schedule disappears.

**Tutor:** Cash-only would indeed delete the collection schedule, the bad debts and a fair slice of the bookkeeping — along with most of the sales. The argument is a genuine business trade-off, and strong answers weigh it rather than moralise it. Credit is a sales tool: schools, clubs, businesses and most large customers simply cannot or will not pay on delivery — their own cash cycles forbid it — so a supplier who refuses credit hands their business to a competitor who will extend it. The five percent is therefore not waste; it is a COST of accessing the ninety-five percent, exactly as rent is a cost of having a shop. The management question is never how to reach zero bad debts — a business with zero bad debts is usually turning away profitable customers out of excessive caution — but whether the cost sits at the level the credit policy accepts. That is what the pattern in the schedule monitors: five percent failing while three-quarters collects within two months is a policy working; watch the drift, because a slide toward ten percent bad with slower layers is credit control failing in real time. And the levers are graded, each with a price: credit checks and limits filter the riskiest customers at the door; early-settlement discounts speed the layers but surrender margin; interest on overdue accounts compensates for late money but can chase customers away; suspension and formal collection recover some debts at real cost. The sentence to remember: bad debts are a planned cost of a credit policy, budgeted at the expected rate, monitored through the collection pattern, and managed — never assumed away.

**Mandy:** Last one: with budget-against-actual, how do I avoid the trap of calling every underspend favourable and every overspend unfavourable? The lesson's advertising example makes me distrust the labels completely.

**Tutor:** Keep the labels but demote them: favourable and unfavourable are only the ARITHMETIC verdict — which direction the variance moves the surplus — and the analysis begins, not ends, there. The real credit sits in three questions you ask after labelling. Question one: is the line CAUSAL or CONSEQUENTIAL? Advertising, maintenance, training and credit-control spending are causal — they generate or protect other lines, so an underspend there must be read against what it was supposed to cause. Dube Cycles saved R4 500 of advertising and lost R24 000 of sales: arithmetically favourable, economically a disaster — the false economy. Consequential lines like commission or delivery costs MOVE with sales, so an overspend beside higher sales may be pure health, and the fair test is proportion: commission at five percent of actual sales is on-budget behaviour even when the rand amount overshoots. Question two: is the variance PRICE or QUANTITY? Wages overspent because of a negotiated increase is a different management problem from wages overspent because casual hours ran uncontrolled — same rand variance, different explanation, different action, and answers that separate the two read like management, not memorisation. Question three: was the BUDGET itself honest? A variance measured against a padded or fantasy budget measures the lie, not the performance — which is where the ethics content re-enters: padding, targets set to impress lenders, and blame-shifting all corrupt the comparison that internal control depends on. So the disciplined pattern for any variance question is: state the figures and the arithmetic label; explain the likely cause, pairing related lines; then judge — favourable, unfavourable, or favourable-looking-but-false — with the reason. Label, explain, judge — three moves that work on every variance any question can print.
