# Part 1 — Expert

Every statement this course has built so far looked backwards: what was sold, what was spent, what remains. Budgeting swings the whole apparatus around to face the FUTURE: what will October's sales be, when will September's credit customers actually pay, can the business afford a new delivery scooter in the same month the loan instalment falls due? Grade 12 works with two forward-looking documents — the PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT and the CASH BUDGET — and the skill your final exams reward is ANALYSIS and INTERPRETATION: reading the forecasts, holding them up against what really happened, and judging the decisions the numbers expose. The debtors' collection schedule is the topic's one demanding calculation, and it turns up in almost every budgeting question you will ever face.

## Subtopic: Two Forecasts, Two Questions

The two documents answer different questions, and confusing them is the topic's cardinal error.

The PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT asks: will the business be PROFITABLE next period? It is an ordinary income statement written before the fact — projected sales, projected cost of sales, projected expenses — governed by the ACCRUAL rules the whole subject runs on: income when earned, expenses when incurred. Depreciation belongs there, because the equipment will wear whether or not a cent moves. Bad debts belong there, because some credit sales will never be collected. Whether cash changes hands is beside the point.

The CASH BUDGET asks: will there be MONEY IN THE BANK? It records only cash, and only when it actually moves. Receipts: cash sales, collections FROM debtors — arriving on the delay the schedule works out — rent income received, a loan received, capital contributed, interest earned on an investment. Payments: cash purchases, payments TO creditors on their own delay, wages, expenses actually paid, equipment bought, loan instalments, drawings. Receipts less payments gives the month's surplus or shortfall, which carries the opening bank balance to the closing one, month after month.

Now the items every exam uses to probe the boundary. DEPRECIATION: projected income statement yes, cash budget never — no rand ever leaves the bank for wear. BAD DEBTS: income statement yes; in the cash budget they exist only as money that never arrives inside the collection schedule. A NEW DELIVERY SCOOTER: cash budget yes, in full, in the month of payment; income statement no — only its depreciation belongs there. A LOAN RECEIVED: cash budget yes; income statement no — borrowing is not income. DRAWINGS or DIVIDENDS: cash budget yes; income statement no — distributions are not expenses. Discount allowed: income statement yes; and in the cash budget it shows itself as debtors settling for less than face value.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two questions stated, accrual against cash, and the boundary items placed on the correct side.

## Subtopic: Debtors' Collections and Creditors' Payments

The collection schedule converts projected CREDIT SALES into projected CASH, and it is nothing but disciplined arithmetic.

Dube Cycles sells bicycles and parts on credit, and experience has taught the owner, Lerato, this collection pattern: 25 percent of a month's credit sales pay in the SAME month; 55 percent pay in the FOLLOWING month; 15 percent pay in the SECOND month after; and 5 percent never pay at all — bad debts. Credit sales: July R60 000, August R90 000, September R110 000.

Compute SEPTEMBER's collections, layer by layer. From September's own sales: 25 percent of 110 000 — R27 500. From August's sales, now one month old: 55 percent of 90 000 — R49 500. From July's sales, now two months old: 15 percent of 60 000 — R9 000. September collections: 27 500 plus 49 500 plus 9 000 — R86 000. The 5 percent of each month simply never surfaces — that absence IS the bad debt, the exact point where the income statement's bad-debts line and the cash budget's silence describe the same loss.

Read the pattern before moving on, because the interpretation marks live inside it: only 25 percent pays within the month — Lerato is financing seventy-five percent of every month's credit sales for at least a month. If the pattern deteriorates — say 20 in the month, 50 following, 20 second month, 10 bad — the same sales deliver less cash, later, and the budget must show that strain. Stricter credit vetting, early-settlement discounts, or interest charged on overdue accounts are the levers, and each one carries its own cost.

The CREDITORS' side is the mirror image. Dube Cycles buys stock on credit and pays in the month AFTER purchase, earning a 4 percent settlement discount for prompt payment. August purchases were R70 000, so September pays 96 percent of 70 000 — R67 200. The R2 800 discount is income earned purely by paying on time — and a business that begins missing its settlement discounts is quietly announcing its own cash trouble.

Stop for this section's questions now — September's three layers totalled, the missing five percent named, and the discounted creditors' payment.

## Subtopic: Building and Reading the Cash Budget

Assemble September for Dube Cycles, spoken the way the statement flows.

RECEIPTS. Cash sales: R64 000. Collections from debtors, as computed: R86 000. Commission income on repairs done for the local courier firm: R5 000. Total receipts: R155 000.

PAYMENTS. To creditors, with the discount: R67 200. Salaries and wages: R41 000. Deposit on the new delivery scooter: R18 000. Loan instalment to the bank, including interest: R8 800. Sundry operating expenses: R12 000. Total payments: R147 000.

The month's result: receipts 155 000 less payments 147 000 — a SURPLUS of R8 000. Dube Cycles opened September with an OVERDRAFT of R3 000, so the closing balance is minus 3 000 plus 8 000 — R5 000 favourable. The budget's last three lines — surplus or shortfall, opening balance, closing balance — chain every month to the next, which is exactly why cash budgets run in linked monthly columns.

Now READ it, because reading is the examinable skill. One: the scooter deposit of R18 000 dwarfs the month's surplus — without it September would have shown R26 000 of operating surplus; the business is buying capacity out of a single month's cash, and the remaining instalments must appear in the months ahead. Two: the overdraft clears in September — overdraft interest stops, a healthy sign. Three: test the shape of the receipts — more than half of September's cash arrives through the collection schedule from sales already made; one weak sales month will echo through two further months of collections, so the budget must be re-run the moment sales projections shift. Four: ask the what-if questions the exam asks: if the scooter dealer demanded the full price immediately, or if collections slid to the weaker pattern, would the overdraft return, and could the facility absorb it?

A shortfall month is not failure — it is INFORMATION, delivered early enough to act on: arrange the overdraft before the month begins, postpone the discretionary payment, chase collections harder, or retime the capital purchase. That early warning is the entire reason budgets exist.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the budget assembled to R5 000, the three chained lines, and the what-if reasoning.

## Subtopic: Budget Against Actual, and the Ethics of Forecasting

A budget nobody measures against reality is only a wish. The discipline is VARIANCE analysis: place the budgeted figure beside the actual, label the difference favourable or unfavourable, and — the grade 12 move — explain it and then judge it.

October's comparison for Dube Cycles, three lines of it. SALES: budgeted R150 000, actual R126 000 — unfavourable by R24 000. ADVERTISING: budgeted R6 000, actual R1 500 — on its face favourable, R4 500 saved. Read the two lines TOGETHER and the story reverses: the advertising was cut and the sales collapsed — a FALSE ECONOMY, saving four and a half thousand to lose twenty-four. Spending less than budget is not automatically a virtue; the question is always what the spending was FOR. SALARIES AND WAGES: budgeted R41 000, actual R47 000 — overspent by R6 000. Explanation before judgement: overtime for the year-end stock count, a negotiated increase, or casual staff hired without control? The variance opens the question; the explanation answers it; the judgement follows the explanation, never the raw number.

Then the ETHICS of forecasting, which travels with every budgeting question. PADDING: managers inflating expense budgets so that underspending later looks like discipline — a lie told in advance that misdirects the business's resources. UNREALISTIC sales targets: pitched to impress a bank or to drive staff, they poison every downstream figure — collections, purchases, cash — and a loan granted on a fantasy forecast is money lent into a fiction. BUDGETS AS WEAPONS: slashing a department's allocation without reason, or blaming staff for variances that management's own choices caused. And the governance reading: a budget approved, then quietly overspent on directors' comforts while the training line stays frozen, publishes where the business's real priorities sit — read variances for VALUES, not only for rands.

Internal control closes the loop: budgets prepared before the period, approved at the right level, compared monthly against actuals, variances explained in writing, and corrective action minuted. That cycle — plan, act, compare, correct — is the managerial cousin of the reconciliations this subject keeps teaching: two records of one reality, forced to account for their differences.

The final questions of this part are with you now: variances computed and judged in pairs, the false economy named, and padding with its cost.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic at street level — because anyone who has ever run a side hustle through December has already done everything this lesson teaches.

## Subtopic: The Month Written Down Before It Happens

Karabo is a matric-age photographer who shoots weddings, matric dances and school sports days on weekends. At the end of every month she does something her friends tease her about and her bank manager wishes everyone did: she writes down NEXT month before it happens.

Two separate lists, kept strictly apart. The first list asks: will the photography MAKE MONEY next month? The bookings she expects, what the prints and albums will cost, the travel, the editing software subscription — and a little wear on her camera bodies, because shutters do not last forever. That list is her projected income statement: profit on paper, counting things when they are earned or used up, whether or not money moves that month.

The second list asks the harder question: will there be money IN THE ACCOUNT on the right days? Because the software subscription debits on the first, the taxi to town costs cash every Saturday, and the wedding couple only pays her AFTER the album is delivered on the twenty-eighth. Profitable and broke are different conditions, and the second list — the cash budget — exists because bills arrive on dates, not on averages.

Watch the items that live on one list only. The wear on her cameras: first list, never the second — nobody collects cash for wear. The deposit on the new portrait lens: second list in full — real money out — while only its wear ever reaches the first. The R2 500 her sister repays her: second list — money in — but never income; that is her own money coming home. And the client who might never settle the invoice: a small sad line on the first list, and on the second list simply money that fails to appear.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the two lists with their two questions, and each boundary item on its side.

## Subtopic: When Will the Money Actually Arrive?

The photography business runs mostly on trust: schools and wedding couples pay Karabo after the event. Experience has taught her the rhythm, and the rhythm IS the calculation.

Of every hundred rand she invoices, twenty-five comes in the same month, fifty-five in the next, fifteen the month after that — and five rand, from hard experience, never comes at all. Her invoices: July R6 000, August R8 000, September R10 000. So what lands in SEPTEMBER? Three layers, oldest last: twenty-five percent of September's own R10 000 — R2 500. Fifty-five percent of August's R8 000 — R4 400. Fifteen percent of July's R6 000 — R900. September's collections: R7 800.

Say what the layers mean, because this is the insight the whole topic stands on: September's bank balance is mostly AUGUST's work. Karabo shoots in August and eats in September — on most of every invoice she is her clients' unpaid banker for a month or two. And the five percent that never arrives is no surprise by the time it fails to appear; it was budgeted as a loss from the start, which is the honest way to plan.

Her suppliers run the mirror rhythm. The print lab lets her settle next month, and takes four percent off when she pays on time. August's print bill was R5 000; in September she pays R4 800 and keeps R200 for punctuality. That R200 is real income earned by discipline — and the first month she misses it, her budget is telling her something is wrong with her cash, not with the lab.

Put September together: R7 800 collected, plus R6 000 from cash shoots paid on the day, minus the print lab, the taxi money, the subscription, the lens deposit — and the month shows a surplus that turns her tiny overdraft into a positive balance. The specific rands are not the point: the point is that she KNEW IN AUGUST. Every crisis a budget reveals in advance is a crisis with options; the same crisis discovered on the day has none.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three layers of September, whose work September's money really is, and the discount as a warning light.

## Subtopic: The Plan Meets the Month

End of October. Karabo sits with two columns: what she PLANNED, and what HAPPENED. This half-hour is the most valuable in her business month.

Bookings: planned R15 000, actual R11 800. Down R3 200 — why? Then her eye lands on the next line. Boosted posts and flyers at the schools: planned R600, actual nothing — she skipped the marketing to save money. There is her answer, staring back from two rows: she saved six hundred rand and lost three thousand two hundred in bookings. The saving was the most expensive thing she bought all month. Underspending is not automatically winning — it depends entirely on what the money was FOR.

Travel: planned R1 200, actual R1 900. Overspent — but October brought two extra out-of-town weddings, and those same trips carried the extra bookings that softened the fall. Judge variances in PAIRS and in stories, never one number at a time.

And because Karabo answers only to herself, honesty is easy — but scale her up and the ethics arrive. A branch manager who pads the expense budget, requesting more than needed so that underspending later reads as skill, is lying to the business in advance. An owner who shows the bank a fantasy bookings forecast to secure a loan is borrowing against income that exists only on paper. A company that freezes the training budget while the directors' travel line swells has published its true priorities in numbers. Budgets are where intentions become visible — which is why exam questions pair every budgeting calculation with a judgement question, and why the honest comparison of plan against outcome, month after month, is an internal control as real as any safe.

Her cycle, and the subject's: write the month before it happens; live it; compare the two columns; explain every gap in words; fix what the explanation demands; and carry the lesson into next month's plan.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the false economy in her two rows, the paired reading of the travel line, and the padding fraud at company scale. This is the final new topic of the course — from here everything turns to drawing the whole subject together for the final exams.
