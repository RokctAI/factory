# Part 1 — Expert

Value-Added Tax rides inside every till slip in South Africa, and grade 12 asks the vendor's question: at the end of the period, how much must the business pay over to SARS — or claim back? The machinery from grade 11 returns — output tax, input tax, the fifteen percent, the inclusive and exclusive calculations — and grade 12 layers on the complications that make the real computation: returns, bad debts, drawings, discounts, and the VAT control account that gathers them all in one place. Then the part this course refuses to treat as optional: the ethics. VAT money never belongs to the business that holds it, and the frauds built around it are theft from every taxpayer. Calculations first, ledger second, conscience third.

## Subtopic: VAT Machinery Revisited

VAT is a tax on consumption, charged at FIFTEEN percent on most goods and services, collected in stages by registered VENDORS. A business must register as a vendor once its taxable turnover passes one million rand a year; smaller businesses may register voluntarily.

The vendor's two streams. OUTPUT TAX: the VAT a vendor charges its customers on sales — collected on behalf of SARS. INPUT TAX: the VAT a vendor itself pays its suppliers on purchases and expenses — claimable back from SARS. The vendor pays over the difference: output less input. That difference is precisely the tax on the VALUE THE VENDOR ADDED — which is where the tax gets its name.

The two calculations, drilled until they are reflex. From an EXCLUSIVE price — the price before VAT — the tax is fifteen percent: a desk priced at R3 000 exclusive carries VAT of R450, invoiced at R3 450. From an INCLUSIVE price — the till-slip price, VAT already inside — the tax is the inclusive amount times fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: R3 450 inclusive times fifteen over one-fifteen gives the same R450. The classic error is applying fifteen percent directly to an inclusive amount — R517,50 instead of R450 — so always ask first: does this figure already contain the VAT?

Not everything carries fifteen percent. ZERO-RATED supplies are taxed at zero percent — basic foodstuffs like brown bread, maize meal, rice, vegetables, fruit, eggs, milk and vegetable oil, kept tax-free to protect poorer households. A vendor selling zero-rated goods charges no output tax yet still claims input tax on its costs. EXEMPT supplies stand outside the system altogether — financial services, residential rent, and educational services: no output tax, and no input tax claims either. Zero-rated is in the system at zero; exempt is out of the system — a distinction worth two easy marks whenever it appears.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: output against input, both calculations both ways, and zero-rated separated from exempt.

## Subtopic: Output Less Input — the Amount Owed to SARS

Now the grade 12 computation: one VAT period for Naledi Building Supplies, a registered vendor, all base figures exclusive of VAT unless stated otherwise.

The plain streams first. Sales for the period: R380 000 — output tax at fifteen percent, R57 000. Purchases of stock and taxable expenses: R260 000 — input tax, R39 000. If the story stopped there, the amount owed would be R18 000. It never stops there; four adjustments follow, and each one is examinable.

Adjustment one: DEBTORS' ALLOWANCES — customers returned goods and received credit notes totalling R16 000 exclusive. The original sales charged output tax the business will never keep, so output falls by fifteen percent of 16 000 — R2 400.

Adjustment two: DRAWINGS of stock. The owner took stock costing R4 000 exclusive for personal use. The business claimed input tax when it bought that stock, but the goods were consumed, not sold on — so VAT of R600 is charged back, increasing the amount owed. The principle: input credit belongs only to goods used to make taxable sales.

Adjustment three: BAD DEBTS written off — R6 900 INCLUSIVE. The business paid output tax across on those sales when they were invoiced, but the money never came. SARS returns the tax on the lost amount: 6 900 times fifteen over one-fifteen — R900 of input tax relief. Watch the inclusive calculation: the debt the customer owed included VAT.

Adjustment four, when it appears: DISCOUNTS allowed to customers reduce output tax the same way returns do, and discounts received from suppliers reduce input tax.

Assemble Naledi's period. Output side: 57 000 less 2 400 for returns, plus 600 for drawings — R55 200. Input side: 39 000 plus 900 for bad debts — R39 900. AMOUNT PAYABLE TO SARS: 55 200 less 39 900 — R15 300. Had input exceeded output — a major equipment purchase, or mostly zero-rated sales — the difference would flip into a REFUND receivable from SARS, and questions flip it regularly.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four adjustments with their directions, the inclusive bad-debt calculation, and the R15 300 assembled.

## Subtopic: The VAT Control Account

The ledger tells the same story in one account: VAT CONTROL — the running slate between the vendor and SARS. Learn it by sides.

The CREDIT side gathers everything owed TO SARS: output tax on sales, and the drawings adjustment. The DEBIT side gathers everything owed BACK to the business: input tax on purchases and expenses, VAT on debtors' allowances, VAT relief on bad debts written off — and every payment made to SARS, because a payment settles part of the slate.

Speak Naledi's account through the period. It opens with a CREDIT balance of R9 800 — last period's VAT still owed at the start. During the period the business pays SARS R9 800, settling that opening debt: debit VAT Control, credit Bank. Then the period's own entries land: credit side, output tax R57 000 and drawings R600 — R57 600 of new credits. Debit side, input tax R39 000, returns R2 400, bad debts R900 — R42 300 of new debits. Balance the account: credits 9 800 plus 57 600 against debits 9 800 plus 42 300 — a CREDIT balance of R15 300. The account agrees with the calculation, as it must — same facts, second route.

Read the balance like every other slate in the subject: a credit balance is a CURRENT LIABILITY — VAT owed to SARS; a debit balance is a CURRENT ASSET — a refund due. And the payment rhythm: most vendors file a return every TWO months on SARS eFiling and pay by the deadline; late filing and late payment draw penalties and interest, which are expenses the income statement must then carry — the price of poor administration, not a cost of trading.

The discipline that keeps this account honest is documentary: a vendor may only claim input tax on a valid TAX INVOICE showing the supplier's VAT registration number. No invoice, no claim — a rule that shuts the door on invented input tax, and the first thing a SARS auditor asks to see.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the sides of the control account, the R15 300 balanced out of the ledger, and the balance read in both directions.

## Subtopic: Ethics, Fraud and the Vendor's Duty

Here is the fact that reframes the entire topic: VAT collected by a vendor is NEVER the vendor's money. From the moment a customer pays R3 450 for a R3 000 desk, the R450 belongs to SARS; the vendor merely holds it in passing. A business in cash trouble that dips into collected VAT to pay wages is not borrowing — it is spending money that was never its own, and the law treats it accordingly.

The frauds, named the way exam scenarios name them. UNDER-DECLARING output: sales rung up off the books, cash taken without invoices — the classic till fraud, which understates output tax and steals from the fiscus sale by sale. INFLATING input: claiming VAT on fictitious purchases, on invoices from ghost suppliers, or on private expenses dressed up as business costs — each claim a direct extraction from SARS. FAILING TO PAY OVER: charging customers VAT and simply keeping it — and collecting tax without being registered is the aggravated version, charging fifteen percent with no intention of paying anything across. And FALSE REFUND claims: engineering a period where input appears to exceed output, then pocketing the refund.

Why it matters beyond the law: VAT is the fiscus's broadest revenue stream — it funds the grants, clinics, schools and roads every scenario in this subject depends on. A vendor stealing VAT steals from every household in the country, including the poorest, who pay VAT on almost everything they buy. That is why VAT honesty is taught as an ETHICS topic and not merely a compliance one.

The controls, internal and external. Internally: till sales reconciled to bankings daily, sequential invoice numbers with gaps investigated, input claims matched to valid tax invoices, VAT returns prepared and reviewed by different people, and the VAT control account reconciled to the returns filed. Externally: SARS audits, penalties, interest, and criminal prosecution for evasion. And the professional duty stated plainly: an accountant asked to prepare a false return must refuse — assisting evasion makes the accountant party to the crime, and no employer's instruction changes that.

The final questions of this part are with you now: whose money the R450 is, the four frauds with the control that answers each, and the accountant's duty stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from behind the counter of a corner shop — because VAT makes complete sense the day you realise the shopkeeper is not really a taxpayer here; she is an unpaid tax collector.

## Subtopic: The Fifteen Cents That Was Never Yours

Farida runs a corner shop near the taxi rank. A customer buys a bag of sugar for R46. Inside that R46, exactly R6 is not Farida's — it never was, not for one second. It is VAT, riding inside the price, and she is holding it for SARS the way a taxi conductor holds the fares for the owner.

That is the whole mental model: every till slip splits invisibly into two piles. The bigger pile — R40 — is the shop's: it covers the stock, the rent, the profit. The small pile — R6 — belongs to the tax man and is just sleeping in her till until payday, which comes around every two months.

Learn the two little sums on her calculator, because everything else stands on them. When she knows the BEFORE-tax price and wants the tax: fifteen percent. A carton priced at R300 before VAT means R45 of tax and R345 on the shelf. When she knows the SHELF price and wants the tax hiding inside it: times fifteen, divided by one hundred and fifteen. The R46 sugar: 46 times 15 over 115 — R6. Never take fifteen percent of a shelf price — the tax is already inside that number, and taxing the tax gives the wrong answer every single time.

One more thing Farida knows from her own shelves: the brown bread, the maize meal and the eggs carry NO VAT — the law keeps the survival basket tax-free, so those are zero-rated. But her airtime, sweets and sugar all carry the fifteen. And when she pays rent for the little flat above the shop, no VAT appears at all — residential rent lives outside the system entirely. Zero-rated is inside the system at a rate of nothing; exempt never entered the building.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the two piles in the till, both calculator sums, and the bread separated from the rent.

## Subtopic: Collected In, Paid Out, and What Remains

Every two months Farida settles with SARS, and her whole return is one subtraction with a story on each side.

Side one: the VAT she COLLECTED from customers — on everything standard-rated that crossed the counter at fifteen percent. Say her period's standard-rated sales were R38 000 before tax: she is holding R5 700 of SARS's money.

Side two: the VAT SHE paid — because Farida is also a customer. The wholesaler charged her VAT on stock; the electrician charged VAT on the rewiring; the delivery bakkie's fuel and parts carried VAT. Suppose those purchases came to R26 000 before tax: she paid out R3 900 of VAT to other vendors. That money SARS owes back to her — she should not carry tax on her way to collecting tax.

Settle: collected 5 700, paid 3 900 — she owes SARS R1 800. Now look at what that difference really taxes: she bought for 26 and sold for 38 — the R12 000 of value SHE added — and fifteen percent of 12 000 is exactly the R1 800. The name of the tax is its own explanation.

Then life happens, and each event nudges one side. A customer returns a faulty iron and gets her money back: the VAT on it goes back too — collected shrinks. Farida takes a bag of sugar home off her own shelf: she claimed input VAT on sugar that never got sold to anyone — she must give that claim back, so her bill grows. A customer who bought on credit disappears without paying: Farida paid SARS output tax on money she never received — SARS gives it back, and her bill shrinks. Returns down, drawings up, bad debts down — three nudges, and every exam question is these nudges wearing bigger numbers.

And if in some period she buys a big new freezer and her VAT paid outweighs her VAT collected? Then SARS owes HER, and the refund arrives on the same slate. The slate points both ways.

Your questions for this part are up now: the subtraction with its two sides, the value-added check, and the three nudges with their directions.

## Subtopic: Why Cheating VAT Is Stealing Twice

Now the uncomfortable scene this course insists on. Farida's brother-in-law, who also runs a shop, offers her advice one Sunday: keep two tills. Ring the card sales through the machine, let the cash sales vanish, and your VAT bill halves. Everyone does it, he says.

Take his advice apart, because you will be asked to. The R6 on that bag of sugar never belonged to the shop. A customer PAID it, believing it was going to the state; keeping it is not clever bookkeeping — it is stealing money already collected from someone else. And it is stealing twice over: once from SARS, and once from every person who depends on what VAT funds — the clinic queue, the school feeding scheme, the grant on which half the shop's own customers survive. A shopkeeper hiding cash sales is picking the pockets of her own community and calling it business.

The brother-in-law's other tricks are the same theft in different clothes. Inventing invoices from a supplier who does not exist, to inflate the VAT claimed back — that is not hiding income; that is actively pulling money OUT of SARS. Charging customers fifteen percent without ever registering as a vendor — collecting a tax you never intend to deliver. And the refund game: cooking the period so SARS appears to owe you.

How do such tricks get caught? Boringly, which is the beauty of it. SARS sees the wholesaler's records — every invoice Farida's supplier issued to her sits in the supplier's own VAT return, so a shop buying R26 000 of stock and declaring R12 000 of sales is a red light on a screen. Invoice numbers run in sequence, and gaps ask questions. Bankings that dwarf declared sales ask louder ones. The two-till shop is not a secret; it is a delayed penalty — with interest, and with a criminal case where the amounts justify it.

So Farida keeps one till, files every two months, claims only what her tax invoices prove, and sleeps at night. The exam-ready sentence: VAT collected is held in trust for SARS; evasion is theft from the state and the public; and the vendor's controls — honest tills, valid invoices, reconciled returns — protect the business and the country at once.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: whose R6 it was, the brother-in-law's three tricks named as theft, and the boring ways they get caught. Master the slate and the nudges — your final exams will set this exact machinery with different numbers.
