# Part 1 — Expert

Until now, every business we studied BOUGHT its goods ready-made and sold them at a mark-up. Term four opens the workshop door: what changes when a business MAKES what it sells? Cost accounting answers the maker's first question — what does one unit truly cost to produce? — and Grade 10 lays down the vocabulary and the basic calculations: direct and indirect materials and labour, factory overheads, prime cost, fixed and variable behaviour, and work-in-progress. Master the sorting first; the arithmetic is short once every cost sits in its right bin.

## Subtopic: Direct and Indirect — the First Sorting

Our example for the whole topic: Lerato's Woodcraft, a small workshop making wooden stools — three hundred stools in an ordinary month. Every cost of making them lands in one of the bins we now define, and the first sorting question is always: can this cost be traced to ONE SPECIFIC stool?

DIRECT MATERIALS: materials that become part of the product and are traceable to each unit — the timber, R60 of it per stool. Cut the planks for stool number 148 and you know exactly which stool carries that cost. DIRECT LABOUR: the wages of the hands that make the product — the carpenter paid R30 per stool assembled. Traceable again: his work IS the stool.

Now the INDIRECT family — genuine costs of making, but spread across ALL the stools and traceable to none in particular. Indirect materials: glue, sandpaper, saw blades — consumed in production, but nobody can say how much glue went into stool 148. Indirect labour: the foreman who oversees the whole floor — his wage serves every stool equally and no stool specifically. And the other workshop costs: the rent on the floor, its electricity, the depreciation on the saws and drills. All these indirect production costs gather under one name: FACTORY OVERHEADS.

The test, every time: traceable to one unit? Direct. Serves production in general? Indirect — overheads. And mind the boundary of the factory: the SHOP'S costs — the sales assistant at the counter, the showroom's rent — are not production costs at all. Cost accounting fences the workshop and counts only what happens inside the fence.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the sorting test applied cost by cost — direct material, direct labour, or overheads — and what stays outside the fence.

## Subtopic: Prime Cost, Overheads and the Cost of One Stool

Now assemble the month's production cost, layer by layer, in the order the formula expects.

Layer one: PRIME COST — the direct costs on their own: direct materials plus direct labour. The month: timber, three hundred stools at R60 — R18 000; the carpenter, three hundred at R30 — R9 000. Prime cost: R27 000. The name explains itself: prime, first — the costs that touch the product directly.

Layer two: add the FACTORY OVERHEADS. The month's indirect production costs: workshop rent R4 200; the foreman R2 400; electricity R900; glue, sandpaper and blades R900 — R8 400 in total. TOTAL COST OF PRODUCTION: prime cost R27 000 plus overheads R8 400 — R35 400.

Layer three: the cost of ONE stool — the UNIT COST: total cost of production divided by units produced: 35 400 over 300 — R118 per stool. And with it, every pricing decision finds its floor: sell at R150 and each stool clears R32; sell below R118 and every sale funds a loss. The unit cost is to the maker what the cost price was to the trader — the number beneath the mark-up.

One more concept the syllabus names: WORK-IN-PROGRESS. At month-end, some stools stand half-assembled — legs cut, seats unfixed. They are no longer raw timber, not yet finished goods: they are work-in-progress, production's halfway house, and their cost stays in the workshop until completion carries them out. Grade 10 needs the concept and its place; the full ledger treatment waits for Grade 11.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three layers computed in order: prime, total, unit — and work-in-progress placed between raw and finished.

## Subtopic: Fixed and Variable — the Second Sorting

The first sorting asked WHERE a cost attaches; the second asks HOW A COST BEHAVES when production rises or falls — and this distinction drives every planning decision.

VARIABLE COSTS move WITH output, in step: make more stools, buy more timber, pay for more assembly. Timber at R60 and the carpenter at R30 are variable: three hundred stools cost 27 000 in variables; four hundred stools, 36 000. Per UNIT, though, the variable cost stands still — every stool carries its own R90, whether it is the first or the four-hundredth.

FIXED COSTS ignore output within the month: the workshop rent is R4 200 whether the floor makes one stool or four hundred; the foreman's wage likewise. Our overheads — R8 400 — are broadly the fixed family (electricity has a variable element; Grade 10 may treat it simply). Total fixed cost: constant. Per UNIT, the fixed cost FALLS as output rises — the same R8 400 spread over more stools.

Watch the consequence at unit level. Three hundred stools: variable R90 plus a fixed spread of R28 — unit cost R118. Four hundred stools: variable still R90, but 8 400 over 400 is R21 — unit cost R111. Making more made each stool CHEAPER — not by cutting a single cost, but by spreading the fixed ones wider. That is the logic of scale, standing in one small workshop: busy factories out-price idle ones because their rent lies thinner on each unit.

And the reverse is the warning: a quiet month of one hundred and fifty stools carries R56 of fixed cost per unit — R146 in total — and the comfortable R150 price suddenly clears almost nothing. Fixed costs are why quiet months hurt more than they look.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — behaviour sorting and the unit-cost effect: compute at different volumes, and explain why the unit cost moved.

## Subtopic: The Calculations, Formalised

Gather the topic into its assessment shapes.

Shape one: CLASSIFICATION — a list of costs, each to its bin: direct material, direct labour, indirect material, indirect labour, other overheads; then, along the other axis, fixed or variable. Every cost wears BOTH labels: timber is direct AND variable; workshop rent is overhead AND fixed; the foreman is indirect labour AND fixed. Two sortings, one table — answer both dimensions when both are asked.

Shape two: the COST STATEMENT in layers: direct materials plus direct labour — prime cost; plus factory overheads — total cost of production; divided by units — unit cost. Labelled lines, in that order, every time: 18 000 plus 9 000 — 27 000; plus 8 400 — 35 400; over 300 — R118.

Shape three: the WHAT-IF — recompute at a new volume, respecting behaviour: variables scale, fixeds stand. At four hundred: 24 000 plus 12 000 — prime 36 000; plus 8 400 — 44 400; over 400 — R111. The error the marker hunts: scaling the fixed costs with volume — stretching the rent by four-thirds because output rose — which contradicts the very definition of fixed.

Shape four: the DECISION comment: given unit cost and price, advise — with the four-move anatomy from term three intact: state, judge, explain, recommend. Unit cost R146 in a quiet month against a R150 price: state the sliver of margin, judge it risky, explain the fixed-cost spread, recommend building volume or trimming overheads.

Why does this matter beyond the workshop? Because next year's manufacturing accounts, and every business decision about pricing, outsourcing and scale, stand on today's two sortings. Where a cost ATTACHES tells you what a product truly costs; how it BEHAVES tells you what changing your plans will do.

The final questions of this part are with you now: all four shapes — classify on both axes, build the statement, run the what-if, and advise.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same workshop from the kitchen — because anyone who has ever cooked for a big family gathering already understands manufacturing costs; they just haven't priced the pots yet.

## Subtopic: Cooking for the Function

Your neighbour Mrs Pillay takes an order: five hundred samoosas for a family function. Watch her costs sort THEMSELVES into the famous bins.

The pastry and the filling that go INTO each samoosa — she can tell you to the cent what each one carries: that is direct material, the timber of the samoosa. The helper she pays per samoosa to fold and fry — those hands make the product: direct labour. Now the quieter costs: the gas bottle heating everything — which samoosa did the gas cook? All of them; none in particular. The wear on her big pots, the cousin she pays to watch the frying while she takes the next order — all real costs of making, none traceable to one samoosa. That whole family is the overheads: the cost of KEEPING THE KITCHEN COOKING, as against the costs inside each samoosa.

And the sharp line she draws without ever studying accounting: her taxi fare to DELIVER the order is not a cooking cost at all — the kitchen fence stops at the kitchen door. Selling and delivering live outside it.

One question sorts everything, hers and Lerato's alike: can I point at ONE unit and say "this cost is yours"? Yes — direct. No, it serves the whole kitchen — overhead. Sort with that question and the vocabulary is finished.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the function order are coming to you right now: sort each cost with the pointing question, and mind the kitchen fence.

## Subtopic: What One Samoosa Costs

Now Mrs Pillay's real question — the one every maker must answer before naming a price: what does ONE cost me to make?

She stacks the costs in three layers, exactly like the workshop. First, what sits inside each unit: pastry and filling, say R2,50 per samoosa, and the helper's folding and frying, R1,50 — R4 of direct cost each; five hundred units: R2 000 of prime cost — the FIRST layer, the costs that touch the product. Second, the kitchen's running costs for the job: gas R450, pot wear R50, the pot-watcher R250 — R750 of overheads. Total cost of the order: R2 750. Third, the division that answers everything: R2 750 over five hundred — R5,50 per samoosa.

Now she can price with her eyes open: sell at R8 and each unit clears R2,50; the whole order clears R1 250. Price at R5 because a rival did — and every single samoosa sells at a fifty-cent loss that FEELS like business while it quietly eats her savings. The unit cost is the floor under the price; a maker who does not know it is not pricing, only guessing.

And the half-made tray at the end of the day — filled pastry, not yet fried? That is her work-in-progress: no longer ingredients, not yet product, its costs waiting in the kitchen overnight. Every maker has that tray; accounting simply gives it a name and keeps its costs counted.

Your questions for this part are up now: build the three layers for a new order — prime, total, per-unit — and find the floor under the price.

## Subtopic: The Rent Doesn't Care How Busy You Are

Last idea — the one that separates makers who survive from makers who wonder where the money went: some costs SCALE with the work, and some costs stand still and must be fed regardless.

The pastry scales: double the samoosas, double the pastry, rand for rand — yet each unit's share never changes: R4 of direct cost per samoosa, always. The gas bottle and the pot-watcher do not scale that way — and a kitchen RENT, if she paid one, would not budge at all: R750 of standing costs for the job, one order or three.

Now watch the standing costs do their quiet arithmetic on the unit. A busy order, five hundred units: R750 spread over 500 — R1,50 each; unit cost R5,50. A great month, seven hundred and fifty units: the same R750 thins to R1 each — unit cost R5. She cut no cost and bargained with nobody — busyness itself made every unit cheaper. That is why the big bakery undercuts the home kitchen: its ovens' rent is sliced thinner on every tray.

And the warning runs in reverse: a slow month, one hundred and fifty units, and the R750 lands R5 on each — unit cost R9, ABOVE the R8 price, and every sale now loses money at the old price. The rent doesn't care how busy you are — which is exactly why YOU must care how busy you are. Standing costs are a drumbeat: match your volume to it, or it plays you out of business.

That is the whole topic, honestly: sort costs by where they attach, sort them by how they behave, stack the layers, divide — and read what volume does to the floor under your price.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: scale an order up and down, keep the standing costs standing, and explain — in one clear sentence — why the unit cost moved. Next lesson closes the year: budgets, the art of writing the future down before spending it.
