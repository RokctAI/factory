# Part 1 — Expert

Every topic this year looked backwards — transactions already made, statements of what they produced. The final topic of the year swings the whole subject around to face the road ahead: BUDGETING, the habit of writing money down BEFORE it is spent. Grade 10 wants the concepts — cash budget, capital budget, zero-based budgeting, medium-term and long-term planning — and the core calculation, which is the cash budget: a month-by-month forecast of what will flow in, what must flow out, and the balance left standing. We will build one full quarter today, and with it the year's toolkit closes.

## Subtopic: Writing the Future Down

Start with the difference in kind. A financial statement REPORTS: drawn up after the events, from records, its figures provable. A BUDGET ESTIMATES: drawn up before the events, from plans and expectations, its figures targets. The two documents meet later, when the real months have happened and actual figures sit next to budgeted ones — and every gap between them, every VARIANCE, must be explained. Sales short of budget: why? Electricity over budget: why? The budget is the plan; the comparing afterwards is the control.

Then the family, each member answering one question of its own. The CASH BUDGET is the everyday workhorse: month by month, money expected IN, money that must go OUT, and the balance that survives — the question of survival itself: can we pay our way each month? The CAPITAL BUDGET plans the big, rare purchases — machinery, vehicles, equipment — long-life assets bought with large sums, planned apart from the daily running of the business. Then the two horizons: MEDIUM-TERM budgets look one to three years out — growing the range, taking on staff; LONG-TERM budgets carry the strategy further still — own premises, a second branch. The further out the plan looks, the rougher its strokes: next month is planned in rands; five years ahead is planned in direction.

Last, a METHOD with its own name: ZERO-BASED budgeting. The comfortable method — incremental budgeting — takes last year's figures and adds a percentage. Zero-based budgeting refuses the shortcut: every single line begins at NOTHING and must argue for its whole amount all over again — why does this cost exist, what does it deliver, what would break without it? It costs more effort, and it earns its keep by killing dead costs that would otherwise ride along unexamined, year after year.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: statement against budget, each family member matched to its question, and zero-based set against the copy-and-add habit.

## Subtopic: The Cash Budget, Built

Now build the workhorse for Lerato's Woodcraft — a small workshop selling handmade stools. The next quarter: October, November, December — one column per month, and the unchanging skeleton: RECEIPTS, then PAYMENTS, then the arithmetic of survival.

Receipts first, with the estimate spelled out. Sales run at R30 000 a month: seventy percent for cash, thirty percent on credit, and credit customers pay the FOLLOWING month. October's receipts: cash sales of R21 000, plus the collection of September's credit sales — thirty percent of September's R30 000 — R9 000. Total in: R30 000. November and December repeat the pattern while sales hold level. Mark the discipline underneath: a cash budget records money when it ARRIVES, not when it is earned. A credit sale enters the budget a month after the invoice, on the day the debtor actually pays. Accrual thinking belongs to the statements; the cash budget thinks in cash alone.

Payments next: timber and materials R18 000 a month, paid in the month; wages R4 500; workshop rent R2 500 — R25 000 of routine outflow. And one planned exception: in November, a wood lathe, R9 000 — a CAPITAL item, scheduled in the capital budget, and LANDING in the cash budget in its purchase month, because the cash budget must see every rand that leaves, whatever the reason.

Then the survival arithmetic, month by month: receipts minus payments gives the month's SURPLUS or DEFICIT; add the OPENING bank balance; the answer is the CLOSING balance — which walks forward to become next month's opening. October: in 30 000, out 25 000 — surplus 5 000; opening 3 000; closing R8 000. November: the routine surplus of 5 000 minus the lathe's 9 000 — a deficit of 4 000; opening 8 000; closing R4 000. December: surplus 5 000; closing R9 000.

Stop for this section's questions now — the skeleton and the quarter: receipts with the collection lag, payments with the capital landing, and the balances rolling forward.

## Subtopic: Reading the Budget — the Decisions Inside

A budget earns its keep through the decisions it makes possible BEFORE any money moves. Read the quarter the way its owner should.

First reading: November survives — narrowly, and on purpose. The lathe's R9 000 lands in a month that generates only R5 000 of routine surplus, and the balance dips to R4 000. Seen IN ADVANCE, the dip is manageable: it was placed on the calendar deliberately, in a month the balances could carry. Seen too late — a lathe bought on impulse in a thin month — the same purchase could have emptied the account. The budget's first gift is timing: big purchases go where the balances can hold them. And had the projection dipped below zero, the budget offers its choices while they are still cheap: shift the lathe a month later, or arrange an overdraft IN ADVANCE — a bank prices a calm, planned request kindly and a desperate one dearly.

Second reading: the credit-sales lag is a standing tax on timing. Thirty percent of every month's earning arrives one month late — October EARNS R30 000 but only holds R30 000 of cash because September fed the pipeline. And if sales GROW, the lag bites harder: a bumper month's cash lands only after that month's costs were already paid. The cash budget exposes what the income statement never shows: profit and cash run on different calendars.

Third reading: the budget is also the watchdog. When December actually happens, its real figures line up beside these estimates, and every variance asks its own question. Materials above budget — a price rise, or waste? Sales below — the market, or slipping service? Variance by variance, the budget turns surprises into investigations. A business without a budget meets its troubles as emergencies; a budgeted business meets them as variances, months smaller.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — read the quarter: the November decision, the effect of the lag, and variances as early warnings.

## Subtopic: The Wider Family and the Year's Close

Now place the rest of the family with worked meaning. The CAPITAL BUDGET standing behind November's lathe: a schedule of intended asset purchases — the lathe now, perhaps a bandsaw next year, a delivery bakkie the year after — each with its amount, its timing and its funding: paid from savings, or borrowed. It hands the cash budget its landing dates, and it disciplines growth: assets arrive by plan, not by appetite. The MEDIUM-TERM view asks where Lerato's Woodcraft goes over one to three years — a wider product range, a second craftsman, sales beyond R30 000 a month. The LONG-TERM view sketches further still: premises owned rather than rented, perhaps a showroom — strategy in outline, sharpened every year as it comes closer.

And ZERO-BASED budgeting, aimed where it pays best: at the expense lines that renew themselves by habit. The R2 500 rent — is the space still right, or is the workshop paying for corners it never uses? Every subscription, every standing arrangement — justify it from zero, or cut it. Households and businesses alike bleed money through lines nobody has questioned in years; zero-based budgeting is spring-cleaning with a calculator.

Technique for assessment questions, four habits. One: obey the cash-only rule — depreciation NEVER appears in a cash budget, because no rand leaves; and credit sales enter at COLLECTION, not at sale. Two: keep the skeleton's order — receipts, payments, surplus or deficit, opening balance, closing balance — and roll every closing into the next opening. Three: label each estimate with its logic — "thirty percent of September's sales" written beside the figure is where the marks live. Four: comment like a decision-maker — a dip is a timing question, a shortfall is a financing question, a variance is an investigation question.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the family placed, the cash-only traps avoided, the quarter read for its decisions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic at the kitchen table in the last week of the month — because every household that makes it from payday to payday is already running a cash budget; tonight it simply gets its formal name.

## Subtopic: The Month Written Before It Happens

Month-end at home. Before the salary lands, somebody sits down with a pen and writes out a month that HASN'T HAPPENED yet: what is coming in — the pay, the money an uncle promised to return; what has to go out — rent, electricity, groceries, transport, the stokvel contribution. Then the one subtraction that matters: does the in cover the out, and what is left standing?

That page IS a cash budget — the identical tool Lerato uses for the workshop, the identical tool a city uses for its year, different only in the number of zeros. And feel how it differs from everything else this year taught you: the statements looked BACKWARD — events recorded and proved. The budget looks FORWARD — what we EXPECT, written down so the month can be steered instead of endured. A report is a rear-view mirror; a budget is the headlights.

Why write it at all — why not simply be careful? Because a plan that was never written can never be checked. When the month closes, the written page stands there and the real month stands next to it: groceries cost R400 more than planned — why? The transport money finished in week three — why? The written plan turns "the money just disappeared" into precise questions with findable answers. That comparison — plan against actual — is the entire discipline of control, and it cannot happen without the plan on paper first.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the headlights are coming to you right now: budget against report, and why the writing-down is where the power sits.

## Subtopic: Watching the Purse Through Three Months

Now run Lerato's quarter the way a family watches its purse — because the drama of a cash budget lives in the running balance.

October: R30 000 comes in — the till's cash plus the September credit customers finally paying. R25 000 goes out — timber, wages, rent. The month puts R5 000 INTO the purse, which opened with R3 000: purse now R8 000. Comfortable.

November — the planned knock: the wood lathe, R9 000, landing in a month that only adds R5 000. The purse gives back R4 000 and closes at R4 000. Thin — and that thinness is the whole point: Lerato KNEW November would be thin before November began. She chose the month, saw the dip on paper, and confirmed the purse could take it. Now picture the neighbour who buys the same lathe on a good feeling, in a lean month, and watches the rent debit bounce — same machine, same price, disaster instead of a dip. The difference was never the money; it was the LOOKING AHEAD.

December: no surprises, R5 000 in, and the purse recovers to R9 000 — stronger than the quarter began. And catch the one sly rhythm inside the figures: thirty percent of every month's sales arrives a month LATE, because credit customers settle next month. The workshop can be selling beautifully and still feel squeezed this month — earning and receiving keep different calendars. Households know that rhythm well: the work done this month, the money seen next month.

Your questions for this part are up now: run the purse through the quarter — in, out, dip, recovery — and say why November was safe for Lerato and dangerous for the neighbour.

## Subtopic: Why Every Rand Must Re-Apply for Its Job

Final idea of the year, and a sharp one: the ZERO-BASED habit. Most budgets get written by copying — last month's list, the same lines, maybe ten percent extra for prices. Comfortable, and quietly dangerous, because dead costs love a copied list. The streaming service nobody watches anymore. The delivery arrangement from when the workshop stood on the other side of town. The data bundle sized for a phone that was replaced last year. A copied budget carries its dead forever.

Zero-based budgeting dismisses the entire list once a year and makes every rand RE-APPLY for its job. Begin from nothing: why does this line exist? What does it buy us NOW — not three years ago? What actually breaks if we cut it? The rent defends itself in one sentence — no workshop, no stools. The forgotten subscription stands silent — cut it. It is spring-cleaning for money: harder work than copying, and it pays for itself in recovered rands every single time a household or a business does it honestly.

Now step back, because the year ends here. You can record any transaction through six journals; post and balance a ledger; reconcile the detail to its controls; adjust the books until they tell the truth; close a year and prove it; present the results as formal statements; read those statements with the ratios; cost a product down to one unit; and write the future down before spending it. That is not a school checklist — it is the working toolkit of financial stewardship, the same one that runs corner shops, stokvels, companies and cities.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the budget family matched to their jobs, the purse run once more, and one zero-based interrogation done properly. Finish them, and Grade 10 Accounting is — in the fullest sense — accounted for.
