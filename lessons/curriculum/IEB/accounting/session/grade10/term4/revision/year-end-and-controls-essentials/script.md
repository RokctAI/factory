# Part 1 — Expert

The first revision session rebuilt the recording machine; this one revises what happens when the year ENDS — and what guards the machine all year long. Four stations: the adjustments that make the figures true; the final accounts and the two statements that report them; the percentages and ratios that read the results; and the guard-rails — internal control, ethics, GAAP — together with the cost and budget arithmetic that closed the year. One business carries every figure: Lerato's Trading, year ended the twenty-eighth of February, and every number will balance on the way through.

## Subtopic: The Adjustments That Make the Year True

The trial balance at year-end is honest about the past but wrong about the year, because money's calendar and earning's calendar disagree. Adjustments repair that difference — the MATCHING principle at work: this year's statement must carry this year's income and this year's expenses, no more, no less.

Run Lerato's four. DEPRECIATION: the vehicle cost R60 000, straight-line at fifteen percent — R9 000 for the year. The equipment cost R20 000, diminishing balance at twenty percent, accumulated depreciation R4 000 — so twenty percent of the carrying value of R16 000: R3 200. Total depreciation R12 200, an expense with no cash movement, and the accumulated depreciation accounts grow by the same amounts. ACCRUED EXPENSE: telephone paid R3 300, but February's account of R300 is unpaid — the expense becomes R3 600, and R300 stands as a current liability. PREPAID EXPENSE: insurance paid R2 400 includes R400 covering next year — the expense becomes R2 000, and R400 stands as a current asset. ACCRUED INCOME: rent income received R8 800 at R800 a month, one month outstanding — income becomes R9 600, and R800 stands as a current asset.

Each adjustment is the same two-sided move: correct the income or expense to the TRUE year figure, and park the difference on the statement of financial position as something owed or something owned.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: both depreciation methods computed, and the four timing repairs with their balance-sheet homes.

## Subtopic: From Final Accounts to the Two Statements

Year-end closes the books in a fixed sequence. The TRADING ACCOUNT sets sales against cost of sales: sales R150 000, cost of sales R100 000 — gross profit R50 000, and check the policy: fifty percent mark-up on cost, one hundred thousand times one comma five. The PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT takes the gross profit, adds other income — rent income R9 600 — and absorbs the operating expenses: salaries 22 000, telephone 3 600, insurance 2 000, depreciation 12 200 — R39 800 in all. Net profit: 50 000 plus 9 600 minus 39 800 — R19 800, closed away to Capital. Then drawings of R7 400 close against Capital too, and the POST-CLOSING trial balance carries only balance-sheet accounts into the new year.

The statements dress the same figures formally. The STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME tells the year as a story: sales down to gross profit, down to net profit R19 800. The STATEMENT OF FINANCIAL POSITION photographs midnight on the last day: non-current assets at carrying value R90 800 — land and buildings 45 000, vehicles 60 000 less accumulated depreciation 27 000, equipment 20 000 less 7 200; current assets R19 200 — trading stock 9 500, debtors 5 200, bank 3 300, prepaid insurance 400, accrued income 800. Total assets R110 000. Against them: owner's equity — opening 88 000 plus profit 19 800 less drawings 7 400 — R100 400; current liabilities R9 600 — creditors 9 300 plus accrued expenses 300. Total R110 000. The photograph balances.

Stop for this section's questions now — the closing sequence in order, net profit built, and both sides of the photograph totalled.

## Subtopic: The Gauges — Reading the Results

Statements report; ratios INTERPRET — and every comment needs a comparison: against last year, against the policy, against the danger line.

Profitability first. Gross profit on cost: 50 000 over 100 000 — fifty percent, exactly the mark-up policy, so no stock leaked away unrecorded. Gross profit on sales: 50 000 over 150 000 — thirty-three comma three percent. Net profit on sales: 19 800 over 150 000 — thirteen comma two percent: of every hundred rand through the till, about thirteen stayed behind as profit after all costs.

Liquidity next — can the near bills be paid? CURRENT RATIO: current assets 19 200 over current liabilities 9 600 — two to one: two rand of near money for every rand of near debt, comfortable. ACID TEST strips out stock, the slow asset: 9 700 over 9 600 — just over one to one: without selling a single item, the business can cover its immediate debts — sound, and worth keeping there.

SOLVENCY: total assets 110 000 against total liabilities 9 600 — everything owed could be paid more than eleven times over; failure is nowhere in sight. And the owner's question, RETURN ON EQUITY: net profit 19 800 over average equity — 88 000 opening, 100 400 closing, average 94 200 — twenty-one percent. Set it against what a bank pays on savings: several times better, and the difference is the reward for effort and risk. The technique for every comment: figure, comparison, judgement — never a number left standing naked.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — each gauge computed, and each one commented with its comparison.

## Subtopic: Guards, Rules and the Cost of One Unit

What kept the figures believable all year? Three layers. INTERNAL CONTROL: division of duties — the one who records is not the one who banks; documents for every movement, numbered and kept; authorisation before money leaves; physical safeguards and regular independent checks — stock counted, cash reconciled. Every control answers a named risk, and scenario questions reward naming both. ETHICS: the bookkeeper's figures must carry integrity, objectivity, confidentiality and competence — because a control system run by a dishonest hand protects nothing. GAAP: the agreed rules that make one business readable by another — historical cost, recording at what was paid; prudence, anticipating losses but never profits; matching, the engine behind every adjustment; going concern, valuing the business as continuing; business entity, the owner and the business as separate persons; materiality, disclosing whatever would change a reader's decision.

And the year's last arithmetic, revised in one breath. COST: making 500 candles took direct materials R3 500 and direct labour R2 500 — prime cost R6 000; overheads R1 500; total R7 500; unit cost R15. Selling at R20 leaves R5 per candle. BUDGET: next month written in advance — receipts 11 000, payments 8 500, surplus 2 500, opening balance 1 500, closing R4 000 — with the cash-only rule holding: no depreciation, credit collections counted when they arrive.

The final questions of this part are with you now: controls matched to risks, principles matched to scenarios, and the unit cost and budget skeleton computed clean.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the year-end from the stoep of the shop — because closing a year is something every household already understands: the honest stocktake before the new start.

## Subtopic: Tidying the Room Before the Photo

Year-end is photo day: one picture of the business, taken at midnight on the last day, that outsiders will trust. But before any photo, you tidy the room — and that is all the adjustments are.

The bakkie in the yard is not worth what it cost three years ago; the books must admit the wearing — that is depreciation, R12 200 of honesty this year, no cash moving, only truth arriving. The February phone calls billed in March belong to THIS year's picture — pull them in, R300 still owed. The insurance paid ahead for next year does not belong in this picture — push R400 out; it is cover the business still OWNS. The tenant's last month of rent, earned but not yet received — pull it in, R800 owed to us. Four movements, one principle: the photo must show what this year truly used and truly earned, not what the bank account happened to witness.

And feel why anyone bothers: an untidied photo flatters or slanders. Skip the depreciation and the business looks richer than it is; skip the owed phone account and the expenses look smaller than they were. The neat photo is not for the photographer — it is for everyone who must trust the picture: the bank, the tax office, the owner's own decisions.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the tidying are coming to you right now: each adjustment named, and where its leftover piece stands in the photo.

## Subtopic: The Report Card and the Health Check

Two documents come out of photo day, and they answer two different questions a parent would ask about a child.

The REPORT CARD — the statement of comprehensive income — answers: how did the year GO? Read Lerato's like a story with three floors. Top floor: bought for one hundred, sold for one hundred and fifty — the buying-and-selling game earned R50 000. Middle floor: add the tenant's rent, R9 600. Bottom floor: subtract everything it cost to keep the doors open — salaries, calls, cover, wearing — R39 800 — and the year's true mark is R19 800. One page, and the owner knows whether the effort was worth it.

The HEALTH CHECK — the ratios — answers: how is the body DOING underneath the mark? Money for the month's bills: two rand ready for every rand owed — healthy. Without selling stock: still just over a rand for every rand — standing steady. If everything ended tomorrow: assets cover the debts more than eleven times — no danger anywhere. And the owner's own question: the money left in the business earned twenty-one out of every hundred rand — far better than the bank pays, so the sweat was worth it. Numbers alone say nothing; numbers COMPARED say everything. Every comment is the same little sentence: here is the figure, here is what I compared it to, here is my judgement.

Your questions for this part are up now: the three floors of the report card, and the health check read out loud with comparisons.

## Subtopic: Locks on the Doors, and What One Candle Costs

Last stretch — the guards, and the two street arithmetics.

The guards first, as a story. A tuck shop where one person takes the money, counts the money, records the money and banks the money is not a system; it is an invitation. The fixes are old and simple: split the jobs so no hand touches money end to end; a slip for every movement, numbered so a missing one shouts; nothing paid without a second nod; the stock counted by someone who does not sell it. And underneath the locks, the person: rules cannot save books kept by a dishonest hand, which is why the ethics list — honesty, fairness, keeping confidences, doing the job properly — is not decoration. It is the foundation the locks stand on.

Then the candle arithmetic, the year's most useful street skill. To know what ONE candle costs, gather everything the batch consumed: wax and wicks R3 500, the hands that poured them R2 500, the table and the burner R1 500 — seven and a half thousand rand for five hundred candles — fifteen rand each. Sell at twenty and every one carries five rand home. Price without knowing the fifteen, and you might work all year to go backwards. And the last habit: before the month starts, write it down — what should come in, what must go out, what survives. Eleven thousand in, eight and a half out, purse grows to four. The year taught many formats; underneath them all it taught three street laws: guard the money with split hands and slips, know what one unit truly costs, and write the month before living it.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the weak tuck shop repaired, one candle costed, and the month written in advance. Finish them and the year's whole toolkit — recording, closing, reading, guarding, costing, planning — is revised and yours.
