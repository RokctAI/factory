**Thandi:** Revision questions coming in! First: the two depreciation methods. Some of us still mix up WHICH base each method uses — is there a way to lock it down for good?

**Tutor:** Lock it to the name of the method, because each name announces its base. Straight-line says: the SAME line every year — so the base never changes: cost. The vehicle at sixty thousand and fifteen percent gives nine thousand this year, nine thousand next year, a flat staircase down to nothing. Diminishing balance says: the balance DIMINISHES — so the base must be the carrying value, cost minus everything already written off. The equipment: twenty thousand cost, four thousand accumulated, so twenty percent of sixteen thousand — three thousand two hundred — and next year the base shrinks again, so the charge shrinks with it. When a question gives you accumulated depreciation, that figure is the flag: subtract it first if the method diminishes, ignore it in the calculation if the line is straight. One more anchor: straight-line suits assets that wear evenly with time; diminishing suits assets that lose most value early, like vehicles and computers. Name, base, shape of the staircase — three hooks, and the methods stop swapping places in your memory.

***

**Thandi:** Next: prepaid and accrued still tangle people. The lesson says "pull in" and "push out" — how do we know which way each item moves?

**Tutor:** Give every item one interrogation: does it belong to THIS year's story? Two questions in sequence. First, was it USED or EARNED this year? Second, has the money MOVED yet? The February phone account: used this year, money not yet moved — so pull the R300 in as an expense, and because somebody is still owed, it stands as a current liability. The insurance covering next year: money moved, but the cover is not used this year — push the R400 out of the expense, and because the business owns paid-for cover, it stands as a current asset. The tenant's outstanding month: earned this year, money not arrived — pull the R800 into income, and the claim on the tenant is a current asset. Notice the pattern: whatever you pull in or push out never vanishes — it parks on the statement of financial position as something owed BY us, or something owed TO us, or something we still own. If your adjustment has no parking place, you have only done half of it — and half an adjustment is a photograph with a thumb over the lens.

***

**Thandi:** Third: the ratios. In a comment question, how much working do we show — and what makes a comment score full marks instead of half?

**Tutor:** Full marks live in a three-part sentence, and most learners write only the first part. Part one, the figure: the current ratio is two to one. Part two, the comparison — and this is the part that separates comments from statements: compared to the safe benchmark, or to last year's one comma six, or to the policy. Part three, the judgement in business language: liquidity is comfortable; the business can meet its short-term debts twice over. Figure, comparison, judgement — one sentence each, and never a number left standing naked. As for working: show the fraction before the answer — nineteen thousand two hundred over nine thousand six hundred — because a wrong answer with a right method still earns method marks, while a bare wrong answer earns nothing. And choose the comparison that fits the gauge: profitability ratios compare to policy and to last year; liquidity to the safe lines; return on equity to what the bank pays. A comment that compares to the WRONG thing — judging liquidity against the mark-up — signals guessing. The gauges are only half the skill; aiming each one at its own benchmark is the other half.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: internal control questions give us a scenario and ask what could go wrong and how to fix it. What is the cleanest way to structure that answer?

**Tutor:** Name the risk, name the control, and make them shake hands — one pair per problem. Scan the scenario for the classic weaknesses, and there are only a handful: one person handling money end to end; no documents, or unnumbered ones; no authorisation before money leaves; nobody independent checking; assets standing unguarded. For each weakness, write the pair: the RISK — cash can disappear between the till and the bank without a trace; the CONTROL — divide the duties, so the person who records is not the person who banks, and reconcile the two records monthly. Then the document pair: missing slips hide missing money — number every slip, so a gap shouts. The authorisation pair: unauthorised spending drains the account — nothing paid without a second signature. What loses marks is naming controls in the air — "they need better security" — without tying each one to the specific risk it kills. And close with the foundation when the question invites it: controls are locks, but locks on a door guarded by a dishonest hand protect nothing, which is why the ethics of the bookkeeper — integrity, objectivity, confidentiality, competence — sit underneath every control that works. Risk, control, handshake. Repeat until the scenario runs out of holes.
