# Part 1 — Expert

Final exams reward the learner who sees the year as ONE machine, not ten loose topics. This revision session rebuilds the recording machine end to end: the accounting equation that governs every entry, the six journals that capture a month, the posting that gathers the month into the ledger, the trial balance that proves it, and the control accounts that check it. One small month of transactions travels through the whole machine, every figure balancing along the way — the same month any exam will hand you, only wearing different numbers.

## Subtopic: The Equation Behind Every Entry

Everything stands on one sentence: ASSETS equal OWNER'S EQUITY plus LIABILITIES. What the business OWNS is financed by what the owner put in and what outsiders lent — always, at every moment, after every transaction. And because the equation must stay level, every transaction has TWO effects: a debit and a credit, equal in amount. That is DOUBLE ENTRY — not a rule invented to keep teachers busy, but the arithmetic of the equation defending itself.

Hold the debit-credit compass for the five account types. ASSETS live on the debit side: they increase debit, decrease credit. EXPENSES and DRAWINGS behave like assets — increase debit. OWNER'S EQUITY, LIABILITIES and INCOME live on the credit side: they increase credit, decrease debit. Every entry the year threw at you obeys this compass; when in doubt, ask two questions — WHICH two accounts, and WHICH one increases.

Test it on the month we will carry through the whole session: Lerato's Trading. The owner deposits R15 000 capital. Bank — an asset — increases: debit Bank R15 000. Capital — owner's equity — increases: credit Capital R15 000. The equation after one entry: assets R15 000 equal equity R15 000 plus liabilities nought. Level. Every transaction that follows, however dressed up, is this same move in different clothes.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the equation stated, the compass applied, and the double effect named for a capital deposit.

## Subtopic: Six Journals, One Month

The journals are the diaries — each type of transaction has its own book and its own source document. Cash IN goes to the CASH RECEIPTS JOURNAL from receipts and till tapes. Cash OUT goes to the CASH PAYMENTS JOURNAL from EFT records and cheque counterfoils. Credit SALES go to the DEBTORS JOURNAL from duplicate invoices; goods a debtor sends back go to the DEBTORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL from credit notes. Credit PURCHASES go to the CREDITORS JOURNAL from original invoices; goods returned to suppliers go to the CREDITORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL from debit notes. And whatever fits no diary — corrections, write-offs, interest charged — goes to the GENERAL JOURNAL.

Now record Lerato's month. Cash sales R7 500, cost of sales R6 000 — the CRJ carries both, because the perpetual system records the sale AND the shelf's loss together; check the mark-up: 6 000 times one comma two five is 7 500 — twenty-five percent on cost. A credit sale to Mr Naidoo, R2 500, cost R2 000 — Debtors Journal. Stock bought for cash R6 500, rent R2 000, wages R1 200 — CPJ. Stock bought on credit from the wholesaler, R4 000 — Creditors Journal. Mr Naidoo returns goods of R500, cost R400 — Debtors Allowances Journal, credit note issued. He then pays R1 200 on his account — CRJ. And Lerato pays the wholesaler R2 500 — CPJ.

Eight transactions, six doors, every one chosen by two facts only: cash or credit, and which direction the value moves.

Stop for this section's questions now — documents matched to journals, and the month's transactions sent through the right doors.

## Subtopic: Posting, Balancing and the Trial Balance That Proves It

Month-end gathers the diaries into the GENERAL LEDGER — totals for the big columns, item by item for sundries — and then every account is balanced. Follow the month home. Bank: in came 15 000 plus 7 500 plus 1 200 — R23 700; out went 6 500 plus 2 000 plus 1 200 plus 2 500 — R12 200; balance R11 500 debit. Trading stock: in 6 500 plus 4 000 plus the R400 cost of the return; out 6 000 plus 2 000 as cost of sales; balance R2 900 debit. Debtors control: 2 500 owed, less the 500 allowance, less the 1 200 payment — R800 debit. Creditors control: 4 000 owed less 2 500 paid — R1 500 credit. Sales: 7 500 plus 2 500 less returns 500 — R9 500 credit. Cost of sales: 6 000 plus 2 000 less 400 — R7 600 debit. Rent R2 000, wages R1 200, capital R15 000.

Now the TRIAL BALANCE — the month on a scale. Debits: bank 11 500, trading stock 2 900, debtors control 800, cost of sales 7 600, rent 2 000, wages 1 200 — R26 000. Credits: capital 15 000, sales 9 500, creditors control 1 500 — R26 000. The scale sits level — and notice carefully what that proves and what it cannot. Equal totals prove the double entry held; they cannot see an amount posted to the WRONG account, or a transaction left out on both sides. The trial balance is a strong test, not a perfect one — which is exactly why the next layer of checking exists.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the balances computed, the trial balance totalled, and its blind spots named.

## Subtopic: Control Accounts — the Second Count

The general ledger holds ONE account called Debtors Control; the debtors ledger holds a PAGE PER CUSTOMER. The same information, counted twice, from different sources — the control account built from JOURNAL TOTALS, the individual accounts built from INDIVIDUAL POSTINGS. At month-end, the list of individual balances must equal the control balance; and when the two counts of the same street disagree, an error exists and its hiding place is already half-found.

Run the check on Lerato's month. Control account: debit side, credit sales 2 500; credit side, allowances 500 and receipts 1 200; balance R800. The list: Mr Naidoo alone — invoice 2 500, less credit note 500, less payment 1 200 — R800. Agreement. The creditors side mirrors it all: control credited with purchases 4 000, debited with payments 2 500, balance R1 500 — and the wholesaler's page in the creditors ledger tells the same R1 500.

What the agreement catches: a receipt written onto the wrong customer's page disturbs the list but not the control; a journal column miscast disturbs the control but not the list; an invoice never posted to the individual account leaves the list short. Each kind of disagreement points at its own kind of error — internal control in miniature: two independent records, built along different routes, made to face each other monthly. The favourite exam question here hands you a control balance and a list that differ, plus four errors; correct each record, and watch the two counts meet.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the two counts distinguished, the agreement performed, and errors matched to the record they disturb.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole machine again, on the kitchen table — because the year's bookkeeping is nothing more than a corner shop's ordinary week, written down properly.

## Subtopic: One Slip, Six Doors

Picture the shoebox every small shop keeps: every scrap of paper from the month tossed inside — till tapes, invoices, credit notes, EFT stubs. Bookkeeping begins as a SORTING job. Six boxes stand on the table, and every slip belongs in exactly one. Money came IN — the receipts box. Money went OUT — the payments box. Sold on trust — the invoice-out box. A customer brought goods back — the credit-note box. Bought on trust — the invoice-in box. Sent goods back to the supplier — the debit-note box. And the odd slip that fits nowhere — the catch-all box at the end of the row.

Two questions sort every slip: did cash move, or a promise? And which way did the value travel? Lerato's till tape of R7 500 — cash in. Mr Naidoo's invoice of R2 500 — a promise, value out to him. The wholesaler's invoice of R4 000 — a promise, value in from them. Sort the month and the shoebox chaos becomes six tidy piles — and each pile becomes one journal, already totalled, ready for the ledger. The system the year taught is nothing more mysterious than that sorting, done with discipline, every single month.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the sorting are coming to you right now: slips to boxes, and the two questions that never fail.

## Subtopic: The Month That Adds Up Twice

Why post everything into a ledger at all? Because the shoebox answers "what happened" but never "where do we STAND". The ledger regroups the month by THING instead of by date: one page for the bank, one for the stock, one for what customers owe, one for what the shop owes. Ask the bank page and it answers R11 500 in a single line. Ask the stock page — R2 900 still on the shelf. Ask Mr Naidoo's page — R800 still promised.

Then comes the weigh-in. Every entry all month was a pair — something debited, something credited, equal amounts. So if the machine was fed honestly, all the debit balances heaped on one side of the scale and all the credit balances on the other must WEIGH THE SAME. Lerato's month: twenty-six thousand on the left, twenty-six thousand on the right. Level scale, honest month.

And one street-smart caution: a level scale proves the pairs were equal — it cannot smell a pair written onto the wrong pages, or a slip that never reached the shoebox at all. Someone who pockets a slip before it is recorded steals invisibly, as far as the scale knows. The scale is the first guard, not the only one — which is why the shop counts the street twice.

Your questions for this part are up now: what the ledger answers that the shoebox cannot, and what the level scale proves and misses.

## Subtopic: Counting the Street Twice

The second guard. The shop keeps an exercise book with a page for every customer who buys on trust — Mr Naidoo's page, and everyone else's. Separately, the main books keep ONE line: the total owed by all customers together. Two records of the same street, built from different papers — the exercise book from each slip as it happens, the total line from the month's journal totals.

At month-end, the shop adds up every page in the exercise book and lays the sum next to the total line. They must agree — Lerato's month: eight hundred rand, both counts. When they do not, nobody panics and nobody guesses: the DIFFERENCE itself gives directions. A payment written on the wrong customer's page? The exercise book is wrong, the total line right. A journal column added wrongly? The total line is wrong, the pages right. Two witnesses who never compared notes, telling the same story — that is what makes books BELIEVABLE: to the owner, to the bank, to anyone entitled to ask.

Step back and see the year whole: sort the slips, write the diaries, gather the ledger, weigh the trial balance, count the street twice. Five habits, one machine — and every exam question is simply the machine running with different slips. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the two records named, the agreement performed once more, and each error sent to the record it damages. Master the machine, and the marks follow it home.
