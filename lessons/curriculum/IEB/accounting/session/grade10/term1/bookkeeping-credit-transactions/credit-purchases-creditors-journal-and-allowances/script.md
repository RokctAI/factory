# Part 1 — Expert

Last session the business extended trust; today it receives trust. Buying on credit brings the mirror image of everything you mastered for debtors: the Creditors Journal for credit purchases, the Creditors Allowances Journal for goods sent back, a Creditors Ledger with one personal account per supplier, and a Creditors Control account summarising them all. The machinery is familiar; the direction reverses. Command the reversal and the whole credit system — both sides — belongs to you.

## Subtopic: Buying on Credit — the Invoice Received and the CJ

When Lindi's General Store buys stock on credit from Coastal Wholesalers, the same event wears two names in two sets of books. In COASTAL'S books, Lindi is a debtor — money receivable. In LINDI'S books — the books we keep — Coastal is a CREDITOR: a liability, money the business owes. Same invoice, opposite sides. Fix that mirror in place; it untangles every confusion this topic can offer.

The source document is the INVOICE RECEIVED — Coastal's original invoice, travelling with the goods. It carries Coastal's own number, so the business renumbers it into its own sequence for filing and reference. And before it is recorded at all, internal control demands a matching ritual we will detail later: does this invoice agree with what was ordered and with what actually arrived?

Credit purchases are recorded in the CREDITORS JOURNAL — the CJ. Its column design follows the traffic, as journal design always does: a CREDITORS CONTROL column for the total owed per invoice — every line strikes it — and analysis columns for what the money was owed FOR: TRADING STOCK, the constant traffic; STATIONERY, frequent enough for a lane in most businesses; EQUIPMENT and SUNDRY ACCOUNTS for the rest. Note what is missing, deliberately: no cost of sales column. Buying loads the shelf at cost — one truth, not two; the two-truths pattern belongs to SELLING, where goods leave at cost while money arrives at selling price.

Pause here — questions on the mirror, the invoice received and the CJ's design are with you now. Answer from the direction of the debt: who owes whom?

## Subtopic: Recording and Posting the Creditors Journal

The month's purchases. On the 4th, trading stock from Coastal Wholesalers, R8 500 — renumbered invoice 91. On the 10th, stationery from Karoo Stationers, R600 — invoice 92. On the 16th, a display counter from FitOut Traders, R4 000 — invoice 93: the counter is kept for use, an ASSET — Equipment, not stock and not an expense lane.

Each line carries: date, invoice number, the CREDITOR'S NAME, the total into Creditors Control, and the same amount into its analysis column. Daily, each purchase posts to the supplier's personal account in the CREDITORS LEDGER — and here the mirror bites for the first time: Coastal's personal account is CREDITED R8 500. Creditors are liabilities; liabilities grow on the credit side. Where a debtor's page grew on the debit side because they owed US more, a creditor's page grows on the credit side because WE owe more.

Month end: total and cross-cast. Creditors Control column: R13 100. Analysis columns: Trading Stock 8 500, Stationery 600, Equipment 4 000 — sum 13 100. The journal proves itself, then posts. Credit CREDITORS CONTROL R13 100 in the general ledger — the group liability grew. Debit each analysis total to its account: Trading Stock R8 500 — the shelf loaded at cost; Stationery R600 — an expense of running the business; Equipment R4 000 — an asset acquired. Debits 13 100, credit 13 100. Balanced.

Set the architecture beside the DJ deliberately: same timing — personal accounts daily, control by monthly total; same lock — the list of personal balances must equal control; opposite sides throughout — because the debt now points out of the business, not in.

Stop for this section's questions now: record, post to both ledgers, and name the side each entry takes — reasoning from asset up, expense up, liability up.

## Subtopic: Returns to Suppliers — the Debit Note and the CAJ

Goods travel back the other way too. On the 12th, a box of stock from Coastal arrives damaged: R700 worth, returned. On the 19th, the stationery from Karoo proves to be the wrong type: R150 returned. The business does not wait for the supplier's paperwork — it ISSUES its own document: the DEBIT NOTE, numbered, declaring that the supplier's account is being debited — reduced — by the value returned, and why. The supplier answers in due course with a credit note confirming; the debit note is our record on our timeline.

The book is the CREDITORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL — the CAJ — mirroring the CJ column for column: Creditors Control, Trading Stock, Stationery, Equipment, Sundries. Line one: debit note 31, Coastal Wholesalers, Creditors Control R700, Trading Stock R700. Line two: debit note 32, Karoo Stationers, Creditors Control R150, Stationery R150.

Month-end totals: Creditors Control R850; Trading Stock R700; Stationery R150. Posting reverses the CJ's directions exactly: DEBIT Creditors Control R850 — the group liability shrank; CREDIT Trading Stock R700 — the shelf gave the damaged goods back; CREDIT Stationery R150. Daily, the personal accounts: Coastal's page debited R700 on the 12th; Karoo's page debited R150 on the 19th — we owe each of them less.

Notice the pleasing symmetry with the debtors side, and the one structural difference worth stating precisely: returns to suppliers carry NO cost-of-sales adjustment — the goods were bought at cost and return at cost; only selling ever creates the two-truths pair. And notice what the return does NOT touch: no money moved. Returns adjust the DEBT; payment is a separate event, in a separate journal, on a separate day.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: debit note or credit note first — who issues which — then the entries, both ledgers, both timings.

## Subtopic: The Creditors Ledger, the List, and the Discipline of Paying

Balance the personal accounts. Coastal Wholesalers: credit 8 500, debit 700 — balance R7 800 CREDIT: the business owes Coastal R7 800. Karoo Stationers: credit 600, debit 150 — R450 credit. FitOut Traders: R4 000 credit. Credit balances, all — the natural state of a creditor's account, mirroring the debit balances that were natural for debtors.

The month-end lock: list the creditors — 7 800, 450, 4 000 — total R12 250. Creditors Control: credit 13 100, debit 850 — balance R12 250 credit. Agreement, by construction; disagreement, by error — and the monthly comparison catches it while it is one month old.

Now the discipline that separates businesses that thrive on credit from businesses that drown in it. First, the THREE-DOCUMENT MATCH before any invoice is recorded or paid: the ORDER — what we asked for; the DELIVERY NOTE — what actually arrived, checked and signed at receiving; the INVOICE — what the supplier claims. All three must tell one story; an invoice matching no order, or billing goods never delivered, is a question, not a payment. Second, PAY ON TERMS — not early, not late. Late payment burns the relationship and the credit record; needlessly early payment burns cash the business was entitled to hold all month. Third, watch for SETTLEMENT DISCOUNTS — suppliers rewarding prompt payment — a topic that grows in later grades, but the habit of reading terms starts now. Fourth, the standing rule: the person recording creditors is never the person authorising payments.

Creditors are not merely amounts owed — they are the business's cheapest financing and its most important relationships. The books you built today keep both in good repair.

The final questions of this part are with you now: balance the pages, build the list, run the lock — and for each control scenario, name the matching discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now cross the counter. All of last lesson you were the store trusting customers. Today you are the customer being trusted — and the whole system flips like a reflection.

## Subtopic: The Other Side of the Counter

Remember Mr Sithole's page in Lindi's exercise book? Today Lindi discovers what it feels like to BE Mr Sithole. When she takes R8 500 of stock from Coastal Wholesalers on credit, somewhere in Coastal's office there is a page with LINDI'S name on it. She is a name in someone else's book now.

But Lindi cannot run her store by guessing what other people's books say about her. She keeps her own book about THEM: a page for Coastal, a page for Karoo, a page for FitOut — everyone the store owes. That book is the Creditors Ledger, and the summary line — the whole "we owe" world in one number — is Creditors Control.

Feel the flip in your hands. In the debtors book, a bigger balance meant MORE COMING IN someday — an asset, growing on the debit side. In the creditors book, a bigger balance means MORE GOING OUT someday — a debt, growing on the credit side. Same machinery, mirrored: pages per name, one summary line, daily detail, monthly totals, and the lock — pages adding up to the line — standing guard on this side of the counter too.

One more flip to savour: when Lindi buys stock, only ONE truth gets written — the shelf loads at cost, R8 500, done. No second number. The two-numbers-per-line dance belonged to SELLING, where goods left at cost while the customer owed selling price. Buying is simpler; enjoy it.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the flip are coming to you right now: for each amount, say whose book, whose page, and which way the debt points.

## Subtopic: The Month of Buying on Trust

Walk the month like a story. Early on, the big stock order from Coastal: R8 500, goods on the shelf, nothing paid — Coastal's page grows by R8 500; the summary line grows with it. Midway, the small things: R600 of stationery from Karoo — till rolls, price stickers, the paper the store runs on. And the new display counter from FitOut, R4 000 — pause on that one: the counter is not stock, because it will never be sold, and not stationery, because it will not be used up this month; it is a POSSESSION the store keeps — equipment. Three purchases, three pages, three different homes for the analysis amounts — stock, expense, asset — all owed, none paid.

Then the box from Coastal comes up damaged: R700 of stock, unsellable. Watch what Lindi does — she does not phone and hope. She sends the box back WITH A LETTER THAT HAS NUMBERS: the debit note. "We are reducing what we owe you by R700 — here is why." Her own document, her own number, on her own timeline, into her own books: Coastal's page shrinks by R700, and the shelf gives up the damaged goods at their cost. When Karoo's stationery turns out to be the wrong type, the same move: back it goes, debit note, R150 off their page.

Month end, the pages read: Coastal R7 800, Karoo R450, FitOut R4 000. The list: R12 250. The summary line: R12 250. The lock clicks, this side of the counter too.

Your questions for this part are up now: sort each purchase to its home — stock, expense or asset — then run the pages and the lock.

## Subtopic: Paying Like a Pro

Last skill, and it is the one that keeps a store's name golden: how to owe money WELL.

Rule one: never pay an invoice on its own say-so. Before Coastal's invoice earns a cent, three papers must tell one story: the ORDER — what Lindi asked for; the DELIVERY NOTE — what the driver actually offloaded, counted and signed at the door; and the INVOICE — what Coastal says she owes. Three papers, one story: pay. Any mismatch — invoiced for ten boxes, received nine — is a debit note conversation, not a payment. This single habit stops a business paying for goods it never received, which happens to careless businesses far more often than anyone admits.

Rule two: respect the calendar, in both directions. Pay LATE and the store's name tarnishes — the next order arrives slower, the terms get harsher, and in a small trading community, word travels. Pay TOO EARLY and the store starves itself of cash it was entitled to hold all month — supplier credit is free financing, and leaving it unused is leaving a gift on the table. On terms, on time, exactly.

Rule three: keep the friendship professional. The monthly statement Coastal sends? Check it against Coastal's page in Lindi's own book — two records, independently kept, must agree; where they differ, the documents decide. Queries raised young are queries solved friendly.

Owing money well is a skill: matched papers, a respected calendar, reconciled pages. Do it right, and suppliers compete to serve you — which is worth more than any discount.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: match the papers, read the calendar, settle the differences — the pro's checklist, question by question. Both sides of the counter now belong to you.
