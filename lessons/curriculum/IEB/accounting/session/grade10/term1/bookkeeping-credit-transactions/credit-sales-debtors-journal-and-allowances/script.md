# Part 1 — Expert

Cash transactions built the machine; credit transactions now put it under load. Today the business starts selling on credit — goods now, money later — and two new books of first entry arrive: the Debtors Journal for credit sales, and the Debtors Allowances Journal for goods returned and allowances granted. Beside them opens a second ledger: the Debtors Ledger, one personal account per customer. Master today's machinery and the debtors side of every reconciliation question through matric rests on it.

## Subtopic: Selling on Credit — the Invoice and the Debtors Journal

Why would any business hand over goods without payment? Because credit sells. Customers buy more, and sooner, when they may settle at month end — and a business that refuses credit delivers those customers to a competitor who will not. The price of the advantage is RISK and ADMINISTRATION: money owed must be tracked per person, collected on time, and sometimes written off. Today's books are that administration, done with discipline.

The transaction's source document is the INVOICE — issued in duplicate, numbered in sequence: original to the customer, duplicate to the bookkeeper. The invoice records the parties, the date, the goods and the amount owed. It is the birth certificate of a debt: from the moment it is issued, the customer is a DEBTOR — an asset of the business, money receivable.

Credit sales are captured in the DEBTORS JOURNAL — the DJ — a book of first entry as specialised as the CRJ was: it records credit sales of trading stock, and NOTHING else. Its columns, under the perpetual system, hold no surprises: document number, day, debtor's name, folio, SALES — the selling price now owed — and COST OF SALES, the cost of the goods that left the shelf. Two truths per sale, exactly as at the till; the only change is that the money truth reads "owed" instead of "received".

Each sale must ALSO reach the customer's own record: the Debtors Ledger keeps a personal account per debtor, telling that person's full story — bought, returned, paid, currently owing. The DJ feeds both destinations: totals to the general ledger monthly, personal accounts line by line, daily.

Pause here — questions on credit selling, the invoice and the DJ's design are with you now. Answer with the definitions and the two destinations.

## Subtopic: Recording and Posting the Debtors Journal

The month, at Lindi's General Store — mark-up still 50 percent on cost. On the 5th, invoice 81 to M. Sithole: goods R2 400; cost — divide by 1,5 — R1 600. On the 11th, invoice 82 to P. Govender: R1 800; cost R1 200. On the 19th, invoice 83 to M. Sithole again: R900; cost R600. Each line carries: document number, date, the debtor's NAME — names matter now — the Sales amount, the Cost of Sales amount.

Daily, each sale posts to the personal account: M. Sithole's account is debited R2 400 on the 5th — he owes more; debtors grow on the debit side, like every asset. Govender debited R1 800 on the 11th; Sithole again R900 on the 19th. The folio column notes DJ 1 beside each entry.

Month end: total the columns. Sales R5 100; Cost of Sales R3 400. Now post the totals to the general ledger. Debit DEBTORS CONTROL R5 100 — the general ledger's one-line summary of all debtors, which must always equal the sum of the personal accounts. Credit SALES R5 100 — credit sales are still sales; the Sales account does not care when the money arrives. And the perpetual pair: debit Cost of Sales R3 400, credit Trading Stock R3 400 — the shelf surrendered goods regardless of how the customer pays.

Check the month's double entry as a whole: debits — Debtors Control 5 100, Cost of Sales 3 400; credits — Sales 5 100, Trading Stock 3 400. Balanced, 8 500 each side. And hold the timing architecture, because questions probe it: PERSONAL accounts are posted DAILY — a debtor's balance must be current the moment they phone to ask; CONTROL and the other general ledger accounts receive TOTALS MONTHLY. Detail daily, summary monthly.

Stop for this section's questions now: strip mark-ups, write the lines, and post to both ledgers — naming side and timing for each destination.

## Subtopic: Returns, Allowances and the DAJ

Goods come back. On the 13th, M. Sithole returns goods invoiced at R600 — wrong size, undamaged, straight back to the shelf. On the 21st, P. Govender reports a scratch on an item she is keeping; Lindi grants her R200 off. Two different events, one book: the DEBTORS ALLOWANCES JOURNAL — the DAJ.

The source document is the CREDIT NOTE — numbered, issued to the customer — declaring that their debt has been REDUCED. It is the invoice's mirror: the invoice created debt; the credit note cancels part of it.

Separate the two events with precision, because the recording differs. A RETURN: goods physically come back. Sithole's R600 return enters the DAJ's Debtors Allowances column at R600 — and because the goods return to stock in saleable condition, the COST OF SALES column also carries their cost: R400. An ALLOWANCE: the customer keeps the goods; only the price adjusts. Govender's R200 enters the Debtors Allowances column — and the Cost of Sales column stays EMPTY: no goods moved, so no stock entry exists.

Month-end totals: Debtors Allowances R800; Cost of Sales R400. Post to the general ledger: debit DEBTORS ALLOWANCES R800 — a contra-income account gathering the year's returns and allowances, later subtracted from Sales so turnover reads honestly; credit DEBTORS CONTROL R800 — debtors as a group owe less. And the stock pair, reversed for the return only: debit TRADING STOCK R400 — the shelf regained goods at cost; credit COST OF SALES R400 — that cost is no longer the cost of anything sold. Daily, the personal accounts: Sithole credited R600 on the 13th; Govender credited R200 on the 21st.

Why keep a separate Debtors Allowances account — why not simply debit Sales? Because management needs the truth in two figures, not one: gross sales AND how much came back. A business whose allowances creep from two percent of sales toward ten has a quality problem or a description problem, and folding returns into Sales would bury exactly that signal.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: return or allowance first, then the entries — and let the cost column follow the goods, not the money.

## Subtopic: The Debtors Ledger at Month End

Balance the personal accounts. M. Sithole: debits 2 400 and 900; credit 600 — balance R2 700 debit. P. Govender: debit 1 800; credit 200 — balance R1 600 debit. Each account holds one customer's entire story at a glance, which is the point: when Sithole queries his account, the answer is one page, not a month of journals.

Now the month-end discipline that grows into a full topic in later grades: the LIST OF DEBTORS. Write every personal balance in a list and total it: 2 700 plus 1 600 — R4 300. Compare with Debtors Control in the general ledger: debits 5 100, credits 800 — balance R4 300 debit. They agree, and they MUST: control carries the totals of exactly the journals whose lines built the personal accounts. Any disagreement is an error with an address — a line posted to the wrong person, a total miscast — and the monthly comparison finds it now, not eventually.

Close with the internal control that keeps credit selling safe, because scenario questions live here. Before credit is granted: screen the customer and set a CREDIT LIMIT per debtor. Monthly: send every debtor a STATEMENT — their personal account's story, delivered — so debts stay visible and disputes surface early. Watch the AGE of debts: the older a debt, the colder; follow up the moment terms are exceeded. And division of duties, as always: the person recording debtors should never be the person receiving their payments. Credit is a privilege the business extends and manages — never a courtesy it forgets.

The final questions of this part are with you now: balance the accounts, build the list, test it against control, and name the control measure each scenario calls for.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same week again, but seen from the counter — where selling on credit begins with a face you know, and a promise.

## Subtopic: The Exercise-Book of Names

Every neighbourhood shop that ever gave credit had one: the exercise book under the counter. A page for each person. Mr Sithole's page. The Govenders' page. Take now, pay month end — and the shopkeeper writes it down, because memory is generous and business cannot afford generous.

Lindi's store has grown that book up, properly. When Mr Sithole takes R2 400 of goods on trust, two papers make it official: he receives an INVOICE — the promise, in writing, numbered — and his PAGE receives the entry: owes R2 400 more. That page-per-person book is the Debtors Ledger, and it answers the question the counter actually hears: "Lindi, how much do I owe you?" One page, one glance, done.

But Lindi needs a second view of the same truth. At month end she does not want to page through forty names to learn what the WHOLE street owes — she wants one number. So the promises also flow, as monthly totals, into one summary account in the main cabinet: Debtors Control — the whole street on one line. Pages for the people, one line for the street — and here is the beautiful lock: add up all the pages, and the total MUST equal the street's one line. The same promises, counted two ways. If the counts ever differ, a promise got lost or doubled somewhere — and Lindi knows to hunt before the month closes.

And the shelf still tells its truth: goods that left on a promise still left. Cost of sales rides with every credit sale, exactly as it did for cash — the shelf does not care how the customer pays.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the book of names are coming to you right now: the page, the street-line, and the lock between them.

## Subtopic: The Week Mr Sithole Changed His Mind

Real credit weeks have reverse gear. Wednesday: Mr Sithole is back — the R600 shirt is the wrong size, unworn, tags still on. Friday: Mrs Govender phones — the kettle she bought has a scratch; she will keep it, but wants something off. Two different stories, and the books tell them differently.

The shirt COMES BACK. So two things must un-happen. His debt shrinks: his page is credited R600 — he owes less — and the paper making it official is a CREDIT NOTE: the anti-invoice, numbered, "your debt is reduced". And the shirt goes BACK ON THE SHELF: the stock account regains it at what it had cost — R400 — and that R400 stops being a cost of sales, because that sale, in part, unsold itself. Money story and shelf story, both reversed.

The kettle STAYS with Mrs Govender. Only the PRICE moves: her page is credited R200 — a credit note again — but the shelf? Untouched. No goods moved, so no stock entry. That is the whole return-versus-allowance distinction in one pair of stories: follow the GOODS to know whether the cost column writes. Goods back — cost comes back. Goods stay — price moves alone.

Why do these reversals earn their own book — the Debtors Allowances Journal — instead of minus-lines scrawled in the sales book? Because Lindi needs to SEE the coming-back separately. Sales of R5 100 with R800 coming back is a very different store from sales of R4 300 with nothing returned — same net number, different story. The first store might have a sizing problem, a quality problem, an over-promising problem. Separate books keep separate truths visible.

Your questions for this part are up now: for each story, follow the goods first — then write both pages, and only then the shelf.

## Subtopic: Keeping the Names Good

Last stretch: the habits that stop the book of names becoming a book of losses.

Before a name gets a page at all: decide how much trust it carries. That is a CREDIT LIMIT — Mr Sithole, good for R3 500; a brand-new customer, maybe R500 to begin. Limits are not insults; they are the reason the store can keep saying yes.

Every month end, each name receives its STATEMENT: a copy of their page — bought this, returned that, owe so much. Statements do two quiet jobs: they remind politely, and they invite corrections early — "Lindi, I never bought that kettle" is a conversation you want in week one, not month six.

And watch the calendar on every debt. Money owed for thirty days is normal; at ninety it is drifting toward being a gift. The older the promise, the harder the chase — so the chase begins the day a promise goes overdue: kindly, monthly, in writing.

One more habit, straight from the tuck shop: the person who writes the pages should not be the person who takes the payments. Two sets of hands, and every rand moving between them leaves paper behind.

Do all of this, and credit becomes what it should be: the engine that grows the store, with brakes that work. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each scenario is a name, a promise, or a drift — name the habit that answers it. Then close the book gently; you have run a full credit month, both ledgers, forwards and in reverse. Respect.
