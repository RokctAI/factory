**Thandi:** Class questions before we close the books! First: if the customer hasn't paid a cent yet, how can we already call the sale income?

**Tutor:** Because income is earned by DELIVERING, not by collecting. The moment the goods change hands against an invoice, the business has done its part: stock left the shelf, the customer holds the goods, and a legal claim to the money exists. That claim is itself an asset — the debtor. So the entry says exactly that: debit Debtors Control, the new asset, and credit Sales, the income earned. When the cash finally arrives next month, no new income happens — one asset simply swaps for another: Bank up, Debtors down. Sale first, settlement later; the Sales account cares only about the first.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the return and the allowance both credit the customer's page, so why do the books treat them differently at all?

**Tutor:** Because only one of them moves goods, and the perpetual system tracks goods relentlessly. In a return, the shirt physically comes home: the stock account must regain it at cost, and that cost must leave Cost of Sales, because the sale partly unsold itself. So the DAJ line carries both figures — allowance six hundred, cost four hundred. In an allowance, the kettle never leaves the customer's kitchen: only the price adjusts, so the money side moves alone and the cost column stays blank. The test never fails: follow the goods. Goods back, cost back. Goods stay, price only.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the one that started a debate: why keep forty personal pages AND a control account? Isn't that recording everything twice?

**Tutor:** It is — deliberately, and the duplication is the safety device. The pages answer the customer's question: what do I owe? One page, current to this morning, because personal accounts post daily. The control account answers the owner's question: what does everyone owe together? One line, fed by monthly journal totals. Now the lock: both views were built from the same journals by different routes, so the sum of the pages must equal the control balance. When they agree, the month is confirmed from two directions. When they differ, an error exists with an address — a line on the wrong page, a miscast total — and you find it this month, not at year end. Two views, one truth, checked against each other.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: what actually goes wrong at a business that gives credit but skips the limits, statements and follow-ups?

**Tutor:** Slowly, then suddenly, it finances its own customers into its grave. Without limits, trust is unpriced — one big name can quietly owe more than the store's whole bank balance. Without statements, debts fade from the customer's memory and disputes surface months late, when nobody recalls the kettle. Without age-watching, promises drift from thirty days to ninety, and old debts collect like cold coffee — the older, the harder to recover. And without dividing recording from receiving, a dishonest hand can take a payment and simply not write it down. Each habit is a brake on a real failure mode. Credit without brakes is not generosity — it is a slow leak wearing a friendly face.
