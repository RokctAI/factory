**Thandi:** Class questions rolling in! First: division of duties sounds expensive. A spaza with one owner and one helper can't split every job — so is internal control only for big businesses?

**Tutor:** No — the principle scales down; only the machinery changes. A two-person spaza cannot give every function its own employee, so it substitutes cheap controls for extra people: the owner counts the float with the helper at opening and closing, so cash is never in one person's sole knowledge; a numbered receipt book records every sale; takings are banked daily so no pile accumulates. Look at the tuck shop — three learners and a notebook achieved division of duties with zero cost. And where duties genuinely cannot be split, the compensating control is monitoring: more frequent counts, more frequent reconciliations, the owner's own eyes on the records weekly. The exam skill is proportionality: recommend controls the business in the scenario can actually afford and run.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: risk assessment and monitoring feel like the same thing — both involve someone checking the business. What's the actual difference?

**Tutor:** Point them at different targets and the difference snaps into focus. Risk assessment looks OUTWARD and FORWARD at the business's activities: what could go wrong here, before it does? It produces a list of dangers — cash changes hands at the till, stock passes through the loading door. Monitoring looks INWARD and BACKWARD at the controls themselves: the rules we wrote — are they actually being followed? It produces verdicts — the surprise count matched, the stocktake found a gap. So risk assessment asks 'what should we control?' and monitoring asks 'are our controls still alive?' In a scenario, a business that never imagined a danger failed risk assessment; a business that had the right rule but let it rot failed monitoring.

***

**Thandi:** Here's a sharp one from the front row: in the café scenario, banking the takings daily was called a control — but how does banking money actually STOP anything?

**Tutor:** Trace what daily, intact banking removes and creates. It removes opportunity: a tin holding ten days of takings is a growing temptation and a single point of catastrophic loss — fire, burglary, one bad moment; a tin emptied daily is never worth much. That is the physical-safeguard half. The subtler half is what it creates: a verification trail. Banked intact — the whole day's takings, nothing spent out of the tin first — means every deposit on the bank statement should equal one day's recorded cash. Now two independent records must agree, day by day, and any skimming shows up as a mismatch almost immediately. Delay the banking and mix the days, and that comparison dissolves. So the control does not stop a thief's hand; it shrinks the prize and guarantees the alarm.

**Thandi:** Last question: for the three-move answer, how specific does the recommendation really have to be? Where's the line between 'be more careful' and full marks?

**Tutor:** Test your sentence with three checks: who, what, and against which document. 'Be more careful' fails all three — no person, no action, no paper. 'Deliveries must be counted' names an action but no person, so it is still half a mark at best. Full strength: 'A named employee OTHER than the person who ordered must count each delivery against the delivery note before goods are shelved, and the owner pays only against matched documents.' Who — a different named person; what — count before shelving; against which document — the delivery note and the order. If your recommendation could be printed as a rule on the storeroom wall and followed tomorrow morning without anyone asking a question, it is specific enough. If it reads like advice to a friend, it is not.
