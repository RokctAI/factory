**Thandi:** Class questions incoming! First: if everyone can see the land is worth over a million now, isn't showing R650 000 actually LYING to the reader?

**Tutor:** It would be lying only if the books claimed to show current market value — and they never claim that. The statements promise something narrower and stronger: verifiable fact. The R650 000 sits on a signed deed of sale; the million-one hundred sits in one agent's opinion, and a different agent next week might say nine hundred. Print opinions and every business would print its most flattering one — the bank could trust nothing. So historical cost trades away freshness for reliability, on purpose. And note the safety net: if the land's value ever FELL below cost, prudence would step in and demand the loss be shown. Good news waits for proof; bad news is recorded early. That asymmetry is not deception — it is what makes the books worth believing.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the materiality principle feels dangerously vague. Who decides whether R60 or R6 000 is 'too small to matter'?

**Tutor:** The reader decides — that is the anchor that stops the vagueness. The test is never the amount alone; it is whether leaving the item out or simplifying it would change a reader's DECISION about the business. A R60 calculator changes nobody's lending decision, whichever way it is recorded, so it may be expensed and forgotten. A R380 000 vehicle absolutely shapes how a reader sees the business, so it is disclosed as a fixed asset, visibly. And yes, scale is relative: R6 000 might be immaterial to a supermarket chain and very material to a one-person spaza. When in doubt, ask the anchoring question — would a sensible reader care? — and if the answer is even maybe, disclose it separately. Materiality is judgement, but judgement with a fixed test.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the subtle one: going concern sounds obvious — of course the business plans to continue. Why does it need to be a principle at all?

**Tutor:** Because half the figures in the statements quietly depend on it. Spreading a vehicle's cost over five working years assumes those five years will happen. Valuing stock at cost assumes it will be sold in ordinary trade, not dumped in a closing-down sale. Even keeping land at historical cost leans on the plan to keep USING the land. Now flip the assumption: if the business were closing next month, every one of those figures would be wrong — assets would need quick-sale values, and spreading costs into a future that will not exist becomes meaningless. So the principle earns its place by marking the boundary: continue, and the normal rules hold; close, and a different, harsher valuation takes over. Stating the assumption is what makes all the dependent figures honest.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in a scenario with lots happening at once, how do we stop ourselves grabbing the first principle that sounds right?

**Tutor:** Slow down and run the two-step drill. Step one: SPLIT the story into separate actions — one sentence each. The van kept at cost. The groceries taken and unrecorded. The dead debt kept alive. Three actions, not one story. Step two: for each action, ask the pointing question — what is the accountant refusing to do, or insisting on doing? Refusing to use an opinion instead of an invoice: historical cost. Insisting the owner's taking is written as drawings: entity rule. Refusing to keep a hopeless asset: prudence. One principle per action, each with its reason. The learners who lose marks read the whole scenario, feel a vibe, and stamp one principle across it. The learners who score split first, point second — every time.
