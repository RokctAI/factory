# Part 1 — Expert

Every discipline has its working vocabulary, and Accounting's must be exact before the bookkeeping of the coming weeks begins. Today we consolidate the core concepts — the sole trader, debit and credit, assets, liabilities, equity and capital, journals and ledgers, profit and loss, discounts, the final accounts — and we master the system on which all Grade 10 to 12 recording runs: the perpetual inventory system. Precision now saves marks all year.

## Subtopic: The Sole Trader and the Elements of the Books

The business form for this year is the sole trader: one owner, trading in their own right. Two legal facts define it, and both echo through the books. First, the owner and the business are one person in LAW — the owner is personally liable for the business's debts. Second, despite that, the RECORDS treat them as separate — the business entity rule — because profit must measure the business, not the household.

Now the elements. Assets: possessions and rights of value the business controls — vehicles, equipment, trading stock, money in the bank, debtors. Liabilities: amounts the business owes outsiders — loans, creditors, a bank overdraft. Owner's equity: the owner's interest in the business — what would remain for the owner if the assets settled all the liabilities. Capital is the owner's contribution INTO equity; drawings are the owner's withdrawals OUT of it; profits grow it and losses shrink it. Equity is therefore not a fixed number but a running story of the owner's stake.

Debit and credit: the left and right sides of every account — nothing more mystical than that. The conventions you will use daily: assets increase on the debit side; liabilities and owner's equity increase on the credit side; and because incomes grow equity while expenses shrink it, incomes live on the credit side and expenses on the debit side. Say the whole scheme in one breath: debit what grows on the left — assets and expenses; credit what grows on the right — liabilities, equity and income.

Pause here — questions on these elements are with you now. Classify each item precisely: asset, liability, or a movement in owner's equity, and name its growing side.

## Subtopic: The Pipeline Words — Journal to Ledger to Final Accounts

Next, the vocabulary of the recording pipeline, in the order the work flows.

A journal is a book of first entry: transactions listed daily, by type, from source documents. A ledger is the book of accounts: every item — Bank, Sales, Wages — has its own account, built from journal totals. The trial balance lists all ledger balances to test that total debits equal total credits. The final accounts — the Trading account and the Profit and Loss account — are ledger accounts opened at year end to calculate profit: Trading yields gross profit, the margin between sales and cost of sales; Profit and Loss absorbs all other incomes and expenses and yields net profit, which transfers to Capital. Profit, precisely: income earned minus expenses incurred — earned and incurred, not merely received and paid; that distinction matures in later grades, but plant it now.

Two discount terms complete the vocabulary, and they are treated differently. Trade discount: a reduction OFF the price at the moment of sale — bulk buyers, loyal customers. It is never recorded as a separate item; the sale is simply recorded at the reduced price, because that reduced price IS the price. Cash discount — also called settlement discount: a reward for prompt payment of an account, granted AFTER the sale existed at full price. It IS recorded: discount allowed, an expense, when we grant it to debtors; discount received, an income, when creditors grant it to us. The test: was the price reduced before the transaction was recorded, or was an existing debt settled for less? Before — trade discount, invisible. After — cash discount, recorded.

Stop for this section's questions now: pipeline order and discount classification — answer with the definitions and the before-or-after test.

## Subtopic: Financial versus Managerial Accounting

Accounting has two working branches, and the distinction between them is a clean table of contrasts.

Financial accounting produces the formal record for parties OUTSIDE the business: the financial statements read by the owner in their formal capacity, by banks, by SARS. It reports on the PAST — the year just ended. It follows agreed rules — GAAP — so outsiders can trust and compare. Its output is standardised: the Statement of Comprehensive Income, the Statement of Financial Position.

Managerial accounting serves decision-makers INSIDE the business. It looks FORWARD: budgets, cost calculations, projections — what will the new delivery route cost, what price makes the product profitable. It follows no external rulebook, because its only test is usefulness to the manager; its reports take whatever form the decision needs, and outsiders never see them.

Anchor the contrast with one pair of questions about the same shop. "What profit did the shop make last year?" — financial accounting answers, by GAAP, for anyone entitled to ask. "Should the shop open a second branch next year?" — managerial accounting answers, with budgets and cost estimates, for the owner's eyes only. Same shop, same figures underneath — different audience, direction and rules. In Grade 10 you study mostly financial accounting; cost accounting and budgeting later in the year are your first taste of the managerial branch.

The questions on this section are with you now — each names a report, a user or a purpose. Assign it to its branch and justify with the audience-direction-rules contrast.

## Subtopic: The Perpetual Inventory System

Now the system decision that shapes every entry you will make from next week: how does a business account for its trading stock? Two systems exist; this course runs on the PERPETUAL inventory system, so command it thoroughly.

Perpetual means continuous: the Trading Stock account is updated at EVERY transaction. Buy stock — Trading Stock increases by the COST of the goods. Sell goods — TWO things are recorded at the moment of sale: the sale itself, at selling price; and simultaneously, Cost of Sales — the cost price of the goods just sold — moves out of Trading Stock. Every sale therefore writes two truths at once: what the customer paid, and what the goods had cost. The gross profit on any single sale is visible immediately — selling price minus cost — and the Trading Stock account's balance shows, at any moment, the cost of stock that SHOULD be on the shelves.

Contrast briefly with the periodic system — met in some businesses and later studies: purchases accumulate in a Purchases account, no cost is recorded at each sale, and only a physical stocktake at period end reveals what was sold and what remains. Between stocktakes, the records are silent about stock.

Why does the perpetual system matter beyond bookkeeping neatness? Internal control — connect the lessons. Under perpetual records, the stocktake CHECKS; under periodic, the stocktake DISCOVERS. If the Trading Stock account says R52 000 should be on hand and the physical count finds R50 200, the R1 800 gap is a question with a name — theft, breakage, error — demanding investigation. The periodic system cannot even see that gap: whatever is missing is silently assumed sold. Perpetual records turn stock loss from an invisible cost into a visible, answerable number.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: for each transaction, state exactly which accounts move under the perpetual system — and remember that every sale moves two pairs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now pour all of that vocabulary into one small shop and one busy week — because words stick when they are attached to a story you can see.

## Subtopic: One Shop, Every Word

Meet Sipho. He opens a small shop selling school backpacks. Watch the words arrive one by one, exactly when his business needs them.

He starts alone, with his own savings — one owner, trading for himself: a sole trader. He puts R70 000 of his savings into the business bank account. His stake in the game — that is capital, and the whole of his interest in the business, growing or shrinking from here, is owner's equity. He buys shelves and a card machine: things kept for use — assets. He takes a R25 000 loan from his aunt for the first stock order: now the business owes an outsider — a liability. The backpacks themselves, bought to resell — trading stock, also an asset, recorded at what he paid: cost price.

Every evening he writes the day's happenings in a notebook, in order — his journal. On Sundays he sorts everything into pages: a page for Bank, a page for Sales, a page for the loan — his ledger. Every entry lands on a left side or a right side of those pages — debit and credit, just left and right, behaving by fixed habits: his assets grow on the left; the loan and his own stake grow on the right.

At year end he asks the two big questions. Did the backpacks earn more than they cost me? — the Trading account answers: gross profit. After rent, electricity and the card machine fees, what is truly left? — the Profit and Loss account answers: net profit, which flows into his capital, because the profit is HIS.

One shop, one story — and you have just used nearly every concept on the list, in its natural habitat.

## Subtopic: The Report Card and the Game Plan

Sipho's books get used in two completely different ways, and the difference IS financial versus managerial accounting.

In January, the bank asks: "Show us how the shop performed last year." Sipho hands over his statements — the year that already happened, prepared by the standard rules so the bank can trust and compare them. That is financial accounting: the REPORT CARD. It looks backward, it follows the rules, and it is written for people outside the shop.

That same week, Sipho sits at his kitchen table with a different question: "If I start stocking pencil cases and lunchboxes for winter, what will it cost, and what must I charge?" He scribbles estimates: stock costs, a second shelf, maybe a helper on Saturdays. No rules, no set format, nobody outside will ever see the page. That is managerial accounting: the GAME PLAN. It looks forward, it answers only to usefulness, and it is written for the coach — him.

Report card and game plan. Past and future. Outsiders and insiders. Rules and freedom. Every question on this contrast is one of those four pairs wearing a longer sentence.

Your questions for this part are up now — for each situation, ask: report card or game plan? Then say why, using one of the four pairs.

## Subtopic: The Fridge That Counts

Last idea, and it is the system your whole bookkeeping year runs on. Think of a cooldrink fridge with a counter on the door — every bottle in, counted; every bottle out, counted. At any second, the counter tells you what is inside without opening the door.

That is the perpetual inventory system. Sipho's Trading Stock account is the counter: every backpack bought at cost goes IN; and here is the special move — every time he sells one, TWO things get written at the till. The customer pays R450 — that is the sale. And the backpack that walked out had cost him R270 — so R270 leaves the stock counter as cost of sales, at the same moment. The till knows both numbers on every sale: money in, cost out. Which means Sipho can see his R180 gross profit per bag the moment it happens — not months later.

And the counter gives him a superpower: the honest stocktake. In December he counts the shelves. The counter says stock costing R52 000 should be there; his count finds R50 200. Under this system, that R1 800 gap has a name and demands an answer — miscounting? damage? theft? Without the counter — in the old periodic way, where you only count at the end — the missing bags would simply look like they were sold, and the loss would hide inside the year forever. The counting fridge turns "stuff seems to disappear" into "R1 800 is missing since June — let's find out why."

Counter in, counter out, two entries at every sale, and a stocktake that checks instead of guesses. That is the perpetual system — and from next week, every journal you write will feed that counter.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each is a moment at Sipho's till or shelves. Say what the counter does — what goes in, what goes out, and what the gap would mean. Finish strong; the vocabulary is yours now.
