# Part 1 — Expert

A business can have an honest owner, popular products and a busy till — and still lose money every week to mistakes, theft and plain carelessness. The discipline built to stop those losses is internal control, and this lesson establishes its meaning and its five basic processes: the control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information systems and communication, and monitoring. These five follow you through every grade of Accounting, attached in turn to cash, stock, debtors and fixed assets — master them properly once and they carry you all the way to your final exams.

## Subtopic: What Internal Control Is — and What It Protects

Definition first, stated precisely: internal control is the system of policies, procedures and checks a business puts in place to safeguard its assets, ensure the accuracy and reliability of its records, promote efficient operations, and encourage adherence to its policies. Count the objectives — four of them: assets protected, records accurate, operations efficient, policies followed. A full answer can name all four.

Know the enemies the system faces. Errors: honest mistakes — a delivery miscounted, an amount posted into the wrong account. Fraud: deliberate dishonesty — cash slipped into a pocket, stock walking out the back door, an invoice invented for goods that never existed. Waste: value leaking without any crime at all — lights burning all night, stock expiring on a forgotten shelf. Internal control does not treat employees as criminals; it treats people as human, and builds a structure in which errors surface fast and dishonesty becomes both difficult and detectable.

One principle stands above all the others, so meet it now: DIVISION OF DUTIES — also called segregation of duties. No single person may control a transaction from its start to its finish. Whoever receives the cash must not be the one who records it; whoever orders the stock must not also receive it and pay for it. The reasoning is cold and simple: fraud attempted alone must now slip past a second, independent pair of eyes to survive — and collusion between two people is far rarer, and far more dangerous for the fraudster, than a quiet private temptation. Division of duties turns trust in individual people into confidence in a SYSTEM.

Keep that foundation in view. The five components we build next are the framework this topic names — the architecture inside which division of duties and every other check does its work.

Pause here, because the questions on this section reach you now. They test the definition, its four objectives, and the logic behind division of duties — answer in exactly the terms just laid down.

## Subtopic: The Five Components of Internal Control

Component one: the CONTROL ENVIRONMENT. This is the tone at the top — management's own visible attitude to honesty, discipline and rules. If the owner helps herself to till cash for the weekend, no laminated procedure on the wall will persuade the staff that rules are real. The control environment is the soil: every other control either grows in it or dies in it.

Component two: RISK ASSESSMENT. The business deliberately asks itself: what can go wrong here? Where does cash change hands? Where could stock disappear? Which record could be quietly adjusted? A risk that has been named can be controlled; the question never asked is the one that ends up costing money. Risk assessment is a repeated habit, not a once-off — a new till point, a new driver, a new supplier each bring risks of their own.

Component three: CONTROL ACTIVITIES. The concrete rules and checks that answer the identified risks — the visible, working machinery of control. They are important enough to get their own subtopic, next.

Component four: INFORMATION SYSTEMS AND COMMUNICATION. Controls only function when accurate information flows to the people who need it, in time for them to act. The recording system itself — source documents, journals, ledgers — is an information system; so is the staff meeting where a procedure is explained, and the notice telling a cashier exactly what to do when the till will not balance. A rule nobody was told about controls nothing at all.

Component five: MONITORING. Somebody verifies that the controls exist in practice and not only on paper: surprise cash counts, spot checks, a manager paging through the day's documents, the annual stocktake set against the stock records. Left unwatched, controls quietly decay — monitoring is the maintenance schedule of the entire system.

Learn the five as one flowing sentence: leadership sets the tone, the business asks what can go wrong, rules and checks answer the risks, information keeps everyone informed, and monitoring proves the whole thing still works.

## Subtopic: Control Activities — the Working Toolkit

Control activities are where scenario questions live, so assemble the toolkit piece by piece, each with its purpose attached.

Division of duties — already established: receiving, recording and authorising split between different people, so that no transaction ever has a single master.

Authorisation: defined transactions require defined approval — a credit sale above a set limit needs the owner's signature; a payment happens only against an approved invoice. Purpose: any decision with consequences passes a responsible eye before money moves.

Documentation: every transaction generates a numbered source document — receipt, invoice, till slip. The purpose is double: the document is evidence for the records, and the numbering is itself a control, because a missing number in the sequence is a question that demands an answer.

Physical safeguards: assets locked away and access limited. Cash kept in a safe and banked promptly and intact; the storeroom locked, with named keyholders; fixed assets marked as business property and listed in an asset register. Purpose: opportunity removed is temptation removed.

Independent checks and reconciliation: one record tested against another record created independently of it — the bank statement against the cash journals, the physical stock count against the stock records, the debtors list against the control account. Purpose: two independent records that are supposed to agree will expose an error or a theft at the exact moment they stop agreeing.

Now aim the toolkit at the three assets Grade 10 scenarios favour. Cash: two people count the till together at close; takings banked daily; the person who banks is not the person who records; petty cash paid out only against vouchers. Stock: deliveries counted against the delivery note by somebody other than the person who ordered; storeroom locked; regular counts compared with the records. Fixed assets: an asset register listing every item with its cost and location; each item marked; the register physically checked at intervals.

Pause for this section's questions — each presents a control activity, or a gap where one belongs. Name the control and state its purpose in one sentence, exactly as the toolkit was built.

## Subtopic: Reading a Scenario Like an Examiner

You will almost never be asked to recite the five components. Instead you get a scenario — a business doing something carelessly — and three demands: identify the weakness, explain the risk, recommend the control. Build that three-move answer here.

Scenario: at Zwane Building Supplies, one clerk orders the cement, signs for the deliveries when they arrive, and pays the suppliers at month-end. Move one — the weakness, named in control language: no division of duties; a single person controls the entire purchasing cycle. Move two — the risk, made concrete: the clerk could order goods for his own building project, or approve payment for loads that never arrived, and no second person would ever notice. Move three — the recommendation, practical and proportionate: split the functions — one person orders, a second receives and counts deliveries against the order, and the owner authorises payment only against matched documents.

Mark the standard the recommendation must reach: specific, actionable, and aimed at the exact weakness. "The owner should supervise more closely" earns nothing; "deliveries must be counted against the order by somebody other than the person who placed it" earns everything.

Second scenario, at pace: a café keeps its takings in a tin under the counter and banks "whenever the tin gets full" — sometimes ten days later. Weakness: cash not banked promptly, and no physical safeguard. Risk: theft or loss of an ever-growing pile, and no way to check the records against regular deposits. Control: bank daily, intact, and reconcile each deposit against the cash journal.

When a scenario leaves you unsure, let the component framework organise you: ask which of the five is missing — tone, risk-awareness, activities, information, or monitoring — and the weakness names itself. The final questions of this part are with you now: three moves each — weakness, risk, recommendation — in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the identical system, rebuilt from a place you have all stood in a queue: the school tuck shop. No definitions up front — just watch things go wrong, and let the rules invent themselves.

## Subtopic: The Tuck-Shop Disaster

Your class takes over the tuck shop for a term to raise funds. Stock is bought, first break arrives — chaos. One learner, Bongani, does everything: takes the money, passes out the chips, keeps the float in his blazer pocket, and promises to write everything down after school.

By Friday the float is short. Did Bongani steal? Probably not — but sit with the uncomfortable truth: NOBODY CAN KNOW. Not even Bongani. Business money shared a pocket with his own money; the sales lived only in his memory; nothing was counted at the start of break or the end. If he took nothing, he cannot prove it. If he took plenty, nobody can prove that either. The system — one person, no records, no counting — has made honesty and theft perfectly identical.

Replay the same week with three small changes. Zanele takes the money and gives change from a separate cash box. Bongani passes out the stock. Karabo ticks every sale on a sheet, and at closing Zanele and Karabo count the box TOGETHER and write the total down. Same learners, same chips — but now the cash box either matches the tick sheet or it does not, and either way everyone knows by the end of break on Friday, not at month-end. And look at what the change did for Bongani: the system now PROTECTS him. When the box balances, his honesty is provable.

That is internal control in a single story: not suspicion — protection. Rules that make the honest path visible and the dishonest path difficult. The entire formal framework is this tuck shop, scaled up.

Quick check before we push on — tuck-shop questions are on their way to you right now. For each one ask: could anyone PROVE what happened here, or only hope?

## Subtopic: Five Questions Every Business Must Answer

The textbook wraps the system in five formal names. Set the names aside for a minute — they are answers to five plain questions that every tuck shop, spaza and supermarket must face.

Question one: WHO SETS THE EXAMPLE? If the teacher in charge borrows from the cash box "just till Monday", every learner instantly learns that the box is borrowable. The example at the top decides whether any rule is real. That is the control environment.

Question two: WHAT COULD GO WRONG HERE? Sit next to the cash box and think like trouble: money and chips pass many hands in a rush; wrong change is easy; stock can vanish between the crate and the counter. Listing the dangers before they arrive — that is risk assessment.

Question three: WHAT RULES STOP IT? Different learners on money and stock; every sale ticked as it happens; the box counted by two people together; leftover stock counted daily. The practical rules — control activities.

Question four: DOES EVERYONE KNOW WHAT'S HAPPENING? The tick sheet, the counting page, Friday's team briefing Monday's team on what was decided. Information flowing to the people who need it — information and communication.

Question five: WHO CHECKS THAT THE RULES STILL HAPPEN? The treasurer springs a surprise count some breaks; the teacher compares a week of tick sheets against the money actually banked. Checking the checkers — monitoring.

Say the five questions out loud once — example, dangers, rules, information, checking — and the framework is yours. The formal names are simply these five questions wearing their Sunday clothes.

## Subtopic: Spot the Weakness — Your Turn on Patrol

The last skill is the one that earns the marks: walking into any scenario and SEEING the gap. Train the eye on three quick patrols.

Patrol one: a car-wash owner lets his brother run the yard alone on Saturdays; the brother takes every payment, buys soap and polish from the money bag, and pays himself his Saturday wage out of the same bag before going home. Recognise the tuck-shop pattern? One person, every role, wages self-served from uncounted cash. The fix costs nearly nothing: a page listing each wash as it happens, a counted float at opening and closing, and wages paid by the owner from money that has been counted.

Patrol two: a shop props the storeroom door open all morning on delivery days because "everyone is busy". What could go wrong needs no imagination at all. The rule that answers it: the storeroom stays locked, and ONE named person receives each delivery and counts it against the delivery note before a single item reaches a shelf.

Patrol three: a business writes out receipts on loose slips of paper, and only "when the customer asks for one". With no numbers, a missing receipt is invisible; with no receipt for every sale, recorded income is simply whatever somebody chose to write down. The answer: a numbered duplicate receipt book, a receipt for every sale — and then the number sequence itself becomes the watchdog, because a gap in the numbers asks its own question out loud.

Feel the reflex you just used: on every patrol you found the gap by running the five questions in order and stopping where the answer came back "nobody" or "no rule". That reflex IS this topic — and it will keep answering internal-control questions for you all the way to matric.

And the final questions of the lesson arrive right now: three scenarios, three patrols. Find the gap, name the rule that closes it, and — as the tuck shop taught you — remember that the rule exists to protect the honest ones. That is a system worth building.
