# Part 1 — Expert

Numbers are only half of accounting; the other half is the trust that makes anyone believe the numbers. This lesson works through the code of ethics that binds every party in the financial environment, and the seven characteristics this topic names — leadership, discipline, transparency, accountability, fairness, sustainability and responsible management. Scenario questions test these exactly the way GAAP is tested: a short story, and the demand that you name the characteristic at stake and argue why. By the end, that is a skill you own.

## Subtopic: Why Ethics Is Accounting's Foundation

Start from the awkward truth the whole topic exists to answer: money gathers temptation around itself. A cashier touches cash all day; a manager signs the payments; an owner chooses what the books will admit. Every financial role is also an opportunity, and a business where opportunity never meets an ethical boundary will leak value sooner or later — stolen cash, massaged figures, standards quietly dropped when nobody objects.

So define the tool that draws the boundary. A code of ethics is a formal, written statement of the values and standards of behaviour that a business commits itself to — the business answering, in advance and in public, the question "how will we act when no one is checking?" It binds ALL parties in the financial environment: the owner, the managers, the accountants and bookkeepers, every employee, and the business in its dealings with customers, suppliers and the state.

Why does Accounting, of all subjects, insist on this? Because financial information is worthless unless it can be TRUSTED. A bank studying the statements, an owner reading the profit line, the revenue service reading a return — every one of them relies on the figures having been honestly made. Once records can be bent to suit whoever writes them, they stop being information and become wallpaper. Ethics is therefore not decoration stuck onto the discipline; it is the ground the discipline stands on. Every rule you have already met — the entity rule, prudence, the checks of internal control — quietly assumes people willing to obey rules even when obeying costs them something.

Carry one link across from the previous lesson: internal control makes dishonesty DIFFICULT; ethics makes it UNWANTED. Neither survives alone — the sharpest control system falls to people determined to beat it, and the warmest culture drifts without any checks. Two hands, one grip.

Pause on that foundation, because questions on it reach you now. They probe the definition of a code of ethics, whom it binds, and why trust is the discipline's precondition — answer in exactly those terms.

## Subtopic: The Seven Characteristics, Defined for Marks

Seven characteristics carry this topic. Learn each one as a definition PLUS a one-line business example — that pairing is precisely what the marks are given for.

Leadership: the people in charge set the ethical example and take responsibility for the direction of the organisation. An owner who obeys every rule she sets for staff — banking each day's takings in full, never dipping into the till — is leading; the tone at the top you met in internal control is leadership under its ethics name.

Discipline: sticking to rules, procedures and standards consistently, including under pressure. The bookkeeper who writes up the journals every day, completes the documents the same way every time, and refuses shortcuts in stocktake week is showing discipline.

Transparency: openness — decisions, prices and records visible to everyone entitled to see them. Full disclosure in the statements, prices marked honestly on the shelf, no charges hidden in small print.

Accountability: answering for your own decisions and actions. The manager who reports a loss to the owner instead of burying it, the club treasurer who accounts for every rand at the meeting — accountability means reporting back, and accepting what follows.

Fairness: treating every party justly, without favouritism — staff paid what was promised, customers charged what was advertised, suppliers paid on the agreed date, no advantage squeezed from the weaker side of a deal.

Sustainability: running the business so that it can last — using resources responsibly, respecting the community and the environment, and refusing any profit today that eats the foundation of profit tomorrow.

Responsible management: careful, competent stewardship of the business's resources — planning ahead, cutting waste, insuring the assets, and deciding with the long-term interest of the business in mind.

Notice the overlaps on purpose: leadership breeds discipline, and transparency is what makes accountability possible. Many scenarios genuinely fit more than one characteristic — the examinable skill is picking the BEST fit and defending the choice.

Hold it there for this section's questions — each presents a characteristic or its example. Match name to definition exactly as they were paired above.

## Subtopic: Ethics Under Pressure — the Scenarios That Matter

Definitions collect some marks; applied judgement collects the rest. Work three scenarios at full depth.

Scenario one: the owner of Mokoena Hardware tells his bookkeeper to keep the Saturday cash sales out of the records "so the tax comes down". Name every party and every characteristic breached. The owner breaks leadership — dishonesty ordered from the very top — and fairness, because every rand of tax he dodges lands on taxpayers who declare honestly. A bookkeeper who obeys breaks discipline and accountability, and burns the one professional asset a bookkeeper owns: records people can rely on. A complete answer finishes with the consequence: books false in one corner are suspect in every corner, and discovery brings penalties, criminal charges and the collapse of the business's standing with its bank.

Scenario two: a cellphone-repair shop fits second-hand parts into customers' phones while advertising and charging for new parts. Transparency fails — the customer cannot see what was actually done; fairness fails — the buyer's ignorance is being farmed for profit; and responsible management fails as well, because a short-term margin is being bought with the shop's least replaceable asset, the trust of its customers.

Scenario three: a car wash pipes its chemical runoff into the storm drain that feeds the community stream, because proper disposal costs money. That is sustainability, head-on — profit today charged against the environment and community the business will need tomorrow — with responsible management and leadership implicated in whoever signed off the pipe.

Now lift out the pattern: nearly every ethical failure is a trade — a small, certain, immediate gain bought with a large, uncertain, delayed loss. And trust, once spent, does not restock.

Stop for this section's questions: each is a scenario in miniature. Name the best-fit characteristic, and be ready to defend it against the near-misses.

## Subtopic: The Exam Method for Ethics Questions

Ethics questions reward structure as much as they reward insight, so build the four-move method and use it every time.

Move one: state the ethical issue in plain words — what is being done, and to whom? "Saturday cash sales are being deliberately left out of the records to evade tax."

Move two: name the characteristic — or characteristics — at stake, and say it as a claim: "This breaches transparency and fairness."

Move three: argue the link in one or two sentences, welding definition to scenario: "Transparency demands records that show what actually happened; omitting sales makes the records deliberately incomplete. Fairness fails because the unpaid tax becomes someone else's burden."

Move four: consequence and correction — what happens if this continues, and what must happen instead: "The business faces penalties and prosecution, and its records lose all credibility. Every sale must be recorded, and the code of ethics must be enforced from the owner downward."

Then discipline yourself against the two classic mark-losers. First, moralising without naming: "this is dishonest and wrong" earns nothing on its own — the characteristic must be NAMED and its definition used in the argument. Second, naming without applying: reciting "transparency, accountability, fairness" like a shopping list earns almost nothing — each name must be tied to a specific fact in the scenario.

The last questions of this part are with you now. Run all four moves on each one — issue, name, argument, consequence — in full sentences, every time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Time to lift ethics off the page and put it back where you already live with it — the sports field, the till, the street where you shop. None of this is new to you; we are simply organising what you already know.

## Subtopic: The Captain Picture

Picture the best team captain you have ever watched — netball, soccer, chess team, it makes no difference. Ask why the team follows that person, and all seven characteristics walk out of hiding.

The captain trains harder than anyone and never demands from the team what she will not do herself — leadership: the example set from the front. She is at practice on time, every time, whether she feels like it or not — discipline: the right thing done consistently, not occasionally. When the starting line-up is chosen, she explains the choices to everyone's face instead of letting rumours do it — transparency. After a heavy defeat she stands up first and owns her share of it, rather than hunting for someone to blame — accountability. The brand-new player gets exactly the same shot at a position as her oldest friend — fairness. She refuses to win by hurting opponents or bending rules, because there is a next season and the team's name has to survive into it — sustainability: never winning today in a way that poisons tomorrow. And she looks after the bibs, the balls, the fixture list — the whole quiet machinery that keeps the team running — responsible management.

One captain, seven characteristics, and you have known them your whole sporting life. A business is just a team with money at the centre — the same seven, with higher stakes.

Quick check before we move on — the questions arriving now each describe one thing a captain does. Name the characteristic it shows, straight from this picture.

## Subtopic: Ethics at the Till

Now three moments from ordinary shop-and-street business life. In each one, do not ask "is this wrong?" — your gut answers that instantly. Ask the exam question instead: WHICH characteristic is being tested?

Moment one: the till slip shows you were charged twice for the same loaf of bread. You go back; the manager pulls the till record, sees the double ring-up, apologises and refunds you on the spot. Which characteristics just passed? Accountability — the shop answered for its own mistake; fairness — you left with exactly what was yours; and transparency, because there was a till record that could be pulled at all. Notice internal control and ethics working as partners: the record is what made the honesty provable.

Moment two: an electrician quotes a customer two prices — one with an invoice, and a cheaper one "cash, no paperwork". What is being sold off? Transparency first, because the whole deal is designed to leave no trace; accountability next, because with no record nobody can ever be held to anything — including when the wiring fails; and unfairness flows downstream, because untaxed cash work quietly shifts the burden onto everyone who declares honestly.

Moment three: a tuck-shop owner is offered cool drinks at half price from "the back of a warehouse, no questions asked". Cheap stock, fatter margin — and now weigh the trade: one bargain today against the shop's entire name in its community tomorrow. Sustainability and responsible management both say NO, because the shop's real asset was never the stock in the fridge; it is the trust of the street it serves.

Your questions for this section are up now — three tills, three moments. Name the characteristic under test in each, the way we just did it together.

## Subtopic: Why Good Ethics Is Good Business

Finish with the argument you can carry into any long question: ethics is not a tax paid on top of profit — it is an engine of profit, running quietly underneath it.

Run the logic through a hair salon on your own high street. The salon that charges exactly the price on the board, every client, every visit, earns something no advertising budget can buy: clients who stop checking, and who come back — fairness converting into loyalty. The owner who keeps honest, complete records can show a bank precisely what the salon earns, and honest records unlock the loan for the second chair that a shoebox of slips never will — transparency converting into credit. Products paid for on the agreed date, month after month, buy the salon supplier credit in a slow January when late payers are put on cash-only terms — discipline converting into breathing room. And a salon that treats its staff, clients and neighbourhood well is still standing, still trusted, in ten years — sustainability converting into survival itself.

The reverse compounds too, like interest on a debt: one discovered dishonesty makes every past figure suspect. Nobody half-trusts a till or a set of books.

So when a question asks you to discuss the importance of a code of ethics, you hold a two-sided answer that beats any list: ethics protects the business from the costs of dishonesty, AND earns the business the profits of being trusted. Name the characteristics as you go and tie each one to its business advantage.

And the final questions of the lesson reach you right now: each shows a business behaving well or badly. Name the characteristic, then name the business consequence that follows from it — two names per answer, and the marks are yours.
