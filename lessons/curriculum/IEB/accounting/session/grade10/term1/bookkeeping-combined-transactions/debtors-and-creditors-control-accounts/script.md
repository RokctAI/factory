# Part 1 — Expert

Today we build one of the most powerful checking tools in bookkeeping: the Debtors Control account, together with its twin, the Creditors Control account. By the end of this session you will post real journal totals into a control account, balance it, and — most importantly — explain what that balance must agree with.

## Subtopic: What a Control Account Is and Why We Need One

Picture a business selling on credit to forty customers. Each customer — each debtor — has a personal account in a separate book called the Debtors Ledger, showing exactly what that one person owes. Forty debtors, forty little accounts.

Now the owner asks a simple question: how much do all my debtors owe me altogether? Adding forty accounts every time would be slow and error-prone. So the general ledger keeps one summary account, the Debtors Control account, recording the totals of everything that happened to debtors as a group. One account, one balance, one answer to the owner's question.

Here is the principle to hold for the whole lesson: whatever is posted to the individual debtors' accounts in detail must be posted to the control account in total. Detail in the Debtors Ledger, totals in the control account — two views of the same facts.

That gives us the built-in check that makes this topic the backbone of reconciliation work in every grade ahead: at month end, the balance of the Debtors Control account must equal the total of the list of individual debtors' balances. If summary and detail disagree, an error exists, and it must be hunted down. This is internal control in action — the books policing themselves.

Which side does what? Debtors are an asset: they owe us money. So the control account increases on the debit side when debtors owe us more, and decreases on the credit side when they owe us less. Keep one question running for every entry: did this event make debtors owe us more, or less?

Pause here — questions on the control account's purpose and its agreement rule are with you now. Answer with the detail-versus-totals principle exactly as stated.

## Subtopic: Posting the Debit Side — When Debtors Owe Us More

Work with real August figures for our business. The Debtors Journal — the book recording credit sales — totals R52 600 for the month. The Cash Receipts Journal's debtors column shows R36 400 received from debtors. The Debtors Allowances Journal shows R3 100 of goods returned by debtors, and one debtor's debt of R1 200 must be written off as a bad debt.

Start with the debit side. The account opened on 1 August with a balance of R18 700 — what debtors owed us from July, an asset balance, resting on the debit side as "Balance b/d".

Now the credit sales. When goods are sold on credit, the debtor owes us more, so Debtors Control is debited. We post the total of the Debtors Journal — the whole R52 600 — as one entry on the debit side, contra account Sales, with Cost of Sales handled separately under the perpetual system; the control account itself receives the selling-price total.

Pause on a classic pitfall: learners sometimes post individual invoices to the control account. Never. Individual invoices belong to the personal accounts in the Debtors Ledger. The control account receives only the journal total. That is the entire meaning of the word control.

So far the debit side holds R18 700 plus R52 600. Debtors, as a group, have owed us as much as R71 300 during August. Now for everything that reduced that debt.

Stop for this section's questions now — opening balances and the Debtors Journal total, sides and reasons. Totals only, never individual invoices.

## Subtopic: Posting the Credit Side — When Debtors Owe Us Less

Three things in our example made debtors owe us less, so all three post on the credit side.

First, payments received. The Cash Receipts Journal keeps a dedicated column for money received from debtors, and its August total is R36 400. When a debtor pays, their debt shrinks, so credit Debtors Control R36 400, contra account Bank.

Second, returns. Some debtors sent goods back or received an allowance for damaged items — recorded in the Debtors Allowances Journal, total R3 100. A return cancels part of the original credit sale, so the debtor owes us less: credit Debtors Control R3 100, contra account Debtors Allowances.

Third, the bad debt. One debtor simply cannot pay R1 200, and the business gives up hope of collecting. We remove the amount from debtors by crediting Debtors Control R1 200, and the contra account is Bad Debts, an expense. Hold the logic: writing off a debt does not mean money was received — it means the business absorbs the loss and stops pretending the asset exists. That is also the prudent path: assets must never be overstated.

Now balance the account, exactly as you balance any ledger account. Total the debit side: R18 700 plus R52 600 gives R71 300. Total the credit side: R36 400 plus R3 100 plus R1 200 gives R40 700. The difference — R71 300 minus R40 700 — is R30 600. Write R30 600 on the credit side as "Balance c/d" to level the sides, and bring it down on the debit side on 1 September as "Balance b/d".

The debit balance of R30 600 tells the owner: at the end of August, debtors as a group owe the business R30 600.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the credit-side entries and the balancing. For each, answer the standing question first: more owed to us, or less?

## Subtopic: The Agreement Check and the Creditors Mirror Image

We said the control account polices the books. Here is the check in practice. The bookkeeper takes the Debtors Ledger, lists every individual debtor's balance, and adds the list. That total must land on exactly R30 600 — the same figure as the control account balance.

What if the list totals R31 800 instead? Then somewhere, detail and summary took different roads: perhaps an invoice reached a personal account but missed the journal total, perhaps a receipt was posted twice, perhaps a journal column was miscast. The disagreement does not name the error — it announces that one exists and roughly where to hunt. This discipline is exactly what reconciliation questions in the senior grades examine, so build the habit now.

Finally, meet the mirror image: the Creditors Control account. Creditors are people we owe — a liability — so everything flips. The balance sits on the credit side. Credit purchases, from the Creditors Journal, make us owe more: credit the account. Payments to creditors and returns to suppliers make us owe less: debit the account. Its balance must agree with the list of balances from the Creditors Ledger. Truly understand the debtors version and the creditors version comes free — just reverse every side.

Verify the method one last time. For any transaction, ask: does it change what debtors owe us, or what we owe creditors? More owed to us — debit Debtors Control. Less owed to us — credit it. Post totals, never individual entries. Balance the account. Check it against the list. That is the whole machine.

The final questions of this part are with you now: run the whole machine — post, balance, and state the agreement check for both control accounts.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same account, from the shop floor — because a control account is really just the ready-made answer to a question every owner asks at month end: what does the whole street owe me?

## Subtopic: Counting the Street Twice

Remember the exercise book with a page per customer? Beautiful for one question — what does Mr Sithole owe? — and hopeless for the other: what does EVERYBODY owe me altogether? Paging through forty names every time the owner wonders is not a system; it is a chore.

So the books keep the street's answer ready-made: one summary account — Debtors Control — receiving the month's TOTALS. All the credit sales of the month, one number in. All the payments received from debtors, one number out. All the returns, one number. The pages hold the people; the control line holds the street.

And here is what makes it a CONTROL and not merely a convenience: the two records are built along different roads. The pages are written line by line, person by person, every day. The control line is fed by column totals, once a month. Same transactions, two independent journeys — so at month end, the sum of all the pages MUST land exactly on the control line's balance. When two separate counts of the same street agree, you can trust both. When they differ, one journey dropped a passenger — and you know to search before the month closes.

Counting the street twice is not double work. It is the street auditing itself.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two counts are coming to you right now: which record answers which question, and why do two roads beat one?

## Subtopic: The Month in One Line

Now run August down the control line, asking the only question that matters at every step: did the street end up owing us MORE, or LESS?

The street already owed us R18 700 when August opened — last month's unpaid pages, brought down on the debit side, because money owed TO us is an asset. Then the month's credit sales: R52 600 of goods went out on trust — the street owes us more. More means debit: R52 600 in.

Then the reducers, one by one. Customers paid in R36 400 — the street owes us less: credit. Goods worth R3 100 came back through returns and allowances — less again: credit. And one sad exit: R1 200 written off as a bad debt, because that customer is gone — the street owes us less, even though not a cent arrived: credit.

Close the month: the debit side carried R71 300; the reducers total R40 700. The line settles at R30 600, debit side — the street, all together, owes the store R30 600 as September begins. One line, one number, the owner's question answered before it is asked.

Feel how every entry obeyed the single question. No memorised sides — just: more owed, debit; less owed, credit. That question will not fail you in any assessment this account ever appears in.

Your questions for this part are up now: walk another month down the line, asking more-or-less at every entry.

## Subtopic: When the Two Counts Disagree

Last scene: month end, and the counts do NOT match. The pages add up to R31 800; the control line says R30 600. Twelve hundred rand apart. What now?

First: no panic, and no "fixing" by rubbing anything out. The disagreement is the system WORKING — it has just told you, a month early, that somewhere a passenger fell off one of the two journeys. Second: read the clue. Twelve hundred rand... look back through the month. The bad debt was R1 200. Check the pages: there it is — the write-off went into the control totals but was never entered on that customer's page. The page still claims R1 200 the business has already buried. Fix the page, recount: R30 600 equals R30 600. The street is honest again.

That is the whole reason both records exist. One record can be quietly wrong forever; two independent records that must agree catch each other monthly. Honest systems are built from records that check records.

And the creditors side? The same machine standing in the mirror. Pages for everyone the store OWES, one control line for that whole street, and the same monthly handshake between them. Only the direction flips: we owe THEM, so their line grows on the credit side, and the question becomes: do we owe more, or less? Master one street and you own them both.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: two counts, one disagreement, one clue to read — find the missing passenger, then flip the mirror for creditors. Month closed, both streets counted. Well run.
