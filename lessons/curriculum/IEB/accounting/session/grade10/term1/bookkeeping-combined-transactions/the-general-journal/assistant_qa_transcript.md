**Thandi:** Question time! First from the class: how do we actually decide whether something belongs in the General Journal or one of the other six... I mean, five?

**Tutor:** Run the front doors in order. Did money come in? Cash Receipts Journal. Did money go out? Cash Payments Journal. Did we sell stock on credit? Debtors Journal. Buy on credit? Creditors Journal. Did goods come back, either direction? The allowances journals. If every one of those doors stays shut — no cash moved, nothing sold, bought or returned — the event falls through to the General Journal. Test it on the write-off: no cash, no sale, no purchase, no return... GJ. On the drawings of stock: no cash left the bank, nothing was sold... GJ. The journal is not a choice; it is what remains when the specialised doors have all said no.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the interest on Pillay's late account go through the GJ? Interest sounds like money — shouldn't it be in a cash journal?

**Tutor:** It will be — on the day it is actually PAID. But at the moment the interest is charged, watch what really happened: no rand moved anywhere. What changed is the SIZE OF THE DEBT: Pillay now owes two thousand and sixty instead of two thousand, and the business has earned income it has not yet received. Growth of a debt with no cash flow fits no cash journal, so the GJ records it: debit Debtors Control, credit Interest Income. Later, when Pillay settles, THAT event is money arriving and goes through the Cash Receipts Journal like any other receipt. Two separate events, two separate books, in their own time.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the popular one: the owner took goods home for herself — why is that priced at cost and not at the shop price? She'd have paid full price as a customer!

**Tutor:** Because she was never a customer in that moment — she is the owner, reaching into her own stake. A sale needs two parties: a business selling and an outsider buying. When Lindi carries goods home, there is only one party wearing two hats, and no sale occurred — so no profit can exist. The shelf lost goods that cost the business R480, and that exact amount is what leaves through Drawings. Record it at selling price and you would be inventing profit on a transaction with nobody on the other side — the books would claim the business earned margin by handing goods to itself. Cost out, cost recorded: the drawings account measures what the business actually gave up.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: if a correcting entry fixes the books anyway, why does everyone make such a fuss about never erasing the original mistake?

**Tutor:** Because the two methods produce the same numbers and completely different TRUST. Erase the error and the books end up correct but unverifiable — any number could have been quietly changed, yesterday or last year, and no reader can tell. Correct in pen and the books end up correct AND self-proving: the error is visible, the fix is dated, the narration explains it, and anyone auditing can walk the whole path. That visible path is the audit trail, and it is the difference between records that are believed and records that are merely tidy. Remember it as a principle bigger than bookkeeping: systems earn trust not by never failing, but by showing exactly how their failures were found and repaired.
