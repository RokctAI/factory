# Part 1 — Expert

Four specialised journals now handle the business's routine traffic — cash in, cash out, credit sales, credit purchases. But every month produces events that fit none of them: a debtor who will never pay, interest on an overdue account, the owner carrying stock home, an error needing correction. For these, bookkeeping keeps its oldest and most flexible book: the General Journal. Today you master its format, its classic entries, and the discipline that makes it trustworthy — because a book that can record ANYTHING needs the tightest rules of all.

## Subtopic: The Journal for Everything Else

Place the General Journal — the GJ — precisely: it is a book of first entry for transactions that no specialised journal accommodates. Not a dumping ground — a specialist in exceptions, with a format that enforces care.

Each entry is a complete statement of double entry in a fixed shape. First line: the account to be DEBITED, amount in the debit column. Second line, indented: the account to be CREDITED, amount in the credit column. Beneath them, the NARRATION: one sentence stating the reason — "Debt written off as irrecoverable", "Stock taken by owner for personal use". The specialised journals never needed narrations because their columns explained everything; the GJ records the unusual, so every entry must explain itself. An entry without a narration is incomplete and forfeits its mark.

Why does the GJ demand the debit-credit pair written out in full? Because no column design does the thinking for you here. In the CRJ, every line debited Bank by construction. In the GJ, YOU name both sides of every entry — making it the purest exercise of the double-entry analysis you learned weeks ago: which two accounts, which direction each, equation intact.

One structural feature completes the format: because many GJ entries touch debtors or creditors, the journal carries ANALYSIS COLUMNS — Debtors Control debit and credit, Creditors Control debit and credit — accumulating those amounts for monthly posting to the control accounts, exactly as the column totals of the other journals do. Personal accounts, as always, are posted daily.

Pause here — questions on the GJ's role and format are with you now: the shape of an entry, the narration's job, and the control columns' purpose.

## Subtopic: The Classic Entries

Three entries dominate Grade 10 GJ work. Learn each as an analysis, never a recipe.

Bad debts. N. Mahlangu owes R1 100 and has vanished — final demands returned, phone dead. The business decides the debt is irrecoverable and writes it off. Analysis: the asset "money receivable from Mahlangu" no longer exists; pretending otherwise overstates assets — prudence forbids it. Debit BAD DEBTS, an expense: the business absorbs the loss. Credit DEBTORS CONTROL R1 100 — and Mahlangu's personal account — the asset is removed. Narration: "Debt written off as irrecoverable." Note what did NOT happen: no cash moved; an asset died and an expense was born.

Interest on overdue accounts — both directions. A debtor, T. Pillay, is sixty days late on R2 000; the business charges interest at the agreed rate — R60 for the period. Analysis: Pillay now owes MORE — debit Debtors Control and his personal account R60; the business has EARNED income — credit INTEREST INCOME R60. Mirror: the business itself pays Coastal Wholesalers late, and Coastal charges R150. Debit INTEREST EXPENSE — the cost of slowness; credit Creditors Control and Coastal's account R150. Same logic, opposite direction — and note that interest arrives through the GJ because no money moved yet: the DEBT grew, not the cash flow.

Drawings of stock. The owner takes goods costing R480 home for personal use. Analysis: the shelf lost an asset — credit TRADING STOCK R480, at COST, always at cost: the owner takes goods, not profit margin, and no sale occurred. Debit DRAWINGS R480 — equity withdrawn in goods rather than money. The familiar test applies: the business received nothing; this is the owner's pocket, in kind.

Stop for this section's questions now — each is one of the classics. Analyse first: which account, which side, what died, what was born; then the narration.

## Subtopic: Correcting Errors Without Erasing

Bookkeeping's iron rule: NOTHING is ever erased, torn out or painted over. A record that can be altered invisibly is a record nobody can trust — every correction must itself be a visible, dated, explained entry. The GJ is where corrections live.

The method: establish what WAS done, what SHOULD have been done, and write the entry that moves the books from the first to the second. Worked example: stationery of R320 was incorrectly debited to Trading Stock in the Creditors Journal — the analysis clerk misread the invoice. What was done: Trading Stock debited R320. What should have been: Stationery debited R320. The credit side — Creditors Control — was correct all along, so it stays untouched. The correcting entry: debit STATIONERY R320, credit TRADING STOCK R320. Narration: "Correction of error — stationery incorrectly debited to Trading Stock." One entry, and the books stand exactly as if the error never happened, while the trail shows honestly that it did — and was fixed.

The discipline to internalise: correct ONLY the leg that was wrong. The commonest failure here is redoing the whole original transaction — debiting Stationery and crediting Creditors again — which double-counts the liability. Isolate the error; fix the error; touch nothing else.

Why does this matter beyond neatness? Because the audit trail is the business's credibility. When the owner, an auditor or SARS asks "why does Trading Stock dip in March?", the GJ answers with a dated, narrated correction. Books that explain themselves get believed — and the alternative, invisible alteration, is exactly where fraud lives. The correction entry is honesty, in ink.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: diagnose what was done versus what should have been, correct only the broken leg, and write the narration that explains it.

## Subtopic: Control Columns and Posting the GJ

Now the month-end mechanics. Our GJ carries: bad debt R1 100 — Debtors Control credit; interest charged to Pillay R60 — Debtors Control debit; interest charged by Coastal R150 — Creditors Control credit; drawings of stock R480; and the stationery correction R320 — the last two touching no control account.

The analysis columns have been accumulating: Debtors Control debit column R60; Debtors Control credit column R1 100; Creditors Control credit column R150. Month end, those TOTALS post to the control accounts: Debtors Control is debited R60 and credited R1 100; Creditors Control is credited R150. The personal accounts — Mahlangu, Pillay, Coastal — were posted daily as each entry was made. The remaining accounts — Bad Debts, Interest Income, Interest Expense, Drawings, Trading Stock, Stationery — post individually from their GJ lines.

Verify the month's double entry across the whole journal, the standing habit: total debits — Bad Debts 1 100, Debtors Control 60, Interest Expense 150, Drawings 480, Stationery 320: R2 110. Total credits — Debtors Control 1 100, Interest Income 60, Creditors Control 150, Trading Stock 480 plus 320: R2 110. Balanced. The GJ, like every journal, proves itself before it posts.

And now see the system whole, because this completes the set: six journals cover everything — CRJ and CPJ for cash, DJ and CJ for credit trading, DAJ and CAJ for returns, and the GJ for the exceptions. Every transaction in any business finds exactly one front door. That completeness is what makes month-end posting mechanical and the trial balance meaningful.

The final questions of this part are with you now: accumulate the control columns, post everything to its destination, and prove the journal balances — the full month-end drill.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same book, from the shop floor — because the General Journal is really just the notebook for the stories the other diaries cannot tell.

## Subtopic: The Odd-Jobs Notebook

Lindi's store now runs on a full set of diaries, each with one job. Money in — one book. Money out — another. Sold on trust, bought on trust, things coming back — all covered. The system hums.

Then Tuesday happens. Mrs Mahlangu, who owes R1 100, has moved — no forwarding address, phone dead, letters returned. That R1 100 is not coming. Which diary takes this? Money in? No money came. Money out? None left. Sales, purchases, returns? None of those happened either. Every specialised diary shrugs.

So the store keeps one more notebook: the odd-jobs book — the General Journal — for exactly these moments. And because its stories are unusual, every entry must be told in full: THIS account goes up, THAT account goes down, and here is WHY — one honest sentence underneath, called the narration. The column diaries never needed the sentence; their columns were the explanation. The odd-jobs book explains, or it fails.

Here is the comfort in it: writing a GJ entry is nothing but the five-step transaction analysis from week one — name the two accounts, classify them, decide up or down, apply the sides, check the equation. The odd-jobs book is that skill, holding a pen.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the notebook's job are coming to you right now: for each event, say which diary takes it, and why the odd ones fall through to the GJ.

## Subtopic: Three Stories the Big Diaries Can't Tell

Story one: the write-off. Mrs Mahlangu's R1 100 is gone — accept it. Her page cannot keep claiming an asset the store will never touch; that would be the books lying kindly to themselves. So: her page goes down by R1 100, the street's summary line goes down by R1 100 — and the loss lands where losses live: an expense called Bad Debts. The narration says it plainly: "debt written off as irrecoverable." No cash moved anywhere — an asset was buried, and the funeral was recorded. That refusal to flatter yourself is the prudence principle from GAAP, working a real shift.

Story two: the owner shops at her own shelf. Lindi takes home goods that cost R480 — supper, not sales. The shelf lost R480 of stock; and who received it? Not the business — Lindi the person. You know this move from the money version: drawings. Only the currency changed — she drew goods instead of rands. And catch the detail: R480 at COST, never at the marked-up price, because nothing was SOLD — there is no profit in carrying your own groceries home.

Story three: the price of slowness. Pillay is two months late on his account, and the agreement says late accounts earn interest — R60. His page grows by R60; the store earns Interest Income. And the mirror stings: when LINDI pays Coastal late, Coastal's page grows by R150, and the store books Interest Expense — the cost of its own slowness. Two lessons in one entry: lateness has a price, and the books charge it in both directions without favouritism.

Your questions for this part are up now — three stories, retold with different names and numbers. Find what died, what was born, and which direction the lateness points.

## Subtopic: Fixing Mistakes in Pen

Last story, and it carries the deepest rule in the book. In March, someone wrote R320 of stationery into the stock records — till rolls filed as trading stock. Found in April. Now — what does an honest store do?

What it NEVER does: erase, paint over, tear out the page. Not because neatness matters, but because TRUST does. A book where numbers can quietly change is a book that proves nothing — not to the owner, not to the bank, not to anyone. The entire value of the records is that they cannot be silently rewritten.

So mistakes are fixed IN PEN, with a new entry that reverses exactly the broken piece: Stationery up R320, Trading Stock down R320, and the sentence underneath: "correction of error — stationery wrongly recorded as trading stock." The wrong entry stays visible; the correction stands beside it, dated and explained. Anyone reading the book sees the mistake, sees the fix, sees the honesty. That is called an audit trail, and it is worth more than perfection — because nobody believes perfect books; everybody believes books that show their own repairs.

And mind the classic slip: fix ONLY what broke. The supplier's side of that stationery purchase was recorded correctly — touch it again and you create a second error while fixing the first. Surgeon's rule: operate on the broken leg, leave the healthy one alone.

That is the General Journal: the book of goodbyes, home-shopping, lateness and honest repairs. Small book, big character.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each gives you a mistake or an odd event — choose the surgery, write the sentence, and leave the trail clean. Six journals down; the whole system is in your hands now.
