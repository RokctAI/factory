# Part 1 — Expert

The journals have squeezed a month of trading into disciplined columns; today that work travels home. We post the Cash Receipts Journal and the Cash Payments Journal to the general ledger, balance the accounts, and prove the entire month with a trial balance. This is the engine room of the accounting cycle — and the month we post is Lindi's General Store's own month, so every figure is one you have already met.

## Subtopic: The Ledger's Architecture

The general ledger is the book of ACCOUNTS: one account for every item the business tracks, each shaped like a T — debit side on the left, credit side on the right. The journals answered "what happened this month, in order?"; the ledger answers "what is the full story of Bank? Of Sales? Of Wages?" — one account, one item, every movement gathered in one place.

The ledger is arranged in two sections, and the trial balance will respect this order later. The BALANCE SHEET SECTION holds the accounts describing what the business owns, what it owes, and the owner's stake: Capital, Drawings, Land and Buildings, Equipment, Trading Stock, Debtors and Creditors when they arrive, and Bank. The NOMINAL SECTION holds the incomes and expenses — Sales, Cost of Sales, Rent Income, Wages, Rent Expense — accounts that exist to measure this year's performance and will close into the profit calculation at year end. Assets and equity endure; incomes and expenses reset every year. That is the two-section logic.

Every posting carries FOLIO references — the grammar of the paper trail. In the ledger account, the folio column names the journal page the amount travelled from: CRJ 1, CPJ 1. In the journal, the folio names the account that received the posting. Anyone can follow any amount from document to journal to ledger and back — and you must, whenever an error needs hunting.

One discipline before we post: totals post, not lines. The entire point of the column journals was to deliver monthly TOTALS — one Sales figure, one Bank figure — so the ledger receives a handful of postings instead of a hundred. Only sundry items, named individually in the journals, post individually.

Pause here — questions on the ledger's architecture are with you now: sections, folios, and what posts as a total versus by name.

## Subtopic: Posting the Cash Receipts Journal

Take Lindi's completed CRJ: Bank column R67 700; Sales R4 500; Cost of Sales R3 000; Sundries R63 200 — being Capital R60 000 and Rent Income R3 200. Post it, entry by entry, each with its reasoning spoken.

The Bank total: money came IN — the asset Bank grew. Debit Bank R67 700. The details line in the Bank account reads "Total receipts", folio CRJ 1.

The Sales total: sales are income, and income grows on the credit side. Credit Sales R4 500, folio CRJ 1. A month of selling, one posting — the column design paying off.

The Cost of Sales total is the double posting to memorise: R3 000 posts TWICE. Debit Cost of Sales — the expense measuring what the sold goods had cost; and credit Trading Stock — the shelf surrendered R3 000 of goods at cost. This pair is the perpetual system's signature in the ledger: the sales income above, the cost of earning it alongside, and the stock account staying honest throughout.

The sundries go home by name: Capital R60 000 — the owner's stake grew, and equity grows on the credit side: credit Capital. Rent Income R3 200 — income: credit Rent Income.

Now audit the month's double entry as a whole, because the journal promised it balances: debits posted — Bank R67 700 and Cost of Sales R3 000 — total R70 700. Credits posted — Sales 4 500, Capital 60 000, Rent Income 3 200, Trading Stock 3 000 — total R70 700. Equal. Every rand of debit found its credit; the CRJ posts in perfect balance. Checking this BEFORE moving on is the professional's habit — and mind the classic slip: Trading Stock's credit partners the Cost of Sales debit, never the Bank figure.

Stop for this section's questions now: destinations, sides, and the double posting of cost of sales — reason every answer from "what grew, what shrank".

## Subtopic: Posting the CPJ and Balancing the Accounts

The CPJ's totals: Bank R25 300; Trading Stock R9 500; Wages R3 600; Sundries — Rent Expense R4 200, Equipment R5 500, Drawings R2 500.

Bank total: money OUT — the asset shrank: credit Bank R25 300, "Total payments", folio CPJ 1. Trading Stock total: the shelf loaded at cost: debit Trading Stock R9 500. Wages: an expense, and expenses grow on the debit side: debit Wages R3 600. The sundries by name, all debits: Rent Expense R4 200; Equipment R5 500 — an asset acquired; Drawings R2 500 — equity withdrawn, debited because it shrinks the owner's stake. Balance check: credits 25 300; debits 9 500 plus 3 600 plus 4 200 plus 5 500 plus 2 500 — 25 300. Balanced.

Now BALANCE the accounts that carry forward. Bank: debit side 67 700, credit side 25 300. The difference, 42 400, is entered on the credit side as "Balance c/d" to level the sides, then brought down on the debit side as "Balance b/d" — R42 400, a debit balance: the business holds money. Trading Stock: debits 9 500, credits 3 000 — balance b/d R6 500 debit: goods on the shelf at cost. Equipment: R5 500 debit. Capital: R60 000 credit. Drawings: R2 500 debit.

The NOMINAL accounts — Sales, Cost of Sales, Wages, Rent Expense, Rent Income — are not balanced month by month; they simply accumulate, because their job is to measure the period. They will be closed into the final accounts at year end. For the trial balance, each shows its total to date.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: post, balance, and state each closing balance with its side — reasoning aloud, asset up debit, asset down credit.

## Subtopic: The Trial Balance — Proving the Month

Now the proof. List every account with its balance or total, debits in one column, credits in the other, balance sheet section first, nominal section after.

Debits: Bank 42 400; Trading Stock 6 500; Equipment 5 500; Drawings 2 500; then the nominal debits — Cost of Sales 3 000; Wages 3 600; Rent Expense 4 200. Total: 67 700. Credits: Capital 60 000; then Sales 4 500; Rent Income 3 200. Total: 67 700. The columns agree, and the month stands proven — every posted debit found its credit, every addition survived the check.

Understand exactly what this proves and what it does not — a distinction tested year after year. The trial balance proves ARITHMETIC equality: double entry was maintained and the casting is consistent. It does NOT prove the books are correct. Post the R4 200 rent to Repairs instead of Rent Expense — correct side, wrong account — and the trial balance balances anyway. Omit an entire transaction from the journals — nothing posted anywhere — and it balances still. The trial balance is a smoke detector, not a full inspection: silence is necessary, never sufficient.

When it does NOT balance, hunt with method, not panic. Take the difference between the columns. A difference equal to one of your amounts: a posting made once instead of twice. A difference divisible by two: an amount posted to the wrong SIDE — a side error doubles itself. A difference divisible by nine: transposed digits — 4 200 captured as 2 400 leaves a gap of 1 800, and 1 800 divides by nine. Three diagnostic questions before any re-adding, and most errors surrender quickly.

The final questions of this part are with you now: build the trial balance, test it, and diagnose the classic failures by their fingerprints.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same journey again, but this time with luggage in hand — because posting is really just a month of sorted paper finding its way home to a filing cabinet.

## Subtopic: The Filing Cabinet

Picture the back room at Lindi's store. The journals — her two diaries — are the SORTING ROOM: everything that happened this month, in order, receipts on one page, payments on another. Brilliant for "what happened on the 16th?" — hopeless for "how much money do I actually have?" To answer THAT from the diaries, you would re-read the whole month every single time.

So the back room gets a filing cabinet: one drawer per THING. A drawer for Bank. A drawer for Sales. Drawers for Wages, for Stock, for the shelving, for Lindi's own stake. Posting is nothing grander than the monthly trip from sorting room to cabinet: carry each column total to its drawer, lay it on the correct side, note where it came from.

The correct side — you know this dance from the double-entry lesson: every drawer has a left page and a right page, and every kind of drawer has its growing side. Money drawers and possession drawers grow on the left. Drawers about what the business owes, and the owner's-stake drawer, grow on the right. Earning drawers grow right; spending drawers grow left. Nothing new — the cabinet just makes the habit permanent.

And those little codes — CRJ 1, CPJ 1 — written beside every amount? Return tickets. Any number in any drawer can say exactly which diary page it travelled from, so a mistake can be chased backwards in seconds. A cabinet without return tickets can never be audited.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the cabinet are coming to you right now: which drawer, which side, and why.

## Subtopic: One Month Comes Home

Now carry Lindi's actual month to the cabinet, total by total, and watch the drawers fill.

From the money-IN diary: R67 700 arrived — into the Bank drawer, left side, one line. The R4 500 of sales — into the Sales drawer, right side, because Sales is an earning drawer. The cost-of-sales pair travels together, exactly as it did at the till: R3 000 onto the left of the Cost of Sales drawer — what the sold goods had cost — and R3 000 onto the right of the Stock drawer, because the shelf gave those goods up. The two named visitors go home by name: R60 000 to the right of Capital — Lindi's stake grew; R3 200 to the right of Rent Income.

From the money-OUT diary: R25 300 left — Bank drawer, right side. R9 500 of new stock — Stock drawer, left side; the shelf loaded up. R3 600 of wages — Wages drawer, left. And the visitors: rent R4 200 left of Rent Expense; the shelving R5 500 left of Equipment; Lindi's R2 500 left of Drawings.

Now ask the cabinet the owner's favourite question: how much money do we have? Open the Bank drawer: left page 67 700, right page 25 300. Difference: R42 400, resting on the left — the business HAS forty-two thousand four hundred rand. One drawer, one subtraction, instant answer — the exact question the sorting room could never answer without re-reading the month. Same for Stock: 9 500 in, 3 000 out — R6 500 of goods on the shelf at cost. The cabinet knows everything, drawer by drawer.

Your questions for this part are up now: carry the totals home yourself — drawer, side, and the balance the drawer then shows.

## Subtopic: The Weigh-In

Last move of the month: prove the cabinet. Here is the beautiful fact underneath everything since the double-entry lesson: every amount that ever travelled landed in TWO drawers — a left somewhere, a right somewhere, always equal. So if the month came home faithfully, all the left-side balances together must weigh exactly what all the right-side balances weigh.

That weigh-in is the trial balance. List every drawer and its balance: lefts — Bank 42 400, Stock 6 500, Equipment 5 500, Drawings 2 500, Cost of Sales 3 000, Wages 3 600, Rent Expense 4 200. Rights — Capital 60 000, Sales 4 500, Rent Income 3 200. Add each side: 67 700 and 67 700. The scales sit level, and Lindi's month is proven — not guessed, PROVEN.

Two honest warnings about the scales, so you never over-trust them. Level scales cannot see a filing error: slide the rent into the Repairs drawer — wrong drawer, correct side — and the scales stay level, wrongly. And they cannot see what never arrived: forget a transaction entirely, both halves missing, and level they remain. The scales test the WEIGHING, not the FILING. That is why the diaries keep their return tickets and the documents keep their numbers — different checks for different errors, layered on purpose.

But when the scales DON'T level, they whisper what went wrong: a gap equal to one of your amounts — something was dropped once; a gap that halves neatly — something sat on the wrong side; a gap divisible by nine — two digits swapped places somewhere. Read the gap before re-adding anything.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: weigh the cabinet, read the gaps, and say what level scales do and do not prove. You have just closed a full month — sorting room to cabinet to level scales. That is the cycle's engine room, and you drove it yourself.
