**Thandi:** Class questions coming in! The big one first: the owner works in the store every day, so why isn't the money she takes for herself just wages?

**Tutor:** Because of who she is to the business. An employee sells their labour to the business at an agreed price — the business receives work and pays for it: an expense. But a sole trader IS the owner of the whole enterprise; the business itself is her stake. When she takes money out, the business is not buying anything from her — she is withdrawing part of what already belongs to her. That is why it reduces capital directly, as drawings, and never appears in the profit calculation. Keep the roles straight: the same person can be paid a true wage in a company structure later in your studies, but a sole trader taking money out is always drawings.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: why does buying stock go to a Trading Stock column? An older study guide in our library keeps talking about a Purchases account.

**Tutor:** That guide describes the periodic system, and this course runs the perpetual system. Under perpetual, the Trading Stock account is a live record of what is on the shelf: buy stock and the asset is debited immediately at cost; sell stock and cost of sales moves it out immediately. There is no waiting room called Purchases — the shelf account absorbs every movement as it happens. The reward is a stock figure you can trust on any day and a gross profit visible per sale. So when the CPJ has a Trading Stock column, it is the perpetual system talking: buying loads the asset directly. Retire the Purchases account; in this system it simply does not exist.

**Thandi:** Here's one about the tin: why fix the float at R600 at all? Why not just top it up with whatever seems reasonable?

**Tutor:** Because the fixed number is what makes the tin impossible to fool. With a fixed float, one equation holds at every moment: cash plus vouchers equals R600. Anyone can test it in thirty seconds, any afternoon, and a shortfall announces itself the same day it happens. Refill by guesswork and that equation dissolves — nobody can say what SHOULD be in the tin, so nobody can prove what is missing. The restoration rule completes the trap: the refill equals the voucher total exactly, so the vouchers are audited by arithmetic every month. A fixed number, a slip for every rand, one named keeper — small money guarded as fiercely as big money.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: if the bank statement already lists every payment, why keep a CPJ at all?

**Tutor:** Because the two records answer to different masters, and control lives in the gap between them. The bank statement is the bank's story — what actually moved through the account, including things you did not initiate: charges, debit orders, errors. The CPJ is the business's story — what it authorised, to whom, for what, against which numbered document. Reconcile the two monthly and each one polices the other: a payment in the statement with no CPJ line is money that left without authority; a CPJ line that never reaches the statement is a payment that vanished en route. Drop the CPJ and you have surrendered your half of that conversation — the business would be marking its own homework with the answers missing.
