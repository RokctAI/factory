# Part 1 — Expert

Money flowing out needs even stricter discipline than money flowing in — spending is easier than earning, and a payment error costs you twice. Today we master the Cash Payments Journal — the CPJ — the book of first entry for every payment from the bank account, together with its small companion, the Petty Cash Journal and its imprest system. By the end you will record the standard payments of a sole trader, split expenses from drawings without blinking, and run a petty cash float that polices itself.

## Subtopic: The CPJ — Job, Documents and Columns

The CPJ's single job mirrors the CRJ's: it records ALL payments made from the business bank account, in date order, for one month. Receipts never cross into it. One direction of flow per book, as always.

The source documents are the proofs of payment: once the cheque counterfoil, today almost always the EFT proof of payment, numbered in strict sequence. The journal's document column carries that number; the NAME column carries the PAYEE — the person or business the money went to. Notice the contrast with the CRJ, which recorded what the money was for; the CPJ names who was paid, because a payment is traced payee first.

The money column is Bank — every payment shrinks the bank balance, so every line strikes Bank. Analysis columns then sort the traffic: TRADING STOCK, because a trading business restocks constantly; WAGES, because staff are paid every week; and SUNDRY ACCOUNTS for everything without its own lane — rent expense, equipment, the owner's drawings — each named in the details column. The same architecture as the CRJ: regulars get lanes, visitors get named.

One perpetual-system point before we touch the transactions, because it separates this course from older textbooks: when this business buys stock, the amount goes to the TRADING STOCK column — the asset — never to a "Purchases" account. Under the perpetual inventory system the stock account updates at every transaction: buying loads the shelf account directly at cost. There is no Purchases account in this system; if an older book handed you one, retire it today.

Pause here — questions on the CPJ's job, documents and column logic are with you now. Answer with the definitions, and drop each item into its lane.

## Subtopic: The Standard Payments — and the Drawings Trap

Run the month for Lindi's General Store, transaction by transaction, columns spoken aloud.

On the 3rd, stock is bought from Coastal Wholesalers for R9 500, paid by EFT 201. Name: Coastal Wholesalers. Bank: R9 500. Trading Stock column: R9 500. The shelf account loads at cost — the perpetual system working on the buying side.

On the 9th, wages of R1 800 are paid, EFT 202. Name: the employee, or simply Wages. Bank R1 800; Wages column R1 800. An expense — money consumed keeping the store running — and it repeats on the 29th: another R1 800 into the same column.

On the 16th, rent of R4 200 goes to the landlord, EFT 203. No dedicated column — Sundries: R4 200, detail: Rent expense.

On the 21st, new display shelving is bought for R5 500, EFT 204. Think before writing: is this an expense? No — the shelving will serve the store for years: an ASSET. Sundries R5 500, detail: Equipment. The distinction bites at year end: expenses reduce profit; assets do not — they stand on the books as possessions. Mixing them misstates the profit and the asset position in one stroke.

On the 26th, the owner transfers R2 500 to herself for personal use, EFT 205. Here is the trap of the whole lesson. This is NOT an expense — the business received nothing for the money: no service, no stock, no benefit. It is DRAWINGS: the owner withdrawing part of her own equity. Sundries R2 500, detail: Drawings. At year end, drawings never enter the profit calculation — they reduce the owner's capital directly. A business that hides drawings inside expenses understates its real profit and deceives itself about performance. The test for every payment: did the BUSINESS receive value? Stock or assets — yes, possessions. Wages, rent — yes, services consumed: expenses. The owner's personal use — no: drawings.

Stop for this section's questions now: classify each payment — asset, expense, or drawings — before naming its column, in exactly that order.

## Subtopic: Totals, the Cross-Cast, and What Posts Where

Month end: rule off, total, prove. Bank column: 9 500 plus 1 800 plus 4 200 plus 5 500 plus 2 500 plus 1 800 — R25 300. Trading Stock: R9 500. Wages: R3 600. Sundries: R12 200.

Cross-cast: the analysis columns must explain the Bank column. Trading Stock 9 500, plus Wages 3 600, plus Sundries 12 200 — R25 300. Equal. The journal proves itself, and only a proven journal may post.

The posting picture, previewing the ledger lesson ahead: Bank's total credits the Bank account — payments shrink the asset. The Trading Stock total debits Trading Stock — the shelf grew. The Wages total debits the Wages expense account. Each sundry item posts individually by name: Rent expense debited, Equipment debited, Drawings debited. One month, six postings, every one anchored to a numbered proof of payment.

And the internal-control thread that runs through every payments scenario: payments made only against approved documents; EFT references numbered and sequential; the person who approves a payment never the person who captures it; and the bank statement reconciled monthly against the CPJ and CRJ — the bank's independent record testing ours. The CPJ is where a business's money leaks when control is loose; the format's discipline is the first line of defence.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: total, cross-cast, and state each posting's destination and side.

## Subtopic: The Petty Cash Journal and the Imprest System

No business EFTs seventy-five rand for stamps — small payments need small cash, and small cash needs its own tight system: the petty cash float, recorded in the Petty Cash Journal.

The design is the IMPREST system, and its logic is beautiful. The float is fixed — say R600. Every payment out of the tin is swapped for a numbered PETTY CASH VOUCHER stating what, for whom, how much, signed by the person receiving the money. At any moment, cash in the tin PLUS vouchers must equal exactly R600 — the tin always adds to its imprest amount, in coin or in paper. At month end the vouchers are totalled — say stamps R75, taxi fare R90, tea and coffee R140, cleaning materials R115: R420 — and the float is RESTORED with a payment of exactly R420 from the bank, recorded in the CPJ, standing the tin back at R600 for the new month.

Watch the control built into the design. The restoration amount always equals the vouchers, so the vouchers face an arithmetic audit every single month. A tin holding R180 cash and R420 of vouchers balances; a tin with R180 cash and R390 of vouchers is R30 short and announces it instantly. The petty cashier — one named person, another division of duties — accounts by voucher for every rand.

The PCJ itself copies its big siblings: date, voucher number, details, a total column, and analysis columns for the regular small items — postage, transport, refreshments — with sundries for the rest. Totals post monthly: each expense column to its own expense account.

The final questions of this part are with you now: run the tin — vouchers, the balance test, and the restoration amount — strictly by the imprest logic.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Money-out time — and exactly like the money-in diary, we let the system assemble itself out of one busy month and a few honest mistakes nearly made.

## Subtopic: The Money-Out Diary

Lindi's other diary. Same idea, opposite direction: every time money LEAVES the bank account, one line — the date, the payment proof number, WHO was paid, how much. Who, not what, leads when money goes out — because when you chase a payment later, you chase the person it went to.

The columns grow out of traffic, just like before. What does Lindi pay for constantly? Stock — the shelves must stay full: stock earns a lane. Wages — every single week: wages earns a lane. Everything else — the rent, the new shelving, the day she moved money to herself — visitors: one open column, each named on arrival.

And one habit walks straight over from the money-IN diary: the handshake. Every rand that left the bank must be explained by a lane or a named visitor. Add the lanes and the visitors; the sum must equal the Bank column. If the diary says R25 300 left the bank, the explanations must total R25 300 — to the rand.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the money-out diary are coming to you right now: lane or named visitor, for each payment.

## Subtopic: The Day Lindi Paid Herself

The month's most important line hides inside an ordinary moment. On the 26th, Lindi transfers R2 500 to herself — groceries, airtime, life. And as she writes the diary line she nearly writes it in the same breath as the rent: "just another cost of the month."

Stop her right there. Ask the question that sorts every payment: what did the STORE get for this money? For the R9 500 to Coastal Wholesalers — the store got stock; the shelves are fuller. For wages — the store got a week of work. For rent — a month under a roof. For the shelving — a fitting it will use for years. And for the R2 500 to Lindi? The store got... nothing. No stock, no work, no roof. The OWNER got something — perfectly allowed; it is her business — but it is not a cost of running the store. It is the owner dipping into her own stake: drawings.

Why does the difference carry so much weight? Because of the question the books must answer honestly at year end: did the STORE win this year? If Lindi's personal R2 500 hides among the expenses, the store looks R2 500 weaker than it truly performed — and a bank reading those books would judge a good store unfairly. Keep the owner's pocket and the store's costs apart, and both stories stay true. The same rule cuts the other way for the shelving: it is not a "cost of the month" either — it is a possession the store keeps. Costs are what the month CONSUMED: the work, the roof, the electricity. The test, every time: what did the store get, and how long does it last?

Your questions for this part are up now: for each payment, ask the sorting question aloud — what did the store get? — then name the class.

## Subtopic: The Tin That Always Tells the Truth

Last piece: the small money. Stamps, a taxi to the wholesaler, tea for the back room — nobody EFTs seventy-five rand. So Lindi keeps a tin, and the tin lives by one beautiful rule: it always starts the month worth R600 — some in cash, the rest in paper.

Here is how the paper works. Anyone who takes money from the tin leaves behind a signed slip — a voucher: what for, how much, signed. So at ANY moment, cash plus slips equals R600. Count the tin any afternoon: R260 in notes and coins, R340 in slips — perfect. R260 in cash but only R310 in slips? The tin itself just told you R30 is missing — today, not at year end. The tin audits itself every time the lid opens.

Month end: the slips come out — stamps R75, taxi R90, tea and coffee R140, cleaning materials R115. Add them: R420. And the refill is never a guess and never "whatever makes it look right" — the refill IS the voucher total: R420 from the bank, through the money-out diary, and the tin stands at R600 again for the new month. Slips explain the spending; the refill matches the slips; the tin returns to its fixed number. Month after month, pressed back to the same amount — which is exactly why it is called the imprest system.

One person owns the tin — one name, one set of keys — so every question has an address. Small money watched as closely as big money; that is the habit that keeps stores alive.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: count the tin, test cash-plus-slips, and set the refill. If your tin tells the truth, so do your books — strong work today.
