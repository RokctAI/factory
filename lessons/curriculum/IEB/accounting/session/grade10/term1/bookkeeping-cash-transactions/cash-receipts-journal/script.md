# Part 1 — Expert

Real bookkeeping starts now. The first book of first entry every sole trader opens is the Cash Receipts Journal — the CRJ — the daily record of every rand that flows into the business. By the end of this session you will rule the journal properly, record cash sales under the perpetual inventory system, handle capital and other receipts, and total the journal so that it proves its own accuracy. The format earns its marks; the discipline behind it carries the whole term.

## Subtopic: The Job of the CRJ and Its Source Documents

One sentence holds the journal's entire job: the CRJ records ALL money received by the business, in date order, for one month. Money flowing out never touches it — payments live in the Cash Payments Journal. One direction of flow per book; that separation is the first piece of order bookkeeping brings to a crowded month.

No entry without evidence — the rule that never bends. The CRJ's source documents: the cash register roll, totalled at close of business, proves each day's cash sales; a duplicate receipt is written for money received away from the till — the owner depositing capital, a tenant paying rent; and the duplicate deposit slip proves what actually reached the bank. Every document's number lands in the journal's document column, so any line can be traced straight back to its proof.

Now the column that trips learners in their first week — settled here instead: the ANALYSIS OF RECEIPTS column. A business does not run to the bank with every rand the moment it arrives; the day's takings are collected and banked together — daily, as sound internal control insists. The analysis column captures each amount AS IT IS RECEIVED through the day; when the collection is deposited, the TOTAL of that day's analysis amounts moves into the Bank column as one figure. Analysis answers "what came in, item by item?"; Bank answers "what was deposited, and when?" On a quiet day with one receipt banked at once, both amounts share a line; on a busy Saturday, five analysis amounts collapse into one Bank figure.

Pause here — questions on the journal's purpose, its documents and the analysis column are with you now. Answer with the definitions exactly as stated.

## Subtopic: Recording Cash Sales — Two Truths per Sale

Our working business for the month: Lindi's General Store, owned by L. Cele, selling goods at a mark-up of 50 percent on cost. On the 4th, the register roll shows cash sales of R1 800.

Remember the perpetual inventory system from the previous lesson: every sale records two truths — what the customer paid, and what the goods had cost. The CRJ is built to hold both. Enter the date and the document number — CRR 1. Details: Sales. The R1 800 goes into the analysis column, and — banked the same day — into the Bank column, and into the SALES column.

Now the second truth. At 50 percent on cost, goods that sell for R1 800 cost R1 200 — divide the selling price by 1,5 to strip the mark-up away. That R1 200 is written in the COST OF SALES column on the same line. It is not money moving today — nothing left the till — it is stock leaving the shelf, recorded at cost, so the Trading Stock account keeps telling the truth. One CRJ line therefore feeds four accounts at month end: Bank, Sales, Cost of Sales, and Trading Stock.

Check the mark-up arithmetic in both directions — the habit that catches slips: R1 200 cost times 1,5 returns R1 800 selling price — consistent. Gross profit for the day: R600, visible immediately, exactly what the perpetual system promises.

Two pitfalls to bury today. First: taking 50 percent OFF the selling price — R900 — wrong, because the mark-up was ON COST; always divide by one plus the mark-up rate. Second: leaving the Cost of Sales column blank "because no cash moved" — the column exists precisely to carry the non-cash truth of every sale.

Stop for this section's questions now — register-roll amounts and mark-ups. Strip the mark-up by division, and drop every figure into its correct column.

## Subtopic: Receipts That Are Not Sales

Money also arrives for reasons that have nothing to do with selling stock, and the CRJ receives all of it — through the SUNDRY ACCOUNTS column.

On the 6th, the owner deposits R60 000 as additional capital. Receipt 71 is written. Details: Capital. The amount passes through the analysis column, into the Bank column — and then, since no dedicated column exists for capital, into the Sundry accounts amount column, with "Capital" written in the sundry details column. The sundry column is the journal's guest room: any receipt without a column of its own stays there, NAMED, so month-end posting knows precisely which account it belongs to.

On the 14th, the tenant renting the storeroom pays R3 200. Receipt 72, details: Rent Income — through analysis, Bank, and Sundries: Rent income. Same machinery.

The discipline question: why do Sales and Cost of Sales deserve dedicated columns while Capital and Rent income do not? Frequency. Cash sales happen every trading day — a dedicated column lets an entire month of selling be posted to the ledger as ONE total. Capital appears rarely; each sundry item is posted on its own. Columns are efficiency decisions: heavy traffic earns its own lane; occasional visitors are handled by name. Meet a business one day with daily commission income and you will simply widen the journal with a Commission column — the format serves the business, never the reverse.

Note what does NOT appear in a Grade 10 CRJ this term: money received from debtors — that column joins the journal when credit transactions arrive in the weeks ahead. Build the format cleanly now and the extension bolts straight on.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: for each receipt, name the document, the columns struck, and the sundry detail where one is needed.

## Subtopic: Totalling, Cross-Casting and Month-End Discipline

A journal is only finished once it is totalled and PROVEN. At month end, rule off and total every money column. For Lindi's General Store — our transactions plus a closing cash sale of R2 700, cost R1 800, on the 27th: Bank column — R1 800 plus R60 000 plus R3 200 plus R2 700: R67 700. Sales column: R4 500. Cost of Sales: R3 000. Sundries: R63 200.

Now the proof — the CROSS-CAST. The Bank column total must equal the sum of the totals of the columns feeding it: Sales R4 500 plus Sundries R63 200 gives R67 700 — matching the Bank total exactly. The journal balances internally. Watch carefully: Cost of Sales stands OUTSIDE the cross-cast — it is not money received; it is the parallel stock record. Cross-cast before posting anything, every single month: an unproven journal smuggles its error into the ledger, where it costs hours to find later.

The totals then travel to the general ledger — the full mechanics belong to the posting lesson ahead, but glimpse the destination now: Bank total debited to Bank; Sales total credited to Sales; Cost of Sales total debited to Cost of Sales and credited to Trading Stock; every sundry item posted by name. A whole month, a handful of postings — the column design earning its keep.

Close on the internal-control thread stitched through this journal: takings banked daily and intact — the analysis column exposes any gap between what arrived and what reached the bank; documents numbered in sequence — a missing receipt number is a question demanding an answer; and ideally the person writing the CRJ is not the person holding the cash. This journal is not paperwork after the event; it is the cash control system running live.

The final questions of this part are with you now: total, cross-cast, and state what each total will do at month end — precisely as laid out here.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now we build the very same journal again — starting from a shoebox of paper and one simple question: where did the money come from? No formats first. Watch the format invent itself.

## Subtopic: The Money-In Diary

Picture Lindi running her store with one habit: every time money arrives, the proof goes into a box — till slips, receipts she wrote, deposit slips from the bank. By Friday the box is a mess. Her uncle, who kept books for years, offers a single piece of advice: "Keep a diary. One page per month. Every time money comes in, one line: the date, the paper that proves it, who it came from, how much."

That diary IS the Cash Receipts Journal. Nothing scarier than that: a money-IN diary, in date order, with proof stapled on. Money going OUT gets its own separate diary — never mixed, because one question per book keeps every answer findable.

Now watch the diary grow columns all by itself. After a week Lindi notices she keeps writing one word over and over: Sales, Sales, Sales. So sales gets its own column — now she just drops the number in, and adding up the month takes seconds. But some money is NOT sales — the day she moved her own R60 000 savings into the business, the day the tenant paid rent. Those are visitors, not regulars — so the diary keeps one open column for them, where she writes the amount AND what it was for. Regulars get lanes; visitors get named. That is the entire architecture of the journal: laziness, organised brilliantly.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the diary are coming to you right now. For each amount that arrives, decide: regular lane, or named visitor?

## Subtopic: The Double Line — What the Customer Pays and What the Shelf Loses

One column still needs explaining, and it is the cleverest of them all. When Lindi sells for cash, TWO things happen in the same instant — and only one of them is money.

Saturday: R1 800 in the till from the day's sales. That is the money truth — into the money columns it goes. Now walk to the shelf: the goods that left had COST Lindi something. Her store marks everything up by 50 percent on cost, so goods that sold for R1 800 arrived on the shelf at R1 200. To find that number, do not chop 50 percent off — that is the classic slip. Reverse the mark-up: R1 800 divided by 1,5 equals R1 200. Prove it forwards: R1 200 plus half of R1 200 — plus R600 — is R1 800. Lands perfectly.

That R1 200 takes its own column on the same line: cost of sales. No cash moved for it today — it is the shelf's diary entry, riding beside the till's. And look at the gift the pair of numbers hands Lindi: R1 800 in, R1 200 off the shelf — R600 gross profit made TODAY, and known today, not at year end. That is the perpetual system from last lesson, alive inside a single journal line.

And the analysis column? Think of it as the counting-tray: money lands at different moments through the day — the tray collects each amount as it comes; when Lindi banks the tray, the tray's total becomes one Bank entry. What arrived, then what got banked. Two questions, two columns.

Your questions for this part are up now: each gives a till total and the mark-up. Divide to find the shelf's number, and set both truths on the line.

## Subtopic: The Month-End Handshake

Final skill: proving the month before believing it. At month end Lindi adds up every column. And here is the beautiful check built into the design: every rand in the Bank column had to come from SOMEWHERE — either the Sales lane or the named-visitors lane. So Sales total plus Sundries total MUST equal the Bank total. R4 500 plus R63 200 — R67 700. Bank column: R67 700. The columns shake hands.

If the handshake fails — say the lanes add to R67 700 but Bank shows R68 100 — the month holds a mistake, and the design even whispers what kind: an amount reached Bank without being explained in a lane, or a lane amount never made it into Bank. Chase the difference — R400 — and you will usually find a line with a number on one side and a blank on the other. The journal catches its own errors BEFORE they infect the ledger. And note again: the cost of sales column sits out of the handshake — it is the shelf's diary, not money, so it never joins the money arithmetic.

Take one habit from the whole lesson: this diary is also Lindi's protection. Takings banked every day, intact; every receipt numbered; the diary agreeing with the bank's own statement. If a rand ever vanishes between the till and the bank, the analysis column points at the exact day it happened. The CRJ is not admin — it is the store standing guard over its own cash.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: total the columns, test the handshake, and if it fails, say what kind of error you are hunting. You have just built your first real book of accounts — out of a shoebox. Walk home proud of that.
