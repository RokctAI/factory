# Part 1 — Expert

Today we lift our eyes from single entries and study the full production line: the accounting cycle. By the end of this session you will place the six stages in their fixed order — source documents, journals, posting to the ledger, trial balance, final accounts, financial statements — and state precisely what each stage delivers to the next.

## Subtopic: Why the Accounting Cycle Exists

Start with the problem the cycle was invented to solve. Picture a sole trader — S. Khumalo, who runs Khumalo Stores. In March she rings up cash sales, buys stock from wholesalers, pays the rent and the assistant's wages, and banks money received from customers who owe her. By the thirty-first there are dozens of transactions behind her. If all she kept was a drawer full of slips, she could never answer the two questions every owner eventually asks: did my business make a profit, and what is it actually worth?

The accounting cycle is the fixed route that every single transaction travels, from the second it happens to the day it appears in the financial statements. Pieces of this route are already familiar from earlier grades — you have completed source documents and entered simple journals before. The Grade 10 step up is seeing the pieces as one connected sequence, and being able to say what each stage receives and what it passes on.

Here is the sequence to fix in your memory, because every question about the flow of information through the books follows this exact path: first, source documents. Second, journals. Third, posting to the ledger. Fourth, the trial balance. Fifth, the final accounts. Sixth, the financial statements.

Notice the word cycle. When March closes, April's transactions begin the identical journey. The route repeats period after period — which is why accountants call it a cycle rather than a line.

One warning before we walk the route: learners love to swap the ledger and the trial balance, or to slide the financial statements in front of the final accounts. The order is not tradition — each stage is literally impossible without the output of the stage before it. Let us prove that, stage by stage.

Pause here — the questions on the sequence and its purpose are with you now. Put the stages in order, and defend the order by what each stage needs from the one before.

## Subtopic: Stage One and Two — Source Documents and Journals

Stage one is the source document. Accounting refuses to record anything it cannot prove, so every transaction must leave written evidence. When Khumalo Stores sells goods for cash on the fifth of March, the till prints a cash register slip. When the rent is paid by electronic transfer, the payment record and the bank statement stand as proof. When stock arrives on credit, the wholesaler's invoice is the evidence.

What does stage one produce? Proof — an objective record of the date, the amount and the parties. No document, no entry. That rule has no exceptions.

Stage two is the journals, the books of first entry. The key idea is that we do not scatter transactions straight into the ledger as they happen. First we sort them into daily lists by type. Money coming in — the cash sales of the fifth — is listed in the Cash Receipts Journal, the CRJ. Money going out — rent, wages — is listed in the Cash Payments Journal, the CPJ, with small payments running through the Petty Cash Journal. Later this year you add the Debtors Journal and the Creditors Journal for credit dealings.

Why sort before posting? Efficiency and control. If Khumalo Stores rings up eighty-five cash sales in March, nobody wants eighty-five separate ledger entries. The journal queues similar transactions in one column so that a single total can travel onward.

What does stage two produce? Chronological, classified lists of the month's transactions, columns totalled at month end, ready for posting.

Stop for this section's questions now — documents and journals, matched to their transactions and to what each stage delivers. Answer with the stage outputs exactly as stated.

## Subtopic: Stage Three and Four — Posting to the Ledger and the Trial Balance

Stage three is posting to the general ledger. Journals are arranged by date; the ledger is arranged by account. The owner needs the combined effect on Bank, on Sales, on Rent Expense — and the ledger account is the meeting place where everything about one item gathers.

At the end of March the bookkeeper posts the journal totals into the ledger using the double-entry principle: every amount appears on the debit side of one account and the credit side of another. The bank column total of the CRJ is debited to Bank; the sales column total is credited to Sales. Then each account is balanced or totalled.

What does stage three produce? A balance on every account — one clean figure that says exactly how much money the business holds, or exactly what it has sold this period.

Stage four is the trial balance — the pause where we check our work. Every ledger balance is listed in one of two columns, debits left, credits right. Double entry promises that every debit has a matching credit, so the two column totals must agree. If they differ, an error crept in during stage two or three, and it must be hunted down before anyone moves on.

Now the favourite trap: a balancing trial balance does not prove the books are perfect. Post R700 for water and electricity to the debit of Telephone — right side, wrong account — and the columns still agree. The trial balance tests arithmetic equality, never complete accuracy.

What does stage four produce? A checked list of every balance — the raw material for the statements.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — posting, and the trial balance, including that trap about what a balancing trial balance does and does not prove.

## Subtopic: Stage Five and Six — Final Accounts and Financial Statements

Stage five is the final accounts, drawn up when the financial year closes. Profit is calculated inside the ledger itself. The Trading account opens, Sales and Cost of Sales are transferred into it, and the difference is the gross profit. That gross profit moves on to the Profit and Loss account, where every other income and expense — rent income, wages, insurance — is gathered. The figure left standing is the net profit, and it is transferred to the Capital account, because profit belongs to the owner.

What does stage five produce? Gross profit and net profit, formally computed through ledger accounts.

Stage six is the destination: the financial statements, the polished reports prepared for the owner and anyone else entitled to read them. The Statement of Comprehensive Income reports the incomes, the expenses and the profit for the period. The Statement of Financial Position reports the assets, equity and liabilities as they stand on the final day.

What does stage six produce? Answers. Did Khumalo Stores make a profit this year? The Statement of Comprehensive Income gives the number. What does the business own and owe? The Statement of Financial Position sets it out.

Let us run the whole of March through the machine in one pass. A cash sale happens: the till slip is the source document. Its amount joins a line in the CRJ. At month end the CRJ totals are posted to Bank and Sales in the ledger. The ledger balances are listed in the trial balance, and the columns agree. At year end Sales flows into the Trading account and the final accounts extract the profit. Finally that profit is printed in the statements the owner reads.

Here is your self-test: for any stage, can you name what it receives from the one before and what it delivers to the one after? Master that in both directions and you hold the backbone of everything this subject will ever ask of you.

The last questions of this part are with you now: apply the receives-and-delivers test to each stage, exactly as demonstrated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same six stages, second lap — but now as a journey you can picture, because a route you can see is a route you never forget.

## Subtopic: The Taxi Route Through the Books

Treat every transaction as a passenger, and the accounting cycle as the taxi route it is forced to ride — six stops, fixed order, nobody skips.

Stop one: the passenger buys a TICKET — the source document. No ticket, no boarding: a till slip, a receipt, an invoice — proof of who, when and how much. Stop two: the RANK — the journals, where passengers heading the same way line up together: a money-in queue and a money-out queue. Why line up at all? Because eighty-five passengers each demanding a private trip is madness; eighty-five in one vehicle is a single journey — exactly why journal columns are totalled.

Stop three: DROP-OFF — posting to the ledger, where every amount is delivered to its own account's front door: bank amounts to the Bank address, sales to the Sales address. Stop four: ROLL CALL — the trial balance, where the whole suburb is counted: the left-hand totals must equal the right-hand totals, or a passenger went missing en route and must be found before the journey continues.

Stop five, once a year: the WEIGH-UP — the final accounts, where the year's selling is set against the year's spending and the profit steps forward. Stop six: the FRONT PAGE — the financial statements, the neat report that the owner, the bank manager and anyone entitled actually reads.

Ticket, rank, drop-off, roll call, weigh-up, front page. Repeat it three times and the six stages belong to you permanently.

Quick check before we move on — the questions on the route are reaching you right now: name each stop, and explain what breaks if a passenger skips it.

## Subtopic: One Slip Rides the Whole Route

Now load one real passenger and watch every stop earn its keep. Tuesday morning: a customer buys a school backpack for R240, cash. Climb aboard.

The till prints a slip — R240, today's date, one backpack. Ticket issued: stage one complete, and note that the slip goes nowhere near the ledger yet. That evening the R240 takes its place in the money-in queue: one line of the Cash Receipts Journal, shoulder to shoulder with the day's other sales. There it waits until month end — and then the entire column travels as ONE total: into the Bank address as money the business now holds, and into the Sales address as income the business has earned. Two addresses, one passenger — double entry working quietly in the background.

Month end brings roll call: the trial balance counts every address's balance, left column against right column. Our R240 sits inside both counts — inside Bank's balance and inside Sales' total — and the columns agree, so the month is proven. Year end brings the weigh-up: Sales, our R240 among them, flows into the Trading account, meets the cost of that backpack, and the profit on it joins the year's profit. And on the front page the owner reads a Statement of Comprehensive Income in which, invisibly but genuinely, Tuesday morning's R240 helps tell the story.

One slip, six stops, zero shortcuts. And the beautiful part: EVERY transaction this business will ever record — thousands upon thousands — rides precisely this route. Learn the route once and it carries everything, forever.

Your questions for this part are up now — ride a different passenger through the six stops, and say what each stage does with it.

## Subtopic: The Three Clocks on the Wall

One last picture: the cycle does not turn all at once. Three clocks drive it, each at its own speed.

The FAST clock ticks daily: tickets and queues — source documents completed the moment money moves, journal lines written the same day. Skip a few days and slips pile up while memory starts inventing; the fast clock keeps the record honest while events are still fresh.

The MIDDLE clock ticks monthly: drop-off and roll call — journal totals posted into the ledger, the trial balance drawn and proven. Once a month the books stop, tidy themselves and check themselves before the next month's traffic arrives.

The SLOW clock ticks once a year: the weigh-up and the front page — final accounts computing the year's profit, financial statements announcing it. The big questions deserve a full year's evidence: is this business winning, and what is it worth?

Daily record, monthly check, yearly answer. And then — this is why the word is CYCLE — April's first till slip sets the fast clock ticking again, on the same unchanged route, round and round for as long as the business breathes.

Hold the taxi route and the three clocks together in your mind and no question about stages, order or outputs can rattle you: you always know where every passenger is, and what time it is on the wall.

And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: each one names a bookkeeping task — say which clock it runs on, and which stop of the route it belongs to. Then step off the taxi; you can drive this route yourself now.
