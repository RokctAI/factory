**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we wrap the board! First up: the ledger and the trial balance still blur together for a lot of us — aren't both of them just lists of accounts?

**Tutor:** They use the same account names, but they do opposite jobs. The ledger is the workshop: every account collects its own entries on a debit side and a credit side until a balance emerges — it RECORDS. The trial balance records nothing. It is a single-page checklist that copies each ledger balance into a debit or a credit column and tests whether the two columns agree. Picture the ledger doing the month's work and the trial balance marking that work. And keep the limit of the marking in mind: it proves arithmetic equality only. A trial balance that agrees can still hide an amount filed at the wrong address. The ledger works; the trial balance checks; you need both.

***

**Thandi:** Next question: why can't a transaction go straight from its source document into the ledger? The journal stop feels like doing the same job twice.

**Tutor:** Run the month without it and watch what happens. Eighty-five cash sales posted one by one: eighty-five entries crowding the Bank account, eighty-five more in Sales, and every single one a fresh chance to slip. The journal exists to gather. Similar transactions queue in one column, the column is totalled once, and the ledger receives ONE posting for the whole month's sales. Efficiency is half the argument; the other half is control. A journal ordered by date and type means any till slip can be traced forward to its line, and any line traced back to its slip. The stop that looks like double handling is precisely what keeps the route fast and traceable.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the classic trap question: my trial balance agrees, so my books are correct — true or false?

**Tutor:** False — and being able to explain WHY is what separates a memorised answer from understanding. The trial balance tests exactly one promise: that total debits equal total credits. Now list the errors that keep that promise while still being wrong. Post seven hundred rand of water and electricity to Telephone — correct side, wrong account: the columns still agree. Leave a whole transaction out of the journals — both halves missing together: still agreement. Capture a sale twice — both halves doubled: agreement again. Treat the trial balance as a smoke alarm, not a full inspection. Silence rules out one family of errors — arithmetic ones — and no more. The other families are caught by different guards: numbered documents, folio references, and the reconciliations waiting for you later this year.

***

**Thandi:** Last one from the class: does the whole cycle really run every single day? That sounds exhausting.

**Tutor:** The cycle runs continuously, but its stages tick on three different clocks. The daily clock drives the front of the route — source documents written the moment money moves, journal lines captured while the day is fresh. The monthly clock drives the middle — journal totals posted to the ledger, the trial balance drawn and proven, the books pausing to check themselves. The yearly clock drives the destination — final accounts weighing the year, statements reporting it. So no single day carries all six stages: each day carries its slice, each month its checkpoint, each year its verdict. Then a new month opens, the fast clock ticks again, and the cycle earns its name all over.
