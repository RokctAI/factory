# Part 1 — Expert

Today we lay the foundation stone under all of accounting: the double-entry principle and the accounting equation. Every journal you will ever complete, every ledger you will ever balance, every statement you will ever draft rests on what we build right now.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Equation

We begin with a statement so plain it hardly seems worth writing down: everything a business owns was paid for by somebody. Either the owner put in the money, or an outsider did.

What the business owns, we call assets — the letter A. Vehicles, equipment, trading stock, money in the bank, debtors. What the owner supplied, we call owner's equity — the letter O. What outsiders supplied, we call liabilities — the letter L. Loans, and the creditors still waiting to be paid.

Since every asset traces back to one of those two sources, the accounting equation follows: A equals O plus L. Assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities. Nobody invented this to make school harder — it is a statement of fact, true at every second of a business's existence. The moment it stops balancing, the books hold an error. No exceptions.

From earlier grades you know each ledger account carries a debit side on the left and a credit side on the right. Here is the bridge between the equation and those sides: assets grow on the debit side; owner's equity and liabilities grow on the credit side. Decreases sit on the opposite side in every case. Keep that mapping visible — we are about to lean on it again and again.

The double-entry principle itself states: every transaction touches at least two accounts, and the total of the debits always equals the total of the credits. Two entries, equal in size, opposite in placement, holding the equation level. Let us prove it with four real transactions at a small business, Naidoo Stationers.

Pause here — questions on the equation and the sides are with you now. Recite the mapping precisely: what grows on the debit side, what grows on the credit side, and why the equation can never stop holding.

## Subtopic: Transactions (a) and (b) — Capital and a Cash Asset Swap

Transaction (a): the owner, R. Naidoo, deposits R80 000 into the business bank account as capital.

Reason it out before naming any debit or credit. What happened to assets? The business bank balance rose by R80 000 — assets up. Who funded it? The owner — so owner's equity rose by R80 000 as well.

In equation form: A up R80 000, O up R80 000, L untouched. Test the balance: left side grew R80 000, right side grew R80 000. The equation holds.

Now translate into the double entry. Bank is an asset that increased, so debit Bank R80 000. Capital is owner's equity that increased, so credit Capital R80 000. One debit, one credit, identical amounts.

Notice the business entity idea working quietly underneath: the business received the money, and the business now answers to the owner for it. We always record the business's point of view, never the owner's private affairs.

Transaction (b): the business buys a delivery scooter for R24 000 and pays cash.

What happened? One asset arrived — a vehicle worth R24 000 — and another asset left — R24 000 of bank money. This is an asset swap.

In equation form: A rises R24 000 and falls R24 000 in the same breath. Net effect on A: nil. O untouched, L untouched. The equation stays level — zero equals zero.

Here lurks the pitfall: learners read "no effect on the equation" and conclude "no entries needed". Wrong. There are always two entries. Vehicles is an asset increasing: debit Vehicles R24 000. Bank is an asset decreasing: credit Bank R24 000. Two entries inside A, cancelling in the equation, both fully recorded in the books.

Stop for this section's questions now — the capital deposit and the asset swap, equation effects and entries alike. Argue each answer from what grew and what shrank.

## Subtopic: Transactions (c) and (d) — Bringing in Liabilities

Transaction (c): the business buys trading stock on credit for R15 000.

Stock arrives — an asset — but not a rand is paid today. Instead the business now owes a supplier. That supplier is a creditor, and creditors are a liability.

Equation effect: A up R15 000 because trading stock came in; L up R15 000 because the amount owing grew; O untouched. Left side up R15 000, right side up R15 000 — level.

Double entry: Trading Stock is an asset increasing, so debit Trading Stock R15 000. Creditors Control is a liability increasing, and liabilities grow on the credit side, so credit Creditors Control R15 000.

Transaction (d): the business pays a creditor R6 000.

Part of the debt is settled. Money leaves the bank: an asset falls by R6 000. The debt shrinks: a liability falls by R6 000.

Equation effect: A down R6 000, L down R6 000, O untouched. Both sides dropped equally — still level.

Double entry: Creditors Control is a liability decreasing, and decreases sit opposite increases, so debit Creditors Control R6 000. Bank is an asset decreasing: credit Bank R6 000.

Step back and study the pattern across all four transactions. Each produced exactly one debit and one credit of equal size. Each left the equation balanced — both sides climbing together, both falling together, or a shuffle contained inside one side. That is not coincidence. That is the machine working as designed.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the credit purchase and the payment to the creditor. Name both accounts, both directions, both sides, and end with the equation's balance.

## Subtopic: Verifying the Whole Picture

Now we total everything and verify, exactly as you should at the end of any accounting-equation exercise.

Assets: Bank received R80 000, paid out R24 000, paid out R6 000 — leaving R50 000. Vehicles R24 000. Trading stock R15 000. Total assets: R89 000.

Owner's equity: capital of R80 000.

Liabilities: creditors of R15 000 less the R6 000 paid — leaving R9 000.

Test the equation: does A equal O plus L? O plus L is R80 000 plus R9 000, which is R89 000. Assets are R89 000. The equation balances to the rand. If your totals ever disagree, retrace transaction by transaction until you find the one where debit and credit were unequal or landed on the wrong sides.

One further layer, because Grade 10 will demand it shortly: where do incomes and expenses belong? They are not a fourth element — they are movements inside owner's equity. Income builds the owner's profit, so income increases O and sits on the credit side. Expenses erode the owner's profit, so expenses decrease O and sit on the debit side. Meet a transaction like "paid wages R1 800" and the analysis runs: Bank, an asset, down R1 800 — credit Bank; owner's equity down R1 800 through the Wages expense — debit Wages.

Your permanent routine for every transaction, from today to matric: one, name the two accounts affected. Two, classify each as asset, owner's equity or liability — remembering incomes and expenses live inside O. Three, decide increase or decrease for each. Four, apply the sides: assets up means debit; O and L up means credit. Five, confirm the equation still balances. Five steps, every time, without shortcuts.

Own this routine and nothing in Accounting will ever feel mysterious — because everything that follows is this, repeated.

The final questions of this part are with you now: run the five-step routine on each transaction and close every answer with the equation check.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same foundation, built the way it actually clicks — with pockets, piles and a kitchen table. No symbols until the very end.

## Subtopic: The Two-Pocket Rule

Start with something you own — say, your bicycle. One question: where did the money for it come from? Either it was YOUR money — saved pocket money, holiday earnings — or it was SOMEBODY ELSE'S money you still have to repay — borrowed from a cousin, owing on a lay-by. There is no third option. Everything anyone owns was paid for from one of two pockets: the own-money pocket or the owed-money pocket.

A business works identically, only with bigger numbers. Everything the business OWNS — bank money, stock, its scooter — was funded either from the OWNER'S pocket or from an OUTSIDER'S pocket that must be repaid. Accounting simply names the three piles: the owned things — assets. The owner's pocket — owner's equity. The owed pocket — liabilities.

Suddenly the famous equation is not a formula at all — it is a sentence you already believe: what the business owns EQUALS the owner's pocket PLUS the owed pocket. A equals O plus L. It cannot ever stop being true, for the same reason your bicycle could not have been paid for by thin air: every rand of owning has a source, and sources come in exactly two kinds.

That is the entire secret. When the books "balance", they are simply confirming that no rand appeared from nowhere and no rand dissolved into nowhere.

Quick check before we push on — questions on the two pockets are coming to you right now: for each item, name what is owned and which pocket paid for it.

## Subtopic: Four Moves on the Kitchen Table

Now replay the four transactions as moves on a kitchen table, with three piles laid out in front of you: OWNED, OWNER'S POCKET, OWED.

Move one: the owner puts R80 000 into the business. The OWNED pile grows by R80 000 — that bank money is real and sitting there. Where from? The owner's pocket — so the OWNER'S POCKET pile grows by R80 000 too. Both sides of the table rise equally. Level.

Move two: the business buys the scooter for R24 000 cash. Watch closely — everything happens INSIDE the owned pile: R24 000 of bank money slides out, R24 000 of scooter rolls in. The owned pile is worth exactly what it was; the other piles never twitched. Level again — but hear this loudly: two real things still happened, and both get written down. A still table is the RESULT of two moves cancelling, never permission to skip the writing.

Move three: R15 000 of stock arrives on credit. The OWNED pile grows — real stock on real shelves. Nobody has paid for it, so the OWED pile grows by the same R15 000. Both sides up together. Level.

Move four: the business pays that supplier R6 000. Bank money leaves the OWNED pile; the OWED pile shrinks by the same R6 000. Both sides down together. Level.

Four moves, and the table never tipped once — up together, shuffle inside, up together, down together. That steadiness IS double entry: every move touches two places, equally, always.

Your questions for this part are up now: play each move on the table — which piles, which directions, and does the table stay level?

## Subtopic: The Question That Never Fails

Final piece: the single question that unlocks every transaction you will ever meet: WHICH TWO PLACES, AND WHICH WAY?

Every event touches exactly two places on the table. Train the reflex on wages: the business pays R1 800 in wages. Place one: bank money — owned pile down. Place two — and here is the only genuinely new idea: those wages bought no THING; they bought work that is already used up. Spending of that kind quietly eats the owner's pocket — because whatever the business loses without gaining something owned must, in the end, come off what the owner's stake is worth. So expenses live inside the owner's pocket, on its shrinking side. Incomes live there too, on its growing side — every sale quietly fattens the owner's stake.

That is the real reason the books place expenses on the debit side and incomes on the credit side: they are the owner's pocket shrinking and growing, dressed in work clothes.

So the complete routine, in plain words: name the two places. Say which way each one moves. Check the table stays level. Then — and only then — translate into book language: owned things up, debit; owner's pocket and owed things up, credit; decreases opposite. The translating is the easy part once the table picture is right; nearly every mark lost in this topic is lost by skipping the picture and guessing at words.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: for each event, two places, two directions, one level table — and the debits and credits will name themselves. This foundation is yours now; the rest of Accounting is the same game with better scenery.
