# Part 1 — Expert

Last term assembled the machinery piece by piece: six journals, a debtors ledger, a creditors ledger, a general ledger, a trial balance — each introduced on its own, practised on its own. But a trading business never experiences them on their own. A real month arrives as one tangled stream — a cash sale before breakfast, a supplier delivery at ten, a customer paying off an old account after lunch — and the skill that now matters is ROUTING: reading any transaction cold and sending it through the correct journal to the correct ledgers, first time, every time. Today we take one complete month and run it from the first slip to a level trial balance, then wrap the internal-control and ethics thinking around it, because integrated scenarios are exactly where strong marks are earned.

## Subtopic: Six Journals, One System

Start with the whole map, small enough to hold in one hand. Every transaction enters the books through exactly one journal, and two questions choose it: did MONEY move, and in which DIRECTION — and if no money moved, what actually happened?

Money flowing in — Cash Receipts Journal, no matter the source: cash sales, the owner's capital, sundry income, and now also money collected from debtors, which is why the CRJ carries a Debtors Control column this term. Money flowing out — Cash Payments Journal: stock bought for cash, wages, rent, and now settlements to creditors through the CPJ's Creditors Control column. No money, but goods handed over on trust — Debtors Journal. No money, goods received on trust — Creditors Journal. Goods travelling backwards: from a customer, the Debtors Allowances Journal; back to a supplier, the Creditors Allowances Journal. And when neither money nor goods moved — a write-off, interest charged, an error corrected — the General Journal takes it, always with a narration.

Sharpen the reflex on rapid-fire calls. A debtor hands over two thousand four hundred rand: money in — CRJ, Debtors Control column, and that debtor's personal page. Seven thousand rand paid to Bayside Suppliers on account: money out — CPJ, Creditors Control column, Bayside's page. Goods sold on credit: DJ. A tenant pays rent in cash: CRJ, sundries. An old debt declared irrecoverable: nothing moved — General Journal.

One standing warning about the new columns. Money collected from a debtor touches Bank and the debtor's DEBT — never Sales, because the sale was recorded in full on the day the goods left. Equally, paying a creditor never touches Trading Stock — the stock entered the books the day it was delivered. The control columns exist to settle old promises; the trading columns belong to the original events.

Pause here — the routing questions for this section are with you now: for each transaction, name the journal, the columns struck, and any personal account involved.

## Subtopic: A Combined Month, Journalised

Now the month itself. The business is Zanele's Traders, mark-up fifty percent on cost, opening with one balance carried in from the previous month: P. Khumalo, a debtor, owing four hundred rand — with capital standing at the matching four hundred, and every other account starting clean for our exercise.

Route each event on the day it happens. The 1st: Zanele deposits thirty thousand rand capital — CRJ, sundries: Capital. The 3rd: trading stock bought on credit from Bayside Suppliers, twelve thousand rand — CJ: Creditors Control and Trading Stock both twelve thousand; Bayside's page credited. The 6th: cash sales of nine thousand rand — CRJ: Bank and Sales nine thousand, Cost of Sales six thousand, because fifty percent on cost means goods costing six thousand sell for nine. The 9th: credit sale to T. Mokoena, four thousand five hundred rand — DJ: Sales four thousand five hundred, Cost of Sales three thousand; Mokoena's page debited. The 11th: rent paid, four thousand rand — CPJ, sundries: Rent Expense.

The 13th: Mokoena returns undamaged goods, nine hundred rand — DAJ: Debtors Allowances nine hundred, Cost of Sales six hundred back; his page credited. The 16th: Mokoena pays two thousand four hundred on account — CRJ, Debtors Control column; his page credited. The 19th: paid Bayside seven thousand on account — CPJ, Creditors Control column; Bayside's page debited. The 21st: damaged stock returned to Bayside, one thousand rand — CAJ: Creditors Control one thousand, Trading Stock one thousand; Bayside's page debited, debit note issued and numbered. The 26th: wages, two thousand two hundred — CPJ, Wages column. The 29th: Khumalo's four hundred rand is declared irrecoverable after months of chasing — GJ: Bad Debts debited, Debtors Control credited, narration written; his page ruled off.

Eleven events, six journals, and not one moment of doubt — every single one answered the same two questions. Notice the texture of a real month: the same customer, Mokoena, appears in three different journals — a sale, a return, a receipt — and his personal page quietly gathers the whole story: four thousand five hundred debit, nine hundred credit, two thousand four hundred credit — one thousand two hundred rand still owing.

Stop for this section's questions now — the month's transactions come back to you: journal, columns, pages, and your reason in a single clause.

## Subtopic: Posting the Month and Proving It

Month end. Cross-cast every journal so its columns prove themselves, post the totals to the general ledger — the personal pages having been posted daily — and then prove the entire month at once.

The balances, account by account. Bank: receipts of thirty thousand plus nine thousand plus two thousand four hundred against payments of four thousand plus seven thousand plus two thousand two hundred — forty-one thousand four hundred in, thirteen thousand two hundred out, twenty-eight thousand two hundred rand debit. Trading Stock: in twelve thousand, out six thousand and three thousand to cost of sales, six hundred back from Mokoena's return, one thousand out to Bayside — two thousand six hundred debit. Debtors Control: opening four hundred, plus sales four thousand five hundred, less allowances nine hundred, less receipts two thousand four hundred, less the write-off four hundred — one thousand two hundred debit, and the list agrees exactly: Mokoena one thousand two hundred, Khumalo closed. Creditors Control: purchases twelve thousand, less the payment of seven thousand, less the return of one thousand — four thousand credit, and Bayside's page says the same. Capital: thirty thousand four hundred credit. Then the nominal accounts: Sales thirteen thousand five hundred credit; Debtors Allowances nine hundred debit; Cost of Sales six thousand plus three thousand minus six hundred — eight thousand four hundred debit; Rent Expense four thousand; Wages two thousand two hundred; Bad Debts four hundred — all debits.

The trial balance. Debits: twenty-eight thousand two hundred, two thousand six hundred, one thousand two hundred, nine hundred, eight thousand four hundred, four thousand, two thousand two hundred, four hundred — forty-seven thousand nine hundred. Credits: thirty thousand four hundred, thirteen thousand five hundred, four thousand — forty-seven thousand nine hundred. Level. And the deeper proofs hold as well: each control account agrees with its list, and every journal cross-cast before a single total was posted. Three layers of proof, each one catching what the others cannot see.

Keep the equation view ready, because questions often demand it per transaction: every event moved assets, owner's equity and liabilities in balance — the credit purchase lifted A and L together; the rent payment dropped A and O together; Mokoena's receipt was a pure swap inside A, Bank up and Debtors down.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: balances, the trial balance, and equation effects — calculated, never guessed.

## Subtopic: The Control Web Around the Month

Now lay the internal controls from last term over this living month, because scenario questions almost always braid the two together.

Cash: the forty-one thousand four hundred rand that passed through Bank was protected by daily intact banking, by the CRJ's analysis column exposing any gap between the till and the deposit, and by the month-end comparison with the bank statement — an outside record testing our inside one. Stock: the perpetual system priced every movement as it happened, so the two thousand six hundred rand balance is a CLAIM that the next physical count can test — any shortage now has an exact size and a date range. Debtors: Mokoena traded under a credit limit; his statement went out at month end; and Khumalo's four hundred was written off only after documented, dated follow-up — prudence acting on evidence, never on irritation. Creditors: Bayside's invoice was matched against the order and the delivery note before anything was recorded; the damaged goods travelled back under a numbered debit note; and payment followed the agreed terms rather than whoever telephoned loudest.

And through all of it runs the ethics pair the syllabus names: accountability and transparency. The bookkeeper who writes a narration under the write-off is accountable — the decision sits in the book, visible, dated, explained. The owner who banks intact daily is transparent — cash and records agree for anyone with the right to look. An integrated question will hand you a messy scenario — takings banked short, a cousin's account far over its limit — and ask for the weakness, the risk, the recommendation and the principle. After today you hold every tool that answer needs.

The final questions of this part are with you now: integrated scenarios — route the transaction, name the control, cite the principle. One system, examined whole.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same month again — but this time from behind the counter, because a combined month is not eleven separate puzzles. It is one busy shop having an ordinary month, and every piece of paper it produces already knows where it belongs.

## Subtopic: Eleven Slips, Six Doors

Month-end evening at Zanele's Traders. She empties the document tray onto the counter: till slips, two invoices, a debit note, a bank deposit book, and one sad letter confirming Khumalo will never pay. Eleven pieces of paper. In the back room, six doors. The entire job of bookkeeping is getting every paper through its right door — and each door only asks simple questions.

The first door asks: did money COME IN? The capital deposit, the till slips, Mokoena's two thousand four hundred — straight through. The second door: did money GO OUT? The rent, the wages, the seven thousand to Bayside. The third door: goods handed out on TRUST? Mokoena's big credit sale. The fourth: goods received on trust? Bayside's twelve-thousand-rand delivery. Doors five and six take anything travelling BACKWARDS — Mokoena's return through five, the damaged box back to Bayside through six. Which leaves the letter about Khumalo: no money, no goods — it slips through the little seventh gap at the end, the odd-jobs door, and it must carry its explaining sentence.

Feel how quick the sorting becomes when each paper answers just two questions: money or no money? Which direction? Eleven papers, well under two minutes. That reflex — two questions asked eleven times, not eleven separate procedures memorised — is the whole skill this term is testing.

And one trap lives at the doors: Mokoena's two thousand four hundred goes through the money-in door, but it is NOT a sale. His sale went through the trust door back on the 9th. This paper only shrinks the amount his page says he owes. Money that settles an old promise never enters the Sales lane — and the same on the way out: paying Bayside settles Bayside's page; the stock came through its own door weeks before.

Quick check before we move on — papers are landing with you right now: two questions each, name the door.

## Subtopic: The Month That Used Everything

Now watch the sorted month flow home and prove itself — honestly the most satisfying moment in the whole subject.

The personal pages each tell a short story. Mokoena's page: bought four thousand five hundred, sent back nine hundred, paid two thousand four hundred — owes one thousand two hundred, and if he phoned right now, Zanele could tell him so in five seconds flat. Bayside's page: delivered twelve thousand, took back one thousand of damaged goods, received seven thousand — the shop still owes four thousand. Khumalo's page: opened at four hundred, closed at nothing, with the reason recorded where anyone can read it.

The big accounts tell the big story. Bank: forty-one thousand four hundred in, thirteen thousand two hundred out — twenty-eight thousand two hundred ready for next month. The stock account: filled with twelve thousand, gave up nine thousand at cost to customers, took six hundred back, sent one thousand away — two thousand six hundred rand of goods that SHOULD be standing on the shelf, and any evening Zanele cares to count, the shelf must answer for it. Sales: thirteen thousand five hundred earned. And the month's costs line up beside it: eight thousand four hundred of goods, four thousand of rent, two thousand two hundred of wages, four hundred of bad debt.

Then the weigh-in. Every left-side balance added: forty-seven thousand nine hundred. Every right-side balance: forty-seven thousand nine hundred. Dead level — and think about what that levelness certifies: eleven messy events, six doors, two ledgers of personal pages, one general ledger, and not a single rand lost or invented on the way through. That is not luck. That is the machine you spent a whole term building, running at full speed for the first time.

Your questions for this part are up now: read the pages, find the balances, and weigh the month yourself.

## Subtopic: The Owner's Friday Walk

Last picture: what all of this is actually FOR. Every Friday at closing time, Zanele takes a ten-minute walk through her own shop with the books open — and the month we just ran gives her every stop on the route.

Stop one, the till: count the cash against today's analysis column. If they agree, the whole day's takings are banked tonight, intact, nothing skimmed for pockets or petty favours. Stop two, the shelves: pick a handful of lines and count them against what the stock account claims. The book says two thousand six hundred rand of goods; the shelves must back that up. Stop three, the names: run a finger down the debtors list — who is close to a limit, whose debt is quietly ageing? Mokoena at one thousand two hundred, recent and fine. Khumalo's four hundred is the cautionary tale: chased hard in week one, that letter might never have needed writing. Stop four, the suppliers: Bayside's statement against Bayside's page — four thousand in both books, agreed, with payment diarised for the terms date, not before and not after.

Ten minutes, four stops — and notice the quiet condition that makes the walk possible at all: the books exist. Without them, Friday has questions but nothing to check the answers AGAINST. Records first, control second, trust throughout — the shop watches over itself, and the owner sleeps properly.

And the ethics pair, translated into shop language: everything on that walk can be shown and explained to anyone with a right to ask — a partner, the bank, one day a buyer. A business run like that has nothing to hide and everything to show. That is what accountability and transparency mean once they step out of the textbook.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: walk the Friday route through a scenario shop — what gets checked, against which record, and what smells wrong. Last term built the machine; today you ran it, whole. From here on, everything in Accounting is refinement.
