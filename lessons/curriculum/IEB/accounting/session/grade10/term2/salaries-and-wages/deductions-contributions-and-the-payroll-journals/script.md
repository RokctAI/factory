# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson stopped at gross pay — what the work earned. Nobody takes gross home. Today the payroll machinery finishes the job: the deductions that shrink gross to net, the contributions the employer stacks on top, the Salaries Journal and Wages Journal that record every rand, and the ethics questions that ride on payday. Bring along the figures we built: the administrator's eleven thousand rand monthly salary, Ndlovu's two thousand two hundred and twenty-five rand week, Adams's one thousand nine hundred and thirty-six.

## Subtopic: From Gross to Net — the Deductions

A deduction is an amount taken OUT of the employee's gross before payment — money the employee earned, redirected on the employee's own behalf. The Grade 10 set is five, each with its own logic and its own destination.

PAYE — Pay As You Earn — is income tax, deducted according to SARS tables and paid over to SARS every month; the employer is the collector of that money, never its owner. The pension fund deduction is the employee's own retirement saving, usually a percentage of gross — seven comma five percent in our examples. UIF — the Unemployment Insurance Fund — takes one percent of gross, buying the employee cover if work is ever lost. Medical aid is the employee's share of the monthly scheme membership — a fixed rand amount, not a percentage. And the union subscription, a fixed fee, deducted and paid over to the union.

Run the administrator's month. Gross: eleven thousand rand. PAYE from the tables: one thousand four hundred and thirty. Pension at seven comma five percent: eight hundred and twenty-five. UIF at one percent: one hundred and ten. Medical aid: five hundred and twenty. Union: seventy-five. Total deductions: two thousand nine hundred and sixty. NET pay — gross less total deductions: eight thousand and forty rand. That eight thousand and forty is what lands in her bank account; the other two thousand nine hundred and sixty also leaves the business, but travels to SARS, the pension fund, the UIF, the medical scheme and the union — each rand in her name.

Nail the vocabulary now: gross is earned; net is received; deductions are the difference — and every deduction is the EMPLOYEE'S money being redirected, never a cost the employer adds.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the five deductions, their bases and destinations, and the administrator's run from gross to net. Percentage bases against fixed amounts — keep them strictly apart.

## Subtopic: What the Employer Adds — Contributions

Now the second family, and the distinction every payroll question tests somewhere. A CONTRIBUTION is paid by the EMPLOYER, over and above gross — it never enters the employee's payslip arithmetic and never shrinks net pay. It is the employer's own additional expense, paid to the same funds.

The Grade 10 contributions: pension — commonly rand-for-rand with the employee, so eight hundred and twenty-five from the employer beside the administrator's eight hundred and twenty-five; UIF — the employer matches the one percent, another one hundred and ten; medical aid — rand-for-rand in our examples, five hundred and twenty; and the Skills Development Levy, the SDL — one percent of gross, one hundred and ten, paid to SARS to fund training across the economy. Note the SDL's odd shape: it has no employee half at all — a purely employer-side levy.

Total employer contributions for her month: eight hundred and twenty-five plus one hundred and ten plus five hundred and twenty plus one hundred and ten — one thousand five hundred and sixty-five. And now the figure that separates strong candidates: the TOTAL COST of employing the administrator. Not her net. Not even her gross. Gross of eleven thousand PLUS the employer's one thousand five hundred and sixty-five — twelve thousand five hundred and sixty-five rand. The worker sees eight thousand and forty arrive; the business pays out twelve thousand five hundred and sixty-five in her name — and every rand of the difference is real money at a real destination: tax, retirement, insurance, medicine, training.

The classification test, one line long: does it reduce the employee's pay? Deduction. Is it the employer's extra expense on top? Contribution. UIF famously lives in both families at once — one percent from each side.

Stop for this section's questions now — deductions against contributions, the SDL's one-sided nature, and the total-cost calculation. Classify before you calculate.

## Subtopic: The Salaries Journal, the Wages Journal and the CPJ

Now record it all. The Salaries Journal — monthly — and the Wages Journal — weekly — are books of first entry with a fixed column architecture: one row per employee, a GROSS column, a column per DEDUCTION, a NET PAY column, and beside them the employer's CONTRIBUTION columns. The administrator's row in the SJ reads: gross eleven thousand; PAYE one thousand four hundred and thirty; pension eight hundred and twenty-five; UIF one hundred and ten; medical five hundred and twenty; union seventy-five; net eight thousand and forty; then employer pension eight hundred and twenty-five, employer UIF one hundred and ten, employer medical five hundred and twenty, SDL one hundred and ten. Ndlovu's row in the week's WJ, with simpler deductions: gross two thousand two hundred and twenty-five; PAYE two hundred and twenty-two rand fifty; UIF twenty-two rand twenty-five; net one thousand nine hundred and eighty rand twenty-five.

The double entry, reasoned from first principles. The full gross is the expense of employing — debit Salaries, or Wages. But only the net leaves for the employee's bank account — credit Bank through the CPJ with eight thousand and forty. The deductions withheld are now OWED to their destinations — credit each one as a creditor: SARS for PAYE, the pension fund, the UIF, the medical scheme, the union. The employer's contributions: debit their own expense accounts — Pension Contributions, UIF Contributions, Medical Contributions, SDL — and credit the same creditors, because those amounts are owed to the same funds.

Then the settlements follow through the CPJ in the days after payday: one payment to SARS combining PAYE and SDL — one thousand four hundred and thirty plus one hundred and ten; one to the pension fund carrying both halves — eight hundred and twenty-five plus eight hundred and twenty-five; one to the UIF — one hundred and ten plus one hundred and ten; and so on, each payment debiting the creditor and crediting Bank. Whether the journal lives on paper or in a spreadsheet, the architecture is identical — the spreadsheet merely adds the columns for you, and the bookkeeper still owes the system a check that every column total reaches its ledger account.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: rows, columns, double entries and the settling payments — trace each rand from gross to its final door.

## Subtopic: Ethics on the Payroll

Four ethical matters attach to payday, and integrated questions will hand you scenarios carrying each one.

First, the CONTRACT. Every employment relationship rests on a written agreement — hours, rate, overtime multipliers, and deductions authorised by signature. Every calculation across these two lessons was legitimate only because a contract authorised it. An employer who deducts what was never agreed, or pays below what was signed, is not making an arithmetic error; that employer is breaking an agreement, with legal consequences attached.

Second, NEPOTISM — appointing or advancing family and friends over better-qualified candidates. Its payroll signature is a name earning outside the scale: a nephew appointed above the advertised notch, promoted past waiting colleagues. The harm lands twice — the business loses competence, and every honest employee loses faith in the ladder from last lesson; a scale only motivates while it binds everyone equally.

Third, payment IN LINE WITH RESPONSIBILITIES — equal work deserving equal pay, and pay matching the true weight of the job. The scale is fairness's instrument: positions graded, notches earned by service, never by favour. When two people carry the same responsibility on different pay without a defensible reason, the payroll records an injustice in perfectly neat columns — correct books, wrong conduct; the numbers can balance while the practice cannot.

Fourth, the TRADE UNION — the workers' collective voice, negotiating wages, multipliers and conditions no single worker could win alone. The union subscription we deducted is the membership fee of that voice, and the employer's duties are three: deduct it faithfully, pay it over promptly, and respect the union's role. Many of the multipliers and minimums written into the contracts came from exactly those negotiations.

The final questions of this part are with you now: scenario judgements — name the ethical issue, the harm, and the remedy, alongside the calculations they ride on.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now payday from the kitchen table — the envelope that shrinks, the second envelope nobody ever sees, and what a fair deal looks like when the name on the payslip is yours.

## Subtopic: The Envelope That Shrinks

Picture payday as an envelope with your name on it. Inside sits everything you earned this month: eleven thousand rand. But between the shop's safe and your hands, the envelope makes five stops, and at each stop something comes out — with your permission, written into your contract.

Stop one, the tax office: one thousand four hundred and thirty out — your share of the roads, the schools and the hospitals, collected before you can spend it. Stop two, the retirement jar: eight hundred and twenty-five — posted to your own future self; you will thank yourself at sixty. Stop three, the just-in-case insurance: one hundred and ten — one percent, so that if the job ever vanishes, something still arrives while you search. Stop four, the doctor cover: five hundred and twenty — your share of the medical aid. Stop five, the union: seventy-five — your membership of the voice that speaks for all of you at once.

The envelope arrives holding eight thousand and forty rand. Now the crucial way to SEE it: nothing was taken FROM you at those stops. Every rand that left was YOUR money, sent ahead to places you agreed it should go. Earned: eleven thousand. Received: eight thousand and forty. Redirected in your name: two thousand nine hundred and sixty. Gross, net, deductions — the three words on every payslip, and now they are pictures.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the five stops are coming to you right now: what leaves, where it goes, and whose money it was all along.

## Subtopic: The Boss's Second Envelope

Here is the part most workers never see. On the same payday, the boss quietly fills a SECOND envelope — and not one rand of it comes out of yours.

Look inside. Another eight hundred and twenty-five for your retirement jar — the shop matching your saving, rand for rand: save one, get one free. Another one hundred and ten for the just-in-case insurance — the shop matches your one percent too. Another five hundred and twenty standing beside your medical share. And one hundred and ten you have probably never heard of — the skills levy — one percent the shop pays into the country's training pot, so that workers everywhere, one day including you, can learn more and earn more.

That second envelope holds one thousand five hundred and sixty-five rand, and it means the true cost of your job is bigger than your payslip will ever show: your eleven thousand gross PLUS the shop's one thousand five hundred and sixty-five — twelve thousand five hundred and sixty-five rand, every month, in your name. You received eight thousand and forty; the shop paid twelve thousand five hundred and sixty-five; and every rand between those two numbers sits somewhere real — the tax office, your retirement jar, the insurance fund, the medical scheme, the training pot.

Why do questions love this so much? Because they test whether you keep the two envelopes apart. YOUR envelope shrinks by DEDUCTIONS. The boss's second envelope is CONTRIBUTIONS — extra, on top, never touching your net. Mix them once and the whole sum collapses; keep them apart and payroll becomes plain addition.

Your questions for this part are up now: two envelopes on the table — sort each amount into the right one, and build the true cost of the job.

## Subtopic: Fair Deals on Payday

Last piece: the fairness rules that keep all this arithmetic honest — because a payroll can balance perfectly and still be crooked.

The written deal. Before your first shift, the deal goes onto paper: your hours, your rate, your overtime prices, every deduction you agreed to. Why paper? Because memory negotiates and paper does not. If the envelope ever arrives lighter than the paper promised, the paper is your case — and without it, payday becomes whatever the stronger side says it is.

The nephew problem. The manager's nephew gets the job — or the promotion — over someone better. That is nepotism, and watch what it poisons: the ladder. Why study for the next notch, why stay loyal for the next rung, if rungs are handed out at family braais? A shop that hires nephews over candidates pays twice: once in weaker work, and once in every good worker who stops trying or walks away.

Pay that matches the job. Two people, same work, same responsibility — their pay should stand on the same scale, and the heavier job should stand on the higher one. The moment pay stops tracking responsibility, the books are recording unfairness in tidy columns. Balancing is not the same as being right.

And the union — the workers' voice, in the plural. One worker asking for a fair overtime rate is a request; every worker asking together is a negotiation. The small subscription from your envelope funds the voice that sat at the table where the multipliers and minimums were agreed. The shop's three duties: take the fee out faithfully, pay it over promptly, and respect the voice.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: payday scenarios — spot what is unfair, name it properly, and say what must change. Then the topic is yours, both lessons of it: you can build a gross, shrink it honestly, add the hidden envelope, record every rand, and judge the fairness of the whole deal. That is payroll — arithmetic wrapped around people.
