# Part 1 — Expert

So far the books have tracked money moving for customers and suppliers; now they must pay the people who actually do the work. Salaries and wages spans two lessons: today the EARNING side — scales, notches, normal time and overtime, finishing at gross pay. Next lesson takes that gross figure through deductions, employer contributions and the payroll journals. Get today's calculations into your hands, because every payroll question you will ever meet begins with a gross figure that must be computed, never copied.

## Subtopic: Wages, Salaries and the Scale

Start with the distinction the topic stands on. A WAGE is pay calculated from time worked — a rate per hour — earned typically by workers whose hours change from week to week, and paid weekly or fortnightly. A SALARY is a fixed annual amount attached to a position, paid in twelve identical monthly slices no matter how frantic or quiet any particular month was. The difference is not about status; it is about the UNIT being sold — the hour for the wage earner, the year for the salary earner — and it decides which book of first entry the next lesson opens: the Wages Journal or the Salaries Journal.

Now the instrument that governs salaries: the SCALE. Read this notation with care: R120 000 × R12 000 — R180 000. Three parts. The first figure is the STARTING notch: one hundred and twenty thousand rand per year. The middle figure is the ANNUAL INCREMENT: twelve thousand rand added to the annual salary for each completed year of service. The final figure is the CEILING: one hundred and eighty thousand, where the climbing stops. An employee appointed on this scale earns one hundred and twenty thousand in year one — ten thousand a month — then one hundred and thirty-two thousand in year two, one hundred and forty-four thousand in year three, one notch per year.

Two calculations that appear again and again. How many increments from bottom to top? The distance — one hundred and eighty minus one hundred and twenty thousand — is sixty thousand; divide by the notch of twelve thousand: five increments, so the ceiling is reached at the START of year six. And any year's monthly pay: that year's annual notch divided by twelve, always, and only at the end.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: wages against salaries, and a scale to read notch by notch. Name the three parts of the notation before you calculate a single figure.

## Subtopic: Normal Time and the Overtime Multipliers

A wage earner's week has a boundary: the NORMAL hours the contract agrees — forty hours per week in our examples. Every hour inside the boundary earns the basic hourly rate. Every hour beyond it is OVERTIME, and overtime never sells at the normal price — it sells at the normal rate times a MULTIPLIER the contract fixes in advance.

The standard multipliers: time and a half — one comma five times the normal rate — for ordinary overtime such as evenings and Saturdays; and double time — twice the normal rate — for Sundays and public holidays. Now the calculation discipline that earns marks: always derive the overtime RATE first, as its own labelled line, and only then multiply by the hours. A normal rate of fifty rand per hour becomes seventy-five rand at time and a half and one hundred rand at double time. Write the rate down before touching the hours — the rate is a scoring step in its own right, and learners who leap straight to a final figure lose it even when their total happens to be correct.

So every wage calculation has one shape: normal hours times normal rate, PLUS overtime hours times overtime rate, category by category when a single week contains both multipliers. That sum is GROSS PAY — what the work earned before one cent is taken off. Keep the word exact: gross is earned; take-home arrives only after next lesson's deductions.

Stop for this section's questions now — rates and multipliers: derive each overtime rate as its own labelled step, exactly as the marking rewards.

## Subtopic: A Week at Zanele's Traders — the Wage Run

Zanele's Traders now employs two weekly-paid workers; run their week to gross.

L. Ndlovu, hourly rate fifty rand, worked her full forty normal hours plus three hours of evening overtime. Normal pay: forty times fifty — two thousand rand. Overtime rate: time and a half — seventy-five rand. Overtime pay: three times seventy-five — two hundred and twenty-five. Gross: two thousand two hundred and twenty-five rand.

K. Adams, hourly rate forty-four rand, worked the full normal week, then gave up two Sunday hours to receive a delivery. Normal pay: forty times forty-four — one thousand seven hundred and sixty. Sunday rate: double time — eighty-eight rand. Sunday pay: two times eighty-eight — one hundred and seventy-six. Gross: one thousand nine hundred and thirty-six rand.

The week's wage bill at gross: two thousand two hundred and twenty-five plus one thousand nine hundred and thirty-six — four thousand one hundred and sixty-one rand. Study the layout in both calculations: every line labelled — normal, rate, overtime, gross — because a payroll clerk's working must be auditable, and because marks are awarded line by line along exactly this route.

One variation to prove the skeleton flexes. Suppose Ndlovu's three overtime hours had fallen on a public holiday instead of a weekday evening. The rate line changes to double time — one hundred rand — and the overtime line becomes three hundred: gross two thousand three hundred. Same skeleton, one substituted rate. Every payroll scenario, however elaborately dressed, is this skeleton wearing different numbers: identify the normal hours, identify each overtime category, derive each rate, sum to gross.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the week at Zanele's Traders, rerun with changed hours and shifted days: build the labelled lines to gross for each worker.

## Subtopic: The Salary Side and Exam Technique

Zanele's office administrator is salaried, now in her second year of service on the scale R120 000 × R12 000 — R180 000. Where does she stand? Appointed on the starting notch of one hundred and twenty thousand; after one completed year of service she stepped up one notch: one hundred and thirty-two thousand per year — eleven thousand rand per month. That monthly figure is what the Salaries Journal will carry next lesson, month after month, untouched by how busy the shop happened to be.

The technique for scale questions, in strict order. One: write the scale's three parts in words — start, notch, ceiling — before calculating anything; misreading the notation is the commonest error in the whole topic. Two: count YEARS OF COMPLETED SERVICE to find the current notch — an employee in her third year stands on the notch reached after two increments. Three: convert annual to monthly only at the very end, dividing by twelve. Four: for reach-the-ceiling questions, divide the scale's full distance by the notch to count the increments, then add those years to the appointment date. Five: label every line, because the route through the working carries marks of its own.

And hold today's two halves against each other, because a favourite discussion question asks exactly this: the wage earner's gross RESPONDS to the week — more hours, more pay, with the multipliers rewarding unsocial time; the salary earner's gross is STABLE — the same twelfth every month, growth arriving once a year through the notch. Each design fits its work: variable hours suit hourly pay; continuous responsibility suits the fixed salary. Next lesson, both streams flow into the same machinery — deductions, contributions, journals.

The final questions of this part are with you now: scales read, notches counted, months derived — the full technique, line by labelled line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the worker's side of the pay packet — because every person in this class will one day hold a payslip, and this is the lesson where its top line stops being a mystery.

## Subtopic: Two Ways of Getting Paid

Two people work at the same shop. Ask each one: how does your money arrive?

The first answers: I sell my HOURS. Every hour I stand behind that counter, the shop owes me fifty rand. Big week, big pay packet; short week, small one. My money is counted by the clock and lands every Friday. That is a wage — pay by the hour, week by week.

The second answers: I sell my YEAR. The shop and I shook hands on one number for the whole year, and every month one-twelfth of it arrives — flat-out month or dead-quiet month, exactly the same. That is a salary — pay by the year, sliced into twelve.

Neither deal is better; they fit different jobs. Packing shelves swells and shrinks with the season — the clock deal fits. Running the office carries the same responsibility in January as in June — the year deal fits. And burn in the one word both deals share: each produces a number called GROSS — what the work earned, before anything comes off. The take-home story, the deductions everyone grumbles about, is next lesson. Today is about how the gross gets built.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two deals are coming to you right now: for each worker, name the deal, the unit it counts, and when the money lands.

## Subtopic: The Multiplier Hours

Why should one hour ever cost more than another hour? Stand in the worker's shoes on a Sunday morning. The whole street is sleeping in; you are signing for a delivery. Your ordinary hour costs the shop fifty rand — but this is not an ordinary hour. This is your weekend, your family's time, your rest. The contract and the law agree: unsocial hours cost EXTRA.

So the price list has three rows. Ordinary hours, inside the agreed week: fifty rand — the plain price. Evening or Saturday overtime: the plain price plus half again — seventy-five rand — time and a half, a small apology stapled to every hour. Sundays and public holidays: DOUBLE — one hundred rand — because those are the hours that cost you most to give.

Watch a week turn into money. Ndlovu works her full forty hours at the plain price — two thousand rand. Then three evening hours at the apology price: three times seventy-five — two hundred and twenty-five. Her gross: two thousand two hundred and twenty-five. Adams works his full week — one thousand seven hundred and sixty — then gives up two Sunday hours at the double price: one hundred and seventy-six. His gross: one thousand nine hundred and thirty-six.

The only trap in the whole calculation: multiplying the EXTRA hours by the PLAIN price, out of pure habit. Beat it with a habit of your own: before touching the hours, write down the price of THAT KIND of hour. Which row of the price list am I standing on? Write that row's price. Then multiply. The row first, every time.

Your questions for this part are up now: weeks of hours, mixed rows — pick each hour's price from the correct row, then build the gross.

## Subtopic: Climbing the Ladder

Now the salary earner's quiet secret: the LADDER. When the administrator was hired, the shop did not offer her a number — it offered a ladder, written like this: start at one hundred and twenty thousand a year, climb twelve thousand more with each year of service, until the ladder tops out at one hundred and eighty thousand.

Every rung is one completed year. Year one: the bottom rung — one hundred and twenty thousand, which is ten thousand a month. Complete the year, step up: one hundred and thirty-two thousand — eleven thousand a month. Another year, another rung. And the ladder has a top: measure it — sixty thousand from bottom to top — and divide by one rung of twelve thousand: five steps, and after the fifth climb the rising stops unless a promotion offers a whole new ladder.

Why build pay this way? Because it makes tomorrow visible. The worker can read the ladder today and know precisely what year four will pay. The shop can budget years into the future. And loyalty is literally priced in: staying is worth a rung a year.

Whether you are reading a payslip or an exam question, the moves are the same three: find the rung being stood on — count completed years of service; turn the year into a month — divide by twelve; and for a question about the top — measure the ladder and divide by the rung. Nothing on the salary side is harder than those three moves.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: ladders to read, rungs to count, months to slice — plus one comparison to argue: whose pay moves with the week, whose moves with the years, and why each design is fair for its own job. Next lesson the deductions arrive — bring today's gross figures with you.
