**Thandi:** Question time! First up, the one half the class asked: the shop pays the pension fund R1 650 in one payment — so why do we insist R825 of it is a deduction and R825 a contribution? It all lands in the same place anyway.

**Tutor:** Because the two halves have different OWNERS on the way there, and the books must tell that story truthfully. The employee's R825 was part of her gross — money she earned, redirected with her signature; it reduced her net pay, and on the payslip it appears as a subtraction. The employer's R825 never belonged to her at all — it is an extra expense the business carries on top of gross, and her net pay never feels it. Record them as one lump and you lose both truths at once: her payslip would show a deduction that never happened, or the business would hide a real expense. The single payment is merely convenience at the settlement stage — one creditor, one cheque. The classification happened earlier, in the journal, where it belongs. One destination, two sources, two very different meanings.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the Salaries account get debited with the FULL eleven thousand when only eight thousand and forty ever leaves for her bank account?

**Tutor:** Because the expense of employing her IS eleven thousand — that is what her month's work cost the business in gross terms, regardless of how the money was then split up for delivery. Think of the gross as one river leaving the business, which immediately branches: the largest stream, eight thousand and forty, flows to her bank account; five smaller streams flow to SARS, the pension fund, the UIF, the medical scheme and the union — but every stream started as her pay. Debit Salaries with anything less and the income statement would understate what employment truly costs. The credits then simply describe the branching: Bank for the net, and a creditor for each fund holding money owed but not yet delivered. Expense recognised in full; delivery handled separately. That separation is the whole elegance of the entry.

***

**Thandi:** A sharp one: between payday and the day the shop pays SARS, where does the PAYE money actually LIVE? It's been taken off the payslip but hasn't gone anywhere.

**Tutor:** It lives in the books as a LIABILITY — and that word is doing precise work. The moment the deduction is made, the money stops being the employee's and never becomes the employer's: it is SARS's money, temporarily in the business's bank account, with a creditor account recording exactly how much is owed and to whom. That is why the credit at payday goes to a creditor called SARS and not to some income account — holding is not owning. And the ethics rides on the same point: an employer who spends withheld PAYE on stock, or delays paying it over, is using someone else's money — the deduction was collected under authority and must be delivered under the same authority. The creditor account is both the reminder and the evidence: the books themselves say this money already has an owner.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the skills levy goes to training for the whole economy — the administrator may never see a cent of it. Why should the shop pay for something so general?

**Tutor:** Because the shop draws from the same general pool it pays into — and the levy prices that honestly. Every business hires workers it did not train from scratch: the administrator arrived knowing bookkeeping someone taught her, the next hire will arrive with skills funded partly by levies other businesses paid. One percent of gross is each employer's share of building the workforce all employers borrow from. And notice the design choice worth quoting in a scenario answer: the SDL deliberately has no employee half. The worker's envelope is untouched, because the beneficiary of a skilled labour pool, in the first instance, is the businesses that hire from it. It sits in the same family as the employer's UIF match — costs of employing placed on the employer because that is where the benefit and the responsibility genuinely sit.
