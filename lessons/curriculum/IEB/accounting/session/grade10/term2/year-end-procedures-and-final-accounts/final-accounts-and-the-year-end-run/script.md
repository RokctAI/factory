# Part 1 — Expert

The accounting cycle's daily and monthly clocks have ticked all year; today the yearly clock strikes. Year-end procedures are the bridge between a trial balance and the financial statements — and the bridge has a fixed sequence: the pre-adjustment trial balance, the adjustments, the post-adjustment trial balance, the final accounts, and only then the statements. Today we walk that sequence at Zanele's Traders, attach the GAAP principle that authorises each step, and compute the year's profit formally. Depreciation, the largest adjustment of all, gets the next lesson to itself.

## Subtopic: The Cycle Reaches Year End

Place yourself in the cycle. Behind you: twelve months of documents, journals, postings and monthly trial balances. Ahead: the financial statements, the cycle's destination. Between them stands a quiet problem: the ledger's balances, honest as they are, describe the year AS RECORDED — and recording follows documents. Some year-end truths have no document yet: stationery bought and expensed during the year is partly unused in the cupboard; stock has gone missing from the shelf without any slip announcing it. The books must be brought level with REALITY before they are allowed to report.

That is what ADJUSTMENTS are: year-end corrections that align recorded figures with actual fact. And the trial balance splits into two versions around them: the PRE-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — the ledger exactly as the year's routine left it — and the POST-ADJUSTMENT trial balance, drawn after the corrections, the version from which the final accounts are built.

The authority behind all of it is GAAP. MATCHING demands that this year's income statement carry this year's incomes and expenses — no more, no less; that principle powers every adjustment. PRUDENCE demands that losses be recognised the moment they are discovered, never when convenient. GOING CONCERN lets assets stand at useful values because the business will continue trading. HISTORICAL COST keeps recorded values anchored to real transaction prices. The year-end run is not ritual; it is these principles, executed in order.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two trial balances, why adjustments exist, and which principle authorises them. Name the principle beside the procedure, every time.

## Subtopic: Adjustments, Introduced

Grade 10 opens the family with two clean members; meet both at Zanele's Traders, year ending 28 February.

First: CONSUMABLE STORES ON HAND. The Stationery account shows one thousand five hundred rand — everything bought during the year, recorded as an expense at purchase. But the year-end cupboard holds three hundred rand of unused paper and printer ink. Did the business USE one thousand five hundred rand of stationery this year? No — it used one thousand two hundred. The matching principle objects to the ledger's claim. The adjustment: move three hundred out of the expense — credit Stationery — and into an asset, Consumable Stores on Hand — debit three hundred. The expense now reports what was consumed; the asset carries what remains across the year-end line, where next year will use it and expense it.

Second: the TRADING STOCK DEFICIT. The perpetual inventory system has priced every movement all year, and the Trading Stock account claims three thousand one hundred rand on hand. The physical stocktake counts two thousand eight hundred and eighty. The books cannot argue with the shelf: two hundred and twenty rand of stock is gone — theft, breakage, error — and prudence demands the loss be recognised now. The adjustment: credit Trading Stock two hundred and twenty to bring the asset down to reality, and debit a new expense, Trading Stock Deficit, two hundred and twenty. The count is the fact; the account bends to it.

After both adjustments, draw the post-adjustment trial balance: Stationery now one thousand two hundred; Consumable Stores on Hand three hundred; Trading Stock two thousand eight hundred and eighty; Trading Stock Deficit two hundred and twenty — and the columns still level, because every adjustment was itself a complete double entry. Adjusted, yet still balanced: that is the state from which final accounts may be prepared.

Stop for this section's questions now — both adjustments: the reasoning, the double entry, and the effect on every account touched. Let matching and prudence do the arguing.

## Subtopic: The Final Accounts — Trading, and Profit and Loss

Now the profit is computed INSIDE the ledger, through two closing accounts — the final accounts.

First, the TRADING ACCOUNT, which isolates the trading result. Close Sales — the year's one hundred and fifty thousand rand — into it: debit Sales, credit Trading. Close Cost of Sales — one hundred thousand — into it: credit Cost of Sales, debit Trading. The Trading account now holds the whole buying-and-selling story in one place, and its balance — a credit of fifty thousand rand — is the GROSS PROFIT: what the mark-up earned before a single running cost. Check it against the pricing policy: fifty percent on a cost of one hundred thousand is exactly fifty thousand — the final accounts confirm what the pricing promised.

Second, the PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT, which gathers everything else. The gross profit transfers in — debit Trading, credit Profit and Loss fifty thousand. The other incomes join it: Rent Income of nine thousand closes to the credit side — fifty-nine thousand now assembled. Then every operating expense closes to the debit side: Salaries and Wages eighteen thousand; Telephone one thousand five hundred and fifty; Stationery — the adjusted one thousand two hundred; Bank Charges four hundred and thirty; and the Trading Stock Deficit, two hundred and twenty. Total expenses: twenty-one thousand four hundred.

The balance of the Profit and Loss account — a credit of thirty-seven thousand six hundred rand — is the NET PROFIT: the year's true earning after every cost. Its destination follows from the oldest principle in the book: profit belongs to the owner, so it transfers to Capital — debit Profit and Loss, credit Capital thirty-seven thousand six hundred. The nominal accounts now stand empty, swept clean for the new year, and the ledger has computed — formally, inside itself — the number the owner has waited twelve months to see.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: closing transfers, both final accounts, and the two profit figures — build each through the transfers, never by shortcut arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The Year-End Run, In Order

Assemble the whole procedure in its fixed order, with a reason standing at every step.

One: the PRE-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — prove that the year's routine bookkeeping balances before touching anything. Two: the ADJUSTMENTS — journalised in the General Journal with narrations, each aligning a balance with year-end fact, each authorised by a GAAP principle. Three: the POST-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — prove the adjustments preserved the equality. Four: the FINAL ACCOUNTS — Trading for the gross profit, Profit and Loss for the net, closing every nominal account along the way. Five: the profit to CAPITAL, where it has always belonged. Six — the next stage of the cycle — the financial statements, reporting what the final accounts computed.

Why so strict an order? Because each step certifies the one after it. Adjust before proving the books balance, and an old error hides among the adjustments. Prepare final accounts before adjusting, and the profit is wrong by every unadjusted rand — a result built on uncounted stock and unexamined cupboards. The order IS the control.

Exam technique, three habits. First: every adjustment is a full double entry — name both accounts and both directions; the half-adjustment is this topic's classic lost mark. Second: trace each adjustment DOWNSTREAM: the three-hundred-rand stationery adjustment changes the expense inside Profit and Loss AND places an asset among the year-end balances — one fact, two appearances. Third: when a question names a procedure, name its principle: matching for the stationery, prudence for the deficit, going concern for why assets carry forward at all. The pairing of step with principle is where the discussion marks live.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the run in order, reasons attached, effects traced end to end.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole year-end, told from behind the counter — because report card day comes for shops too, and the tidying before it is something everyone has done the night before an inspection.

## Subtopic: The Shop's Report Card Day

All year, Zanele's shop has been keeping its own diary: every sale, every payment, every promise, written down day by day, month by month. Now comes the day the diary must become a REPORT CARD — one page answering the owner's two oldest questions: did the shop make money this year, and how much?

But feel the catch in your bones: you cannot photocopy a diary and call it a report card. The diary records what happened AS IT WAS WRITTEN — and by year end, a few of its lines have quietly drifted off the truth. The stationery page says one thousand five hundred rand spent on paper and ink — but open the cupboard: three hundred rand of it is still sitting there, sealed and unused. The stock page claims three thousand one hundred rand of goods — but count the shelf: two thousand eight hundred and eighty. The diary is not lying; it simply does not know what nobody has told it.

So year-end has two jobs, in strict order: first TIDY the diary until it matches the real shop — those are the adjustments; then SQUEEZE the tidied diary down into the report card — those are the final accounts. Tidy first, squeeze second. Squeeze an untidy diary, and every untidiness rides straight through to the owner's page.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on report card day are coming to you right now: what the diary gets wrong, and which job must come first.

## Subtopic: Tidying the Room Before the Photo

Walk the two tidyings like the night before the inspection photo.

Tidying one: the stationery cupboard. The diary wrote "one thousand five hundred, all used up" — but there on the shelf sit three hundred rand of untouched paper and ink. So split the truth in two: one thousand two hundred truly USED this year — that stays as the year's expense; three hundred still WAITING — that becomes a small asset, a parcel handed across the year-end line to next year, which will use it and account for it in its own report. This year's card carries only this year's using. That single sentence is the famous matching rule, wearing its home clothes.

Tidying two: the shelf count. The stock page claims three thousand one hundred. Zanele counts: two thousand eight hundred and eighty. The shelf wins — always. The books come down two hundred and twenty to match reality, and the missing two hundred and twenty goes onto the report card as a loss with its own name: trading stock deficit. Notice the discipline: not "maybe it will turn up", not "let's check again next year" — the loss is admitted NOW, the moment it is discovered. Bad news goes on the card immediately; good news waits to be proven. That is the prudence rule, and it is the reason report cards can be trusted at all.

After both tidyings, one quick re-check that the tidied diary still balances — and the room is ready for its photo.

Your questions for this part are up now: two tidyings — what moves, where it goes, and why the shelf always wins the argument.

## Subtopic: Two Sieves and the Owner's Jar

Now the squeeze: the entire tidied year poured through two sieves, one after the other.

Sieve one catches only the BUYING-AND-SELLING story. In goes the year's sales: one hundred and fifty thousand rand. In goes what those goods had cost the shop: one hundred thousand. What stays caught in the sieve is fifty thousand — the GROSS profit: the reward for buying at one price and selling at another, before a single light bill is paid. And notice — the mark-up promised exactly this: half on top of one hundred thousand is fifty thousand. The sieve confirms the pricing kept its word.

Sieve two catches the RUNNING of the shop. Pour in the fifty thousand from sieve one, add the rent income the back room earned — nine thousand — so fifty-nine thousand of earning sits in the sieve. Now the year's costs drain through: salaries eighteen thousand; telephone one thousand five hundred and fifty; the tidied stationery one thousand two hundred; bank charges four hundred and thirty; and the admitted stock loss of two hundred and twenty — twenty-one thousand four hundred of running costs. What remains is thirty-seven thousand six hundred — the NET profit: what the year TRULY earned after everything.

And where does that thirty-seven thousand six hundred go? Into the owner's jar — Capital — because the profit was always hers; the shop was merely working it up all year. The year's pages now stand empty, clean for the new year, and the number in the jar is the one line the whole report card exists to say.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: pour a year through both sieves yourself — what each sieve catches, what it lets through, and whose jar the final number lands in.
