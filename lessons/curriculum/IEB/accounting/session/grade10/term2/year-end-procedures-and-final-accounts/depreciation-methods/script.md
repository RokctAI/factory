# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson introduced adjustments with the smaller items; today arrives the largest and most examined adjustment of them all: depreciation. Fixed assets work for years, and every working year wears them down — and the books must say so. By the end of this session you will calculate depreciation on both methods — straight-line and diminishing balance — handle an asset bought partway through the year, and maintain the asset register that keeps the whole story auditable. This calculation follows you through every year of Accounting from now to matric; build it correctly once.

## Subtopic: Why Assets Lose Value in the Books

Zanele's Traders buys a delivery bakkie for one hundred and fifty thousand rand on the first of March — the first day of the financial year. The bakkie will work for years; it will also, from its first kilometre, be worth steadily less than it cost. Two GAAP principles insist the books record that. MATCHING: the bakkie earns income across many years, so its cost must be spread as an expense across those same years — dumping the whole one hundred and fifty thousand into year one would crush a single year with a cost that five years share. PRUDENCE: carrying an ageing vehicle at its showroom price overstates what the business owns.

So accounting splits the story across three figures, and you must hold all three apart. COST PRICE: what the asset cost on purchase day — one hundred and fifty thousand, anchored there permanently by the historical cost principle; the Vehicles account never moves. ACCUMULATED DEPRECIATION: the running total of all value written off since purchase — a NEGATIVE asset account standing beside Vehicles, growing every year. CARRYING VALUE: cost minus accumulated depreciation — what the asset is still worth in the books today.

The annual double entry, reasoned from scratch: the year's wear is an expense — debit Depreciation. But we do not credit Vehicles, because cost stays anchored; we credit Accumulated Depreciation on Vehicles, the counter-account that collects the wearing. Expense up, negative asset up, carrying value down — and the income statement carries its fair share of the bakkie's cost.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three figures, the two principles, and the double entry. Keep cost, accumulated and carrying value strictly apart.

## Subtopic: Straight-Line — the Level Slice

Method one: STRAIGHT-LINE, also called the fixed instalment or cost-price method. The rule: the same percentage of the ORIGINAL COST, every year — a level slice of the asset's life.

The bakkie: one hundred and fifty thousand at twenty percent per annum on cost. Year one's depreciation: twenty percent of one hundred and fifty thousand — thirty thousand rand. Year two: twenty percent of one hundred and fifty thousand — thirty thousand again. Year three: thirty thousand again. The slice never changes, because its base — cost — never changes.

Now walk the three figures down the years, because a question can demand any of them at any date. End of year one: cost one hundred and fifty thousand; accumulated thirty thousand; carrying value one hundred and twenty thousand. End of year two: accumulated sixty thousand; carrying value ninety thousand. End of year three: accumulated ninety thousand; carrying value sixty thousand. See the shape: carrying value drops by the same thirty-thousand step, a straight line down the graph — the method's name is its picture.

And the horizon: at twenty percent on cost, the slices consume the whole cost in five years. In practice the books stop just short — a carrying value of one rand is often left standing so that an asset still physically at work never vanishes from the records entirely. An asset fully written off but still running tells you the estimate was conservative, not that anyone erred.

When is straight-line the honest choice? When the asset serves roughly evenly across its life — office equipment, fittings, furniture. Even service, even slices: matching in its plainest form.

Stop for this section's questions now — level slices down the years: depreciation, accumulated and carrying value at any date demanded.

## Subtopic: Diminishing Balance, and Additions During the Year

Method two: DIMINISHING BALANCE — the same percentage, but applied to the CARRYING VALUE, the figure that shrinks every year. The base moves; so the slice shrinks with it.

Same bakkie, twenty percent on diminishing balance. Year one: twenty percent of one hundred and fifty thousand — thirty thousand; carrying value one hundred and twenty thousand. Year two: twenty percent of ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY thousand — twenty-four thousand; carrying value ninety-six thousand. Year three: twenty percent of ninety-six thousand — nineteen thousand two hundred; carrying value seventy-six thousand eight hundred. Each year's charge is smaller than the last, and the carrying value slides toward zero without ever arriving — this method never fully consumes the asset.

Why choose it? Because some assets genuinely serve this way: vehicles and machines give their best, hardest years first — the new bakkie runs every delivery and barely sees the workshop; the old bakkie makes shorter trips between repairs. Diminishing balance charges the big years big and the tired years small — matching, shaped to a machine's life. Lock in the contrast: straight-line, constant base, constant charge; diminishing balance, shrinking base, shrinking charge. The classic trap asks for year TWO on diminishing balance and rewards nothing to the learner who sliced one hundred and fifty thousand instead of one hundred and twenty.

Now the ADDITION DURING THE YEAR. On the first of November — eight months into the financial year — Zanele buys an office computer for twenty-four thousand rand, depreciated straight-line at fifteen percent. A full year's charge would be three thousand six hundred — but the computer served only FOUR months of this year, November to February. The rule: depreciation runs from the month of purchase, pro rata. Four months of service: three thousand six hundred times four over twelve — one thousand two hundred rand. Every mid-year purchase gets this months-fraction, on either method, and the computer's first-year carrying value stands at twenty-two thousand eight hundred.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — shrinking bases and part-year fractions: identify the base, count the months, then calculate.

## Subtopic: The Asset Register and Exam Technique

Every fixed asset the business owns gets its own page in the ASSET REGISTER — the detailed record standing behind the ledger's summary totals.

The page carries: description and identifying details — the bakkie's registration, the computer's serial number; date of purchase and cost price; the depreciation method and rate; then, year by year, the annual depreciation, the accumulated total, and the carrying value. One page, one asset, the whole life story — from purchase to the day it is sold or scrapped.

Why keep it? Control, first: the ledger shows ONE Vehicles figure and ONE accumulated figure for the whole fleet; the register shows each vehicle on its own, and the register's totals must agree with the ledger's balances — the same summary-and-detail architecture as a control account and its list, now applied to assets. Theft and loss surface the same way: an asset on the register that cannot be produced on inspection has a question to answer. Second, decisions: the register tells the owner each machine's age, remaining value and repair history — the facts behind replace-or-repair. Third, the audit trail: every depreciation figure in the statements traces back to a page, a rate, a calculation.

Exam technique for the whole topic, five habits. One: name the method before calculating — the words "on cost" mean straight-line; "on carrying value" or "on diminishing balance" mean the shrinking base. Two: write the base figure down first, especially in the later years. Three: for additions, count the months on your fingers and write the fraction — four over twelve — into the working. Four: keep the three figures labelled — depreciation for the YEAR, ACCUMULATED to date, CARRYING value — because a question asks for one and learners hand over another. Five: the depreciation charge is an adjustment like last lesson's — expense debited, accumulated credited, and the trial balance and the final accounts feel it downstream.

The final questions of this part are with you now: registers read, methods named, bases written, months counted — the full discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the driveway — because everyone has watched a phone, a bicycle or a family car quietly lose its value, and depreciation is simply the books admitting what every owner already knows.

## Subtopic: The Bakkie That Quietly Gets Cheaper

Think of the newest phone in your house. Launch-day price: full and proud. One year later — still working perfectly — try to sell it: maybe two-thirds of the price, maybe less. Nothing broke. Nobody dropped it in the bath. It just... got older, and the world quietly marked it down.

Zanele's new bakkie is the same story with bigger numbers: one hundred and fifty thousand rand on the first of March, and from its very first delivery it is worth a little less every month — not because anything went wrong, but because working wears things, and newer models keep arriving. Now the accounting question: should the books keep announcing "bakkie: one hundred and fifty thousand" for five straight years? That would be a polite lie — and the books do not lie, not even politely.

So the books tell the truth in three numbers, and the trick of this entire topic is keeping them apart. What it COST — one hundred and fifty thousand, written in ink, never changing; that is the receipt. What it has LOST so far — a running total that grows each year; that is the wear. What it is STILL WORTH in the books — cost minus the wear; that is the honest answer today. Receipt, wear, worth. Every question in this topic asks for one of those three — and half the lost marks in it come from answering with the wrong one.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the three numbers are coming to you right now: which is which, and which one never moves.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Slice the Loss

The books must charge the bakkie's wearing to the years it works. There are two honest ways to slice it — picture a loaf of bread that has to last the asset's whole life.

Way one: the SAME slice every year. Take the cost — one hundred and fifty thousand — and cut twenty percent of IT each year: thirty thousand, thirty thousand, thirty thousand, level slices until the loaf is finished in five years. That is straight-line: simple, steady, and fair for things that serve steadily — desks, shelves, the office printer, doing roughly the same job in year four as in year one.

Way two: always a fifth of WHAT IS LEFT. First year: a fifth of one hundred and fifty thousand — thirty thousand, and one hundred and twenty thousand remains. Second year: a fifth of the REMAINING one hundred and twenty — twenty-four thousand. Third year: a fifth of ninety-six thousand — nineteen thousand two hundred. Each slice smaller than the last, and the loaf never quite disappears. That is diminishing balance — and it is honest for machines and vehicles, because they give their biggest, hardest years FIRST: the new bakkie runs every delivery and barely visits the workshop; the old bakkie putters between repairs. Big work years carry big slices; tired years carry small ones.

The one trap between the two ways: in year two, WHICH number do you slice? Straight-line slices the ORIGINAL loaf — always one hundred and fifty thousand. Diminishing balance slices what is LEFT — one hundred and twenty, then ninety-six. Say the base out loud before you cut, and the trap never catches you.

Your questions for this part are up now: slice both ways, year by year — and every time, name the number you are slicing before you slice it.

## Subtopic: Every Asset Gets Its Own Page

Last piece: the paperwork that keeps all of this honest — the asset register, which is really an ID book for everything the shop owns.

Open the bakkie's page: what it is, its registration, the day it arrived, what it cost, which slicing method it lives on, and then — year by year — this year's wear, the wear so far, and what it is still worth. One page per asset, its whole life in columns. The ledger upstairs holds only totals — all vehicles in one line — but the register holds the FACES: this bakkie, that computer, each with its own numbers. Totals upstairs, faces downstairs, and the two must always agree — the same trick as a control account agreeing with its list. And if a page says "office computer, worth twenty-two thousand eight hundred" and nobody can point to the computer? Then the register has just caught a thief — or at the very least started a very serious conversation.

And when something new arrives mid-year — the twenty-four thousand rand computer that came on the first of November — its page starts honestly: it served only four of the twelve months, so its first year's wear is only a third of a year's worth: three thousand six hundred cut down to one thousand two hundred. Assets are charged for the months they actually worked, the same way you would only pay a worker from the day they started, not from the start of the year.

The register is also where the owner thinks: this bakkie is four years old, worth this much, costing that much in repairs — replace it or run it? The page holds every fact that decision needs.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: read a register page, slice both ways, count an arrival's months, and match the totals upstairs to the faces downstairs. Do that, and the biggest adjustment in the book is yours — and the whole year-end topic opens up behind it.
