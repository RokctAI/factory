**Thandi:** Question time! The one that puzzled the most of us: why does the stationery adjustment create an ASSET? Paper and ink feel like the least asset-like things in the shop.

**Tutor:** Test them against the definition rather than the feeling. An asset is a resource the business controls that will bring benefit in the future — and that sealed box of paper passes on every count: the shop owns it, nobody else can use it, and next year it will be consumed in place of paper next year would otherwise have had to buy. Money already spent, benefit not yet taken — that is precisely what an asset is. The size does not matter; the timing does. And notice what the adjustment protects: without it, this year carries an expense of one thousand five hundred for using one thousand two hundred, and next year uses three hundred rand of paper at no recorded cost at all — two years' report cards, both slightly false. One small entry, and both years tell the truth. Matching is not fussiness; it is fairness between years.

***

**Thandi:** Next: for the missing stock — the books said three thousand one hundred, the count said two thousand eight hundred and eighty. Why does the count automatically win? Couldn't the COUNT be the mistake?

**Tutor:** The count can absolutely be mistaken — which is why a good stocktake is disciplined: recounts of odd results, two counters for valuable lines, count sheets signed. But once the count is confirmed, it outranks the ledger for one deep reason: the account is a PREDICTION and the shelf is an OBSERVATION. The perpetual system's balance says "given everything recorded, this much SHOULD be here." The count reports what IS here. When a confirmed observation contradicts a prediction, the prediction has failed somewhere — an unrecorded breakage, a theft, a capture error — and the books must come down to the fact. What the deficit account then does is make the failure VISIBLE: two hundred and twenty rand, named, dated, in the report — where an owner can ask why, and a pattern across years can trigger an investigation. Hiding it inside cost of sales would bury exactly the signal a manager needs.

***

**Thandi:** Here's the sharp one: the gross profit came out at fifty thousand, exactly half of the hundred-thousand cost — exactly the mark-up. Will those always agree? And what would it mean if they didn't?

**Tutor:** They agree in our clean example because every rand of stock left at the full mark-up and every movement was recorded. In a real year they drift apart, and the GAP is one of the most useful numbers in the business. The policy says fifty percent on cost, so the books promise fifty thousand on a hundred-thousand cost of sales. Suppose the Trading account showed only forty-six thousand: four thousand rand of margin leaked somewhere — end-of-season markdowns, discounts to friends, theft at the till, or goods sold below policy by mistake. The comparison of ACHIEVED mark-up against POLICY mark-up is a standing analysis question in later grades, and its logic is pure term-two thinking: the pricing promised a result; the final accounts measured what actually happened; management explains the difference. The agreement in today's lesson is not a coincidence — it is a baseline, so that next year's disagreement means something.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: why does the net profit end up in Capital? The owner didn't deposit anything — it feels strange that her account grows without her doing a thing.

**Tutor:** It feels strange only until you remember whose money was working all year. Capital is not a record of deposits; it is the owner's total CLAIM on the business — what the business owes back to her. She staked the original capital; the shop then spent a year trading with it, and the trading grew the stake by thirty-seven thousand six hundred rand. That growth belongs to nobody else — not to the customers, the suppliers or the staff, all of whom have been fully paid in the expense lines. So the ledger's last act of the year is to deliver the earning to its owner: debit Profit and Loss, credit Capital. It also completes a beautiful symmetry you will meet formally in the statements: profit increases the owner's claim exactly as drawings decrease it. The shop worked; the jar grew; and the equation — assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities — closes the year in balance, as it has closed every single day since the first entry of term one.
