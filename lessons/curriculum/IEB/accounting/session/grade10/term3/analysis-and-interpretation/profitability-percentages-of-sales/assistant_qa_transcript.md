**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: the two gross percentages — thirty-three and fifty — come from the SAME profit. Why do we need both? Isn't one of them redundant?

**Tutor:** They answer two different questions, and only together do they close the control loop. The percentage on cost speaks the PRICING language: it must equal the mark-up policy — fifty on cost — so it tests whether the pricing decision was actually carried out on the shop floor. The percentage on sales speaks the AFFORDING language: thirty-three rand of every hundred is all that exists to pay every expense, so it is the number the expense burden must fit inside. And the pair carries a fixed arithmetic relationship — fifty percent on cost always converts to thirty-three comma three on sales — which hands you a built-in check: compute both, and if your two answers do not correspond, a figure was misfed somewhere. One profit, two bases, two questions, one cross-check. Redundant is the last thing it is.

***

**Thandi:** Next: if the achieved mark-up comes out at forty-four percent against a policy of fifty, how do we know it's something ugly like theft and not just normal business?

**Tutor:** You don't know from the percentage alone — and saying so precisely is part of a full answer. What the percentage establishes is that goods costing a hundred rand brought in a hundred and forty-four, not a hundred and fifty: six rand per hundred of intended margin never arrived. The DISCIPLINED move is to list the possible doors, then let the scenario close them. Authorised markdowns or a clearance sale — legitimate, and usually named in the question. Damaged or expired stock written down — legitimate, documented. Recording errors — findable in the reconciliations. Theft of stock after costing, or takings skimmed — the ugly doors, left standing when the others close. The scenario tells you which doors are open: if it mentions a winter clearance, cite it; if it offers no legitimate cause, recommend the investigation. The percentage is the alarm, never the verdict — and the learner who names the alarm's possible causes always outscores the one who cries theft at every gap.

***

**Thandi:** This one's subtle: why is the base always SALES and not profit or expenses? Who decided sales gets to be the hundred?

**Tutor:** Sales is the river everything else flows out of — every other line of the statement is a piece taken FROM sales, so expressing the pieces per hundred of the river shows the whole split at once, and the pieces even add up: cost sixty-seven plus margin thirty-three makes the hundred; the margin, topped up by the rent's twelve, less the expenses' twenty-five, leaves the twenty of operating profit. Choose another base and the picture fragments — expenses as a percentage of profit says nothing about the counter where both were born. There is one deliberate exception, and you know it: gross profit on COST of sales, based on cost precisely because that is how the pricing policy is written — mark-up is applied to cost, so the check must run on cost. The bases are never arbitrary: each percentage stands on the base its question is asked in. That is why reading the denominators carefully IS the skill.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in interpretation questions, how much should we actually write? Some of us wrote whole paragraphs and still lost marks.

**Tutor:** Length is not the currency — MOVES are. Interpretation answers are paid one mark per move: the stated comparison, the verdict, the cause, the recommendation — typically two to four marks, so two to four crisp sentences. A paragraph can contain zero moves: "the percentage decreased which is not good for the business and should be improved" restates, judges vaguely, explains nothing and recommends nothing concrete — one mark at best, however long it runs. Conversely, four short lines can bank everything: fell from thirty-six to thirty-three — unfavourable, three rand less per hundred to work with — consistent with the clearance sale in the scenario — count stock against the records and review the discount depth. Write the four moves as four visible lines and you make the marker's job effortless, which is always the winning strategy. And one discipline of honesty: judge from the comparison given, not from optimism — if the number worsened, say worsened. Analysis is not marketing; its whole value is that it tells the truth in proportions.
