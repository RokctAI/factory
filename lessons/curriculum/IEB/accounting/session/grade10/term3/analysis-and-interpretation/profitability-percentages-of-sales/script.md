# Part 1 — Expert

The statements are built; now they must be made to TALK. Analysis converts absolute figures into percentages and ratios that can be compared — against last year, against the pricing policy, against other businesses — and interpretation turns those comparisons into meaningful comment. Today, the profitability family: five percentages, all anchored on the statement of comprehensive income, every one waiting on your formula summary. The calculations are short; the marks gather around choosing the right formula and saying something intelligent about the answer.

## Subtopic: Making the Numbers Talk

Why can the statements not speak for themselves? Because absolute figures carry no verdict. A net profit of R10 500 — good or bad? For a pavement stall, magnificent; for a supermarket chain, a disaster. A figure only gains meaning against a BASE, and profitability analysis chooses the most natural base in trading: SALES. Every profitability percentage asks the same shaped question: out of every hundred rand of sales, how many rand ended up as this?

The toolkit: gross profit on sales; gross profit on cost of sales; net profit on sales; operating expenses on sales; operating profit on sales. Each is the named figure, divided by sales — except the second, divided by cost of sales — times one hundred. The figures themselves come from the year you built at Zanele's Trading: sales R60 000; gross profit R20 000; operating expenses R15 200; operating profit R12 000; net profit R10 500. The three-layer statement was the planting; analysis is the harvest.

And the discipline that has held since day one: your formula summary GIVES you every formula — none needs memorising. The skills being tested are selection — which formula answers this question; substitution — the right figures into the right places; and interpretation. Marks are lost not by forgetting formulas but by feeding a wrong figure into a remembered one: operating profit where net profit belongs, cost of sales where sales belongs. Label what you substitute, every single time.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: why percentages beat absolutes, the sales base, and the formula summary's real role.

## Subtopic: The Two Gross Profit Percentages

The first pair of tools measures the trading layer — and it is deliberately a pair, because the same gross profit is read against two different bases.

GROSS PROFIT ON SALES: 20 000 over 60 000, times one hundred — thirty-three comma three percent. Meaning: of every hundred rand that crossed the counter, thirty-three rand thirty was margin. This is the margin the business LIVES on — every operating expense must be paid out of that thirty-three.

GROSS PROFIT ON COST OF SALES: 20 000 over 40 000, times one hundred — fifty percent. Recognise it: this IS the mark-up — and there stands Zanele's pricing policy, fifty percent on cost, confirmed by the year's actual trading. The two percentages are one profit seen from the two ends of the counter: on cost, it is the mark-up applied; on sales, the margin received.

Now the interpretive power: suppose the policy says fifty percent on cost, but the achieved percentage computes to forty-four. That gap is a FINDING — and its causes form a short, learnable list: goods sold below full price in seasonal sales or markdowns; stock lost, broken or stolen after being costed; recording errors; theft of takings. The percentage cannot name the culprit, but it raises the alarm and sizes the leak — analysis pointing at exactly the doors the control lessons taught you to watch. The achieved-versus-policy comparison is the most heavily tested interpretation at this level, so practise reading the gap.

Stop for this section's questions now — both gross percentages: compute, name the bases, and read a gap against the policy.

## Subtopic: The Operating Family and Net Profit on Sales

Three more percentages complete the family, and together they retell the three-layer statement in per-hundred form.

OPERATING EXPENSES ON SALES: 15 200 over 60 000 — twenty-five comma three percent. Of every hundred rand of sales, about twenty-five rand went to running the shop. This is the cost-burden gauge: track it across years — a rising percentage means expenses are growing FASTER than sales, the operating leak the three-floor lesson warned about, and the itemised expense list identifies which line is swelling.

OPERATING PROFIT ON SALES: 12 000 over 60 000 — twenty percent. What the operations kept from every hundred, after the running costs. Note the arithmetic kinship: the margin percentage, plus the rent income's contribution, minus the expense percentage, lands exactly here — the layers stay additive in percentage form, which makes the family self-checking.

NET PROFIT ON SALES: 10 500 over 60 000 — seventeen comma five percent. The final keep-rate: of every hundred rand of sales, seventeen rand fifty survived everything — the cost of the goods, the running costs, and the loan's interest. The distance between operating and net — twenty down to seventeen comma five — is the financing bite, isolated: two and a half rand of every hundred going to the lender.

Read the five as one dashboard: mark-up fifty achieved; margin thirty-three comma three; expenses twenty-five comma three; operations keep twenty; owner keeps seventeen comma five. One glance now tells the whole earning story in proportions — and proportions, unlike rand figures, can be compared with any year and any business, which is the entire point of the exercise.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: compute each percentage, then read the dashboard — where each hundred rand went, layer by layer.

## Subtopic: Interpreting — Comparing to Comment

Percentages alone earn calculation marks; INTERPRETATION marks demand comparison and comment — and comment has a learnable anatomy.

First, the comparison bases. Against LAST YEAR: is the percentage rising or falling — the trend. Against the POLICY: achieved mark-up versus intended — the control check. Against a SIMILAR BUSINESS: are we keeping less of each rand than the shop across the road — the competitive check. A percentage without a comparison is a fact without a meaning; a question signals its intended base by what it supplies — given last year's figures, compare years; given the policy, test the policy.

Then the comment's anatomy, four moves. STATE: name the figure with its comparison — "gross profit on sales fell from thirty-six to thirty-three comma three percent." JUDGE: favourable or unfavourable — "a deterioration." EXPLAIN: offer the likely cause from the scenario — "consistent with the storm-damaged stock written down, or the winter markdowns mentioned." RECOMMEND: one practical action — "count the stock against the records; review the markdown depth." State, judge, explain, recommend — four sentences, full marks. Learners who only restate the numbers — "it went down" — collect the first quarter of the marks and abandon the rest.

Last discipline: respect what each percentage CAN say. Gross percentages speak about pricing and stock control — never about expenses. The expense percentage speaks about cost management — never about pricing. Diagnose from the right gauge, and when a scenario shows the margin healthy but the net thin, say where the leak is NOT before saying where it is: pricing intact, problem downstream. That is reading like an accountant.

The final questions of this part are with you now: full interpretations — compare, then comment in the four-move anatomy, from the correct gauge.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same five tools from behind the counter — because every shopkeeper already runs this analysis in their head: it is the hundred-rand question, asked five ways.

## Subtopic: Out of Every Hundred Rand

Forget the year's totals for a minute. Take ONE hundred-rand note out of the till — and follow it as it breaks up.

A customer hands over R100 for goods. First, the goods themselves must be replaced: sixty-six rand seventy goes straight back into buying stock — the cost of the goods under our fifty percent mark-up. Thirty-three rand thirty remains: the margin — the money the shop actually gets to work with from every hundred. Out of that, the running of the shop takes its share: wages, electricity, insurance, the stock that went missing — about twenty-five rand thirty of every hundred in our year, softened a little by the tenant's rent topping up the jar. The loan's interest nibbles its two rand fifty. When every taker has taken, seventeen rand fifty of the original hundred remains — the owner's keep, from every single hundred that crossed the counter.

THAT journey — a hundred in, sixty-seven replaced, thirty-three margin, twenty-five running, seventeen fifty kept — is the entire profitability analysis. The five official percentages are simply the checkpoint names along the note's journey. And why per hundred instead of the year's totals? Because per-hundred travels: it can stand next to last year when sales were different, next to the bigger shop in town, next to any business anywhere. Rand totals are trapped in their own year; percentages are portable.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the hundred-rand journey are coming to you right now: where the note breaks, and why per-hundred beats totals.

## Subtopic: The Health Check at the Clinic

Think of analysis as the shop's clinic visit — because that is honestly what it is: vital signs, compared, and read together.

One number alone means nothing at the clinic. "Temperature thirty-seven" — fine or fever? You know only by comparing: against the normal range, against YOUR last visit. The shop's percentages work identically. Margin thirty-three percent — healthy? Compare: last year it stood at thirty-six — something has slipped. The policy says the mark-up should deliver fifty on cost — this year's books achieve fifty: the pricing is being followed. The shop across the road keeps nineteen of every hundred and we keep seventeen fifty — close, but worth asking why.

And like a good nurse, never read one vital alone — read the SET, because the pattern points to the organ. Margin fine but the owner's keep thin? The problem is not pricing — examine the running costs: some expense line is swelling. Margin down but expenses steady? The leak is upstream — markdowns, missing stock, prices quietly slipping. Everything proportionally fine but the keep still small in rand? Then the shop is healthy but small — a growth question, not a sickness.

The percentages are the thermometer and the blood pressure cuff of a business. They treat nothing — but they tell you which organ to examine, and they catch sickness while it is still curable. That is why the bank reads them before lending, and why an owner should read them monthly, not yearly.

Your questions for this part are up now: read sets of vitals — compare, spot the pattern, and name the organ to examine.

## Subtopic: Saying Something That Means Something

Last skill — and it is the one that separates the marks: after calculating, you must SAY something, and "it went down" is not saying something.

Here is the four-line recipe, in home language. Line one, WHAT: "The margin fell from thirty-six to thirty-three rand out of every hundred." Line two, SO: "That is bad news — the shop works with nearly three rand less from every hundred it takes." Line three, WHY, from the story: "It fits the flood-damaged stock sold at cost, or the clearance sale the scenario mentions." Line four, NOW WHAT: "Count the stock against the books this week; reconsider how deep the clearance discounts run."

What, so, why, now what. Four short lines and the comment is complete — a number, a verdict, a cause, an action. Practise the recipe until it is automatic, because interpretation answers are marked move by move against exactly that anatomy, and most learners stall after line one.

One warning about line three: the WHY must come from the SCENARIO, not from imagination. If the question mentions a clearance sale, cite the clearance; if it mentions a burglary, cite stock loss. Analysis is detective work with supplied clues — the percentages say where to look, the scenario says what was there to find, and your comment connects the two.

And the deepest habit of all: match the gauge to the question. Asked about pricing — the gross percentages. About cost control — expenses on sales. About the whole year's keep — net on sales. Each of the five gauges answers its own question, and answering from the wrong gauge is the interpretation error markers meet most often.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: full four-line comments, gauges matched to questions, causes taken from the story. Next lesson, the other half of analysis: can the shop pay its bills — liquidity, solvency, and the owner's return.
