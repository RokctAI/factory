# Part 1 — Expert

Profitability measured how well the business EARNS; this session's ratios measure whether it survives. Liquidity asks: when the year's bills arrive, can they be paid? Solvency asks: do all the assets, taken together, cover all the debts? And return on owner's equity asks the investor's question: did the owner's money earn its keep here, or would it have done better in the bank? Four formulas — current ratio, acid-test ratio, solvency ratio, return on owner's equity — every one on your formula summary, every one fed straight from the statement of financial position you assembled, with one figure borrowed from its companion statement. The working rhythm never changes: calculate, compare, comment.

## Subtopic: Can the Bills Be Paid? — the Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity is decided entirely inside the current sections of the statement — the fast-moving piles. The CURRENT RATIO: current assets to current liabilities, written as something-to-one. For Zanele's Trading: current assets R44 400 — inventories 11 100, receivables 8 100, cash 25 200 — set against current liabilities of R11 100. Divide: exactly four. Four to one. Read it plainly: for every rand of debt due within the coming year, four rand of assets are on their way to becoming cash to meet it.

The current ratio has one blind spot, though: it treats STOCK as though it were nearly money — and stock still has to find a buyer, at a decent price, in time. So the ACID-TEST RATIO asks the same question with the inventories removed: current assets minus inventories, to current liabilities. Here: 44 400 less 11 100 — R33 300 — against R11 100: three to one. Even if not a single tin left the shelf, the debtors and the cash alone would cover the year's debts three times over.

Hold the guideline norms lightly — they are GUIDES, never laws: roughly 2 to 1 for the current ratio, roughly 1 to 1 for the acid test. Ours sit comfortably above both — safe, no argument; but the reading that earns full marks is two-sided: a very high ratio is not automatically excellent. Twenty-four thousand five hundred rand dozing in the bank account is money doing nothing — not buying stock that could earn the fifty percent mark-up, not settling the loan that charges ten percent. Liquidity buys safety; EXCESS liquidity quietly spends opportunity. A comment can score in either direction.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: both ratios expressed as x-to-1, the reason stock gets stripped out, and the two-sided reading of a generous ratio.

## Subtopic: Solvency — the Total Question

Liquidity looks one year ahead; SOLVENCY interrogates the whole balance sheet: if everything owned were weighed against everything owed — long-term and short-term together — would the assets cover the debts? The ratio: TOTAL assets to TOTAL liabilities. For our business: R55 600 of assets against R27 600 of liabilities — the loan at its grown 16 500 plus the payables' 11 100. Divide: two to one, near enough. For every rand owed to anyone, on any timeline, two rand of assets stand behind the promise.

The line that matters is one to one. Above it, the business is solvent — in the last resort, every obligation could be met. Below it, INSOLVENT — the debts exceed the assets, and continuing to trade in that condition carries legal consequences: the creditors' money is already partly gone, and accepting more credit becomes reckless towards them. That is why solvency is the first number a banker checks and the auditor's going-concern anchor: carrying assets at their useful values and planning a future both PRESUME the business can ultimately pay its way — a failing solvency ratio shakes the foundation under the entire set of statements.

Keep the two families strictly separate, because questions are built to bait the confusion. Liquidity is the SHORT game — current items only, this year's bills against this year's resources. Solvency is the WHOLE game — everything against everything. A business can be solvent yet illiquid — heavy with equipment, unable to find Friday's wages; or liquid yet sliding towards insolvency — cash in the till today while total debts quietly outgrow total assets. Two gauges, two different questions; identify the right one before you comment.

Stop for this section's questions now — the solvency ratio, the one-to-one boundary, and scenarios sorted into the correct gauge.

## Subtopic: Return on Owner's Equity — Was It Worth It?

Zanele left R22 000 standing in the business at the start of the year, watched the stake grow to R28 000, and drew R4 500 along the way. The closing question of the term's analysis belongs to her as an investor: what rate did that money earn — and could it have earned more somewhere safer?

RETURN ON OWNER'S EQUITY: net profit over AVERAGE owner's equity, times one hundred. Why the average? Because the equity moved during the year — profit flowing in, drawings flowing out — so the honest base is the mean of the opening and closing figures: 22 000 plus 28 000, over two — R25 000. The return: 10 500 over 25 000, times one hundred — forty-two percent.

Now the comparison that gives the number its meaning, because forty-two percent floating alone says nothing. The alternative home for the money: a fixed deposit at, say, seven percent — promised, and nearly riskless. The business returned forty-two — six times the safe rate. Worth it? On the arithmetic, overwhelmingly yes — BUT the complete answer weighs RISK: the deposit's seven percent is guaranteed in advance; the business's forty-two was earned through effort, could have been a loss, and carries no promise of repeating. The principle to state every time: a business return SHOULD beat the safe alternative comfortably, because the owner shoulders the risk and does the work — a return that merely matches the bank's is a warning that the risk is going unpaid.

And notice the architecture: this one ratio joins the two statements in a single fraction — profit from the statement of comprehensive income over equity from the statement of financial position. The term's analysis, closing its own circle.

## Subtopic: The Full Dashboard and Technique

Assemble the term's complete instrument panel. From the income side: mark-up achieved fifty; margin thirty-three comma three; expenses twenty-five comma three; operating keep twenty; net keep seventeen comma five — the earning story. From the statement of financial position: current ratio four to one; acid test three to one; solvency two to one — the safety story. Bridging both: return on equity forty-two percent — the investor's verdict. Nine gauges, one health check.

Technique, five habits. One: write ratios AS ratios — "4 : 1", something to one — and percentages as percentages; the format itself carries marks. Two: show the labelled substitution — "44 400 / 11 100" beneath the named formula — because the route earns credit even when the arithmetic stumbles. Three: use the AVERAGE equity for the return, and say that you did; the unaveraged base is the classic dropped mark. Four: comment with the four moves — state with a comparison, judge, explain from the scenario, recommend — and remember that comments run both ways: too low is dangerous, too high can be idle. Five: match the gauge to the question — bills of the coming months, liquidity; long-term survival, solvency; the owner's decision to stay invested, return on equity — and never answer a liquidity question from a profitability gauge.

One closing perspective: these ratios exist because financial statements are read by people DECIDING things — a bank deciding whether to lend, an owner deciding whether to stay, a supplier deciding whether to grant credit. Every ratio is a decision, turned into a number. Learn them that way and the formula summary stops being a list and becomes a menu of questions you already know how to answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three questions from the kitchen table — because every household already runs exactly these checks: the month-end reckoning, the what-if-everything-collapsed count, and the was-it-worth-it question.

## Subtopic: The Month-End Test

Every family knows the month-end test. The bills queue at the door — rent, electricity, the school transport money. On the table sit this month's incoming money, the tins stacked in the cupboard, and the money your uncle still owes you. One question: can what is coming in cover what is knocking?

That is the current ratio — the business version simply wears bigger words. Knocking within the year at Zanele's Trading: R11 100. On the table within the year: R44 400 — stock, customers' debts, and cash. Four rand ready for every one rand knocking. The family sleeps easy.

But sharpen the question, the way a careful gogo does: "the cupboard counts, yes — but I cannot pay the rent with tins of beans." Strip the stock out — tins must first find a buyer — and ask again using only the near-cash things: R33 300 against R11 100. Three to one. That is the acid test — the month-end test with the hard-to-sell things set aside, the honest version of "could we still pay if nothing sold?"

And one more household truth worth marks: too much sitting money becomes its own small problem. If R24 500 naps in the bank while the loan upstairs charges its ten percent, safety has quietly turned into waste. A healthy household keeps ENOUGH within reach — and sends the rest out to work.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the month-end test are coming to you right now: both versions of the ratio, and the too-much-of-a-good-thing reading.

## Subtopic: If Everything Went Wrong Tomorrow

Now the larger, quieter question families ask in hard seasons: if we had to settle EVERYTHING — every debt, long and short — by selling everything we own, would there be enough?

Count both sides for the shop. Everything owned: the equipment, the stock on the shelves, the money owed to us, the cash — R55 600. Everything owed to anyone, on any timeline: the bank's loan standing at its grown R16 500, plus the suppliers, the electricity account, the tenant's early month — R27 600. Divide: two rand owned for every rand owed. That is solvency — and while the answer sits above one-to-one, the hardest question still gets a yes: in the end, everyone could be paid.

Feel the difference between the two checks. Month-end liquidity is the SPRINT: this year's bills against this year's resources. Solvency is the WHOLE RACE: everything against everything. And the two can disagree — which is exactly why both exist. A family can own a fine house and still miss Friday's rent money: solvent, but illiquid. Another family can hold cash this month while their total debts quietly grow past everything they own: liquid, and sinking. Neither check can answer the other's question.

Below one-to-one the word is insolvent — debts larger than everything owned — and a business trading in that state is spending money that already belongs to its creditors. That is why the bank reads this number FIRST: before asking whether you will pay on time, it asks whether, in the end, you could pay at all.

## Subtopic: Was It Worth the Owner's While?

Last question — the one the owner asks alone when the year closes: "My twenty-two thousand and its growth lived inside this shop all year. Was that the best home for it?"

Run the household version. The money standing in the shop, averaged across the year: R25 000. What it produced: R10 500 of profit. That is forty-two rand earned for every hundred invested — forty-two percent. Now the comparison every adult quietly makes: the bank down the road pays seven percent on a fixed deposit — guaranteed, no dawn deliveries, no stock counts, no worry. The shop paid six times that.

So — worth it? On the numbers, absolutely. But finish the thought like an accountant, because this is where the marks and the wisdom both live: the bank's seven is PROMISED; the shop's forty-two was EARNED — with Zanele's labour, stress and risk. A bad year could have paid nothing, or eaten into the capital itself. That is why a business must beat the bank comfortably, not narrowly: the extra return is the wage of risk. If a shop returned eight percent while the deposit offered seven, the owner would be working a full year, carrying every risk, for one extra rand per hundred — and the honest advice would be: rethink.

And notice what this single ratio quietly achieved: it joined the two statements — the year's profit from the story page, the owner's stake from the photograph — into one number that answers a life decision: stay invested, or step away.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: compute the return on the average stake, set it beside the safe alternative, and advise the owner honestly — numbers first, risk weighed, decision stated. That completes the term's analysis: nine gauges, and you can now read every one.
