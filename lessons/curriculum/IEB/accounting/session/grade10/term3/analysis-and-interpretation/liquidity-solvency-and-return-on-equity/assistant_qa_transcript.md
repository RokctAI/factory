**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: our ratios landed at four-to-one and three-to-one — both way above the norms of two and one. If real businesses can sit that far from the norms, what is a norm actually FOR?

**Tutor:** A norm is the centre of a healthy range, not a target to hit — and the skill is knowing what each SIDE of the range means. Below the norm, the classic danger: bills can arrive faster than assets turn to cash — creditors unpaid, the credit record damaged, deliveries suspended. Above it, the quieter cost: resources standing idle. Our four-to-one says no bill will go unpaid this year — excellent — but it also says some twenty-four thousand rand of cash is dozing while the loan charges ten percent and the shelves could carry stock earning a fifty percent mark-up. So the norm's real job is to be the REFERENCE POINT of your comment: state where the business sits relative to it, then name the consequence of that side — danger below, idleness above. A learner who writes "above the norm, therefore good" has read half the gauge. The full reading is: safe, and possibly too comfortable — recommend putting the excess to work: reduce the loan, or invest in stock.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: why does return on equity demand the AVERAGE equity? The year closed at R28 000 — why not simply divide by that?

**Tutor:** Because the profit was earned by the money as it stood THROUGH the year, not as it stood at the final midnight. The equity opened at R22 000 and only climbed to R28 000 as profit gathered — and part of that closing figure IS the very profit being measured, which makes the closing base partly circular: you would be dividing the profit by itself. The average of opening and closing — R25 000 — approximates the stake actually at work across the months, so the rate is honest. Feel it in the numbers: on the closing figure the return looks like thirty-seven and a half percent; on the opening, nearly forty-eight; the average lands at forty-two, between them, and defensible. And practically: the marker looks for the "(opening + closing) ÷ 2" working, and the unaveraged base is THE classic lost mark of this ratio. Write the average line first, every time.

***

**Thandi:** This one caused an argument: how can a business be SOLVENT and still collapse? If the assets cover every debt, what actually kills it?

**Tutor:** Timing kills it — solvency is a store of value, but bills are settled with CASH, on dates. Picture the solvent-but-illiquid shop: property and equipment worth double its debts, but wages fall due on Friday and the bank account is dry. Selling equipment takes weeks, and forced sales fetch thin prices; creditors will not wait for an auction. Miss the wages and the staff walk; miss the suppliers and deliveries stop; miss the rent and the doors close — the business dies of thirst beside a full dam it cannot drink from in time. That is exactly why BOTH gauge families exist and why the bank reads both: solvency says the last-resort promise is covered; liquidity says the ordinary Friday is covered. And the rescue is usually structural: convert slow assets into working cash — sell surplus equipment properly, raise a loan against the property — before the timing gap becomes a default. Value that cannot reach the door in time pays no bills.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: forty-two percent sounds unreal next to the bank's seven. If businesses really earn that, why does anyone keep money in a fixed deposit at all?

**Tutor:** Because the two numbers are different SPECIES, and that difference is the whole theory of investing. The deposit's seven is contractual: promised in advance, paid even in a terrible year, demanding nothing from you. The shop's forty-two is residual: whatever remains after every risk has taken its chance — computed afterwards, promised never. Behind our forty-two stood a year of Zanele's unpaid hours, stock that could have spoiled, debtors who could have defaulted, a burglary that happened not to happen — and a genuine possibility of zero, or of the capital itself shrinking. Plenty of small businesses earn less than the deposit rate; some lose everything. So the deposit buys certainty and sleep; the business buys a CHANCE at multiples, priced in labour and risk. The principle for your exams and your life: the extra return above the safe rate is the wage of risk — demand it whenever you carry risk, and distrust anything advertising deposit-safety with business-sized returns. When those two promises appear together, the one that is usually false is the safety.
