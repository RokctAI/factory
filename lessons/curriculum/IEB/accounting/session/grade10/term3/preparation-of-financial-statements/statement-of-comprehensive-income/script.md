# Part 1 — Expert

The final accounts computed the profit inside the ledger; now the world outside the ledger must be told. The financial statements are accounting's formal reports, and the first of them — the Statement of Comprehensive Income — presents the year's earning story in a prescribed format every accountant in the country recognises. Today: the format line by line, the adjustments feeding it with workings shown, and the reading of what the finished statement says. In this topic, format carries marks as surely as arithmetic does.

## Subtopic: From Final Accounts to Formal Statement

First, the sorting that decides what belongs here. Every account in the ledger is one of two kinds. NOMINAL accounts — incomes and expenses — measure the year's FLOWS: sales, rent income, wages, depreciation. BALANCE SHEET accounts — assets, liabilities, capital — measure the POSITION at a moment. The Statement of Comprehensive Income is built exclusively from the nominal accounts; the balance sheet accounts wait for next lesson's statement. Sorting an account into the wrong statement is the topic's foundational error — so interrogate every item: does it measure the year's flow, or the year-end's holding?

The statement is the Trading and Profit and Loss accounts, re-dressed for the public. Same figures, same logic — gross profit, then net profit — but presented as a readable report rather than ledger accounts, for readers who may never open a ledger: the owner, the bank weighing a loan, a prospective buyer.

And the heading carries meaning: "Statement of Comprehensive Income of Zanele's Trading FOR THE YEAR ENDED 30 June." For the year ended — a period, not a moment — because flows accumulate across time. Contrast next lesson's statement, drawn "AT 30 June": a position, photographed at midnight. The two prepositions, FOR and AT, are the two kinds of account written straight into the headings — and a wrong or incomplete heading costs marks, so write it in full, every time.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: nominal against balance sheet, flow against position, and the meaning inside the headings. Sort before you build.

## Subtopic: The Format, Line by Line

Now the prescribed shape, with the year's figures flowing through it.

Line one: SALES — the year's turnover, less any debtors allowances: R61 500 earned at the till and on credit, minus R1 500 of allowances — R60 000 net. Line two: COST OF SALES, in brackets as a subtraction: R40 000. Line three, the first ruled result: GROSS PROFIT — R20 000, the trading result, confirming the fifty percent mark-up on cost.

Then OTHER OPERATING INCOME: earnings outside the buying-and-selling core — here, Rent Income at its earned R7 200. Adding it to gross profit gives GROSS OPERATING INCOME: R27 200 — everything the operations brought in.

Then the block that always earns its space: OPERATING EXPENSES, listed by name, each at its ADJUSTED figure, all in brackets: Salaries and Wages R7 800; Water and Electricity R2 940; Insurance R600; Stationery R450; Bank Charges R420; Trading Stock Deficit R190; Depreciation R2 800. Total: R15 200. Subtracting yields OPERATING PROFIT: R12 000 — the profit the operations themselves generated, before any financing.

Last, the financing tail: INTEREST INCOME added, if any — none this year; then INTEREST EXPENSE subtracted — the R1 500 on the loan. The final ruled line: NET PROFIT FOR THE YEAR — R10 500. The order is fixed: trading, then operating, then financing — three layers, each with its own subtotal, and every subtotal a mark.

Stop for this section's questions now — the skeleton in order: which line, which layer, which subtotal, and the brackets where subtraction lives.

## Subtopic: Adjustments With Workings

The statement's figures are the ADJUSTED ones — and the marker wants to see HOW each was reached. The convention: workings in brackets beside the line, or in a workings column, so the route can be credited even where a slip spoils the destination.

Walk the year's adjustments into their lines. Water and electricity: the ledger held R2 640 paid — eleven months; June's winter account of R300 was still owing. Working: 2 640 plus 300 — R2 940 on the statement. Insurance: R1 800 paid on the first of March for a full year of cover; eight months belong to next year. Working: 1 800 minus 1 200 — R600. Rent Income: eleven months received. Working: 6 600 plus 600 — R7 200 among other operating income. Depreciation: equipment of R14 000 at twenty percent on cost — R2 800, straight from the method and the rate. Interest on the loan, capitalised: ten percent of R15 000 — R1 500, sitting in the financing tail, not among operating expenses.

Two placements the markers watch. The trading stock DEFICIT is an operating expense — the cost of running imperfectly — while a SURPLUS would join other operating income. And interest — in both directions — stays OUT of the operating block: operating profit must measure the operations alone, so financing costs and financing earnings frame it from below. Mixing interest into operating expenses is the format error that costs the operating-profit subtotal its mark.

One discipline ties it together: every adjustment from the year-end lessons lands on exactly ONE line of this statement, and its balance-sheet twin lands in next lesson's statement. The pair you learned — statement correction plus balance-sheet item — is about to become two documents. Keep both halves alive.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — figures with their workings: build each line as ledger figure, adjustment, statement figure.

## Subtopic: Reading the Statement

A statement exists to be read; end by reading ours as its three audiences do.

The owner reads the layers. Gross profit R20 000 on sales of R60 000: the pricing policy delivered — every hundred rand of sales carried thirty-three rand of margin. Operating profit R12 000: the operations kept twenty rand of every hundred after the running costs — and the gap between thirty-three and twenty is the expense burden, itemised above for inspection. Net profit R10 500: the financing cost of the loan took its bite. Three layers, three diagnoses — pricing, operations, financing — each isolated by the format. That is WHY the format is prescribed: the layers answer different questions, and a mixed-up statement answers none of them.

The bank reads the same layers for a different verdict: can this business carry more debt? Operating profit against interest expense — R12 000 covering R1 500 — says the operations fund the borrowing eight times over. Comfortable.

And the accountant reads it for integrity: does every line trace to an adjusted ledger figure, every working to a calculation, every subtotal to the lines above it? The statement is the shop window of the whole year's bookkeeping — and next term, the analysis topic will turn these readings into named ratios and percentages. The layers you built today are the raw material of every one of them.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the statement read as owner, banker and accountant — figures, layers and what each layer answers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same statement from the stoep — because this document is really just the shop telling its year as a story, one page, top to bottom, with three pauses for breath.

## Subtopic: The Year's Story on One Page

All year the books have been a thick pile: journals, ledgers, lists, corrections. Now imagine Zanele's mother visits and asks the only question that matters: "So — how was the year?" You cannot hand her the pile. You need ONE page that tells the whole story in an order any reader can follow.

That page is the Statement of Comprehensive Income, and its story has a fixed plot. It opens with what the shop SOLD — the year's big number. It subtracts what those goods had COST — and pauses for the first breath: the trading profit, the reward for buying low and selling high. Then other earnings join — the storeroom tenant's rent — and the running costs file through, each by name — wages, electricity, insurance — and the page pauses again: the operating profit, what the shop's actual work earned. Last, the money-borrowing costs step up — the loan's interest — and the story ends on its final line: the net profit. The year, in one number, at the bottom of one page.

And notice the title's small print: "for the YEAR ENDED". This page tells a year's JOURNEY — everything that flowed. Next lesson's page will be a PHOTOGRAPH — what the shop holds at one midnight. Journey pages and photo pages never mix their contents; that is the first rule of statement-building.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the story are coming to you right now: the plot's order, the pauses, and journey against photograph.

## Subtopic: Three Floors of Profit

Picture the statement as a building with three floors, and the year's money riding the lift down.

Top floor: the TRADING floor. Only two things live here — what customers paid, and what the goods had cost the shop. The difference is the trading profit: R20 000. This floor answers one question only: is the BUYING AND SELLING itself healthy? If the mark-up is too thin, you see it here, before anything else muddies the view.

Middle floor: the RUNNING floor. The trading profit rides down and meets real life: the rent income joins it, then the wages, the electricity, the insurance, the missing stock — every cost of keeping the doors open. What survives is the operating profit: R12 000. This floor's question: is the SHOP AS A MACHINE healthy? A shop can price perfectly upstairs and still leak everything here on costs.

Ground floor: the BORROWING floor. Last stop: the loan's interest — R1 500 — takes its share, and what walks out the front door is the net profit: R10 500. This floor's question: after paying for the money it borrowed, what did the business truly keep?

Why three floors instead of one big room? Because when something goes wrong, the floors tell you WHERE. Thin trading profit — a pricing problem. Trading fine but operating thin — a cost problem. Operating fine but net thin — a debt problem. One-room statements hide the leak; the three-floor format points at it.

Your questions for this part are up now: ride the lift — each floor's residents, each floor's question, and where a given leak would show.

## Subtopic: Where Every Adjustment Lands

Last piece: the adjustments you sweated over — watch each one land on this page, because that is what they were FOR.

June's unpaid electricity account? It lands in the Water and Electricity line: 2 640 plus the owing 300 — the statement charges the full year the year used. The insurance paid ahead? Lands as 1 800 minus 1 200 — only the used third burdens this year's story. The tenant's missing month? Rent Income shows 6 600 plus 600 — the year earned twelve months whether or not twelve arrived. The steady wearing of the equipment? Depreciation, R2 800, a running cost like any other — the year used a year of the machine. And the loan's quiet growth? Interest, R1 500 — but note WHERE: on the ground floor, with the borrowing, never among the running costs. Interest is the price of money, not the price of trading.

Here is the habit that turns this into marks: write the little sum NEXT to the line — "(2 640 + 300)" — every time. That bracket is you showing the marker the road, and the road is worth marks even when a pothole catches the final number. An unexplained figure earns only itself; an explained one earns its journey.

Step back and see what you have: a year of bookkeeping, adjusted to truth, told as a story with three pauses, every number traceable. Anyone — mother, banker, buyer — can read the shop's year in a minute. That is the whole point of accounting, honestly: months of careful work so that one page can be TRUSTED.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: place each adjustment on its line, bracket its working, and name the floor it lands on. Next lesson: the photograph — what the shop HOLDS at midnight, dressed in its own formal page.
