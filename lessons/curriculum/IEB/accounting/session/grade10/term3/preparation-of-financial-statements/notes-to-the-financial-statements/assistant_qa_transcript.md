**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the notes just repeat what we already calculated, why would an exam spend marks on them? It feels like copying.

**Tutor:** The calculation was done once, yes — but the notes test something the statement cannot: whether you know each figure's ANATOMY. The statement asks "what is the total?"; the note asks "who exactly are the members, and does their sum survive inspection?" Plenty of learners can carry R8 100 onto a statement line; fewer can name its three members with their amounts, and fewer still notice when a scenario quietly moves one member — say the prepaid insurance becomes accrued — and the family total must change. The notes are also where the cross-reference habit itself gets tested: a note totalling R8 000 under a line claiming R8 100 is a contradiction you are expected to catch. Repetition of a figure is not repetition of understanding — the note is the figure, opened for surgery.

***

**Thandi:** Next: in the fixed assets note, why do we show cost AND accumulated depreciation at the end, when the statement only wants the carrying value? Isn't the one number enough?

**Tutor:** The one number tells you where the asset stands; the pair tells you where it is in its LIFE — and readers price that difference. Two businesses can both show equipment at a carrying value of R11 200: one bought at R14 000 this year and barely worn; the other bought at R70 000 long ago and nearly written off. Same statement line, utterly different futures — the first has years of service ahead; the second is a replacement bill about to arrive. Cost and accumulated side by side let any reader compute the asset's age in wear, anticipate replacement spending, and check the depreciation policy is actually being applied. The carrying value is the answer; the pair is the evidence — and notes exist to carry evidence.

***

**Thandi:** This one's practical: the interest note showed the loan growing from R15 000 to R16 500 with no payment. If the bank statement never showed interest leaving, how would a bookkeeper even KNOW to record anything?

**Tutor:** By reading the documents that are not the bank statement — which is precisely the discipline this adjustment teaches. The loan agreement states the rate: ten percent on fifteen thousand is R1 500 owed for the year's borrowing, contract-certain whether or not cash moved. The lender's loan statement confirms it: closing balance R16 500. The bookkeeper's month-end and year-end routine must therefore include a document sweep beyond the bank: loan statements, lease agreements, insurance schedules — every contract that creates amounts without creating bank entries. The cash journals capture what moved; the sweep captures what accrued. A set of books built only from the bank statement records the business's cash and misses its obligations — and it is exactly the quiet, cashless items where understatement hides. No cash is a reason for MORE attention, not less.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the stokvel section was beautiful, but how does it actually get tested? What would a question even look like?

**Tutor:** It examines as comparison and principle, not as sentiment. Expect scenarios: a stokvel treasurer keeps a contribution register and announces a running total each month — identify the accounting concepts present. Answer with the architecture: detail records per member agreeing with a control total; regular reporting periods; transparency to stakeholders; documentary evidence per contribution — then name the formal cousins: control accounts and lists, reporting dates, disclosure. Or the reverse: what could a formal business learn from stokvel practice? Monthly member-facing accountability is a fine answer — many businesses report to their stakeholders far less often than a stokvel does. The marks sit in seeing one architecture in two dresses. And beyond marks: it is simply true, and worth knowing, that accountable record-keeping is not an import — it has deep local roots.
