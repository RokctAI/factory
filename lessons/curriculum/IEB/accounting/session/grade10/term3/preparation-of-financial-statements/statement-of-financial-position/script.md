# Part 1 — Expert

The Statement of Comprehensive Income told the year's journey; today its companion photographs the destination: the Statement of Financial Position AT 30 June — what the business owns, what it owes, and what the owner's stake stands at, the instant the year closes. This statement is the accounting equation, dressed formally — and its format of headings and sub-headings carries marks line for line. Today: the structure, both sides built from our year's figures, and the exact addresses where every adjustment's balance-sheet twin finally lands.

## Subtopic: The Photograph at Midnight

The heading first, because it declares the statement's nature: "Statement of Financial Position of Zanele's Trading AT 30 June." AT — a moment, not a period. Flows have finished; this page lists positions: balances that exist as the clock strikes, the same balances the post-closing trial balance proved level.

The skeleton is the oldest equation you know, rearranged for presentation: ASSETS on one side; EQUITY AND LIABILITIES on the other — A equals O plus L, with each side totalled, and the two totals AGREEING as the page's final act. The statement does not compute a profit; it presents a position — and its balance is not luck but inheritance: every double entry of the year, every adjustment, every closing transfer kept the equation level, and this page simply exhibits that levelness formally.

The prescribed sub-headings are the marks' skeleton. Under assets: NON-CURRENT ASSETS — held for years, not for sale in the ordinary course, our fixed assets; then CURRENT ASSETS — cash and everything expected to become cash within the year, arranged in three named families: inventories, trade and other receivables, cash and cash equivalents. Under the other side: OWNER'S EQUITY; then NON-CURRENT LIABILITIES — debts beyond a year, the loan; then CURRENT LIABILITIES — debts falling due within the year, the trade and other payables. Current against non-current is a TIME test — one year — applied to both sides, and misfiling across that line is the format error the marking grid punishes first.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: AT against FOR, the two sides, and the time test that sorts current from non-current.

## Subtopic: The Asset Side

Build the left side, top down, from our year.

NON-CURRENT ASSETS. Fixed assets appear at CARRYING VALUE: equipment that cost R14 000, less accumulated depreciation of R2 800 — R11 200. The statement shows the one figure; the detail — cost, accumulated, movements — lives in the fixed assets NOTE, which next lesson builds. The principle from the depreciation lesson holds: cost anchored, wear accumulated, carrying value presented.

CURRENT ASSETS, in the three families, conventionally from least to most liquid. First, INVENTORIES: trading stock at its counted R10 700, plus consumable stores on hand — the R400 of unused stationery — R11 100. Second, TRADE AND OTHER RECEIVABLES: trade debtors at R6 300 — the control account the lists reconciled — plus ACCRUED INCOME, the tenant's owed R600, plus PREPAID EXPENSES, the insurance's stored R1 200: R8 100. Read the family's name literally: trade receivables are the debtors; the OTHER receivables are the adjustment children — money owed to us and value paid ahead, both convertible into benefit within the year. Third, CASH AND CASH EQUIVALENTS: the bank balance of R24 500, petty cash R450 and the cash float R250 — R25 200.

Total assets: R11 200 plus R11 100 plus R8 100 plus R25 200 — R55 600. Hold that figure; the other side must meet it exactly.

Stop for this section's questions now — the asset side: each family's members, each figure's source, and the carrying-value presentation of the fixed assets.

## Subtopic: Equity and Liabilities

Now the funding side — who supplied the R55 600 of assets.

OWNER'S EQUITY first. The statement shows the closing figure; the movement that produced it belongs to the owner's equity NOTE: capital at the start of the year R22 000, PLUS the net profit for the year — R10 500, arriving from the other statement — LESS drawings of R4 500: closing equity R28 000. See the architecture: the two statements connect at exactly this joint — the income statement's bottom line is a LINE ITEM in the equity movement. That connection is the single most examined link in the topic.

NON-CURRENT LIABILITIES: the loan, at its CAPITALISED balance — R16 500, the original fifteen thousand grown by its unpaid interest. Beyond a year to repay: non-current. Were any portion repayable within the coming year, that portion would shift down to current liabilities — a refinement later grades formalise.

CURRENT LIABILITIES: TRADE AND OTHER PAYABLES — trade creditors R10 200, the control the creditors' list reconciled, plus ACCRUED EXPENSES — June's owed electricity, R300 — plus INCOME RECEIVED IN ADVANCE — the tenant's early month, R600: R11 100. Again the name read literally: trade payables are the creditors; the other payables are the adjustment children owed onward within the year.

Total equity and liabilities: R28 000 plus R16 500 plus R11 100 — R55 600. Agreement with the asset side — the equation exhibited, the photograph developed. If the totals disagree, the error hunt starts at the usual suspects: a missing adjustment twin, drawings left out of equity, the loan at its uncapitalised figure.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the funding side: equity's movement, the loan's balance, the payables' members, and the meeting of the totals.

## Subtopic: Where the Adjustments Landed, and Technique

Assemble the map the two statement lessons have been promising. Every adjustment's statement half landed last lesson; every balance-sheet twin has now found its address. Accrued expenses — current liabilities, inside trade and other payables. Prepaid expenses — current assets, inside trade and other receivables. Accrued income — the same receivables family. Income received in advance — the payables family. Consumable stores on hand — inventories. Depreciation — twice: the year's charge on the income statement, the accumulated total reducing fixed assets to carrying value here. Interest capitalised — the expense there, the swollen loan here. Seven adjustments, seven pairs, two pages — the bookkeeping year, fully filed.

Note discipline: several lines on this statement summarise detail held in NOTES — fixed assets, inventories, receivables, cash, equity, payables — and the statement line carries the note's number in a reference column. Next lesson builds the notes themselves; today's habit is the cross-reference: a marker must be able to travel from any statement line to its note and find the same figure at both ends.

Technique, four habits. One: heading in full, with AT. Two: sub-headings in prescribed order on both sides — non-current before current among assets; equity, then non-current, then current liabilities. Three: the one-year test decides every borderline filing, both sides. Four: brackets and workings beside any combined figure — receivables and payables especially — because the family lines are assembled, not copied, and the assembly is where the marks sit. And always, the final act: total both sides, and let them agree in writing.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the full map — every adjustment to its address, every line to its note, both totals to their meeting.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same photograph from the stoep — because this page answers the question every visitor actually asks about a shop: not "how was your year?" but "so what have you GOT?"

## Subtopic: The Midnight Photograph

Last lesson's page was a movie: the whole year's story, beginning to end. Tonight's page is a PHOTOGRAPH: the shop frozen at midnight on the last of June, everything it holds caught in the frame.

Walk through the frame. There is the equipment in the corner — not at what it cost, but at what it is still worth after its wearing: the honest R11 200. There is the stock on the shelves — the counted R10 700, plus the little parcel of unused stationery. There are the IOUs in the drawer — customers owing R6 300, the tenant's late month, the insurance bought ahead. And there is the money itself: the bank balance, the petty cash tin, the float in the till.

And on the other wall of the photograph: who FUNDED all of it. The owner's stake. The bank's loan — grown quietly by its own interest, remember. The suppliers waiting to be paid, June's electricity account still travelling, the tenant's early rent that the shop still owes back in occupation.

One frame, two walls: what the shop HOLDS, and who PROVIDED it. And the two walls must weigh exactly the same — because nothing in the shop appeared from nowhere. You have known that sentence since the very first lesson of the year: everything owned was funded by somebody. Tonight that old sentence finally gets its formal portrait.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the photograph are coming to you right now: movie against photo, and the two walls of the frame.

## Subtopic: Two Piles That Must Weigh the Same

Now sort the photograph the way the format demands — into piles by ONE question: how soon does this turn into money, or demand money?

The holding wall, top down. The LONG pile: things the shop keeps for years — the equipment. Nobody plans to sell it; it works. Then the FAST pile, in three pockets. Pocket one, things waiting to be sold: the stock, plus the stationery parcel — inventories. Pocket two, money on its way in: the customers' debts, the tenant's owed month, the insurance already paid for next year's cover — all arriving as benefit within the year. Pocket three, money already here: bank, the petty cash tin, the float. Slowest at the top, fastest at the bottom — the page pours toward cash.

The funding wall mirrors it. The owner's stake first — the deepest, most patient money in the building. Then the SLOW debt: the loan, years to run, standing at its grown R16 500. Then the FAST debts, one pocket: suppliers, the electricity account, the tenant's early month — all knocking within the year.

Why sort by speed? Because the shop's next twelve months live in the fast piles. Fast assets R44 400; fast debts R11 100. Any reader sees at a glance: what is coming due can be paid four times over from what is coming in. That single glance — fast pile against fast pile — is next term's famous "liquidity" analysis, already sitting in the format. The sorting is not decoration; it is the first analysis, done by layout.

Your questions for this part are up now: sort each item by the one question — how soon is it money? — and name its pile and pocket.

## Subtopic: The Owner's Jar, Opened and Closed

One line of the photograph deserves its own story: the owner's stake — R28 000. Where did that number come from? The jar was opened, filled, dipped into, and closed — and the statement's note tells it in three moves.

The jar OPENED the year holding R22 000 — last year's closing photograph, carried through midnight. Then the year's profit poured in: R10 500 — and pause on where that figure came from: it is the FINAL LINE of last lesson's movie, walking onto this page. The two statements are not neighbours; they are connected pipes — the journey's ending pours into the photograph's jar. Every marker tests that joint, because learners who memorise pages separately miss the pipe.

Then the dipping: all year, Zanele drew money and goods for the household — R4 500 of drawings. Not an expense of the shop — the shop did not burn that money earning; the owner simply took some of her own out early. So it never touched the profit story; it comes straight out of the jar. Opened at 22 000, plus 10 500 earned, minus 4 500 taken: closed at R28 000. That is the number the photograph shows, and the note behind it shows the three moves.

And a last look at the whole page: holding wall R55 600; funding wall — jar 28 000, slow loan 16 500, fast debts 11 100 — R55 600. Level. The year's first lesson said the equation can never break; three terms of transactions, adjustments and closings later, here it stands in its formal portrait — unbroken.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the jar's three moves, the pipe between the pages, and the two walls weighed. Next lesson we build the notes — the small print behind each line of tonight's photograph.
