# Part 1 — Expert

The adjustment family is now complete: stock deficits, consumable stores, depreciation, and the four timing adjustments with the capitalised interest. Today we finish the year-end machine itself: the stock SURPLUS, the correction of errors and omissions, the post-adjustment trial balance in its proper format, the final accounts run with everything in place — and the destination Grade 10 has not yet seen: the POST-CLOSING trial balance, the list that survives midnight and opens the new year.

## Subtopic: The Full Family — and the Surplus

Line up the adjustments you now command, because questions serve them mixed: trading stock deficit; consumable stores on hand; depreciation on both methods; accrued expenses; prepaid expenses; accrued income; income received in advance; interest capitalised. Eight tools, each a full double entry, each pairing an income-statement correction with a balance-sheet item.

One member remains: the TRADING STOCK SURPLUS — the deficit's rare mirror. The stocktake counts MORE than the books claim: the account says R10 700, the shelves hold R10 745. The books must rise to the fact — the count wins in both directions. Adjustment: debit Trading Stock R45, and credit Trading Stock Surplus, an INCOME account. How does a surplus even happen? An error somewhere behind — a sale recorded twice, a return never entered — or goods received and shelved without paperwork. The surplus is recognised only because the COUNT proves it; prudence never books hoped-for gains, but it does not refuse counted ones.

Note the asymmetry of feeling, though: a deficit is expected in small doses — theft and breakage are life; a surplus should make an accountant slightly suspicious, because stock does not breed. A surplus is booked, and then investigated: somewhere a record is wrong, and next month's routine should find it.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the full family sorted, and the surplus — its entry, its cause, and why it deserves a raised eyebrow.

## Subtopic: Corrections of Errors and Omissions

Year end is also when discovered errors must be put right — through the General Journal, with narrations, before the books may report. Three families cover the favourites.

Family one: the WRONG ACCOUNT, right amount, right side. Advertising costs of R320 were posted to Stationery. The trial balance never noticed — debits equalled credits throughout — but two expense accounts are lying to each other. Correction: debit Advertising R320, credit Stationery R320 — lift the amount out of the wrong pocket, place it in the right one. Total expenses unchanged; the DETAIL now true.

Family two: the OMISSION. An invoice — a credit sale to a debtor for R600 — was never entered anywhere. Both halves are missing equally, so again the trial balance stayed silent. Correction: enter it now, in full — debit Debtors Control R600, credit Sales R600, plus the cost-of-sales pair at our mark-up: debit Cost of Sales R400, credit Trading Stock R400. An omission is corrected by simply DOING the entry that was skipped.

Family three: the WRONG AMOUNT. Wages of R2 700 were entered as R2 070 — a transposition. The entry exists but is R630 short on both sides. Correction: a top-up entry for the difference — debit Wages R630, credit Bank R630. Never reverse and re-enter when a top-up will do; correct by the DIFFERENCE.

The discipline across all three: name what actually happened, name what should have happened, and journalise only the gap between them. And notice which guard caught each error — not the trial balance, which all three slipped past, but the reconciliations, the document checks, the comparisons of detail against summary. The controls you built all year are what feed this correction step.

Stop for this section's questions now — three families of error: diagnose, then correct by the gap, with the narration saying why.

## Subtopic: The Post-Adjustment Trial Balance, In Format

With adjustments and corrections journalised and posted, draw the POST-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — and from term three onward, format carries marks.

The format: a heading naming the business, the document and the date — "Post-adjustment Trial Balance of Zanele's Trading at 30 June". Then two sections, in order. First, the BALANCE SHEET ACCOUNTS section: Capital and Drawings; the fixed assets at cost with their accumulated depreciation accounts beside them; Trading Stock; Debtors Control; the new children of adjustment — Accrued Income, Prepaid Expenses, Consumable Stores on Hand; Bank; then the liabilities — the Loan at its capitalised balance, Creditors Control, Accrued Expenses, Income Received in Advance. Second, the NOMINAL ACCOUNTS section: Sales, Cost of Sales, the surplus if any, then every expense at its ADJUSTED figure — the electricity at twelve months, the insurance at four, the depreciation for the year.

Two columns, debit and credit; the totals must agree, because every adjustment and correction was a balanced double entry. This adjusted, formatted list is the immediate source for everything that follows: the final accounts now, the financial statements in the coming weeks.

Then the final accounts run exactly as term two taught, now with adjusted inputs: Sales and Cost of Sales close to the Trading account — gross profit; the gross profit, other incomes — rent at its earned figure, the surplus — and all adjusted expenses meet in Profit and Loss — net profit; and the net profit transfers to Capital. Same machine, truer fuel.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the format itself: which section, which order, which column, and why the totals still level.

## Subtopic: Closing Off and the Post-Closing Trial Balance

After the final accounts, the closing transfers have emptied every nominal account: Sales, Cost of Sales, each expense, each income — all at zero, their stories summarised into the net profit now resting in Capital. Drawings, too, closes — transferred against Capital, reducing it: the owner's takings settled against the owner's stake.

What remains standing? Only the accounts that describe OWNING rather than the year's earning: assets, liabilities, and the updated Capital. List them, with the heading and the two columns, and you have the POST-CLOSING TRIAL BALANCE — the year-end machine's final document.

Read what it means. It is the business reduced to its position: everything owned, everything owed, and the owner's stake — the accounting equation, itemised and proven level one last time. It contains not a single income or expense, because the year's flows are finished; anything nominal appearing on a post-closing trial balance is an error by definition — the sharpest format trap in the topic. And it is simultaneously next year's OPENING: these balances carry across midnight and stand ready as the new year's first figures, while the nominal accounts wait at zero for July's first transaction.

Step back and see the whole run you now own: pre-adjustment trial balance; adjustments and corrections; post-adjustment trial balance; final accounts; profit and drawings to Capital; post-closing trial balance. Six stations, in order, each certifying the next — and the last one is also the first station of the year to come. That is why the cycle is called a cycle.

The final questions of this part are with you now: what closes, what survives, what the post-closing list may and may never contain.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same finishing work from the shop floor — a toolbox, a chest of drawers, and the one list that gets to cross midnight.

## Subtopic: The Whole Toolbox on One Bench

Lay every year-end tool on the bench and look at them together — because a big question hands you the whole box, not one tool at a time.

The counting tools: shelf says less than the book — deficit, a loss; shelf says MORE than the book — surplus, a small gain, booked because the count proved it. And keep the accountant's frown handy: shelves don't breed stock. A surplus means a record is wrong somewhere — take the gain, then go find the story.

The cupboard tool: unused stationery becomes a parcel for next year. The wearing tool: depreciation, slicing the equipment's cost across its working years. And the four calendar tools from last lesson: the bill still travelling, the cover paid ahead, the rent owed to us, the rent paid to us early — each one placing the amount in its true year and leaving a note or a parcel on the balance sheet. Plus the quiet one: the loan that grew by its own interest.

Nine tools. And here is what makes the box manageable: every single tool works the same motion — find what the year TRULY used or earned, write that on the report card, and let the balance sheet hold whatever is left over. One motion, nine handles. When a question lists five adjustments, you are not facing five puzzles; you are repeating one motion five times.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the toolbox are coming to you right now: pick the right tool for each situation, and name the one motion they all share.

## Subtopic: Fixing the Wrong-Drawer Mistakes

Errors are just clothes in the wrong drawers — and the year-end tidy-up finds three kinds.

Kind one: right cupboard, wrong drawer. The R320 of advertising money was filed under Stationery — both are expense drawers, so the cupboard's total was never wrong, which is exactly why no alarm rang. The fix is a simple move: lift the R320 out of the Stationery drawer, lay it in the Advertising drawer. Nothing added, nothing removed — just filed truly now.

Kind two: the shirt still in the shopping bag. A R600 credit sale was never unpacked into the books at all — no entry anywhere. Both sides missing means the scales never tipped, so again: silence. The fix is to unpack it completely, now: the debtor owes R600, sales earned R600 — and don't forget the second pair our shop always carries: the goods that left cost R400, so stock down, cost of sales up. An omission is fixed by doing, in full, what was skipped.

Kind three: folded wrong. Wages of R2 700 went in as R2 070 — the entry exists, just R630 too small on both sides. Don't rip it out and start over; just add the missing R630 where it belongs. Fix by the DIFFERENCE — the gap, nothing more.

Notice who caught these: not the famous trial balance — all three slipped straight past it — but the humbler guards: the document checks, the lists compared against controls, someone actually reading the drawers. Balance proves arithmetic; only checking proves truth.

## Subtopic: The List That Survives Midnight

Midnight, last day of June. The year's report card is written; the profit has gone home to the owner's jar; the drawings have been settled against it. Now watch what happens to the books as the clock strikes.

The STORY pages — sales, expenses, the surplus, every income — burn down to zero. Not lost: PROMOTED. Their whole year compressed into one number that now lives in Capital. They must start empty, because they measure the year's flow, and a new year's flow must be measured from nothing — July's sales on a clean page, or no one could ever say what the new year earned.

But one list walks straight through midnight untouched: everything the shop OWNS, everything it OWES, and what the owner's stake now stands at. The equipment doesn't stop existing at midnight; the loan doesn't forgive itself; the bank balance doesn't reset. Owning continues — only earning starts fresh. Write that surviving list down, prove it still levels, and you hold the post-closing trial balance: the shop reduced to one honest page — the equation, itemised.

And the beautiful part: that page is ALSO the new year's first page. This year's ending position is next year's opening position — the cycle closing into itself and starting again, which is why it earned the name cycle in the very first lesson of the year.

One trap to carry to any exam: if ANY income or expense appears on a post-closing trial balance, it is wrong — by definition, not by calculation. Flows die at midnight; only positions survive.
