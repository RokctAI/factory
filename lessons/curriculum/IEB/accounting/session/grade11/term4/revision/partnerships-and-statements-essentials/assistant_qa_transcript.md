**Bianca:** Revision questions from the class! First: why does a partner collect BOTH a salary and a share of profit? At my uncle's firm you either earn a salary or you own the place — never both.

**Tutor:** Because a partner wears two hats at the same time, and fairness prices each hat separately. Hat one: worker. Lerato stands on the shop floor every day doing work the partnership would otherwise pay a manager for — the salary appropriation prices that labour, and it stops the harder-working partner from subsidising the other. Hat two: investor. Lerato also locked R240 000 into the business — money that could have earned interest anywhere else — and the interest on capital pays for that patience, in proportion to what each partner planted. Only once both hats are paid does the remainder reward the third thing: joint ownership of the risk. Collapse the three into a single split and unfairness is built in — equal shares would cheat the harder worker; capital-ratio shares would cheat the smaller investor of wages. And note the legal detail your uncle's firm hides: a partner cannot be an employee of their own partnership — the "salary" is not a wage expense with payroll deductions running through the business's costs; it is a first, priority slice of profit promised in the deed. That is precisely why it lives in the appropriation account, below the net profit line.

***

**Bianca:** Next: what if the partnership makes a LOSS, or a profit too small to cover the salaries and interest — surely the pot cannot pay out more than it holds?

**Tutor:** The machinery runs exactly as before — it simply runs into negative territory, and the current accounts absorb the truth. The agreement's promises of salaries and interest do not evaporate in a thin year: they are appropriated in full, and the remainder turns NEGATIVE — a shortfall shared in the profit-sharing ratio and debited to the partners' current accounts. Suppose the salaries and interest total 232 000 but the year's profit is only 180 000: the appropriation account is short by 52 000, so on the three-to-two ratio Lerato's account is charged 31 200 and Karabo's 20 800 — and a partner's current account can swing into debit when their share of the shortfall plus their drawings outweighs their salary and interest. That debit balance is the books stating plainly: you have taken out more than the business earned for you. Nothing has broken — the system is disciplining itself: next year's drawings must shrink, or the agreement must be renegotiated. The question setters usually bury one twist: check whether the ratio for sharing remainders also applies to losses — most agreements say it does, and the expected answer shares the shortfall in that same ratio.

***

**Bianca:** This one trips everyone: the equipment sold for thirty-six thousand and we recorded a PROFIT of six thousand. But the equipment cost ninety! How is selling ninety-thousand-rand equipment for thirty-six a profit?

**Tutor:** Because the profit is measured against what the books still claimed, not against the original price tag — and that distinction is depreciation's whole story coming home. Across its working years, R60 000 of the equipment's cost was already charged against profits as depreciation, piece by piece, as the business used up its life. So on the day of the sale the books carried it at R30 000 — cost 90 000 less accumulated depreciation 60 000 — and that carrying value is the only honest yardstick remaining: it is the slice of cost never yet charged anywhere. Selling for 36 000 recovers the whole remaining 30 000 plus 6 000 more, which whispers that the earlier years depreciated slightly too fast — the business charged a little more wear than the market says truly happened. The profit on disposal quietly corrects the over-charge. Had the equipment fetched 24 000, the 6 000 loss would confess the opposite: depreciation ran too slow. Either way, the four-step disposal account is nothing but a reconciliation between the books' opinion and the market's verdict — and the mark is earned by comparing proceeds to CARRYING value, never to cost.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: seventy-one percent return on equity sounds made up — the bank pays single digits. Should the partners trust their own number?

**Tutor:** Trust it, then read it with both eyes open, because the number is true and still flattering. It is true arithmetically: 300 000 of profit on an average 422 500 of equity is roughly seventy-one percent — the partnership genuinely grew its owners' money at a pace no savings account touches. It flatters because comparing it to a bank rate is not like-for-like, on three counts. First, labour: the 192 000 of partners' salaries sits inside that profit — a bank deposit earns while you sleep, but much of this return was earned standing behind the counter; strip the salaries out and the return on pure capital lands nearer twenty-five percent — still excellent, honestly framed. Second, risk: the bank's rate is close to guaranteed; this return rode on stock that might not have sold and debtors who settle in two months — a fatter return is partly the PRICE of that risk, not a gift. Third, liquidity: bank money is reachable tomorrow; equity is locked inside shelves and equipment. So the polished comment runs: the return of about 71% far exceeds alternative investments, even allowing for the partners' labour inside it, and fairly compensates the risk carried — the partnership is emphatically worth continuing. Figure, comparison, judgement — with the flattery named out loud.
