# Part 1 — Expert

The partnership session revised the year's centrepiece; this session revises the year's toolkit — the four skills that stand guard around any set of books. Reconciliation: making two records of one truth admit the same figure. Costing: knowing what a unit costs and when production starts earning. Inventory and forecasting: valuing what the shelf holds and writing the months before they happen. And VAT: the tax a business gathers without ever owning it. Every station carries a worked example, and every figure will meet its counterpart to the rand.

## Subtopic: Two Records, One Truth — the Reconciliations

The bank keeps a record of your account; you keep your own. One account, two bookkeepers, and month-end forces the two records to face each other. The method never varies: first UPDATE YOUR OWN BOOKS with everything the statement knows and you did not — bank charges R185, a debit order for insurance R515, interest earned R70. The bank account in the ledger stood at R14 730; less 185, less 515, plus 70 — the TRUE book balance, R14 100. Then the BANK RECONCILIATION STATEMENT accounts for what you know and the bank has not yet seen: statement balance R12 900, plus the outstanding deposit of R4 800 still travelling, less payments of R3 600 not yet presented. That lands on R14 100 — the identical figure approached from the other side, and the agreement is the proof. Timing differences reconcile; errors are corrected in whichever record carries them.

The creditors reconciliation is the same discipline pointed at a supplier. Our creditors ledger says we owe Dube Wholesalers R9 400; their statement demands R11 950. Work both sides: the statement lists an invoice of R1 050 for goods delivered but not yet entered in our books — our balance corrects to R10 450. Our payment of R1 500 left too late to reach their statement — their balance adjusts to R10 450. Both records meet, and the debt is KNOWN, not guessed. What the habit purchases: errors surface monthly instead of rotting quietly, disputes are settled with workings instead of raised voices, and no statement is ever paid blind.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: books updated, both statements assembled, and both meetings landing on one figure.

## Subtopic: Costing — Contribution and the Break-Even Line

A manufacturer's ledger splits the trader's single stock account into three — raw materials, work-in-process, finished goods — and costs travel through them as the product takes shape: materials and labour straight into the product, overheads gathered and absorbed, the finished cost settling in finished goods. That structure was term three's labour; revision sharpens its keenest blade — BREAK-EVEN.

Sort every cost by how it behaves. VARIABLE costs ride along with each unit made: ingredients, piece-rate labour. FIXED costs stand rooted whatever the volume: rent, salaries, insurance. Now the numbers: a product sells at R18; its variable cost is R6. Each unit therefore CONTRIBUTES R12 — first toward the fixed costs, and once those are buried, straight into profit. Fixed costs run R9 600 a month. Break-even: 9 600 over 12 — eight hundred units. At 799 the month loses; at 800 it stands level; every unit past the line carries its whole R12 home. Sell 1 040 and the profit is 240 units times 12 — R2 880, provable the long way: sales 18 720, variable costs 6 240, contribution 12 480, less fixed 9 600 — R2 880.

The blade answers management's living questions: rent rises — break-even climbs; the price drops to R14 — contribution shrinks to R8 and break-even leaps to 1 200; a bulk order at a thin price — worth taking only if each unit still contributes. Cost behaviour first, contribution second, decisions third.

Stop for this section's questions now — costs sorted by behaviour, break-even computed, and the profit at one thousand and forty units proved both ways.

## Subtopic: Stock Systems and the Forward Documents

Two ways to know cost of sales. The PERPETUAL system writes it with every sale — the stock account always current, theft exposed as the gap between the account and the physical count. The PERIODIC system waits: purchases gather in a Purchases account, and cost of sales appears only by calculation — opening stock R18 000, plus purchases R52 000, plus carriage on purchases R4 000, less closing stock counted at R20 000 — cost of sales R54 000. Cheaper to run, blinder between counts: the calculation cannot separate sold from stolen. Choose by the trade: high-value items deserve perpetual's constant watch; high-volume small goods often accept periodic's economy.

Then the forward documents, which plan instead of record. The PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT forecasts profit on the accrual calendar; the CASH BUDGET forecasts survival on the cash calendar — and the two can quarrel loudly, because profit is not cash. The bridge between them is the DEBTORS COLLECTION SCHEDULE, which converts planned credit sales into planned receipts. The pattern: thirty percent of a month's credit sales pay in the month of sale, sixty percent the following month, ten percent never — written off. October's collections, with September credit sales of R40 000 and October's R36 000: thirty percent of 36 000 — R10 800; sixty percent of 40 000 — R24 000. Total collected: R34 800. Hear what the ten percent teaches: budgeted receipts always fall short of budgeted sales, and a business that plans on the full hundred percent is planning with money that will never knock.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — cost of sales computed backwards, the two systems matched to the right shops, and the October collections assembled.

## Subtopic: VAT — the Collector's Arithmetic

Value Added Tax, stripped to its skeleton. The vendor CHARGES output tax on sales, RECOVERS input tax on purchases, and pays over the difference — the tax itself belongs to the final consumer and passes through the business untouched. Three categories of supply: STANDARD-RATED at fifteen percent; ZERO-RATED — taxable at nought, the staples and exports — where the vendor still recovers input tax; EXEMPT — outside the system, no output charged, no input recovered. One question splits the confusable pair: may the seller recover its own VAT? Zero-rated, yes; exempt, no.

The arithmetic, both directions. Climbing from an exclusive price: multiply by 1,15. Descending from an inclusive figure — the classic trap — the VAT inside is fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: an invoice totalling R6 900 inclusive holds VAT of 6 900 times 15 over 115 — R900 — on an exclusive R6 000. Never fifteen percent of the inclusive figure. The return: output tax R12 400 for the period, input tax R8 900 — payable to SARS, R3 500. Inputs above outputs would turn the return into a refund.

And the ethics that carry their own marks: the R3 500 is TRUST MONEY — gathered from customers on the state's behalf, never the vendor's to spend. The named sins: raiding the VAT to ease cash flow, claiming inputs on private purchases, invented invoices, charging VAT while unregistered. The named controls: a valid tax invoice behind every claim, the VAT control account reconciled to the return monthly, returns submitted and paid on time. The auditor's single question: does every input claim stand on a real invoice for a real business purpose?

The final questions of this part are with you now: the categories split by the recover question, both calculations run, and the return computed with its ethics stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the toolkit from the pavement — a spaza counter, a koeksister tray, and a till slip — because all four skills are things careful households already practise.

## Subtopic: When Two Notebooks Disagree

You keep a notebook of your bank money; the bank keeps its own. At month-end the two never match at first glance — and that mismatch is NORMAL. The bank knows things you missed: it quietly helped itself to its fee of one eighty-five, the insurance debit order of five-fifteen went off, a little interest of seventy arrived. Enter those first — now your notebook tells the truth: fourteen thousand one hundred. Then turn it around: you know things the bank has not seen — the deposit still travelling, the payments not yet landed. Set those against the bank's number and it walks to the same fourteen thousand one hundred. Two notebooks, each corrected toward the other, shaking hands in the middle — nothing magical, just both sides confessing what they did not know.

The identical trick works on the wholesaler whose statement says you owe eleven nine-fifty while your book says nine four hundred. Do not fight; reconcile. Their statement knows about the goods that arrived before you wrote them up — one thousand and fifty, add it to your book. Your book knows about the payment still on its way to them — fifteen hundred, take it off their statement. Both sides arrive at ten four-fifty, and now you pay what is TRUE, not what is loudest. The habit in one sentence: never argue balances; correct both records toward each other and pay the meeting point.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the meeting notebooks are coming to you right now: who updates what, and where the two sides land.

## Subtopic: The Koeksister Line That Pays the Rent

A koeksister table at the taxi rank. Each koeksister sells for eighteen rand; the flour, oil, syrup and gas inside it cost six. So every koeksister sold carries twelve rand toward the table's fixed life — the pitch fee, the equipment instalment, the fixed things costing nine thousand six hundred a month whether one koeksister fries or a thousand.

Ask the only question that matters on the first of the month: how many koeksisters until the fixed costs are buried? Nine thousand six hundred divided by twelve — eight hundred. Koeksister number one to seven ninety-nine are working for the landlord. Number eight hundred squares the month. And number eight-oh-one is the first one working for YOU — from that one on, every koeksister drops its whole twelve rand into your pocket. Sell one thousand and forty this month? Two hundred and forty past the line, times twelve — two thousand eight hundred and eighty rand of profit, and you can feel exactly which koeksisters earned it.

Now every business decision turns visible. The pitch fee goes up — the line retreats further away; measure the new distance before you agree to stay. Drop the price to fourteen — each koeksister now carries only eight, and the line leaps to twelve hundred; cheaper is not automatically smarter. A church bazaar wants four hundred at a discount — fine, IF each discounted koeksister still carries something toward the line. The break-even line is not textbook decoration; it is the boundary between working for the landlord and working for yourself.

Your questions for this part are up now: find the line, move the line, and count the koeksisters that work for you.

## Subtopic: The Slip, the Shelf and the Month Ahead

Three final street skills, brisk and clean.

The slip: the VAT line at the till is money passing THROUGH the shop, never resting in it. The shopkeeper gathered it for SARS; spending it on stock or wages is spending a stranger's money, and the penalties stack like mashonisa interest. The one calculation to own: the slip says six thousand nine hundred INCLUDING VAT; the tax inside is fifteen parts of one hundred and fifteen — nine hundred rand — never fifteen percent of the total. Climbing, fifteen on a hundred; descending, fifteen on one-fifteen.

The shelf: how does the shop know what its sales cost it? Either the notebook way — write the cost out with every sale, always current, theft exposed the instant the count disagrees with the book — or the counting way: what did the shelf begin with, what arrived, what remains? Eighteen in, fifty-two bought, four of transport, twenty still standing — fifty-four thousand went out. The counting way is cheaper and blinder; it cannot tell sold from stolen. Choose the eye your stock deserves.

And the month ahead: the household that plans knows the gap between EARNING and RECEIVING. Customers on account pay some now, some next month, and some never — plan on thirty percent now and sixty percent later, and release the ten percent before it lies to you. Write the month's money down before living it, and the shop meets its troubles while they are still small enough to carry.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the VAT extracted from a slip, the shelf counted backwards, and next month's collections written down honestly. Finish them, and the grade 11 toolkit — reconciling, costing, counting, collecting, planning — is revised, balanced, and ready for your final exams.
