# Part 1 — Expert

The heart of the grade 11 year was the PARTNERSHIP — two or more owners, one business, and the bookkeeping that keeps each owner's claim separate and honest. This revision session rebuilds that heart from the ground up: the accounts each partner owns, the appropriation account that shares out the profit, the financial statements and notes that report the whole — including the fixed assets living inside them — and the indicators that pass judgement on the result. One partnership supplies every figure: Lerato and Karabo, trading as LK Traders, year ended the last day of June, and every division will balance to the rand.

## Subtopic: Two Owners, Four Accounts

A sole trader managed with one capital account; a partnership demands a PAIR of accounts PER PARTNER, and that split is the entire design. The CAPITAL account holds the partner's long-term stake — Lerato invested R240 000, Karabo R160 000 — and it hardly ever moves: only a deliberate increase or reduction of the stake touches it. The CURRENT account is the account that breathes: each year it receives the partner's share of profit and surrenders the partner's drawings, and its balance announces where the partner STANDS against the business — a credit balance means the business owes the partner; a debit balance means the partner has taken out more than was earned.

Every flow through those accounts obeys the PARTNERSHIP AGREEMENT: the salaries partners earn for their labour, the interest their capitals earn for standing still, any bonus promised, and the ratio that shares whatever remains. The agreement is the constitution; the bookkeeping merely carries it out. And guard the sharpest distinction in the whole topic: partners' salaries and interest on capital are NOT expenses of the business. They are APPROPRIATIONS — slices of a profit already earned. The income statement finishes at net profit; only below that line does the sharing begin. Blur the two and both statements collapse.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: capital against current, the agreement's items named, and appropriations kept apart from expenses.

## Subtopic: Dividing the Profit — the Appropriation Account

Net profit for the year: R300 000. The appropriation account shares it out in the agreement's order, and the discipline is that the account must EMPTY exactly.

First, salaries: Lerato R108 000, Karabo R84 000 — R192 000 appropriated. Second, interest on capital at ten percent: Lerato R24 000 on R240 000; Karabo R16 000 on R160 000 — another R40 000. Third, the agreement promises Karabo a bonus of R8 000 for building the online orders. Now the remainder: 300 000 less 192 000, less 40 000, less 8 000 — R60 000, shared in the capital ratio of three to two as the agreement directs: R36 000 to Lerato, R24 000 to Karabo. Prove it by partner: Lerato 108 000 plus 24 000 plus 36 000 — R168 000. Karabo 84 000 plus 16 000 plus 8 000 plus 24 000 — R132 000. Together: R300 000. The account empties; the division is proved.

Each share settles into its partner's current account, and the year closes there. Lerato: opening credit 6 000, plus 168 000, less drawings 144 000 — closing credit R30 000. Karabo: opening DEBIT 9 000 — last year she overdrew — plus 132 000, less drawings 105 000 — closing credit R18 000. Read them the way a banker would: the business owes Lerato 30 000 and Karabo 18 000; both partners lived inside their earnings this year.

Stop for this section's questions now — the appropriation run in order, the empty-account proof, and both current accounts closed.

## Subtopic: The Statements, the Notes and the Asset That Left

The partnership's statements wear the architecture you already know, with the ownership section rewritten. The STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME: sales R1 200 000, cost of sales R800 000 — gross profit R400 000, fifty percent on cost. Other income holds a profit on the disposal of an asset — R6 000, built in a moment — and operating expenses total R106 000; net profit R300 000, which flows to the appropriation account, never to one partner's capital.

The disposal earns its four steps, because an asset left mid-year: equipment costing R90 000, accumulated depreciation R60 000, sold for R36 000. Step one: move the COST out — credit Equipment, debit Asset Disposal, 90 000. Step two: move the accumulated depreciation across — debit Accumulated Depreciation, credit Asset Disposal, 60 000; the disposal account now shows the carrying value, 30 000. Step three: record the proceeds — 36 000 against the disposal account. Step four: the difference is the profit — R6 000, other income. Four steps, in that order, whatever the payment method.

The STATEMENT OF FINANCIAL POSITION: non-current assets at carrying value R420 000; current assets R240 000 — trading stock 120 000, debtors 80 000, bank 40 000. Total assets R660 000. Financed by: partners' equity — capitals 400 000 plus current accounts 48 000 — R448 000; a non-current loan of R112 000; current liabilities R100 000. Total R660 000. And the NOTES carry the workings: the fixed-asset note walking opening carrying value through additions, disposals and depreciation to closing; the capital and current account notes giving each partner a column of their own — the transparency that lets every partner audit their own treatment.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the disposal's four steps, the statement totals, and what each note discloses.

## Subtopic: The Indicators — Reading the Partnership

Now judge the year. Profitability: gross profit on sales — 400 000 over 1 200 000 — thirty-three comma three percent; on cost, fifty percent, exactly the mark-up policy. Net profit on sales: 300 000 over 1 200 000 — twenty-five percent.

The operating pulse. STOCK TURNOVER: cost of sales 800 000 over average stock 100 000 — eight times a year; the shelves clear and refill every month and a half. DEBTORS COLLECTION: debtors 80 000 over credit sales 480 000, times twelve — two months. Against thirty-day terms, that is slack: money idling in customers' pockets that should be working here.

Liquidity: current ratio 240 000 over 100 000 — two comma four to one; acid test — set the stock aside — 120 000 over 100 000, one comma two to one: the immediate debts covered with room to spare, without selling a single item. Healthy.

Then the gearing pair. DEBT-EQUITY: the loan of 112 000 against equity of 448 000 — nought comma two five to one: one rand borrowed for every four the partners own — lightly geared, low risk; borrowing capacity waits if expansion beckons. RETURN ON AVERAGE EQUITY: 300 000 over average equity of 422 500 — roughly seventy-one percent. Before applauding, read it honestly: partners' salaries sit INSIDE that return — it pays for their labour as well as their capital. Even so, measured against any bank rate the partnership rewards its owners handsomely. The comment method never varies: figure, comparison, judgement — and for partnerships, always ask whose question each indicator answers: the creditor asks liquidity, the lender asks gearing, the partner asks return.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the pulse indicators computed, the gearing pair interpreted, and each indicator matched to the eye that watches it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again, from street level — because a partnership is simply two friends and one till, and every rule exists to keep the friendship breathing.

## Subtopic: Two Friends, One Till, and the Paper That Keeps the Peace

Two friends open a shop together. One brings more money; the other puts in longer hours. Within weeks, the dangerous sentences arrive: "I invested more, so more belongs to me." "I work harder, so more belongs to me." Every partnership quarrel ever recorded is one of those two sentences — and the partnership agreement exists to answer both BEFORE voices rise: a salary for the hours worked, interest for the money that waits, a ratio for whatever is left. Written down, signed, obeyed.

The books then keep two piles per person, and those two piles are the peace treaty. The DEEP pile — capital — is what each friend planted into the business: Lerato two hundred and forty thousand, Karabo one hundred and sixty. It stays planted. The BREATHING pile — the current account — moves every year: in flows each friend's share of the profit, out flows whatever each friend took home. If your breathing pile shows the business owing you, you lived within what you earned; if it shows you owing the business, you ate more than your share grew. One line per friend, and no one argues from memory — the paper remembers everything.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two piles are coming to you right now: which pile breathes, and which two sentences the agreement answers.

## Subtopic: Slicing the Profit Pot

The year ends and the pot holds R300 000. Watch it emptied fairly, scoop by scoop, in the agreement's order.

Scoop one — pay for the hours: Lerato runs the floor full-time, R108 000; Karabo, part-time, R84 000. Scoop two — thanks to the money that waited: ten percent on each friend's planted pile — twenty-four thousand to Lerato, sixteen to Karabo. Scoop three — the promised extra: Karabo built the online orders, and the agreement honours that bonus, R8 000. The pot now holds sixty thousand, and the final scoop shares it three to two, matching the planted money — thirty-six and twenty-four.

Count each friend's bowl: Lerato one hundred and sixty-eight thousand; Karabo one hundred and thirty-two. Together, three hundred thousand — the pot is empty, to the rand. That emptiness is the entire discipline: appropriation never makes or loses a cent; it only shares. And dodge the trap that catches learners every single year: those salaries are not wages the business paid its staff — they are slices of profit. The business first works out what it EARNED; only afterwards does the family gather to share it. Expenses live above the profit line; sharing lives below it. Two different rooms, and the question always checks which room you are standing in.

Your questions for this part are up now: the scoops in order, both bowls counted, and the two rooms kept apart.

## Subtopic: Reading the Scoreboard

Season finished — was the team any good? The statements are the match footage; the indicators are the scoreboard, and each number answers a different supporter.

The shopper's question: are they trading well? Bought for eight hundred, sold for twelve hundred — a third of every sale is gross profit, exactly the planned mark-up: nothing leaking through the floor. The stock turns eight times a year — shelves alive, never a museum. But customers settle in two months against thirty-day terms — the friends are quietly financing their customers, and tightening that single screw would swell the bank balance on its own.

The creditor's question: can they pay me soon? Two rand forty of near money for every rand of near debt; and even leaving the stock untouched, one rand twenty for every rand owed. Sleep well.

The bank's question: how deep is the debt? One rand borrowed for every four the friends own — light gearing; headroom to borrow for growth whenever they choose.

And the friends' own question: was it worth it? The money they keep inside the business returned about seventy-one rand for every hundred — towering over any savings account — with the honest footnote attached: part of that return is simply their own labour arriving home dressed as profit. Read everything the scoreboard way: never a number alone — always against last year, against the terms, against the bank — and the story tells itself.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each supporter matched to their number, the slow payers exposed, and the worth-it verdict argued with figures. Finish them, and the partnership year is revised — shared fairly, reported fully, and judged honestly.
