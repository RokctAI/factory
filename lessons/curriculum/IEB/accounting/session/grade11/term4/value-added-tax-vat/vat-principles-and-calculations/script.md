# Part 1 — Expert

Every receipt printed in South Africa carries a line most shoppers skim past: the VAT. This lesson makes that line make sense — why Value Added Tax exists, the principles that drive it, which supplies are standard-rated, zero-rated and exempt, the four calculations you will be asked to perform, the handling of returns, discounts and bad debts, and the ethics and internal control that surround a tax a business gathers on behalf of the state. VAT earns marks in grade 11 and returns in grade 12; the arithmetic is quick, and it is the classification questions where careless answers bleed marks.

## Subtopic: Why VAT Exists and How It Works

VALUE ADDED TAX is an INDIRECT tax on consumption: it attaches to goods and services rather than to income, and the burden lands on the final CONSUMER, even though businesses do the collecting and the paying over. Its purpose is revenue — VAT ranks among the largest single sources of money for the national budget, funding hospitals, roads and classrooms — and its design carries a practical strength: because it is gathered in small pieces at every stage of production, evasion is harder and the flow of collection is steadier than a tax gathered at one final point.

The mechanism sits inside the name: tax on the VALUE ADDED at each stage. Trace a wooden table through three businesses. Karkloof Sawmill sells timber to a workshop for R400 plus VAT. Msunduzi Woodcraft turns the timber into a table and sells it to a furniture store for R700 plus VAT. Louw's Home Store sells the table to you for R1 000 plus VAT. Each business charges VAT on what it SELLS — that is OUTPUT tax — and each recovers the VAT it PAID on what it bought — INPUT tax. Each hands over only the difference. The workshop, charging output tax on R700 and reclaiming input tax on R400, sends SARS the tax on just R300 — the value ITS work added. Stack the three payments together and SARS holds exactly the VAT on the final R1 000 you paid at the store. Businesses are conduits, not payers: the tax settles wherever the consuming happens.

Timing is governed by two principles. Most VENDORS — businesses registered for VAT, compulsorily once turnover passes the prescribed threshold and voluntarily below it — use the INVOICE BASIS: VAT is recognised the moment the invoice is issued, paid or not. Smaller qualifying vendors may use the PAYMENTS or receipts BASIS: VAT is recognised only when money actually changes hands. On credit transactions the two give very different answers, and you must be able to state the difference cleanly.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the nature of VAT, output against input tax traced through the three-business chain, and the two accounting bases told apart.

## Subtopic: Standard-Rated, Zero-Rated and Exempt

Three categories of supply, and mixing them up is the most dependable way to lose marks on this topic — because two of them look like twins and behave like opposites.

STANDARD-RATED supplies carry VAT at the current standard rate — the bulk of goods and services: furniture, clothing, cellphones, takeaway meals, accountants' fees, courier deliveries. The vendor charges output tax and recovers input tax in the ordinary way.

ZERO-RATED supplies are TAXABLE, but the rate applied is zero. The list shields low-income households from tax on necessities: brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans and lentils, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, vegetable oil, paraffin — and, for an entirely different reason, exported goods, which will be taxed in the country where they are consumed. Because zero-rated supplies stay inside the tax net as taxable supplies, the vendor selling them still recovers INPUT tax on its own purchases: a vendor dealing only in zero-rated goods charges no output tax yet reclaims all its inputs, and SARS pays it a refund.

EXEMPT supplies stand outside the VAT system entirely: no VAT is charged, and — here is the dividing line — NO input tax may be recovered on the costs of providing them. The list includes financial services where interest is earned, residential rental, public transport by road and rail, and educational services from recognised institutions.

Anchor the difference with the input-tax test, because the difference IS the input tax: zero-rated means taxable at nought percent, so inputs ARE recoverable; exempt means outside the system, so inputs are NOT. One question — may the supplier claim its input tax back — and the twins separate on the spot.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three categories with examples, and the input-tax test used to split zero-rated from exempt.

## Subtopic: The Four Calculations

The VAT arithmetic itself is short; the marks are earned by choosing the correct calculation. Work at the standard rate of fifteen percent — VAT is fifteen hundredths of the price EXCLUDING VAT, so the inclusive price is 1,15 times the exclusive price.

Calculation one: ADDING VAT to an exclusive amount. A desk priced at R3 200 excluding VAT: the VAT is 3 200 times 15 over 100 — R480; the inclusive price is R3 680. In a single step: 3 200 times 1,15.

Calculation two: EXTRACTING VAT from an inclusive amount — the classic trap, because instinct reaches for fifteen percent of the inclusive figure and instinct is wrong. The inclusive amount is 115 percent of the exclusive amount, so the VAT inside it is 15 over 115 of the inclusive figure. An invoice totalling R3 680 inclusive carries VAT of 3 680 times 15 over 115 — R480, sitting on an exclusive R3 200. Cross-check against calculation one: the answers must meet, and the check takes seconds.

Calculation three: FINDING THE EXCLUSIVE AMOUNT from an inclusive one: divide by 1,15. R3 680 over 1,15 is R3 200.

Calculation four: COST PLUS MARK-UP PLUS VAT — the pricing chain a real business runs, and the favourite integrated question. Stock costs R1 600; the policy is a mark-up of 75 percent on cost; the selling price excluding VAT is 1 600 plus 1 200 — R2 800; then VAT: 2 800 times 1,15 — R3 220 out of the customer's pocket. Guard the order fiercely: MARK-UP FIRST, applied to the VAT-exclusive cost, THEN VAT on the marked-up price. Swap them and both answers collapse — and notice that the R420 of VAT was never the business's money: the profit is the R1 200 mark-up and nothing more.

Finally the VAT RETURN: total output tax on sales, less total input tax on purchases and expenses, equals the amount PAYABLE to SARS — or, when inputs outrun outputs, REFUNDABLE by SARS.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — all four calculations, computed and cross-checked, with the mark-up order respected.

## Subtopic: Adjustments, Ethics and Control

Three events disturb VAT that has already been recorded, and each carries its own remedy.

GOODS RETURNED: a customer sends goods back, the sale unwinds — and its output tax unwinds with it. The vendor issues a CREDIT NOTE stating the VAT portion and reduces output tax by that amount. Goods sent back to a supplier reverse input tax the same way, on a debit note.

DISCOUNT: a discount shrinks the amount actually charged, so the VAT shrinks with it. Trade discount comes off before any VAT is calculated. A settlement discount granted after invoicing reduces the consideration, and the output tax is corrected through a credit note covering the discount and its VAT.

BAD DEBTS: a debtor's account is written off as irrecoverable. On the invoice basis the vendor has already paid over output tax on that sale — tax on money that will never arrive. The law allows relief: the VAT portion of the written-off debt is recovered as input tax. And if the debtor surprises everyone and pays later, the VAT must be declared all over again. Learn the symmetry, because it is tested: relief when the income evaporates, restoration when it reappears.

Now the ethics, which this topic treats as seriously as the arithmetic. VAT is TRUST money — the vendor gathers it from customers on SARS's behalf and holds it until the return falls due. Spending it on wages or suppliers is not clever cash management; it is spending the state's money, and it is precisely how vendors sink into arrears they cannot climb out of. The named frauds: input tax claimed on private purchases routed through the business; invented invoices manufactured to inflate input claims; charging VAT while unregistered — pocketing a "tax" the customer believes is bound for SARS; suppressing cash sales to understate output tax; and under-invoicing of imports.

The internal controls that answer them: register properly and print the VAT number on every tax invoice; issue valid tax invoices carrying every required detail; retain source documents for the prescribed period; separate the duties of recording, banking and payment; reconcile the VAT control account to the returns every month; and submit and pay on time, because penalties and interest compound fast. The internal auditor's single searching question: does every input tax claim stand on a valid tax invoice for a genuine business purpose?

The final questions of this part are with you now: returns, discounts and bad debts handled correctly, and the frauds matched to the controls that expose them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same tax through the receipt in your pocket — because you have been paying VAT since your first packet of chips, and today it stops hiding.

## Subtopic: The Line at the Bottom of the Slip

Buy anything at the shops and read the slip to the bottom. There is a line marked VAT. You paid it. Nobody asked your permission — and that quiet line is one of the biggest rivers of money the government has, filling the budget for clinics, roads and schools.

Here is the twist most people never see: the shop kept none of it. The shop is a COLLECTOR, not an owner. It lifts the VAT from you at the till, keeps it safe, and passes it along to SARS. Businesses collect; consumers pay. Hold that one sentence and the whole topic unlocks.

And the collecting runs down a clever chain. Think of a wooden table. The sawmill sells timber to the workshop and adds VAT. The workshop builds the table and sells it to the furniture store, adding VAT — but first it claims BACK the VAT it paid the sawmill, so it only hands over tax on the piece of value ITS hands added. The store repeats the trick when it sells to you. Every link pays tax on its own slice, no rand is taxed twice, and when the slices are stacked, SARS holds exactly the tax on what YOU finally paid at the till. That is why the tax is called value added: each business answers only for the value it added.

So when a shop owner grumbles about "paying VAT", listen closely — a registered business does not truly pay it; it channels it. What the business pays over is the gap between what it collected and what it was charged.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the slip are coming to you right now: who collects, who really pays, and what value added means along the chain.

## Subtopic: The Three Baskets at the Till

Not every item on your slip is taxed alike, and the differences are deliberate — they are about fairness.

Basket one: MOST THINGS. Chips, data, takkies, a burger, headphones. Standard rate, VAT added, no drama.

Basket two: THE ESSENTIALS BASKET. Brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, cooking oil, paraffin. These carry ZERO VAT — on purpose, because a tax on food punishes a poor household far harder than a wealthy one; if the basics carried full tax, the family that spends nearly everything on food would hand over the biggest share of its income. So the state zero-rates the staples. Look carefully at which bread, though — an ordinary brown loaf is zero-rated while a fancy artisanal loaf is not; the list guards necessities, not tastes. Exports live in this basket too, for their own reason: those goods will be enjoyed in another country, so that country levies the tax, not ours.

Basket three: OUTSIDE THE SHOP ALTOGETHER. Some things are EXEMPT — no VAT at all, because they never entered the system: the interest your bank charges, the rent on the home you live in, a bus or train fare on public transport, school and university fees.

Now the trap, disarmed in plain words. Zero-rated means inside the tax system, charged at nothing — so the shop selling maize meal still recovers the VAT on its own electricity and shelving. Exempt means outside the system completely — so a landlord letting out flats charges no VAT and recovers NOTHING on the paint and plumbing he paid for; he swallows it. The same zero appears on your slip; opposite stories play out behind the counter. Ask one question — does the seller get its own VAT back — and you will never confuse them again.

Your questions for this part are up now: sort items into the three baskets — and let the get-it-back question split the last two.

## Subtopic: The Fifteen-on-One-Fifteen Trick, and Whose Money It Is

Two things remain: the sum almost everybody fumbles, and the honesty that outweighs the sum.

The sum. Adding VAT is painless — a price of R3 200 becomes R3 680, because you add fifteen percent. Now run it backwards: a slip reads R3 680 INCLUDING VAT — how much VAT is buried inside? Nearly everyone takes fifteen percent of R3 680 and announces R552. Wrong. The fifteen percent was worked out on the SMALLER number — the price before tax. R3 680 is already the full parcel: 100 parts of price plus 15 parts of tax — 115 parts in all. So the tax is fifteen parts out of one hundred and fifteen: multiply R3 680 by fifteen over one-fifteen and out comes R480. Chant it until it is reflex: going up, fifteen on a hundred; coming down, fifteen on a hundred and fifteen.

And one rule of order for anyone running a shop: profit FIRST, tax second. Stock cost R1 600; you want seventy-five percent on cost, so your price is R2 800 — the R1 200 is yours. Only then add VAT on the R2 800, landing at R3 220 at the till. The extra R420 was never profit and never will be; it is SARS's money resting in your till until the return is due.

Which delivers the real lesson. The VAT you collect is TRUST MONEY — someone else's money passing through your hands. Every month, struggling businesses raid it: pay SARS later, cover the wages now. It feels like cash-flow juggling. It is spending money that was never yours, and the penalties stack until the business goes under. The frauds are the same thinking in fancier clothes: pushing the family groceries through the business to claim their VAT back; inventing invoices to reclaim tax never paid; charging customers "VAT" without being registered — which is plain theft from the customer, who believes the money is travelling to SARS.

The controls are simple discipline: proper registration and a VAT number on every invoice, a real document behind every claim, the person who records never being the person who banks, and the VAT account reconciled every single month. One question catches nearly everything: for every rand claimed back, is there a genuine invoice for a genuine business purchase? If not, someone is stealing — from all of us, because it is the clinics and classrooms that come up short.

And here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the fifteen-on-one-fifteen sum run both ways, the mark-up-then-VAT order, and the trust-money test applied to a scenario. Read the bottom of your next slip properly — you will finally know exactly what that line has been saying all along.
