# Part 1 — Expert

Until now, every business in this course BOUGHT the goods it sold. Today's business MAKES them, and that one change rewires the whole ledger. Meet Mokoena Backpack Works of Polokwane, maker of school backpacks: rolls of canvas and zips arrive at one door, finished backpacks leave through another, and the bookkeeping must follow every rand of the journey between them. On today's route: the cost classifications the topic demands — direct against indirect, variable against fixed; the three stock accounts a manufacturer keeps where a trader kept one; the cost accounts — direct materials, direct labour and factory overheads — and the flow of costs through work-in-progress into finished goods; and the ethics and internal controls that belong on a factory floor. The financial year ends on 30 June.

## Subtopic: The Language of Cost

Before a factory can be recorded, its costs must be sorted twice — once by TRACEABILITY and once by BEHAVIOUR.

Traceability first. A DIRECT cost can be followed into the product itself. The canvas, zips and straps in a backpack: direct materials. The wages of the machinists who cut and sew: direct labour. Direct materials plus direct labour form PRIME COST — the costs that physically touch the backpack. An INDIRECT cost keeps the factory alive but cannot be pinned to any single bag: the production supervisor's salary, the factory rent, the machines' electricity, thread and machine oil, depreciation on the sewing machines. These are FACTORY OVERHEADS. Prime cost plus factory overheads gives the TOTAL COST OF PRODUCTION.

Two further cost families never enter production cost at all. ADMINISTRATION costs — the office, the bookkeeper's salary, the office telephone — and SELLING AND DISTRIBUTION costs — advertising, the delivery van's fuel, sales commission. These are period costs: they belong in the income statement as expenses, but they are no part of what a backpack costs to make. The sorting test for any cost: does it arise in the FACTORY, for MAKING? Production. In the office? Administration. In getting sold goods to customers? Selling and distribution.

Behaviour second. A VARIABLE cost rises and falls with output: sew more backpacks, buy more canvas — direct materials, direct labour, and per-unit selling costs like commission are typically variable. A FIXED cost ignores output within a normal range: the factory rent is identical whether the month produces four thousand bags or eight thousand — rent, the supervisor's salary, depreciation and insurance are typically fixed. Now hold both sortings at once, because they cut across each other: factory overheads are mostly FIXED yet fully PRODUCTION costs; sales commission is VARIABLE yet never a production cost. Exam questions live exactly at that crossing. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: costs sorted both ways.

## Subtopic: Three Stock Accounts Where the Trader Kept One

A trader's trading stock account held goods ready for sale, and that was the end of it. A factory's stock exists in three states of becoming, so the ledger runs three stock accounts — four, if you count the consumables.

RAW MATERIALS STOCK: canvas, zips, straps, buckles — bought, but not yet touched. Mokoena Backpack Works opened the year holding R72 000 of raw materials, bought R468 000 more during the year, and paid R12 000 carriage on those purchases — carriage on raw materials joins the cost of the materials, just as carriage on purchases did for a trader. The year-end count valued closing raw materials at R64 000.

WORK-IN-PROGRESS STOCK: bags half-made — cut panels, zips pinned but not sewn — value caught mid-process. Opening balance R38 000; closing R48 000. Work-in-progress is where ALL the production costs will eventually meet.

FINISHED GOODS STOCK: completed backpacks waiting to be sold — the nearest relative of the trader's trading stock. Opening R76 000; closing R96 000.

And CONSUMABLE STORES: factory supplies used up in production without becoming part of the product — machine oil, needles, cleaning materials. Consumables used during the year flow into factory overheads as indirect materials.

Every stock account speaks the grammar you have used since grade 10: opening balance, plus what came in, less what moved on, equals closing balance. Say it for raw materials, because it delivers the year's first key figure: opening R72 000, plus purchases R468 000, plus carriage R12 000, gives R552 000 available; less closing stock of R64 000 — so raw materials ISSUED to production cost R488 000. That R488 000 is the direct materials cost, and its journey is about to begin. Stop for this section's questions now: the three states of stock, and the issue figure computed from the account.

## Subtopic: The Cost Accounts and the Flow to Finished Goods

Now the machinery of the topic: three COST accounts collect the year's production costs and then empty themselves into work-in-progress. Speak the flow aloud.

DIRECT MATERIALS COST account: debited with the R488 000 issued from raw materials stock. DIRECT LABOUR COST account: debited with the machinists' wages of R342 000 plus the employer's contributions of R24 000 that travel with those wages — R366 000. These two accounts close off to work-in-progress carrying PRIME COST: R488 000 plus R366 000 — R854 000.

FACTORY OVERHEAD COST account gathers the indirect production costs, each one named: indirect materials — the consumables used — R34 000; the supervisor's salary R120 000; factory rent R84 000; factory electricity and water R48 000; depreciation on sewing machines R52 000; factory insurance R18 000. Total factory overheads: R356 000. This account closes to work-in-progress too.

The WORK-IN-PROGRESS account now narrates the whole production year. Opening balance R38 000. Add prime cost of R854 000 and overheads of R356 000 — TOTAL MANUFACTURING COSTS of R1 210 000 for the year. In process altogether: R1 248 000. Less the closing half-made stock of R48 000: the COST OF PRODUCTION OF FINISHED GOODS — R1 200 000 — transferred to finished goods stock.

Mokoena completed 20 000 backpacks this year, so the UNIT COST of production is R1 200 000 over 20 000: R60 per backpack — the figure on which every pricing and break-even decision will stand.

Finished goods closes the chain in trading-stock grammar: opening R76 000, plus production of R1 200 000, gives R1 276 000 available; less closing R96 000 — COST OF SALES of R1 180 000. Sales for the year came to R1 770 000, and R1 770 000 less R1 180 000 leaves a gross profit of R590 000 — exactly 50 percent on cost of sales, confirming the mark-up policy. One ledger, one unbroken chain: materials into prime cost, prime cost plus overheads into work-in-progress, work-in-progress into finished goods, finished goods into cost of sales. Pause for this section's questions: the flow traced end to end, and any hidden figure recovered from the chain.

## Subtopic: Ethics and Control on the Factory Floor

A factory gathers temptation in one place: valuable materials in bulk, hours that can be inflated, offcuts that can disappear. The controls must be named specifically, and they follow the flow you have just learned.

Over MATERIALS: order from approved suppliers at quoted prices; count and inspect every delivery against the order and the delivery note before signing; store under lock with a named storeman responsible; issue to production only against signed requisitions; and compare what a production run SHOULD have consumed with what the storeroom issued — a gap means waste, damage or theft, and each has its own remedy. Watch the offcuts: canvas remnants have value, and unrecorded scrap is the classic leak.

Over LABOUR: clock cards or biometric registers so that hours paid are hours worked; overtime approved by the supervisor BEFORE it is worked; wages calculated by someone other than the person who pays them out; and idle time recorded with reasons — machinists waiting for canvas points at planning, not at people.

Over OVERHEADS: a budget for each overhead line with monthly comparison; meters read rather than estimated; maintenance scheduled so that breakdowns never become emergencies bought at any price.

The ETHICS reach past theft. Quality: swapping in cheaper stitching on a bag that must carry a child's books daily is a decision about safety and durability, not merely cost. Fair labour: legal wages, safe machines, guards on the cutting tables. Honest costing: a factory that tips its dye waste into the stormwater drain has not reduced its costs — it has moved them onto the community, and a costing that ignores that is a lie told in numbers. And environmental duty carries teeth: fines and shutdown orders convert moral costs into financial ones very quickly. The final questions of this part are with you now: a control matched to each risk, and the ethical line defended in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same factory from a market-day table — because anyone who has ever cooked to sell already understands manufacturing.

## Subtopic: The Samoosa Stand

You are frying samoosas for the Saturday market, four hundred of them. What does one samoosa COST? Start sorting, and you are already doing cost accounting.

Pastry, mince, spices — you can point at them folded inside the samoosa. Those are DIRECT MATERIALS. Your aunt, who folds and fries all morning for a fee — her pay is DIRECT LABOUR: hands on the product. Add the two together and you have PRIME COST: the costs that literally touch the samoosa.

But the stand also burned gas, and you hired the gas bottle and the table, and a borrowed deep-fryer is slowly wearing out in your service. None of that lives INSIDE any one samoosa, yet without it nothing gets fried at all. That basket of costs is FACTORY OVERHEADS — the cost of keeping the making possible. Prime cost plus overheads: the TOTAL COST OF MAKING.

Two costs stay out of the samoosa completely. The posters you printed to advertise the stand — selling cost. The notebook and the airtime for taking orders — admin. They are costs of the DAY, not costs of the SAMOOSA, and keeping them out of the making cost is what keeps the unit cost honest.

Then the second sorting: which costs swell when you fry more? Pastry, mince, spices — double the samoosas, double the pastry: VARIABLE. The table hire is the same whether you fry forty or four hundred: FIXED. Feel the two families pulling in different directions: the fixed table gets cheaper PER samoosa the more you fry — and that single sentence is the seed of the entire next lesson. Quick check before we carry on — questions on sorting the stand's costs are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: Pastry, Half-Folded, and Ready to Sell

A trader has one box of stock: things ready to sell. Your stand has three, and so does every factory. Look down your row of tables.

Table one: the INGREDIENTS — pastry, mince, spices, bought but untouched. Raw materials. Table two: the HALF-DONE — pastry rolled, samoosas folded but not yet fried, fried but not yet boxed. Work-in-progress: money caught mid-becoming. Table three: the DISPLAY BOX — finished samoosas ready for a customer's hand. Finished goods, the only table a customer may buy from.

Every table runs the same simple arithmetic you have used since grade 10: what was there, plus what arrived, minus what is still there, equals what moved on. The factory's canvas behaves exactly like your pastry: Mokoena Backpack Works began with R72 000 of canvas and zips, bought R468 000 more, paid R12 000 to truck it in, and ended the year with R64 000 still on the shelves. So what got USED in the making: R488 000. Not the purchases — the USED. The storeroom keeps back its closing stock, and only the issued materials become part of the backpacks.

The machine oil, the needles, the cleaning cloths — consumed by the making but never inside the product — get their own small box called consumable stores, and from there they slide into overheads as indirect materials. Everything has a table, every table obeys the grammar, and the exam's favourite move — giving three of the four figures and hiding the fourth — is beaten by the grammar alone. Your questions for this part are up now: place each item on its table, and recover the missing figure.

## Subtopic: One Backpack, Followed All the Way

Now walk one year of the backpack factory end to end, the way a rand walks it.

Materials issued to the floor: R488 000. Machinists' wages travelling alongside: R366 000. Together — prime cost, R854 000: everything that touched the bags. Then the factory's basket tips in: supervisor, rent, electricity, machine depreciation, insurance, oil and needles — R356 000 of overheads. Poured into the making this year: R1 210 000.

But the floor was not bare on the first of July — R38 000 of half-made bags waited from last year — and it is not bare at year end either: R48 000 of half-sewn panels still lie by the machines. So what emerged as finished backpacks: R38 000 plus R1 210 000 minus R48 000 — R1 200 000 of completed bags, carried through to the finished goods store.

Count the bags: 20 000 finished this year. So one backpack cost R1 200 000 over 20 000 — R60 a bag. That number is the factory's heartbeat. Price the bag at R90 and you know, before selling a single one, that R30 per bag is available to carry the office, the deliveries and the profit. Watch the R60 creep upward over the months and you know something on the floor is leaking — more canvas per bag, slower sewing, a heavier electricity bill — long before any statement is drawn.

Final leg: the store. Bags costing R76 000 waited at the start, R1 200 000 marched in, R96 000 remain at year end — so bags costing R1 180 000 went out to customers. Sales of R1 770 000 against R1 180 000: gross profit of R590 000, and the chain closes clean. One rand, one path: shelves, floor, store, customer. Learn the path and no manufacturing question can hide from you. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: walk the path, name each hand-over, and compute the unit cost yourself.
