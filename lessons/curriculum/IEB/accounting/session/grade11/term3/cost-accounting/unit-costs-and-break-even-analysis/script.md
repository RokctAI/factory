# Part 1 — Expert

The previous lesson measured what one backpack COSTS. This lesson answers the question that actually keeps an owner awake: how many must the business SELL before it stops losing money? That number is the BREAK-EVEN POINT, reached through contribution per unit, and it is the sharpest decision tool in this course. Today: cost behaviour made precise — fixed and variable, viewed per unit and in total; contribution per unit and what it exists to pay for; the break-even calculation itself, and why the answer always rounds UP; and break-even at work — margin of safety, comparison against the plan, and what-if testing when prices or costs shift. Our example continues: Mokoena Backpack Works, budgeting the year ahead.

## Subtopic: How Costs Behave When Output Moves

The coming year's budget, stated in full. Planned production and sales: 20 000 backpacks. Selling price: R120 per bag. VARIABLE costs per bag: direct materials R34, direct labour R22, and selling and distribution — packaging and commission per bag sold — R8. Total variable cost: R64 per bag. FIXED costs for the year: factory overheads of R356 000 and administration of R204 000 — R560 000 altogether, owed whether the factory sews one bag or thirty thousand.

Now view each family from both angles, because this is where marks leak. Variable costs are constant PER UNIT — every bag consumes R64 — but grow IN TOTAL with output: at 8 000 bags, R512 000; at 20 000 bags, R1 280 000. Fixed costs are the exact mirror: constant IN TOTAL at R560 000, but shrinking PER UNIT as more units share them — R70 per bag at 8 000 bags, R28 at 20 000. That shrinking is why factories chase volume, and it is also why a unit cost that includes fixed costs is only true AT the output it was calculated for.

The classification traps live here too. Rent is fixed — until a question adds a second workshop for extra output, and the rent steps. Commission is variable, but it is a SELLING cost, not production. Electricity often splits in two: a fixed basic charge plus a variable usage charge — name both parts when a question shows them. The discipline: classify each cost by its behaviour in THIS question's facts, never from memory of the last question. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: costs classified, and both views — per unit and in total — computed.

## Subtopic: Contribution Per Unit

Sell one backpack for R120. That bag's variable costs — R64 — walk out the door with it: canvas, sewing, packaging, commission. What stays behind is R56, and that R56 has a name: the CONTRIBUTION PER UNIT. Selling price per unit, less variable cost per unit.

Contribution is the most misunderstood word in the topic, so pin its meaning down now: it is NOT profit. It is what each bag CONTRIBUTES toward the wall of fixed costs — the R560 000 that stands there regardless. Every bag sold throws R56 at that wall. Until the wall is fully paid, the business as a whole is still losing money; once the wall is paid, each further bag's R56 has nothing left to settle, and only then does it drop through as pure profit.

Prove the year using contribution, spoken in full. Planned sales, 20 000 bags at R120: revenue R2 400 000. Total variable costs, 20 000 at R64: R1 280 000. TOTAL CONTRIBUTION: R1 120 000 — confirm it as 20 000 bags times R56. Less fixed costs of R560 000: budgeted PROFIT of R560 000. The whole income statement of a factory in four lines: sales, less variable costs, contribution, less fixed costs, profit. Every break-even question is that little statement rearranged.

Feel what the two cost families do inside it: the variable R64 scales with every decision made; the fixed R560 000 stands still and waits to be paid off. The entire next subtopic is one question — how many R56 payments does it take to clear R560 000? Stop for this section's questions now: contribution computed, separated from profit, and the four-line statement assembled.

## Subtopic: The Break-Even Point

The BREAK-EVEN POINT is the output at which total contribution exactly equals total fixed costs — no profit, no loss. The formula, in words: total fixed costs, divided by contribution per unit.

Compute it for Mokoena Backpack Works: R560 000 divided by R56 — 10 000 backpacks. Check it at 10 000 bags, in full: revenue is 10 000 times R120 — R1 200 000. Variable costs: 10 000 times R64 — R640 000. Contribution: R560 000 — precisely the fixed costs. Profit: zero. That R1 200 000 is the break-even VALUE — break-even units times selling price — and questions ask for the rand figure as often as the units.

Now interpret the number against the plan, because a bare 10 000 earns only half the marks. Planned production is 20 000 bags, so the business breaks even at exactly half its planned output — 50 percent of it. Every bag from number 10 001 onward drops R56 of pure profit, and the 10 000 bags beyond the line deliver the budgeted R560 000. Say it in exam language: the break-even point of 10 000 bags sits well below the planned 20 000, so the budget carries a substantial cushion.

Two disciplines protect the calculation. First: only FIXED costs go on top of the fraction, and only CONTRIBUTION PER UNIT below it — slipping a variable cost into the numerator, or dividing by the selling price instead of the contribution, are the two classic wrecks. Second: when the division is not exact, round UP, every time. If fixed costs were R560 100, the division gives 10 001 comma 8 — and 10 001 bags would still leave a sliver of loss. The business breaks even only at 10 002. Break-even is a threshold, not an estimate: the wall is either paid or it is not. Pause for this section's questions: the point computed in units and in rand, interpreted against the plan, and rounded the right way.

## Subtopic: Working With Break-Even

Break-even earns its keep the moment things CHANGE. Three standard uses, worked with our figures.

MARGIN OF SAFETY: how far sales may fall before the loss line. Planned 20 000 less break-even 10 000: 10 000 bags — as a percentage of the plan, 50 percent. Sales can halve before Mokoena loses money. A thin margin — five or ten percent — reads as fragile: one poor season from a loss.

WHAT-IF: PRICE. A school-outfitting chain dangles a large contract if the price drops to R112. New contribution: R112 less R64 — R48. New break-even: R560 000 over R48 — 11 666 comma 7, so 11 667 bags. An eight-rand price cut lifted the survival line by one thousand six hundred and sixty-seven bags. That asymmetry is the lesson: a price cut lands directly on the contribution, so a small cut moves break-even violently. The decision question that follows: will the contract's extra volume earn back more than the contribution surrendered on every existing bag?

WHAT-IF: COSTS. Canvas rises by R4 per bag: variable cost R68, contribution R52, break-even R560 000 over R52 — 10 769 comma 2, so 10 770 bags. Or the landlord lifts fixed costs by R56 000: the wall becomes R616 000 over the unchanged R56 — exactly 11 000 bags. Notice the two different routes: a variable increase shrinks every bag's contribution; a fixed increase grows the wall the contributions must pay. Either way the cushion thins — and management sees it BEFORE the lease or the supply contract is signed. That is the tool's entire purpose: it prices decisions before they are made.

The limits, for full marks in a discussion answer: the model assumes the selling price and the unit variable cost hold constant at any volume, that fixed costs never step, and that everything made is sold — assumptions that hold near the planned range and fray far outside it. Used within its range, break-even analysis is the cheapest management consultant a small factory will ever appoint. The final questions of this part are with you now: margin of safety, both what-ifs, and the assumptions named.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea from a Saturday at the sports grounds — because anyone who has ever sold anything at a tournament already carries break-even in their bones.

## Subtopic: The Pile of Stones

You want to sell pap-and-steak plates at the Saturday soccer tournament. Before you dish plate number one, Saturday has already cost you: R340 to hire the braai stand, R220 for the gazebo and the cooler boxes. R560 spent — nothing sold yet. That R560 is your pile of stones. It does not care how your day goes. Sell zero plates, the pile is R560. Sell three hundred, still R560.

Then every plate carries its own little costs that travel WITH it: the steak, the pap, the chakalaka, the takeaway container — say R64 a plate. Sell the plate for R120, let R64 leave with the ingredients, and what stays in your pocket from that one plate is R56.

That R56 is the magic number of the whole lesson: the CONTRIBUTION. It is not profit — not yet. It is one stone lifted off the pile. Plate after plate, R56 at a time, the pile shrinks. All morning you are not earning; you are digging yourself out of the R560 hole that Saturday dug before your first customer arrived.

And watch the two kinds of cost behave differently as the queue grows. Ingredients scale: every extra plate costs another R64, forever. The braai stand does not: whether you dish ten plates or a mountain, the hire stays R560 — so the busier the day, the cheaper the stand becomes per plate. Big sellers win twice: more contributions coming in, and a thinner slice of fixed cost riding on each plate. Quick check before we carry on — questions on the pile and the travelling costs are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: Plate Number Ten

So the only question that matters while you set up: how many plates until the pile is gone? Each plate lifts R56. The pile weighs R560. R560 divided by R56: TEN. Plate number ten pays off the last stone. Plates one to ten: you are working for the braai-stand man, the gazebo man, the cooler boxes. Plate ELEVEN is the first plate you sell for yourself — and from there, every plate drops R56 of pure profit into your pocket.

That is the break-even point, and the factory version just adds zeros: Mokoena Backpack Works' pile is R560 000, each bag contributes R56, so the factory's plate number ten is bag number 10 000. Selling the planned 20 000 bags means 10 000 bags sold entirely for profit — R560 000 of it.

Two habits to carry from the grounds into the exam. First: if the division comes out messy — say the pile over the contribution gives ten comma four — you cannot dish comma four of a plate: you break even on plate ELEVEN. Round the survival line UP, always; at ten you are still a stone short. Second: ask where the survival line sits inside your plan. Expecting to sell twenty plates with break-even at ten: comfortable — ten plates of cushion, half your plan. Expecting eleven with break-even at ten: one rained-out match away from a loss, and you should be nervous. The line means nothing on its own; it means everything next to what you expect to sell. Your questions for this part are up now: find plate number ten in three different setups, and say how much cushion each one carries.

## Subtopic: When the Price of Meat Goes Up

The real power move is asking what-if BEFORE Saturday arrives — because every change in your setup drags the survival line, and you want to watch where it lands while you can still say no.

The butcher raises steak by R4 a plate. Your travelling cost becomes R68, your contribution drops to R52, and the pile of R560 now needs R560 over R52 — ten comma eight — so ELEVEN plates just to survive. One price rise, one extra plate before you earn a cent.

A club manager offers to buy for his whole team — but at R112 a plate. Contribution falls to R48; the survival line climbs to eleven comma seven — TWELVE plates. See the trap in slow motion: an R8 discount FEELS small against a R120 price, but it comes straight out of your R56 pocket-money — one seventh of your contribution, gone. Price cuts always bite harder than they look, because they bite the contribution, not the price. Take the deal only if the extra plates more than repay what every single plate now gives up.

And if the braai-stand man lifts his hire by R56? The pile grows to R616, and at R56 a plate the line moves to exactly eleven. A different route — a bigger pile instead of smaller stones — but the same destination: more plates just to reach zero.

This is why the backpack factory runs the identical sums before signing anything: canvas up R4 a bag pushes break-even to 10 770 bags; a contract at R112 pushes it to 11 667; rent up R56 000 pushes it to 11 000 exactly. None of these are surprises if you calculate BEFORE you commit — and all of them are disasters discovered months later if you don't. The survival line is not a report card at year end; it is a headlight. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: drag the line all three ways — dearer meat, a cheaper selling price, pricier hire — and decide, with numbers, which deals deserve a yes.
