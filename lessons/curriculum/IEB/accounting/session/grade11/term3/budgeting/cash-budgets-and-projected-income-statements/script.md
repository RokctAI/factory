# Part 1 — Expert

In grade 10 the cash budget arrived as an idea; this year it becomes a working tool, and it brings a partner with it — the PROJECTED STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME. A business prepares both documents for the same future months, and month after month they refuse to agree. The disagreement is not an error; it is the whole subject. Today: what each document forecasts and why the answers differ; the debtors' collection schedule and the creditors' payment schedule that supply the cash budget's hardest figures; a complete worked quarter; variance analysis, which is what turns a budget into a management instrument; and the ethics and internal controls that belong to any forecast a lender or an owner will rely on.

## Subtopic: Two Documents, Two Questions

Start with the question each document is built to answer, because every technical difference grows from there.

The PROJECTED STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME asks: what will the business EARN? It forecasts income earned against expenses incurred, month by month, on the ACCRUAL basis you have used all year. A sale on credit belongs to the month the goods change hands, whether or not a cent has been received. Depreciation belongs there too, because the equipment is being used up. So do expected bad debts, because a known slice of credit sales will never be paid.

The CASH BUDGET asks a colder question: what will be in the BANK? It forecasts receipts against payments on the CASH basis. That same credit sale only enters the cash budget when the customer actually pays — often one, two or three months later. Depreciation is nowhere to be found, since no money leaves the account when a machine wears. Bad debts never appear either; they are simply receipts that fail to arrive.

Each document also carries items the other will not accept. The cash budget alone shows capital the owner pays in, drawings taken out, loans raised and instalments of loan capital repaid, equipment bought for cash, and VAT paid over to the revenue service — money moving without any income being earned or expense incurred. The projected income statement alone shows depreciation, bad debts and the accrued portions of expenses — value consumed without money moving that month.

So the sorting question to put to every line an exam question throws at you: does this item move MONEY in this month? Cash budget. Does it earn income or use up value in this month? Projected income statement. Does it do both? Then it appears in both — quite possibly in different months. Learners who interrogate each line this way collect steadily; learners who copy figures from one document into the other lose marks in pairs.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two documents told apart, and a list of items sorted into one, the other, or both.

## Subtopic: The Debtors' Collection Schedule

The single hardest number in any cash budget is what the debtors will actually hand over, and it is worked out in its own schedule before it goes anywhere near the budget.

A typical set of terms: sales are 30 percent for cash and 70 percent on credit. Debtors settle 60 percent of their debt in the month AFTER the sale, earning a 5 percent settlement discount for promptness; 25 percent in the second month after the sale; and 10 percent in the third month. The last 5 percent never arrives and is written off as bad debts.

Now build the schedule for a quarter with total sales of R180 000 in April, R240 000 in May and R280 000 in June, with March sales of R160 000 standing behind them.

Work June's collections. Credit sales are 70 percent of each month's total: March R112 000, April R126 000, May R168 000. In June the business receives: 60 percent of MAY's credit sales, less the discount — 60 percent of 168 000 is 100 800, and 5 percent off leaves R95 760; plus 25 percent of APRIL's credit sales — 25 percent of 126 000 — R31 500; plus 10 percent of MARCH's credit sales — 10 percent of 112 000 — R11 200. June's collection from debtors: R138 460. Add June's own cash sales — 30 percent of 280 000 — R84 000 — and receipts from trading come to R222 460.

Three habits separate full marks from half marks. First: the settlement discount is deducted ONLY from the prompt 60 percent, never from the whole month's collections — the slower payers earned no discount. Second: one month's collections draw on THREE earlier months' sales, so lay the schedule out as a grid, sales down the side and collection months across; attempting it in your head tangles the strands. Third: the 5 percent written off makes no appearance in the cash budget whatsoever — it belongs to the projected income statement as bad debts. One percentage, two documents, two opposite treatments: that split is the very lesson this topic teaches.

The CREDITORS' PAYMENT SCHEDULE is the mirror image on the payments side: stock bought on credit is paid according to the supplier's terms — perhaps 70 percent in the month after purchase and 30 percent in the second month, with a discount for quick settlement — and only the PAYMENTS enter the cash budget, while the purchases reach the projected income statement through cost of sales when the goods are sold.

Stop for this section's questions now — a collection schedule assembled month by month, the discount confined to the portion that earns it, and the bad-debt slice routed to the correct document.

## Subtopic: Building the Cash Budget and the Projected Income Statement

With the schedules done, assemble both documents for the same quarter — and watch where they part ways.

The CASH BUDGET has a fixed frame, repeated for each month. RECEIPTS: cash sales, collections from debtors, rent income received, capital contributed, loans received, proceeds of assets sold. PAYMENTS: amounts paid to creditors, cash purchases of stock, salaries and wages, rent paid, other operating payments actually made, drawings, assets bought, loan instalments, interest paid. Then the chain that holds the whole document together: receipts less payments gives the month's SURPLUS or DEFICIT; add the OPENING bank balance and you have the CLOSING balance — which steps forward to become the next month's opening balance. If a closing balance goes negative, the budget is forecasting an overdraft, and that forecast, seen months in advance, is the most valuable warning the document can give.

The PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT for the same quarter follows the format you know: sales — the FULL figure, cash and credit together, in the month of sale — less cost of sales, giving gross profit; add other operating income; subtract operating expenses, including depreciation, bad debts and the full accrued amounts of expenses; that gives operating profit; subtract interest expense for net profit.

Here is the divergence that discussion questions love: the same business, in the same month, can forecast a strong PROFIT and a frightening cash SHORTFALL. How? June's credit sales earn June's profit, but the money lands in July, August and September; the stock behind those sales was paid for in May; and perhaps a machine was bought for cash in June. The income statement says the business is thriving; the bank column says the month-end salaries are in danger. Both statements are true at once. A complete answer names the timing gap and the specific items driving it — credit sales collected later, stock paid earlier, an asset bought for cash — and never settles for "one is cash and one is profit".

The questions on this section are in front of you now — both documents drafted for one month, and the strong-profit, empty-bank scenario explained with named items.

## Subtopic: Variances, Ethics and Internal Control

Once the forecast month has actually happened, the budget begins its second career. VARIANCE ANALYSIS sets the BUDGETED figure beside the ACTUAL figure, line by line, and demands an explanation for each gap.

A variance is stated fully or not at all: name the item, give both figures, express the difference in rand and as a percentage, and label it FAVOURABLE — income above budget or expense below — or UNFAVOURABLE. Then interpret, because interpretation is where the marks sit. Sales below budget might mean fading demand, a lost contract, a new competitor, or simply a forecast that was too hopeful. An expense above budget might mean price increases, waste, theft, or an estimate set too low. And guard against the comfortable error: an expense UNDER budget is not automatically a victory. Advertising spent at half the planned level may be the exact cause of the sales shortfall on the line above it, and maintenance postponed is a breakdown ordered for next year. Variances are read as a set, never one at a time.

The ETHICS attach to budgets precisely because budgets are guesses. Inflate the projected sales and a bank may approve finance it would otherwise refuse — presenting a projection the preparer knows to be unreachable, in order to obtain a loan, is misrepresentation: a fraud on the lender. Pad the expense lines and a manager builds slack in which later overspending can hide. Inside organisations there is the chronic version: departments requesting more than they need because they expect to be trimmed, until every number in the plan is a bargaining position instead of an estimate.

The INTERNAL CONTROLS answer those risks directly: budgets drawn up WITH the people who must achieve them, not dropped on them; every assumption — collection percentages, price increases, growth rates — written down where it can be challenged and tested; formal approval by the owner or senior management; a monthly comparison of actual against budget, with written explanations required for material variances; and authorisation limits, so that spending beyond the budget needs a fresh decision rather than a shrug. The INTERNAL AUDIT questions follow: do the assumptions square with the business's own past performance; were material variances investigated and explained; and did anyone commit money outside the approved budget without authority?

Hold the through-line: a budget that is never compared with reality is a wish, and a budget built dishonestly is a lie. The monthly comparison is what promotes it to a control.

The final questions of this part are with you now: variances calculated, classified and read together, and the ethical scenarios paired with the controls that defeat them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two documents from an ordinary Sunday-evening money conversation — because anyone who has ever been owed money at month end already understands this entire topic.

## Subtopic: Owed Money Is Not Money

Your brother washes cars on weekends. In June he washes sixty cars at R150 each — R9 000 of work, his best month yet. Ask him how June went and he'll tell you: excellent. But a third of his customers said "sort you out at month end". So on the thirtieth of June he is holding R6 000 in cash and R3 000 in promises.

Watch what each number is for. Did he EARN well in June? Absolutely — R9 000 of washing happened, and the work is complete. Can he buy a new pressure washer on the first of July? Only R6 000 says yes. The first question is a PROFIT question. The second is a CASH question. One month, two truthful answers, and they don't match.

That mismatch is exactly why a business runs two forecasts side by side. One predicts what the coming months will EARN — the projected income statement. The other predicts what the bank account will actually HOLD — the cash budget. They cover identical months and never quite agree, because earning and receiving run on different calendars — the same idea you met when accruals first appeared.

Two sorting rules fall straight out of that, and they harvest most of the marks. Whatever moves money belongs in the cash budget — even things that are not income or expenses at all: the owner drawing cash, buying a delivery scooter, paying off a chunk of a loan. And whatever consumes value without moving money belongs only in the profit forecast — chiefly depreciation: the scooter is wearing out this month, but not one rand left the account because of the wearing.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two forecasts are coming to you right now: earned versus received, and each item filed under its document.

## Subtopic: Working Out What Will Actually Arrive

The trickiest forecast in the whole budget is the money owed to you, because you are predicting other people's behaviour. Businesses tame it with a schedule — and the schedule is friendly once you build it backwards.

Suppose the shop's pattern is: thirty rand in every hundred of sales is cash at the till; seventy is on credit. Of the credit portion, sixty percent arrives in the very next month — those quick payers get a small discount — twenty-five percent arrives the month after that, ten percent the month after THAT, and five percent never arrives at all.

Now ask the backwards question: whose money lands in June? Not June's credit customers — they only start paying in July. Landing in June is: sixty percent of May's credit sales, a quarter of April's, a tenth of March's. Three different months' selling, arriving in one month's bank account — which is exactly why you draw a grid, sales down the side and arrival months across, instead of juggling it mentally.

Put numbers in. Credit sales were R112 000 in March, R126 000 in April, R168 000 in May. In June: sixty percent of May's R168 000 is R100 800, and the prompt payers take their five percent discount, leaving R95 760. A quarter of April's R126 000 — R31 500. A tenth of March's R112 000 — R11 200. Altogether, R138 460 arriving from debtors. Add June's own cash sales and that is what the bank statement will actually show.

Two traps swallow most of the lost marks. The discount belongs ONLY to the group that earned it — the sixty percent paying promptly — never to the whole collection. And the five percent that never comes? It never touches the cash budget; you cannot plan to receive money that will not arrive. It lives in the profit forecast instead, under bad debts. Same five percent, two documents, two opposite treatments.

Your questions for this part are up now: build a collection grid, keep the discount with its own group, and file the bad-debt slice where it belongs.

## Subtopic: Profitable and Broke at the Same Time

Here is the scenario that shuts down businesses in their best season — and being able to explain it means you own this topic.

A shop posts its record month: R280 000 of sales, most of it on credit to reliable customers who will pay in July and August. Genuine profit, honestly earned. Now open June's bank account: the stock behind those sales was paid for in May; wages went out mid-June; the owner bought a delivery scooter for cash; and the customers owing R140 000 haven't paid a cent yet. Money in: a trickle. Money out: a flood. The account hits zero in the very month the business broke its own sales record.

That is why, week to week, the cash budget outranks the profit forecast. Profit measures whether the business deserves to survive; cash decides whether it does. A business can absorb one poor profit month; it cannot absorb one payday with nothing in the account.

And because the budget reveals the squeeze IN ADVANCE, the owner gets the cheap options: postpone the scooter by a couple of months; arrange an overdraft early and calmly, while the bank is looking at a strong business; tighten the credit terms; ask the supplier for another thirty days. Every one of those is a phone call in April and a crisis in June.

Then, when June is over, the budget's second job begins: line up what you PREDICTED against what HAPPENED. Sales R25 000 short — why? Wages R6 000 over — why? And keep the subtle one close: an expense coming in under budget is not automatically good news. If advertising ran at half the plan, look sideways at the sales line — that "saving" may have bought the shortfall. Lines are read together, never alone.

One honest closing note: because a budget is a guess, it can be dressed up. Inflate the sales line and a bank might lend what it shouldn't; pad the expenses and there is space to bury overspending later. That is why sound budgets are built with the people who must deliver them, why the assumptions are written down where anyone can test them, and why actuals are compared every month. A budget nobody checks is a wish. A budget built wrong on purpose is a lie.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: explain profitable-but-broke using the actual items that cause it, and read a set of variances as a set. Get those right and you can genuinely advise a small business — which is a skill worth far more than the marks.
