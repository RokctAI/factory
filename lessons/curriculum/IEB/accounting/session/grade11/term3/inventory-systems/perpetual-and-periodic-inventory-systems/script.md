# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 recorded stock in one way only: the perpetual system, where every sale dragged its cost of sales entry along with it. That was a choice, not a law of nature — and today we meet the alternative. The PERIODIC system records purchases instead of stock movements, and computes cost of sales only at period end, from a formula. On today's route: how each system operates; the cost of sales calculation under both; the gross profit checks that follow; the advantages and disadvantages that decide which system a business should run; and the stock controls and ethics that complete the topic. Questions set this topic both as a comparison and as a calculation — you need fluency in both.

## Subtopic: Two Systems, Two Philosophies

The PERPETUAL (continuous) system keeps the Trading Stock account permanently current. Every purchase is debited to Trading Stock; every sale fires TWO entries — the selling price to Debtors or Bank and to Sales, and at the same moment the COST of the goods out of Trading Stock into Cost of Sales. At any hour of any trading day, the account therefore states what ought to be on the shelf. Returns adjust it in both directions; the physical stocktake tests its claim, and the difference between claim and count becomes the trading stock deficit or surplus you already know from grade 10.

The PERIODIC system keeps no running stock record at all. Goods bought are debited to a PURCHASES account — an expense-type account, not an asset — and sales are recorded at selling price only, with no cost entry alongside. The Trading Stock account stands untouched all year at its opening balance. At period end the business COUNTS its stock physically, and only then works out what was sold — by formula. Between counts, nobody knows the stock figure: the system trades information away for simplicity.

The design difference in one sentence: the perpetual system records COST AT EVERY MOVEMENT; the periodic system records PURCHASES and reasons cost of sales out backwards at the end. Two philosophies — continuous knowledge versus periodic reckoning — and every advantage and disadvantage that follows flows from that one choice.

## Subtopic: Cost of Sales in Both Systems

The perpetual system needs no year-end cost of sales calculation — the account has accumulated it all year. But questions still make you derive it, usually from the mark-up. With a mark-up of 60 percent on cost and sales of R480 000: sales represent 160 percent of cost, so cost of sales is 480 000 times 100 over 160 — R300 000, and gross profit is R180 000. When the mark-up is quoted on SELLING PRICE instead — say 25 percent — cost of sales is simply sales less that quarter. Reading which base the percentage stands on is worth the entire question.

The periodic system's calculation is the topic's signature formula, and it must come out complete:

Opening stock, PLUS purchases, PLUS carriage on purchases, LESS purchases returns, LESS drawings of stock, gives COST OF GOODS AVAILABLE FOR SALE; then LESS closing stock gives COST OF SALES.

Work it with figures. Opening stock R70 000; purchases R480 000; carriage on purchases R15 000 — freight INWARD is part of the cost of landing stock on the shelf, so it joins the calculation, while carriage OUTWARD, delivering to customers, is a selling expense and stays out; purchases returns R10 000; and the owner took stock of R5 000 home for private use. Goods available for sale: 70 000 plus 480 000 plus 15 000, less 10 000, less 5 000 — R550 000. The year-end physical count values closing stock at R88 000. Cost of sales: 550 000 less 88 000 — R462 000.

Then gross profit, identical in either system: sales less cost of sales. Against sales of R660 000, our R462 000 leaves a gross profit of R198 000 — and the mark-up achieved is 198 000 over 462 000, near enough 42,9 percent on cost, which the business holds up against its intended policy exactly as the grade 10 analysis work taught you.

Now name the periodic system's silent flaw, because questions probe it directly: any stock that vanished through theft, breakage or error is sitting INSIDE that R462 000. The formula assumes that everything not counted at the end was sold. There is no deficit figure, because there is nothing to compare the count against — losses hide inside cost of sales and quietly drag gross profit down without ever being named.

## Subtopic: Advantages, Disadvantages and the Choice

Judge each system by what it buys and what it charges.

The PERPETUAL system's advantages: the stock figure is available at any moment, so re-ordering is informed and stock-outs are avoidable; cost of sales and gross profit can be reported at any date without counting; and — decisively — the account's claim can be tested against a physical count, so theft and losses are IDENTIFIED and measured as a trading stock deficit. That is why it is called the control system. Its disadvantages: it demands a cost record for every item and every movement — administratively heavy, and in the days before computers, unworkable for a high-volume shop selling thousands of cheap lines.

The PERIODIC system's advantages: simplicity and low cost — no per-item records, no entry at each sale — which suits small businesses, and goods that are numerous, cheap and hard to track one by one. Its disadvantages: no stock figure between counts, so no informed re-ordering and no interim gross profit without counting; the business must close or count to learn where it stands; and — the serious one — NO CONTROL: losses are invisible, absorbed silently into cost of sales, so theft can run for a full year without one figure raising its hand.

The choice therefore follows the goods and the technology. High-value, individually identifiable items — vehicles, appliances, jewellery — argue overwhelmingly for perpetual: the value of knowing and the cost of losing are both high, and the items are few enough to record. Numerous low-value items once argued for periodic — but modern point-of-sale systems that scan a barcode and post the cost automatically have made perpetual practical even for supermarkets, which is why the periodic system now survives mainly in small businesses without such tills. Make that technological point in a comparison answer; it separates a candidate who understands the systems from one who memorised two lists.

## Subtopic: Stock Control, Ethics and Internal Audit

Whichever system runs in the ledger, the stock itself needs protecting — and the controls must be named specifically.

PHYSICAL controls: a locked, restricted storeroom with responsibility assigned to a named person; deliveries checked against the order and the delivery note before anything is signed; security at the exits; and separation of the duties of ordering, receiving, recording and paying, so that no single person commands a stock item's entire journey.

DOCUMENT controls: pre-numbered orders, delivery notes, invoices and credit notes, filed in sequence so that a missing number becomes a question; and authorisation limits, so that large orders need senior approval.

COUNTING controls: regular stocktakes — at least annually, better cyclically through the year — carried out by people INDEPENDENT of the storeroom, with the count reconciled to the records and every difference investigated rather than merely noted. In the perpetual system that reconciliation produces the deficit or surplus; in the periodic system, a business serious about control keeps informal stock records precisely so that some comparison stays possible.

The ETHICS. Employees helping themselves to stock — from the casual "it's only one" to organised theft — is the most common fraud in retail, and it is beaten by the routines above, not by trust. Managers manipulating the closing stock figure is the graver offence, and understand it mechanically: overstate closing stock and cost of sales falls, so gross profit and net profit RISE — one stroke of a pen that flatters the results, misleads owners and lenders, and rolls the loss into the following year, where it must finally surface. Suppliers short-delivering against full invoices, and staff who collude with them, round out the list.

The INTERNAL AUDIT tests that answer each risk: attend the stocktake and independently re-count a sample; trace a sample of delivery notes into the purchase records, and back the other way; compare the achieved gross profit percentage with the policy mark-up and chase any gap, because an unexplained shortfall is usually the first fingerprint of loss; and inspect slow-moving and obsolete lines, since both distort the closing figure. The through-line of the topic: a system that measures stock earns its keep only alongside routines that verify it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: controls named by category, the closing-stock manipulation traced through to profit, and the audit tests matched to their risks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two systems from behind the counter — because the real question is one every shop owner faces: do I write down what leaves my shelf, or do I count at the end and work it out?

## Subtopic: The Notebook or the Count

Two tuck-shop owners, one taxi rank between them, two ways of running stock.

The first keeps a notebook. Every time a two-litre cooldrink goes over the counter, she writes down not only the R25 she took, but that a bottle costing R18 left the shelf. Every delivery lands in the notebook too. So at any hour of any day she can open that notebook and say exactly what should be standing on her shelves and exactly what her goods have cost her so far this month. That is the perpetual system: record the cost of every movement, as it happens.

The second owner keeps no such notebook. He writes down what he BUYS — deliveries, invoices — and at the till he records only the money coming in. What his goods are costing him as he trades, he does not track. So how does he ever know? He counts. At the end of the year he locks the door, walks the shelves with a pen, and counts everything still standing. Then one sum: everything I started with, plus everything I bought, minus what is still here — that must be what I sold. That is the periodic system: don't track — count, and reason it backwards.

Both owners reach a cost of sales in the end. But look at what the second one surrendered. His sum trusts that everything missing from the shelf was SOLD. If a case of energy drinks slipped out the back door in August, his sum calmly calls it "sold" — because there is nothing left to count, and nothing to compare the count against.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two owners are coming to you right now: what each records, and what the counting owner is quietly assuming.

## Subtopic: Working It Out Backwards

Let's run the second owner's year-end sum properly, because it is exactly the calculation the exam wants.

Start with what stood on the shelves at the beginning: R70 000 of stock. Add everything he bought during the year: R480 000. Add what it cost to GET those goods to his shop — the transport on his purchases, R15 000; that money is part of what the stock cost him, so it belongs in the sum. But be careful with its twin: what he pays to deliver goods TO customers is a selling cost, not a stock cost — it stays out.

Now remove the things that left the pile without being sold. Goods sent back to suppliers: R10 000. And the stock he carried home for his own family: R5 000 — that is drawings, not a sale.

So the full pile that was available to sell: 70 000 plus 480 000 plus 15 000, minus 10 000, minus 5 000 — R550 000 of goods available. He counts the shelves: R88 000 still standing. So what left the shop? 550 000 minus 88 000 — R462 000. That is his cost of sales. And with sales of R660 000, his gross profit is R198 000.

That is the entire formula, in the order that keeps it honest: what I started with, plus what I bought and what it cost to get it here, minus returns and what I took home — that's the pile; minus what's still standing — that's what went out.

Now say the uncomfortable part out loud: that R462 000 includes the case of energy drinks that was stolen. His sum cannot tell theft from a sale, because both leave the same empty space on the shelf. The notebook owner would have caught it — her notebook says R2 100 of drinks should be standing there and the count finds none. His system simply calls the theft a sale, and his profit ends up quietly smaller with nobody able to say why.

## Subtopic: Which One Should the Shop Use?

So which owner has it right? It depends on what the shop sells — and, these days, on what its till can do.

If you sell FEW, EXPENSIVE things — fridges, laptops, cars — track every single one. Losing one item means losing thousands, and there are few enough items to record comfortably. Notebook, no debate.

If you sell THOUSANDS OF CHEAP things — sweets, loose fruit, single airtime vouchers — writing the cost of every item as it leaves is a full-time job that earns nothing. That is why small shops historically counted at the end instead: simple, cheap, good enough.

But one invention changed the whole argument: the barcode scanner. A modern till scans the item, takes the money AND posts the cost entry automatically — the notebook writes itself. That is why supermarkets, moving tens of thousands of items a day, all run the notebook system now, and why the counting system survives mainly where no such till exists. If a question asks you to advise a business, say exactly that: the choice used to turn on how many items you sell; today it mostly turns on whether the till can do the writing for you.

And the final point — the reason this topic is about more than tidy bookkeeping. The notebook system can do something the counting system never can: it lets you CATCH things. Count the shelf, hold it against what the notebook claims, and the gap has a size and a name. Without that comparison, stock can leak for a year and surface only as "profits were disappointing".

Which is where the ethics live. The ugliest trick in this topic is a manager recording the closing stock count HIGHER than it truly is — watch what that does to the sum: a bigger number subtracted at the end means a smaller cost of sales, which means a fatter profit on paper. The shelves are exactly as empty as before; the report glows; the owner and the bank are misled — and next year, when the truth can no longer be counted away, the loss arrives anyway. That is why the counting is done by people who do not run the storeroom, and why the counted figure is verified rather than accepted.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: choose a system for three different shops with reasons, and trace what an inflated closing stock figure does to cost of sales and profit. Master that one, and you understand stock better than many people trading with it.
