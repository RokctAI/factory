**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! Start with the one everyone whispers about: why do some formulas want AVERAGE figures — average stock, average debtors, average equity — while others are happy with the year-end number?

**Tutor:** The rule is about matching the two halves of the fraction in TIME. Look at what each formula divides. Cost of sales is a FLOW — it built up over the entire year, month by month. The stock figure underneath it should therefore represent the entire year too, and a single year-end snapshot cannot do that: stock might have been unusually low on 28 February after a strong summer of renovations, making turnover look faster than it ever truly was. Averaging opening and closing — R232 500 and R217 500 giving R225 000 — is a rough but honest stand-in for the year's typical shelf. The same logic runs through average debtors against the year's credit sales, average creditors against the year's credit purchases, and average equity underneath the year's net profit: a flow over a snapshot is a mismatch; a flow over an average is fair. The current ratio and acid-test, by contrast, compare a snapshot with a snapshot — assets ON the last day against liabilities ON the last day — so no averaging is needed; both feet already stand in the same moment. And a practical note: when a question supplies only the closing figure, use it and move on — the principle bends to the data available. When both opening and closing figures are given, that is a signal that the average is expected, and marks usually sit on that expectation.

**Bianca:** Next: my current ratio came out at 4,2 to 1 and I wrote that liquidity is excellent. Apparently a good answer criticises it too. How can a strong ratio be a problem?

**Tutor:** Because a ratio measures a POSITION, and every position has a price tag. Liquidity is protection: 4,2 to 1 means the shop cannot be embarrassed by its short-term debts, and the acid-test of 2,0 to 1 says that holds even if the stockroom never sells another tin. Superb protection — but protection is bought with idle resources. Money parked in a fat bank balance earns close to nothing; stock beyond what the shelves need ties up cash and risks fashion changes in tiles; and every rand sleeping in current assets is a rand not expanding the business, not settling the twelve percent loan, not earning early-settlement discounts from suppliers. So a complete comment runs in both directions: safe beyond question, AND over-liquid — with a recommendation attached, because advice is the fourth beat of the comment. Suggest putting the surplus to work: widen the range, reduce the loan, negotiate discounts for early payment where suppliers offer them. And the underlying skill transfers to every indicator you will ever meet: always ask what the position COSTS as well as what it protects. Too little liquidity threatens survival; too much squanders opportunity — and the strongest answers are written by learners who can see both edges of the same number.

**Bianca:** A learner sent this in: the return on equity is 62,4 percent, but the partners work in the shop all day. Isn't part of that return really their unpaid wages dressed up as investment income?

**Tutor:** Sharply spotted — and yes, partly, which is exactly why a full comment mentions their labour. Unpack the figure. The net profit of R528 000 was measured BEFORE any reward reached the partners, because their salaries, interest and bonus are appropriations, not expenses. So the 62,4 percent bundles the return on their MONEY together with the return on their WORK. To judge the investment fairly, run the thought experiment the question is quietly inviting: imagine Ngcobo and Venter stepped back and hired managers to replace themselves. Competent managers for the shop floor and the books might cost around R330 000 a year in genuine salary expense. Profit would fall to roughly R198 000, and the return on average equity to about 23,4 percent — R198 000 over R846 300. THAT is the clean return on money alone — and the verdict survives: twenty-three percent still beats the eight-to-ten of a fixed deposit comfortably, with the margin compensating for the business risk being carried. So the polished answer has three layers: quote the 62,4 percent, concede that the partners' labour lives inside it, and show that even after pricing that labour the investment outruns the alternative. Three layers, and the interpretation marks are yours in full.

**Bianca:** Last one: in comment questions I freeze — there is so much I COULD say about a ratio. How do I know what a marker actually wants?

**Tutor:** You stop improvising and play the four-beat bar, every single time. Beat one, FIGURE: quote this year's indicator next to its comparison — last year's figure, the norm, the policy, or the rival's. No naked numbers: "the collection period improved from 21 days to 15,5 days against 30-day terms" is a figure; "15,5" on its own is not. Beat two, DIRECTION: state plainly whether that is an improvement, a deterioration, or better or worse than the benchmark — nobody can award a judgement you never wrote down. Beat three, CAUSE: offer a plausible business reason rooted in this business: stricter screening of credit customers, an early-settlement discount offered, a tighter invoicing routine. You are not expected to be psychic — any sensible cause consistent with the figures earns the mark. Beat four, CONSEQUENCE or ADVICE: what it means or what should happen next — cash arrives sooner, bad-debt risk falls, keep the policy. Four beats, four sentences, and the mark allocation almost always maps onto them one for one. When a question says "comment on the liquidity", choose the two or three relevant indicators, play the bar for each, and close with a single overall verdict sentence. Structure is what a marker is trained to find; hand it over on a plate.
