# Part 1 — Expert

The statements of Ngcobo and Venter Traders are published. Now for the skill everything else has been preparing: READING them. Analysis and interpretation compresses statement figures into indicators — small, comparable numbers that answer the questions every partner, banker and competitor quietly asks. On today's list: the profitability percentages revised from grade 10; the four NEW operating-cycle indicators — stock turnover rate, stock holding period, debtors' collection period and creditors' payment period; the risk-and-reward pair — the debt-equity ratio and the return on average partners' equity; and the discipline of writing a comment that collects full interpretation marks. Every figure comes off the statements of Msunduzi Paint and Tile, built in the last lesson.

## Subtopic: Profitability Revised

Profitability asks one thing: out of every rand of sales, how much survives each stage? Speak each percentage with its formula.

Gross profit on sales: gross profit over sales, times one hundred — R1 080 000 over R2 880 000 — 37,5 percent. Every rand of sales keeps thirty-seven and a half cents after the goods themselves are paid for. Its twin, gross profit on COST of sales: R1 080 000 over R1 800 000 — 60 percent, and that figure should equal the intended mark-up. Here it does, to the decimal — the shop prices at cost plus sixty percent, and sixty percent is what came out. Achieved pricing policy means no undetected theft of takings, no quiet markdowns, no recording errors big enough to surface.

Operating expenses on sales: R600 000 over R2 880 000 — 20,8 percent. Operating profit on sales: R552 000 over R2 880 000 — 19,2 percent. Net profit on sales: R528 000 over R2 880 000 — 18,3 percent. Read them as a waterfall: sixty cents of mark-up per cost rand becomes thirty-seven and a half cents of margin per sales rand, expenses drink twenty-comma-eight, and eighteen-comma-three cents of every sales rand reaches the owners.

But an indicator only speaks when COMPARED — with last year, with the budget, with the mark-up policy, with a similar shop. A gross margin of 37,5 percent floats meaninglessly on its own; a margin that slid from 40 percent last year while the policy stayed at sixty demands an explanation — markdowns, stock stolen before sale, or errors. Interpretation starts the moment the comparison starts. Hold it there — this section's questions are with you now: each percentage computed, and its comparison named.

## Subtopic: The Operating Cycle: Four New Indicators

Grade 11 adds four indicators that time the operating rhythm of the business — how quickly stock sells, how quickly debtors pay, how slowly creditors are paid. Each one is a stopwatch on money moving through the cycle.

STOCK TURNOVER RATE: cost of sales over AVERAGE stock. Opening stock stood at R232 500, closing at R217 500 — average R225 000. R1 800 000 over R225 000: 8 times a year. The shelves empty and refill every six-and-a-half weeks or so. Its mirror image, the STOCK HOLDING PERIOD: average stock over cost of sales, times three hundred and sixty-five — 45,6 days of sales sitting on the shelves. One fact, two languages: the rate counts refills per year, the period counts days per refill.

DEBTORS' COLLECTION PERIOD: average debtors over CREDIT sales, times three hundred and sixty-five. Debtors averaged R61 000 — opening R58 000, closing R64 000. Credit sales were half of total sales: R1 440 000. R61 000 over R1 440 000, times three hundred and sixty-five: 15,5 days. Debtors settle in about a fortnight — strong against 30-day terms, and a sign the provision for bad debts is unlikely to come under strain.

CREDITORS' PAYMENT PERIOD: average creditors over credit purchases, times three hundred and sixty-five. Creditors averaged R93 000 — opening R91 800, closing R94 200 — against credit purchases of R1 356 000: 25 days. The shop pays its suppliers at day twenty-five on 30-day terms.

Now read the three clocks TOGETHER, because that is where the interpretation marks live. Money is locked in stock for about 46 days and in debtors for 15 or so — call it 61 days from buying stock to banking the cash — while suppliers are paid at day 25. The stretch between twenty-five and sixty-one must be financed out of the business's own pocket. Comment: collections are excellent; a six-and-a-half-week shelf suits paint and tiles but deserves watching; and paying creditors at 25 days on 30-day terms is GENEROUS — five days of free credit surrendered, unless an early-settlement discount is being earned in exchange. Pause for this section's questions now: all four indicators computed, and the cycle read as a single story.

## Subtopic: Gearing and Return: Risk and Reward

Two indicators pass judgement on the partnership's financing choices — one measures the risk being run, the other the reward being earned.

DEBT-EQUITY RATIO: non-current liabilities to owners' equity. The loan stands at R185 000; equity at R889 800. R185 000 over R889 800 — 0,2 to 1. For every rand the partners keep in the business, outsiders hold roughly twenty cents of long-term money. That is LOW gearing: light dependence on borrowed funds, light exposure to interest rate movements — a safe structure. But gearing is a dial, not a virtue. Borrowing earns its keep whenever the business makes MORE on the borrowed rand than the borrowing costs — and this business returns far above the loan's 12 percent, so a respectable argument exists for gearing UP to expand. An answer that can turn the dial both ways is the answer that collects the top marks.

RETURN ON AVERAGE PARTNERS' EQUITY: net profit over average equity, times one hundred. Equity opened the year at R802 800 — capital of R800 000 plus the opening current accounts, R9 600 credit less R6 800 debit, R2 800 — and closed at R889 800. Average: R846 300. R528 000 over R846 300 — 62,4 percent. Now set it against the ALTERNATIVE: a fixed deposit would pay the partners roughly 8 to 10 percent, risk-free. Their money inside Msunduzi Paint and Tile earned 62,4 — but remember what hides inside that number: the partners' own LABOUR. Ngcobo and Venter work in this shop; their salaries are appropriations, not expenses, so the return rewards their money AND their working days together. A fair comment says: even after mentally pricing that labour, the return beats the risk-free alternative many times over, so the investment stands.

And the SOLVENCY check seals the set: total assets to total liabilities — R1 175 400 to R285 600 — 4,1 to 1. Every debt could be paid four times over; going concern is not in question. Stop here for this section's questions: both indicators computed, the gearing dial argued in both directions, and the return measured against its alternative.

## Subtopic: Liquidity and the Art of the Comment

Liquidity asks the shortest question in the subject: can the business pay its bills THIS year? Two grade-10 ratios, recomputed on the new statements — then the discipline that converts figures into marks.

CURRENT RATIO: current assets to current liabilities — R420 400 to R100 600 — 4,2 to 1. ACID-TEST RATIO: current assets MINUS inventories, to current liabilities — R202 900 to R100 600 — 2,0 to 1. Even if not one more tin of paint sold, short-term debts are covered twice. Healthy — arguably too healthy: over R134 000 dozing in the bank earns next to nothing, and a sharp comment names the OVER-liquidity and proposes putting the idle cash to work — expansion, early-settlement discounts, or shrinking the loan.

Now the comment discipline, because interpretation questions carry more marks than calculations and are marked to a rhythm. A complete comment has four parts, in order. FIGURE: quote the indicator beside its comparison — this year against last year, against the norm, against the policy. TREND or COMPARISON: state in plain words whether it improved, worsened or beats the benchmark. CAUSE: give a plausible reason rooted in this business — not "sales rose" but "collections at 15,5 days against 30-day terms keep debtors small". CONSEQUENCE or ADVICE: say what it means, or what the partners should do next. Figure, comparison, cause, advice — four sentences, four marks, every time.

And the traps. Never judge one indicator alone: a climbing current ratio can mean strengthening liquidity or unsold stock silting up — only the stock holding period says which. Never mix the two languages: a turnover RATE rising is the identical news to a holding PERIOD falling. And never serve a ratio without its comparison: 4,2 to 1 says nothing until it stands beside last year, a norm, or a rival. The final questions of this part are with you now: both ratios computed, an over-liquidity comment built in four parts, and the paired-indicator traps disarmed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same indicators once more — this time from the athletics trials, because picking a team off a results board and judging a business off its statements is one and the same skill.

## Subtopic: Reading the Stats Sheet

No coach watches every race an athlete has ever run; she reads the results board — personal bests, split times, races finished. Small numbers standing in for a big performance. That is precisely what analysis does to a business: the statements are the whole season of racing, the indicators are the results board.

Profitability is the personal-best column. Out of every hundred rand of sales at Msunduzi Paint and Tile, thirty-seven and a half survive paying for the goods — the gross margin of 37,5 percent. Check it against the training plan: the shop prices everything at cost plus sixty percent, and sixty on cost works out to exactly thirty-seven and a half on sales, so the plan and the performance MATCH. The match matters more than the number: if the policy says sixty but the margin arrives at fifty-two, stock is leaking — taken before sale, quietly discounted, or misrecorded — and the results board has just blown the whistle.

Then the rand-by-rand waterfall: after the goods, the running costs take about twenty-one of every hundred; a touch over eighteen reaches the owners. Eighteen cents of every sales rand, kept.

And now the coach's first rule — the whole subject compressed into one line: a number means nothing alone. Eighteen percent — good? Next to last year's fifteen, wonderful. Next to a rival's twenty-six, worrying. Every number needs its NEXT TO: last year, the plan, the rival. No comparison, no meaning. Quick pause — the questions on the results board are on their way to you right now.

## Subtopic: The Rhythm Section

Some numbers measure talent; these four measure TEMPO — how fast money laps the shop. Picture one rand running the circuit: it becomes a tin of paint on a shelf, waits; becomes a customer's promise to pay, waits; and finally crosses the line as cash in the bank.

Lap one: the shelf. Stock turns over 8 times a year — the shelves empty and refill about every 46 days. Six and a half weeks a refill: perfectly reasonable for paint and tiles, disastrous if it were fresh bread. The norm belongs to the trade — tempo is always judged against the music being played.

Lap two: the promise. Credit customers pay in 15,5 days on average, against 30-day terms. Quick — these debtors run their leg in half the allowed time. Fast collection means less money trapped in promises, less bad-debt risk, more cash working.

Lap three runs the OTHER way: how long before the shop pays its own suppliers? Twenty-five days, on 30-day terms. And here is the one that surprises people: paying FAST is not automatically a virtue. Those unused five days are free credit — someone else's money the shop is entitled to keep using — so unless a prompt-payment discount is being collected, the shop is being polite at its own cost.

Now hear the full rhythm: money sits 46 days on the shelf plus 15 in promises — about 61 days out on the track — but the supplier is paid at day 25. From day 25 to day 61, the shop finances its own lap from its own pocket. Shorten the shelf time, keep the sprinting collections, use the full 30 days of supplier credit, and the lap needs far less of the shop's cash. That sentence, with those numbers attached, is a full-marks answer. Your questions for this section are up now — time all three laps and read the tempo as one piece.

## Subtopic: Was It Worth It?

Two questions end every trial. Can the team last the season? And was this the best use of everybody's talent?

Survival first — liquidity. Bills falling due within the year: R100 600. Resources arriving within the year: R420 400. More than four times covered; ignore every tin of paint on the shelf and it is still covered twice. No payday can embarrass this shop. If anything it is TOO safe — over R134 000 asleep in the bank earning close to nothing, and sleeping money is a quiet cost. The sharp answer says both halves out loud: safe, and slightly lazy.

Then risk. The shop's long-term debt is small beside the owners' money — about twenty cents of loan per rand of equity, a debt-equity ratio of 0,2 to 1. Low risk. But let the numbers argue: the loan costs twelve percent, and the business earns far more than twelve on the money it employs. That is a reason to borrow MORE, not less — carefully — because cheap money earning a fat return multiplies what the owners take home. Gearing is a volume knob, not a sin: low is safe, higher is louder, and the right setting depends on how steady the earnings are.

Finally the question the partners actually ask at the dinner table: was it worth it? Their money in the business — averaging R846 300 across the year — earned R528 000: a return of 62,4 percent. A bank deposit would have paid perhaps nine, risk-free. Even after remembering that the partners also gave the shop their working days, 62,4 against 9 is no contest. The results board's verdict: a profitable, liquid, low-risk shop that collects brilliantly, could use its supplier credit better, and repays its owners many times over the alternative.

And whenever you write any of this under time pressure, keep the four-beat bar: the figure, the thing it stands next to, the likely cause, the advice. Four beats, full marks. Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: hand down the verdict on this business in four-beat comments, indicator by indicator.
