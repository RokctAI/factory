# Part 1 — Expert

The ledger has finished its private work at Ngcobo and Venter Traders: the books are closed and the profit is divided. What remains is to go public. The final accounts were internal machinery, written in debits and credits for trained eyes only; the FINANCIAL STATEMENTS are the formal report the partnership presents to its partners, its bank, and anyone else entitled to look. Today we build both: the Statement of Comprehensive Income, line by line; the Statement of Financial Position, section by section; and then the NOTES — fixed assets, capital accounts, current accounts — where every headline number shows its workings. One business supplies every figure: Msunduzi Paint and Tile, the shop Ngcobo and Venter run in partnership, financial year ended 28 February.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Comprehensive Income

This statement answers a single question — how much better off did the year leave the owners — and it answers in a strict order. Speak it from the top.

Heading first, in full: Statement of Comprehensive Income of Ngcobo and Venter Traders for the year ended 28 February. FOR THE YEAR ENDED — never "on". This statement covers a stretch of time, the way a film covers a story, not a single frame.

Now the body. Sales, R2 880 000. Less cost of sales, R1 800 000. GROSS PROFIT: R1 080 000. Add other operating income — rent income of R45 600 and commission income of R26 400, together R72 000 — for gross operating income of R1 152 000. Less operating expenses, each on its own line, totalling R600 000: salaries and wages R312 000; depreciation R66 000, being R57 000 on the vehicles and R9 000 on the equipment; trading stock deficit R3 400; bank charges R11 200; insurance R24 800; telephone R19 600; provision for bad debts adjustment R600; bad debts R7 400; sundry expenses R155 000. OPERATING PROFIT: R552 000. Less interest expense — the R24 000 capitalised on the Sisonke Bank loan. NET PROFIT FOR THE YEAR: R528 000.

Look at what the year-end adjustments did to these lines. Rent income shows twelve honest months of rent, no more. Insurance excludes the portion paid ahead for next year. Depreciation and the provision adjustment sit here as expenses although not one rand of cash left the bank for either — and the interest expense appears although the bank was paid nothing in cash, because the interest was added onto the loan itself. The statement reports EARNING and CONSUMING, not receiving and paying — that is accrual accounting standing up in public. Notice, too, what is missing: the partners' salaries, interest on their capital, the bonus. Those are appropriations — the owners dividing the profit among themselves — and they happen AFTER the last line of this statement, inside the notes. Hold it there; the questions for this section are with you now, top of the statement to the bottom.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Financial Position

The second statement is the photograph: Statement of Financial Position of Ngcobo and Venter Traders ON 28 February — a date this time, not a period. Its question: what does the business own, and who holds claims against it? Build two towers, and demand that they weigh the same.

Tower one: ASSETS. Non-current assets lead — fixed assets at carrying value, R755 000, with the detail sent to a note. Then current assets, in their order: inventories, meaning the trading stock, R217 500; trade and other receivables — debtors of R64 000 less the R3 200 provision for bad debts gives net trade debtors of R60 800, then prepaid expenses of R3 100 and accrued income of R1 900; finally cash and cash equivalents — bank R134 600 and cash float R2 500. Current assets come to R420 400. TOTAL ASSETS: R1 175 400.

Tower two: EQUITY AND LIABILITIES. Owners' equity: capital accounts R800 000 plus current accounts R89 800 — R889 800, both lines proven in notes. Non-current liabilities: the Sisonke Bank loan at its closing balance of R185 000 — it opened at R200 000, the year's R24 000 of capitalised interest was added on, and R39 000 of repayments brought it down. Current liabilities: trade and other payables — creditors R94 200, accrued expenses R2 300 and income received in advance R4 100 — R100 600. TOTAL EQUITY AND LIABILITIES: R1 175 400. The towers agree, and that agreement is no accident: it is the accounting equation wearing its formal clothes.

Two disciplines of classification carry the marks. The first is time: whatever becomes cash or falls due within twelve months is current; anything longer is non-current — and if an instalment of the loan were payable within the coming year, that slice would migrate to current liabilities. The second is netting: debtors stand NET of the provision, fixed assets NET of accumulated depreciation. The face shows honest values; the notes hold the arithmetic. Pause for this section's questions now — both towers built, every item placed.

## Subtopic: The Notes: Fixed Assets

The face of each statement stays uncluttered because the NOTES absorb the detail, and the fixed assets note is the largest of them all. It reconciles each class of asset from the start of the year to the end. Take it class by class.

Land and buildings: carrying value R560 000, and no depreciation at all — land does not wear out, and the historical cost principle holds it at what it cost, whatever the property market is doing this month.

Vehicles: cost R285 000; accumulated depreciation at the START of the year R114 000; so an opening carrying value of R171 000. Depreciation for the year at 20 percent on cost: R57 000. Closing accumulated depreciation: R171 000. Closing carrying value: R114 000 — cost of R285 000 less accumulated depreciation of R171 000.

Equipment: cost R150 000; accumulated depreciation at the start R60 000; opening carrying value R90 000. Depreciation at 10 percent on the diminishing balance: R9 000. Closing accumulated depreciation R69 000, closing carrying value R81 000.

Now add the three closing carrying values: R560 000 plus R114 000 plus R81 000 — R755 000, precisely the single figure on the face of the statement. That is the anatomy of every note: the face states the conclusion, the note walks the reconciliation, and the two must meet to the rand. If assets had been bought or sold in the year, the note would simply grow rows — additions and disposals each take a line of their own inside the same frame.

The note also spells out the depreciation POLICY in words — vehicles at 20 percent on cost, equipment at 10 percent diminishing balance — because CONSISTENCY binds the business to the same method year after year, and every reader deserves to know which method the figures obey. Stop here for this section's questions: rebuild the note, and recover any one missing figure from the others.

## Subtopic: The Notes: Capital, Current Accounts and the Principles

Two further notes complete the equity story, and both are tables that markers love to hand over half empty.

The CAPITAL ACCOUNTS note runs partner by partner. Ngcobo: opening balance R450 000, no contributions, no withdrawals, closing R450 000. Venter: opening R350 000, closing R350 000. Total: R800 000. An uneventful table — and the stillness is the message: fixed capital moves only on a formal decision between the partners, so most years this note simply certifies that nothing moved.

The CURRENT ACCOUNTS note is where the action is — the term's appropriation work, restated for the public. Two columns, one per partner; read every row. Opening balances: Ngcobo R9 600 credit; Venter R6 800 DEBIT, shown as a negative. Interest on capital at nine percent: R40 500 and R31 500. Partners' salaries: R150 000 and R168 000. Bonus: nil and R12 000. Share of the remaining profit: R72 000 and R54 000. Drawings for the year: minus R224 000 and minus R217 000. Closing balances: Ngcobo R48 100; Venter R41 700; together R89 800 — exactly the current accounts line on the face. Every rand of the appropriation resurfaces here; this note is the appropriation account dressed for church.

Beneath the whole set stand the IFRS-aligned GAAP principles, and questions ask for them in plain words. Historical cost: assets at what they cost, not what someone hopes they would fetch. Prudence: the provision held against the debtors, stock at the figure actually counted. Matching: the accrual adjustments that pinned each rand of income and expense to its own year. Business entity: the partners' private lives kept off these pages. Going concern: every value assumes the shop trades on — a business being shut down would be valued at forced-sale prices instead. Materiality: trivial amounts grouped together, significant ones given their own line. The last questions of this part are with you now: complete both notes, and match each principle to the line it governs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same two statements again — but this time from the touchline at the end of a school rugby season, because everybody already understands the difference between the season highlights and the squad photo.

## Subtopic: The Highlights Reel and the Team Photo

When a season ends, two things get made. The highlights reel — everything that HAPPENED from the first whistle to the last. And the squad photo — everyone lined up on the final day. Nobody mixes them up. Nobody asks the photo what the halftime score was in May.

The Statement of Comprehensive Income is the highlights reel. It runs across the whole year — FOR the year ended — and it tells the earning story: sales of R2 880 000 in the opening scene, the cost of the goods cut away, the running costs cut away, until the closing shot lands on net profit of R528 000. The Statement of Financial Position is the team photo — ON 28 February, one frozen day — everything the shop owns lined up on one side, everyone holding a claim lined up on the other. And the photo has one rule: both rows must stand equally tall. R1 175 400 owned; R1 175 400 claimed. Every time.

Why keep both documents? Because each one is blind where the other sees. The reel cannot say whether the shop can pay next month's accounts — a profitable year can still finish with an empty till. The photo cannot say whether the year went well — a healthy bank balance might simply be borrowed. Reel plus photo, read together, is the whole truth.

One small habit banks a mark on every statement you ever write: ask, period or moment? A period takes FOR the year ended. A moment takes ON the date. Two words, chosen correctly, before a single figure goes down. Quick pause — the questions on reel versus photo are reaching you right now.

## Subtopic: The Fine Print That Shows the Workings

The face of a statement is the scoreboard: clean, final, nothing extra. But every scoreboard number has workings behind it, and the workings live in the NOTES — the fine print that proves the headline is honest.

Take the R755 000 of fixed assets on the photo. Behind it, the note reads like a service logbook. Land and buildings: R560 000, never depreciated — the ground under the shop does not wear away. Vehicles: bought for R285 000, worn down by R171 000 of accumulated depreciation across their working life, so standing at R114 000 in the books today. Equipment: cost R150 000, worn down to R81 000. Add the three: R560 000 plus R114 000 plus R81 000 — R755 000. The note climbs to exactly the number on the face. If it lands anywhere else, something is broken, and a marker will walk your trail until they find it — which is precisely why writing the trail out earns the marks.

The debtors tell the same story. The face refuses to show R64 000, because honesty admits that five percent of it will probably never arrive. It shows R60 800 — the R64 000 dimmed by the R3 200 provision. The face carries the truthful number; the note shows the truth being applied.

So treat notes the way you treat the fine print on a gym contract: that is where the real information lives. Face for the conclusion, note for the proof. Your questions for this section are open now — pair each face figure with the note that stands behind it.

## Subtopic: The Partners' Pages

Two notes belong personally to the partners, and together they tell a story anyone can follow: what each partner PLANTED in the business, and what each partner is currently OWED by it.

The capital note is the planted money: Ngcobo R450 000, Venter R350 000 — opening and closing, not a rand of movement. Deliberately boring: planted money is meant to stand still, and this note is the public certificate that it did. Had a partner injected more capital during the year, this is the page where the whole world would see it happen.

The current accounts note is each partner's running tab, published. Walk down Venter's column: she starts at minus R6 800 — last year she drew ahead of her earnings, and the note says so openly, because statements hide nothing. Then the year pays her: R31 500 of interest for her planted money, R168 000 of salary for managing the shop floor, R12 000 of bonus for beating the target, R54 000 as her slice of what remained. Then the year charges her: R217 000 of drawings taken home. She closes at plus R41 700 — out of the red, and the business owes her that amount. Ngcobo's column runs the same course and closes at R48 100. Together, R89 800 — and that pair of closing figures steps straight onto the photo as the current accounts line inside equity.

And underneath everything, the quiet rules to name whenever you are asked WHY the statements look the way they do: record things at what they cost — historical cost. Never paint the picture prettier than it is — prudence. Every rand in its own year — matching. The shop's books are not the partners' wallets — business entity. Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: finish a partner's column with one figure missing, and name the rule behind each honest choice.
