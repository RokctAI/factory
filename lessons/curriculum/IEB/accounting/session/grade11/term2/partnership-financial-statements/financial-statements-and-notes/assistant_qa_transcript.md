**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: we already balanced the ledger and divided the profit. Why must the same figures be rewritten as statements and notes? It looks like the same work done twice.

**Tutor:** Because the two documents are written for two different audiences, and the audience changes everything. The final accounts — trading, profit and loss, appropriation — are LEDGER machinery: internal closing accounts, expressed in debits and credits, legible only to someone trained in double entry. The financial statements are a REPORT: plain, classified lines for readers who will never open the ledger — the partners weighing their investment, Sisonke Bank reviewing its loan, a prospective third partner deciding whether to buy in. Same underlying figures, entirely different duties. The statements must classify — current against non-current, operating against financing; they must disclose — the notes proving how R755 000 of fixed assets was arrived at; and they must be comparable — the same formats and policies, year after year, business against business. That is why the format is non-negotiable when you are assessed: the question is testing whether you can produce the standard report a stranger could read and trust. The ledger answers "is the double entry complete?" The statements answer "what should a reader believe about this business?" Both questions matter, and neither replaces the other.

**Bianca:** Next one: why does the loan interest get its own line BELOW operating profit instead of sitting in the expense list with telephone and insurance?

**Tutor:** Because the statement deliberately separates two different skills: TRADING and FINANCING. Everything above the operating profit line answers one question — how good is this shop at its actual work, buying and selling paint and tiles? Telephone, insurance, wages, depreciation: costs of running the shop no matter who owns or funds it. Interest expense answers a different question entirely — how is the shop FUNDED? Ngcobo and Venter chose to borrow R200 000 from Sisonke Bank rather than plant more capital of their own; the R24 000 is the price of that choice, and it would disappear tomorrow if the loan were settled, without a single tile selling differently. Blend the two and every useful comparison dies: two identical shops, one debt-funded and one capital-funded, would appear to trade differently when their trading is identical. Separated, the reader sees the layers clearly: operating profit R552 000 — strong trading; less interest R24 000 — a light funding cost; net profit R528 000. And that separation is the doorway to the next lesson, where gearing and return indicators are built directly on it — the format you learn today is the analysis you perform tomorrow.

**Bianca:** A learner sent this one in about the fixed assets note: the bakkie is four years old and secondhand prices have climbed, so why do the statements still show it at cost minus depreciation? Isn't that just outdated information?

**Tutor:** It is less informative about market value — deliberately, because the alternative is worse. The historical cost principle anchors assets at what they verifiably cost, and depreciation then spreads that cost over the years that use the asset up. Cost is a FACT: an invoice for R285 000 exists, an auditor can confirm it, and no two accountants will quarrel over it. Market value is an OPINION: it shifts monthly, differs from valuer to valuer, and — here is the danger — it can be steered. A partnership hungry for a bigger loan could nudge its vehicle values upward, and nobody could prove the number wrong. So the statements choose verifiable-but-conservative over flattering-but-arguable — prudence and reliability working as a team. Notice that the carrying value never claims to be a selling price: it is the unconsumed slice of a real cost, matched against the income the vehicle helped to earn. And the system confesses its own limits: the note discloses cost, accumulated depreciation and the policy, so an informed reader can judge the fleet's age for themselves. When the bakkie is finally sold, the truth arrives anyway — the gap between the selling price and the carrying value lands in the income statement as a profit or loss on disposal, exactly as the fixed assets topic taught you.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the current accounts note I can never decide which figures go in as negatives. Is there a rule so I can stop guessing?

**Tutor:** There is — abandon plus-and-minus thinking and switch to OWED-TO and OWED-BY. The current account measures exactly one thing: what the business owes this partner right now. Whatever increases that debt to the partner enters positive: interest on capital, salary, bonus, share of the remaining profit. Whatever shrinks it enters negative: drawings, always — the partner has already carried that value home — and an opening DEBIT balance, because the year began with the partner owing the business, which is negative "business owes partner". Then each column is pure arithmetic. Venter: minus R6 800 to start, plus R31 500, plus R168 000, plus R12 000, plus R54 000, minus R217 000 — closing at plus R41 700; the business owes her. If a closing balance lands negative, do not hide it — it is information: that partner has drawn ahead of her earnings, the balance sits as a debit, and in the statement of financial position an overdrawn current account is disclosed openly rather than quietly netted away. And run the final check every single time: the two closing balances must add to the current accounts line on the face — R48 100 plus R41 700 giving R89 800 here. If they do not, a sign is wrong somewhere in a column, and this check catches it before a marker does.
