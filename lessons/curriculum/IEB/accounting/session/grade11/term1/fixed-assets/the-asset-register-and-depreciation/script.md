# Part 1 — Expert

Last year taught depreciation as a year-end adjustment; this year makes fixed assets a system. Today: why a business needs a FIXED ASSET REGISTER and what belongs on its pages; recording the acquisition of an asset; depreciation on both methods with assets bought PART-WAY through the year, which is where this grade raises the difficulty; the treatment of assets already fully depreciated; and the internal control and ethics over the movement and use of assets. The register is the spine of this whole topic — every disposal question in the next lesson reads its figures off a register page.

## Subtopic: The Register and What It Carries

The general ledger holds two figures for an entire class of assets: Vehicles at cost, and Accumulated Depreciation on Vehicles. Useful for the statements, useless for management — it cannot tell you which vehicle, how old, how much is left, or whether it still exists. The FIXED ASSET REGISTER supplies exactly that: one page per asset, holding the individual detail behind the ledger's totals.

A register page carries: a DESCRIPTION and identifying detail — registration number, serial number, model; the DATE of purchase and the SUPPLIER; the COST price; the DEPRECIATION METHOD and RATE applied to it; then, year by year, the annual depreciation, the accumulated depreciation to date and the CARRYING VALUE; the LOCATION and the person responsible; and finally, when the asset leaves, the disposal date, the proceeds and the profit or loss.

The control relationship is one you have met twice before — with debtors and their control account, and with the creditors' list. The register is the DETAIL; the ledger is the SUMMARY; and the register's totals must agree with the ledger's balances. Add every page's cost and you must get the Vehicles account; add every page's accumulated depreciation and you must get the Accumulated Depreciation account. A disagreement means an asset recorded in one place and not the other — and that reconciliation is a standing internal control, not an occasional tidy-up.

Why the register earns its keep, in three uses questions return to. CONTROL: an asset on a page can be physically produced on inspection, so theft and unrecorded disposals surface. DECISIONS: the page shows an asset's age, remaining value and history, which is what a replace-or-repair decision actually needs. And AUDIT: every depreciation figure in the financial statements traces back to a page, a rate and a calculation.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the register's contents, its control relationship to the ledger, and the three uses it serves.

## Subtopic: Acquisitions and What Counts as Cost

When an asset is bought, the entry itself is simple — debit the asset account, credit Bank or the Creditor — but the AMOUNT is where marks are won, because cost means more than the invoice price.

The cost of a fixed asset includes everything necessary to bring it into use: the purchase price, delivery and transport charges, installation and assembly, and any initial modification needed before it can operate. A machine invoiced at R120 000, with R5 500 delivery and R9 500 installation, enters the books at R135 000 — and every future depreciation charge computes on that R135 000, never on the invoice's R120 000.

Now the distinction that questions test hardest: CAPITAL expenditure against REVENUE expenditure. Capital expenditure creates or improves an asset and is added to its cost — a rebuilt engine that extends a vehicle's life, an extension added to a building, the installation above. Revenue expenditure keeps the asset running at its existing standard and is an expense of the year — servicing, repairs, fuel, insurance, licences. The test: does the spending EXTEND the asset's life or capacity beyond its original condition, or merely maintain it?

The error and its consequences, spelled out. Treat capital expenditure as an expense — a new engine written off as repairs — and this year's profit is understated, the asset is understated, and future depreciation is understated too. Treat revenue expenditure as capital — routine servicing added to the vehicle's cost — and profit is overstated, the asset is overstated, and the business ends up depreciating money that bought nothing lasting. Both are misstatements; the second is the one that flatters results, which is why auditors sample large repair invoices in both directions.

VAT completes the picture: a registered vendor records the asset at its cost EXCLUDING VAT, because the VAT is claimed back as input tax and never formed part of what the asset cost the business.

Stop for this section's questions now — cost assembled from its components, capital sorted from revenue expenditure, and the consequences of each misclassification named.

## Subtopic: Depreciation, Including Part-Year Assets

Both methods return from last year, and this year adds the complication that assets arrive and leave mid-year.

STRAIGHT-LINE — the fixed instalment method — charges the same percentage of the ORIGINAL COST each year. A machine costing R135 000 at 10 percent on cost charges R13 500 every year, and its carrying value falls in equal steps. DIMINISHING BALANCE charges the percentage on the CARRYING VALUE, so the charge shrinks: the same machine at 20 percent on carrying value charges R27 000 in year one, then 20 percent of R108 000 — R21 600 — in year two, and so on.

Now the part-year rule, which is this grade's real content: depreciation runs from the MONTH THE ASSET WAS BOUGHT, pro rata for the months it was owned in that first year. A vehicle bought on 1 September, in a financial year ending 28 February, was owned for six months — so its first-year charge is the annual figure times six over twelve. Count inclusively from the month of purchase to the year end, write the fraction into the working, and remember that from the SECOND year onward the charge is a full year's.

Work a full example. A delivery vehicle costs R320 000, bought 1 June, financial year ending 28 February, depreciated at 15 percent per annum on the diminishing balance. First year: 15 percent of 320 000 is R48 000 for a full year, but the vehicle was owned nine months — June to February — so 48 000 times nine over twelve gives R36 000. Carrying value at year end: R284 000. Second year: a full year now, 15 percent of 284 000 — R42 600; carrying value R241 400. Third year: 15 percent of 241 400 — R36 210. Each year's figure is entered on the register page beside the accumulated total and the carrying value.

And the FULLY DEPRECIATED asset, which the syllabus names specifically. An asset written down to zero has no further depreciation to charge — you may not depreciate below zero, because the business cannot consume more than the asset cost. In practice a nominal carrying value of R1 is often left standing so that an asset still in use does not vanish from the records. An old machine still working after its depreciation ends is not an error; it means the estimated life was conservative — and it must still appear on the register, still be controlled, and still be produced on inspection.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — both methods across several years, the part-year fraction counted from the purchase month, and the fully depreciated asset handled correctly.

## Subtopic: Control and Ethics Over Assets

Fixed assets are the most valuable and most portable things a business owns, which is why control and ethics attach directly to their movement and use.

ACQUISITION controls: purchases authorised at a level appropriate to the amount, with quotations obtained so the price can be shown to be reasonable; the asset checked against the order and delivery note on arrival; and the register updated at once, because an asset that exists physically but not on paper is an asset nobody is accountable for.

CUSTODY controls: every asset marked with an identifying number matching its register page; a named person responsible for each asset or location; assets secured — vehicles garaged and keyed, equipment behind locked doors; adequate insurance; and a log for shared assets such as vehicles, recording who took what, when, and for what purpose.

VERIFICATION controls: periodic physical inspection of assets against the register, performed by someone INDEPENDENT of the person responsible for them, with every difference investigated. This single routine catches the assets that quietly leave.

The ETHICS, stated plainly. PRIVATE USE of business assets — the company vehicle used for weekend trips, the laptop that never comes back — is drawings whether or not it is recorded as such; the honest treatment is either a formal drawings entry at fair value, or a policy that permits limited private use as part of remuneration, disclosed rather than assumed. THEFT and unrecorded disposal — the asset sold privately and written off as scrapped, caught by the verification routine above. And MISCLASSIFICATION for appearance — capitalising repairs to flatter this year's profit, which is a misstatement dressed up as an accounting choice.

Notice the pattern running through this topic and the disposal lesson together: every control is either a document trail, a separation of duties, or a physical count reconciled to a record. Those three ideas defend every asset a business owns — and the register is what makes all three possible.

The final questions of this part are with you now: controls sorted by stage, the ethics matched to the honest treatment, and the register's role in each.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system from the yard behind the shop — because a fixed asset register is really just the notebook a careful owner keeps about the expensive things, and everyone who has ever owned something valuable already understands why.

## Subtopic: The Notebook of Expensive Things

Open the ledger and it tells you one thing: "vehicles, five hundred and something thousand rand." Useful for the accountant, useless for the person actually running the place. Which vehicles? How old? Which one is nearly finished? Is the small bakkie still parked behind the shop, or did it leave two months ago?

So a careful owner keeps a second book — a page for every expensive thing she owns. On the bakkie's page: what it is and its registration number, the day she bought it and from whom, what she paid, how fast she writes its value down, and then a line for each year — this year's wear, the wear so far, and what it's worth in the books now. Plus who drives it and where it sleeps at night. And one day, when it's sold, the page closes with the date, what she got, and whether that beat the books.

That's the fixed asset register. And it works exactly like two things you already know: the debtors' list behind the debtors control account, and the creditors' list behind creditors control. One book has the TOTAL, the other has the FACES — and they must agree. Add every page's cost and you should land on the ledger's figure exactly. If you don't, something is recorded in one book and not the other, and that difference is worth chasing down today rather than next year.

Why does the notebook earn its place? Three reasons a shopkeeper feels immediately. She can walk the yard and check every page against something she can actually touch. She can answer "should I fix this bakkie or replace it?" by looking at how old it is and what's left in it. And when someone asks her to prove a number in her statements, she can point at the page it came from.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the notebook are coming to you right now: what a page holds, and what must agree with what.

## Subtopic: What the Thing Really Cost

Here's where money gets lost quietly: writing down the wrong price.

She buys a machine. The invoice says R120 000. But the truck that delivered it cost R5 500, and the technician who bolted it down and wired it in charged R9 500. What did that machine cost her? One hundred and thirty-five thousand rand — because until it was delivered and installed, it couldn't earn her a cent. Everything needed to get the thing WORKING is part of what it cost. Write R120 000 and every wear calculation for the next ten years is too small.

Now the other side of the same coin, and it is the distinction that keeps coming back in questions. Some spending BUYS more asset; some spending just KEEPS the asset going. A new engine that gives the bakkie another five years — that's buying more asset, so it goes onto the bakkie's page and gets written down over time. But the service, the tyres, the licence, the fuel — those just keep it running as it already was. Those are this year's expenses, gone.

One question sorts them: does this spending make the thing BETTER or LONGER-LIVED than it was, or does it just keep it where it was? Better or longer — add it to the page. Just keeping up — expense.

And know what going wrong costs, because both directions hurt. Call a new engine "repairs" and this year's profit looks worse than it was, and the bakkie is undervalued. Call routine servicing "part of the bakkie" and this year's profit looks BETTER than it was — you've turned an expense into an asset — and you'll be writing off servicing money for years as if it bought something lasting. That second one flatters the books, which is exactly why auditors go looking for it.

Your questions for this part are up now: build the true cost of an asset, and sort spending into the two piles.

## Subtopic: Bought in June, Not in January

Last piece, and it's the bit this year actually adds: things don't arrive neatly on the first day of the year.

She buys a delivery vehicle for R320 000 on the first of June. Her year ends at the end of February. So how much wear does she charge in that first year? Not a whole year's — she only had it for nine months. June, July, August, September, October, November, December, January, February. Nine.

So work out a full year first, then take the slice she's entitled to. At fifteen percent, a full year would be R48 000. Nine twelfths of that is R36 000. That's the first year's wear, and the vehicle's book value drops to R284 000.

From the second year on, she has it the whole year, so no more slicing: fifteen percent of R284 000 is R42 600. Then fifteen percent of what's left after that. The slicing only ever happens in the year something arrives — or, as you will see next lesson, the year it leaves.

The habit that protects the marks: count the months on your fingers, from the month it arrived to the end of the year, and write the fraction into your working — nine over twelve, right there on the page. Markers give marks for that fraction even when the arithmetic after it slips.

One last thing people find strange: what happens when something is completely written down but still working? Nothing more gets charged — you can't write off more than you paid; the thing has given all it's going to give, on paper. Often a single rand is left on the page so it doesn't vanish from the books entirely. And it stays on the register, stays locked up at night, stays somebody's responsibility. A machine finished on paper is still a machine worth stealing — which is really the whole point of keeping the notebook in the first place.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: slice a part-year properly, handle the fully worn-out machine, and say why it still belongs in the notebook. Next lesson, that same page tells you exactly what happens when the thing is finally sold.
