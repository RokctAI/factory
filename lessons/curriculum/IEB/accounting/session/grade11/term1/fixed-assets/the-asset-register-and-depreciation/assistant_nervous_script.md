Hey, it's Bianca — fixed assets look like a lot because three different things are happening at once: a notebook to keep, a cost to work out, and depreciation with a new twist. Take them one at a time; none of them is hard on its own.

The notebook — the asset register — is just the debtors list wearing a different hat. One page per expensive thing, and all the pages added together must equal what the ledger says. You have reconciled a list to a control account before; this is the same move on different stuff.

The cost is where quiet marks live. Everything needed to get the thing WORKING counts: the price, the delivery, the installation. R120 000 plus R5 500 plus R9 500 is a R135 000 machine, not a R120 000 one. And then the sorting question for later spending — does it make the thing better or longer-lived (add it to the asset), or does it just keep it going (expense it)? New engine: asset. Service and tyres: expense.

The new twist in depreciation is the part-year. Something bought in June, with a February year end, was only owned nine months — so work out a full year first, then take nine twelfths. Count the months on your fingers and WRITE THE FRACTION in your working; markers give marks for that fraction even if the arithmetic wobbles afterwards. And slicing only happens in the year a thing arrives or leaves — every year in between is a full year.

One oddity to be ready for: a fully written-down machine that still works. No more depreciation, often one rand left on the page, and it stays in the notebook because it is still worth stealing.

Notebook, true cost, part-year slice. You've got this!
