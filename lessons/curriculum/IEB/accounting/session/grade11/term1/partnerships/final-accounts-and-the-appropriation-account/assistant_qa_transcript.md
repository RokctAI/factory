**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every single year: why is a partner's salary not an expense? The partner works, the work gets paid — that sounds exactly like wages.

**Tutor:** It sounds like wages, but follow who is on each side of the payment. Wages to an employee are a cost of using an OUTSIDER's labour — the business becomes poorer, an outsider becomes richer, and matching demands the cost be set against the income that labour produced. A partner is not an outsider; a partner is one of the owners. When Amatola Building Supplies allows Smit her R156 000 salary, the business is not paying a cost to somebody else — the owners are agreeing, among themselves, that the pot should favour the partner who works the counter before the leftovers are split. Nothing leaves the ownership circle; the profit is merely divided along an agreed line. That is why the salary sits in the appropriation account, after net profit is measured, and never in the profit and loss account. The practical consequences matter: net profit is comparable between businesses regardless of how generous the partners' agreement is, and a question that buries a partner's salary inside operating expenses has deliberately overstated expenses and understated net profit — your job is to pull it out, restore the profit, and appropriate it correctly. One memory hook: expenses pay outsiders; appropriations sort out insiders.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the appropriation account, what happens if the net profit is too SMALL to cover the interest and salaries the agreement promises?

**Tutor:** Then the appropriation account runs into deficit, and the agreement keeps its promises anyway — that is the part learners find surprising. Suppose the agreement promises R360 000 of interest, salaries and bonus, but the year's net profit is only R330 000. The interest and salaries are still credited to the partners' current accounts in full, because they are contractual entitlements, not wishes. The appropriation account then shows R360 000 distributed against R330 000 received — an overshoot of R30 000, which is a LOSS remaining after appropriations, and that loss is shared between the partners in the profit-sharing ratio, two to one in our case: R20 000 against Khumalo and R10 000 against Smit, DEBITED to their current accounts. Each partner's net result is their entitlements minus their share of the shortfall. The same machinery handles an outright net loss: the loss enters the appropriation account, entitlements are still allowed, and the total shortfall is shared in ratio. The principle to state in words in an answer: appropriations are fixed by the agreement; only the RESIDUE — profit or shortfall — is shared in the ratio.

**Bianca:** A learner asked: why does interest on capital exist at all? The partners will get the profit anyway — why pay them interest first and complicate the account?

**Tutor:** Because without it, the profit split quietly punishes the partner who invested more. Picture the two of them sharing everything fifty-fifty with no interest step. Khumalo locked R500 000 into the business; Smit locked R250 000. That extra R250 000 of Khumalo's is working capital the business uses every day — yet under a bare fifty-fifty split he would earn not one rand more for providing it. Meanwhile his R250 000 could have been earning bank interest with no risk at all. Interest on capital corrects that: it pays each partner for the USE of their money, at an agreed rate, before the rewards for work and risk are handled — exactly as the salary step pays for the use of their time. The design principle behind the whole appropriation account is that it separates the three things a partner supplies — money, labour, and risk-bearing — and prices each one on its own line: interest for money, salary and bonus for labour, the residual split for risk. Get that principle into an answer and you can defend any agreement structure a question invents, including odd ones like unequal rates or no salaries at all.

**Bianca:** Last one: in equation questions I keep getting the direction wrong — my assets go up when they should go down. Is there a fix that actually works under pressure?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the one habit that separates guessing from knowing: never analyse the story — analyse the double entry. Under pressure, stories mislead. Take the classic: interest capitalised on the loan. The story says interest was charged, and charged sounds like money leaving, so panicking learners write assets down. But route it through the entry: debit Interest on loan, credit Loan. Now classify — Interest on loan is an expense, so equity falls; Loan is a liability, and a credit to a liability raises it. Assets were never in the entry, so assets are untouched. Three columns filled, no guessing: assets nil, equity minus twenty-two thousand, liabilities plus twenty-two thousand — and it balances, which is your built-in checker. Every equation question yields to the same four steps: write the two accounts of the entry; classify each as asset, equity or liability; convert debit and credit into up or down using the rules you have known since grade nine; then verify the equation still levels. If it does not level, one classification is wrong — fix it before you answer, not after the marks are gone. The story is decoration; the entry is the truth.
