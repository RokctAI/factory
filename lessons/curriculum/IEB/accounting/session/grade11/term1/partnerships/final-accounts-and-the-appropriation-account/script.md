# Part 1 — Expert

The adjustments are posted; the books of Khumalo and Smit Traders are honest. Now the year must be closed and the profit must be shared — and sharing is where a partnership differs from everything you closed off last year. Today: the closing sequence through the trading account and the profit and loss account; the APPROPRIATION account, where the partnership agreement is executed rand by rand; the partners' current accounts written up in the general ledger; and the accounting-equation analysis that questions attach to every partnership transaction. The figures continue from the last lesson: Amatola Building Supplies, financial year ended 28 February.

## Subtopic: Closing Off: Trading and Profit and Loss

Final accounts are closing accounts — they exist only at year end, to gather the nominal accounts and distil them into one profit figure.

First the TRADING account, home of gross profit. Close Sales to its credit side: R2 856 000. Close Cost of sales to its debit side: R1 904 000. The difference, R952 000, is the gross profit — carried down to the credit of the profit and loss account. Check it against the business's pricing policy: Amatola Building Supplies marks up 50 percent on cost, and R952 000 on a cost of R1 904 000 is exactly 50 percent, so the figure is coherent.

Then the PROFIT AND LOSS account, home of net profit. To its credit: gross profit R952 000, plus the operating incomes — rent income R49 200 and commission income R26 800, R76 000 together. To its debit: every operating expense, each closed off in turn — salaries and wages, depreciation, trading stock deficit, insurance, electricity, bank charges, bad debts, the provision for bad debts adjustment, sundry expenses — R526 000 in total, and then interest on loan of R22 000. Total the two sides in words: credits of R1 028 000 against debits of R548 000 leave a NET PROFIT of R480 000.

In a sole trader's books, that R480 000 would now close to the owner's capital account and the story would end. In a partnership it cannot — because two people own that profit, and a contract says exactly how it is divided. So net profit is carried instead to a third closing account, one last year never needed. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the closing sequence in order, and the two profit figures verified.

## Subtopic: The Appropriation Account

The APPROPRIATION account executes the partnership agreement. Net profit of R480 000 enters on its credit side, and the agreement is paid out from the top, in order.

First, interest on capital, the reward for money invested: Khumalo, 8 percent of R500 000 — R40 000. Smit, 8 percent of R250 000 — R20 000. Together R60 000, debited to appropriation, credited to the partners' current accounts.

Second, partners' salaries, the reward for work: Khumalo R132 000, Smit R156 000 — R288 000 together. Debit appropriation, credit each current account.

Third, the bonus: the store met its sales target, so Smit earns R12 000. Debit appropriation, credit her current account.

Now add what has been appropriated so far: R60 000 plus R288 000 plus R12 000 — R360 000 of the R480 000 is spoken for, leaving R120 000. That REMAINING profit is shared in the agreed ratio of two to one: two thirds of R120 000 is R80 000 to Khumalo; one third is R40 000 to Smit. The appropriation account now balances to the cent — R480 000 in, R480 000 out.

Prove each partner's total earnings in words. Khumalo: interest R40 000, salary R132 000, profit share R80 000 — R252 000. Smit: interest R20 000, salary R156 000, bonus R12 000, profit share R40 000 — R228 000. And R252 000 plus R228 000 returns R480 000 — the whole profit, fully distributed, nothing lost.

One warning that saves marks every year: partners' salaries and interest on capital are APPROPRIATIONS of profit, never expenses. They do not appear in the profit and loss account, they do not reduce net profit, and a question that sneaks a partner's salary into operating expenses is testing exactly this. Wages paid to employees — expense. Salary allowed to a partner — appropriation. Stop for this section's questions now: the account built top to bottom, and the expense-or-appropriation trap.

## Subtopic: The Current Accounts in the Ledger

The appropriation account's debits all landed as credits somewhere — in the partners' current accounts. Write up each account fully, and speak the sides.

Current account of Khumalo. Credit side: balance brought down from last year, R9 600 — the business owed him that. Then this year's earnings: interest on capital R40 000, salary R132 000, share of remaining profit R80 000. Debit side: drawings for the year, R214 000. Balance the account in words: credits total R261 600; debits R214 000; the closing balance is R47 600 CREDIT — the business owes Khumalo R47 600.

Current account of Smit. She starts the year in the red: balance brought down R6 300 on the DEBIT side, because last year she drew more than she earned. Credit side: interest on capital R20 000, salary R156 000, bonus R12 000, profit share R40 000 — R228 000. Debit side: the old R6 300, plus drawings of R195 500 — R201 800. Closing balance: R26 200 CREDIT. She has worked her way out of the debit and the business now owes her R26 200.

Note the discipline in each account: every line names its source — interest on capital, salary, bonus, appropriation share, drawings — because markers award the label as well as the figure. And note what never appears here: capital contributions, which belong in the fixed capital accounts, and cash movements that are not partner flows.

Together the two current accounts now hold R73 800 of undrawn earnings — R47 600 plus R26 200 — and that combined figure joins the capital of R750 000 in the equity section of the statements you will build in term two. Pause for this section's questions: both accounts written up, balances defended, and the debit-opening trap of Smit handled.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Equation, Partnership Edition

Every partnership transaction still obeys assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities — questions simply dress the equation in partnership clothing. Analyse each event in three columns spoken aloud: effect on assets, effect on equity, effect on liabilities.

Smit contributes R60 000 additional capital by EFT. Assets: bank up R60 000. Equity: capital up R60 000. Liabilities: no effect.

Khumalo takes trading stock of R4 200 for personal use. Assets: trading stock down R4 200. Equity: down R4 200 through drawings. Liabilities: nil. Note the account: DRAWINGS, at cost — not sales, because nothing was sold to an outsider.

Interest on capital of R40 000 is allowed to Khumalo. Assets: nil — no cash moves on the allowance. Equity: appropriation shifts R40 000 from accumulated profit to Khumalo's current account claim — total equity unchanged by the allowance itself. This is the subtle one: appropriations REDISTRIBUTE equity between the profit pool and individual partners; they do not create or destroy it. The profit that feeds them was created earlier, by trading.

Interest of R22 000 is capitalised on the loan. Assets: nil. Liabilities: loan up R22 000. Equity: DOWN R22 000 — the expense. The equation stays level: plus twenty-two on one side's liabilities, minus twenty-two on the same side's equity.

The business pays a creditor R16 500. Assets: bank down R16 500. Liabilities: creditors down R16 500. Equity: untouched — settling a debt is not an expense.

The method that never fails: name the two accounts in the double entry first, classify each as asset, equity or liability, then read the effects straight off the debits and credits. Learners who guess effects from the story make sign errors; learners who route through the double entry do not. The final questions of this part are with you now: five transactions, three columns each, every effect with its amount and direction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same closing from the kitchen of the kota shop — because dividing profit between two friends fairly is something everyone already argues about correctly.

## Subtopic: One Pot, Three Ladles

At year end, all the shop's earnings boil down into one pot: the net profit. For Amatola Building Supplies, R480 000. Now two hungry partners stand at the pot — and the agreement is the recipe for serving it. Three ladles come out, in strict order.

Ladle one pays the MONEY for waiting. Whoever planted capital gets interest on it first — eight percent. Khumalo planted R500 000, so R40 000. Smit planted R250 000, so R20 000. Fair: their money could have been earning interest at the bank instead.

Ladle two pays the HANDS for working. Khumalo runs the books, salary R132 000. Smit runs the store every day, salary R156 000. Plus her R12 000 bonus, because the store hit its target. Fair: work deserves wages even between friends — especially between friends.

Ladle three splits WHAT'S LEFT as the reward for risk. Count what the first two ladles served: R60 000 plus R288 000 plus R12 000 — R360 000. The pot held R480 000, so R120 000 remains, and the agreement splits leftovers two to one: R80 000 to Khumalo, R40 000 to Smit. Pot empty, argument over — because the recipe was written down before the cooking started.

And here is the line to underline twice: the ladles serve profit AFTER it is measured; they never shrink it. A partner's salary is not an expense of the shop — the pot is the same size whether the recipe pays big salaries or none. Quick check before we carry on: questions on the three ladles are coming to you right now, in serving order.

## Subtopic: Each Friend's Tab

The ladles don't hand out cash — no partner walks off with R252 000 in an envelope. The servings go onto each friend's TAB: the current account.

Khumalo's tab: it opened with the shop owing him R9 600 from last year. Add this year's servings — R40 000 interest, R132 000 salary, R80 000 leftovers — that side reaches R261 600. But all year he was taking money out to live on: drawings of R214 000. Tab at year end: the shop owes him R47 600.

Smit's tab opens the other way around — R6 300 in the RED, because last year she drew out more than she earned. It happens; it is not a scandal, it is a debit balance. This year she earns R228 000 in servings, draws R195 500 plus that old R6 300 against it, and ends with the shop owing HER R26 200. She worked her way out of the red — the tab shows it plainly, which is exactly what tabs are for.

The tab teaches the difference between EARNING and TAKING. You can earn R228 000 and take R195 500 — tab grows. You can earn less than you take — tab goes red. Either way, the planted capital on the wall never moved. Your questions for this part are up now: run both tabs top to bottom and say who owes whom, and how much.

## Subtopic: The Scale Never Lies

Last tool, and it is grade 8's oldest friend wearing grade 11 clothes: the accounting equation. What the shop OWNS equals what the owners are OWED plus what outsiders are OWED. Every partnership event tips the scale somewhere — and it must always level out.

Smit EFTs in R60 000 more capital: the bank fattens by sixty, the owners' side fattens by sixty. Level. Khumalo takes stock worth R4 200 home for a family event: shelves lighter by four thousand two hundred, owners' side lighter by the same — through drawings, priced at cost, because no customer bought anything. Level. The bank quietly adds R22 000 interest to the loan: outsiders' side up twenty-two, owners' side down twenty-two — the shop got poorer without the bank balance moving. Level, and sneaky, and a favourite in questions.

And the one that catches everyone: interest on capital allowed to Khumalo. Feels like the shop paying out — but no cash moves and the total owners' side does not change. All that happens is that R40 000 of the profit pool gets a name on it: Khumalo's. Sharing the pot doesn't shrink the pot; it just writes names on the servings.

When any equation question comes, refuse to guess from the story. Write the double entry first — which account is debited, which credited — label each account asset, equity or liability, and read the plus and minus straight off. The story can mislead; the entry cannot. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: five events on the scale, and every one must balance before you answer.
