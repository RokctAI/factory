# Part 1 — Expert

Last year you posted the cash journals to the Bank account and took the answer on faith. This year we put that answer on trial. Two independent records exist of the same money — the business's own Cash Receipts and Cash Payments Journals, and the monthly statement the bank issues — and on any given day they will disagree. Today: why they disagree, which differences are our own omissions and must be journalised, which are timing differences and must only be reconciled, and how the Bank Reconciliation Statement proves that the two records tell a single story. Our running example is Khumalo and Smit Traders, trading as Amatola Building Supplies in East London — partners Sipho Khumalo and Annelie Smit — with a financial year ending 28 February.

## Subtopic: Why Two Records of One Bank Account Differ

Begin with the mechanics that open the gap. The business records a receipt on the day the money is banked; the bank records it on the day the deposit clears. The business records a payment the moment an EFT is released; the bank records it when the supplier's account is actually credited. Those lags are TIMING differences — both records are correct, and a few working days will close the gap without anyone lifting a pen.

A second cause is quite different: the bank knows things the business does not. Service fees, cash handling fees and card commission are taken without permission being asked. Interest on a favourable balance arrives without warning. Stop orders and debit orders run on standing instructions signed long ago. A debtor's electronic payment can be dishonoured — reversed because the account behind it was empty — days after we recorded the receipt. None of these reach our journals until the statement lands, because we did not initiate them and nobody told us. On our side they are OMISSIONS, and every one of them must now be entered in the cash journals.

The third cause is the least comfortable: ERRORS. Ours — two digits swapped in the Cash Payments Journal, a column cast wrongly, an amount posted twice. Or the bank's — money taken off our account that belongs to a different client altogether. From this classification flows the rule that governs the entire topic: OUR omissions and OUR errors are fixed in OUR books, through the cash journals; TIMING differences and the BANK's errors never enter our books at all — they wait on the Bank Reconciliation Statement for time, or the bank, to resolve them.

Keep that three-way sort in front of you — timing, our omissions, errors — because every item in every question must be dropped into one of those boxes before a single figure is written. Pause here: this section's questions are with you now, and sorting is exactly what they test.

## Subtopic: Working Through the Statement

The February statement of Amatola Building Supplies arrives. The provisional balance on the Bank account in the general ledger, straight from the two cash journals, is R72 540 favourable — a debit balance, an asset. Comparing the statement against the journals line by line yields seven findings.

One: bank charges of R2 140 — service fees, cash handling fees and card commission — sit on the statement but not in the Cash Payments Journal. Enter them in the CPJ: debit Bank charges, credit Bank.

Two: interest of R520 credited by the bank on the favourable balance. Enter it in the Cash Receipts Journal: debit Bank, credit Interest income.

Three: a stop order of R2 750 for the monthly insurance premium. Debit Insurance, credit Bank.

Four: a debit order of R1 430 to the armed response company that watches the premises at night. Debit Sundry expenses, credit Bank.

Five: an electronic transfer of R5 400, received from a debtor and entered in the CRJ during January, has been DISHONOURED — the debtor's account had no funds. Reverse the receipt and reinstate the debt: debit Debtors control, credit Bank. Grasp the substance: the debtor still owes us; nothing was ever collected.

Six: our own error. A payment to a creditor of R5 720 was written into the CPJ as R5 270. The bank paid the true R5 720, so our record understates the payment by R450 — and notice the fingerprint: a difference that divides exactly by nine points to a transposition. Correct it: debit Creditors control, credit Bank with R450.

Seven: cheque number 0873 for R8 100, issued on 15 July 2025 to a creditor, has never been presented. A cheque older than six months is STALE and the bank will refuse it. Cancel it: debit Bank, credit Creditors control — the money returns to us, and the creditor is owed once more.

Now work the balance: R72 540 less 2 140, plus 520, less 2 750, less 1 430, less 5 400, less 450, plus 8 100 — a corrected Bank account balance of R68 990 favourable. That is the figure the Statement of Financial Position will carry, and every later lesson this year leans on it. Stop for this section's questions now: each finding classified, each double entry stated in full, and the corrected balance defended.

## Subtopic: The Reconciliation Statement

Even after every correction, the R68 990 will not match the bank's closing figure, because the timing differences are still open. The Bank Reconciliation Statement is the bridge across them.

At 28 February the bank statement closes at R67 540 favourable. Three bridging items remain. A deposit of R19 800 was banked late on 27 February and had not cleared by month end — an OUTSTANDING DEPOSIT, in our books but not yet in the bank's. Two electronic transfers released to suppliers on 26 and 27 February had not yet been debited — EFT 0524 for R11 460 and EFT 0531 for R9 840, together R21 300, in our books but not yet in the bank's. And R2 950 was debited to our account by the bank in error; the branch has been notified and will reverse it.

Build the bridge, watching the direction of every step. Start with the balance per bank statement, R67 540. ADD the outstanding deposit of R19 800, because the bank will still add it. ADD the R2 950 taken in error, because the bank will give it back. LESS the outstanding EFTs of R21 300, because the bank still has to take them off. Total: R68 990 — the corrected Bank account balance, to the rand.

Two disciplines carry the marks here. First, the heading in full: "Bank Reconciliation Statement of Khumalo and Smit Traders on 28 February 2026" — a statement drawn at a date. Second, the direction test, asked of every single item as one question: what will the BANK still do to this balance? If the bank will still add it, you add it; if the bank will still subtract it, you subtract it. Learners who chant "deposits are added" collapse the moment a question opens with an overdraft or runs the reconciliation the other way. Reason each direction; never recite it. The questions for this section are in front of you now — build the bridge and justify every sign.

## Subtopic: Traps, Method Marks and Internal Control

Four traps come round again and again, and each has a defence. First, journalising timing differences — the learner who "corrects" the outstanding deposit wrecks both records at once, because outstanding items are already in our books; that is precisely why they are outstanding. Second, direction mistakes on an OVERDRAFT: an unfavourable balance is a credit balance in our ledger, written in brackets, and every add and subtract still answers the same question — what will the bank still do? Third, treating the dishonoured receipt as an expense: it is not a loss, it is a reversal — debit Debtors control, credit Bank — and Bad debts appears only if the debt is later, formally, written off. Fourth, forgetting the stale cheque, which otherwise leaves R8 100 sitting on an outstanding list until the end of time.

Method marks are won by showing structure even when the arithmetic wobbles. Present the corrected Bank account as an account: provisional balance brought down, each correction on its own line with its contra account named, balance carried down. Present the reconciliation statement with a complete heading, every item labelled, brackets around every subtraction, one final total. Markers reward classification and direction independently of the final figure — a wrong total resting on correct workings still gathers most of the marks; a bare correct number earns only itself.

Finally the control purpose, which is examined in words rather than figures. The reconciliation is a DIVISION OF DUTIES turned into arithmetic: an outside party — the bank — keeps its own independent record of every movement of cash, and setting its record against ours exposes theft, omission and error that no purely internal check would catch. Sound practice follows directly: reconcile every month; let it be done by someone who neither handles cash nor writes up the journals; have a partner review and sign it; and investigate every long-outstanding item instead of rolling it forward — a deposit that keeps surfacing only after month end is the classic signature of lapping, where a later receipt covers an earlier theft. The final questions of this part are with you now: traps named, method marks defended, and the control argument written out in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same reconciliation, but from the street — because two people counting the same money and landing on different answers is not really an accounting problem. It is an everyday one.

## Subtopic: Two People Counting the Same Money

Think of a savings club. You keep a notebook at home; the treasurer keeps a book at the bank. Month end arrives, you compare, and the numbers differ. Nobody stole a cent. Nobody is lying. The books differ because two people wrote things down at two different moments.

That is Amatola Building Supplies and its bank, exactly. The shop writes down a deposit the afternoon the bag goes to the branch. The bank writes it down when the money clears — often the next working day. The shop records a payment the second the transfer is released; the bank records it when the supplier's account lights up. Same money, two clocks. Leave it a few days and the two books agree all by themselves.

But there is a second reason the books differ, and this one has nothing to do with time. The bank does things to your money without phoning you first. It helps itself to its fees. It drops in a little interest. It runs the standing instructions you signed months ago — the insurance, the armed response. And when a customer's transfer bounces because their account was empty, the bank quietly takes that money back out again.

So the statement, when it arrives, is really a letter from the bank listing everything it did that you knew nothing about. Those items are not the bank's mistakes — they are your missing entries. Quick check before we go on: questions on the two reasons the books differ are coming to you right now — time on the one side, news on the other.

## Subtopic: The Bank's News and Your Own Mistakes

Sort every line on that statement into three piles and the whole topic turns simple.

Pile one — NEWS. Things the bank did that you never wrote down: fees of R2 140, interest of R520, the R2 750 insurance stop order, the R1 430 armed response debit order, and the customer's R5 400 transfer that bounced. All of it goes into your books now, through the cash journals. Money out lands in the payments journal; money in lands in the receipts journal. The bounced R5 400 is the one that stings — you thought you had been paid, you had not, so the customer goes straight back onto the debtors list.

Pile two — YOUR SLIPS. The bookkeeper wrote a R5 720 payment as R5 270. The bank paid the real amount. Four hundred and fifty rand missing from your book, so fix your book. A tip that has rescued many learners: when a difference divides cleanly by nine, two digits have almost certainly been swapped. And old cheque number 0873 for R8 100, written back in July and never cashed — a cheque expires after six months, like bread going stale. Cancel it, take the money back, and put the supplier back on the list of people you owe.

Pile three — THINGS YOU LEAVE ALONE. The R19 800 deposit that has not cleared. The two transfers of R11 460 and R9 840 that have not gone out yet. And the R2 950 the bank took off by mistake. You touch none of these in your books. Your book is right; the bank is simply behind, or wrong. Your questions for this part are up now: take each item and say out loud which of the three piles it lands in before you do anything with it.

## Subtopic: The Bridge Between the Two Numbers

Once all the news is written in and every slip is fixed, your own book says R68 990. The bank's letter says R67 540. Two honest numbers, still apart — and the reconciliation statement is nothing more than the bridge you build between them.

Walk it. Start where the bank stands: R67 540. The R19 800 deposit is still on its way in — the bank will add it, so add it. The R2 950 the bank grabbed by mistake is coming back — the bank will add that too, so add it. The two transfers totalling R21 300 have not left yet — the bank will take them off, so take them off. Count it: 67 540 plus 19 800 plus 2 950 less 21 300 — sixty-eight thousand nine hundred and ninety. The same R68 990 your own book shows. The bridge holds.

And here is the only question you ever need when you hesitate over add or subtract: what is the bank still going to do to this? Not "is it a deposit?" — that chant snaps the moment a question hands you an overdraft. Ask what the bank still has to do, and follow it.

One last thought, and it is the part that gets asked in words. Why go to all this trouble? Because the bank is an outsider holding its own record of every cent that moved. If the person who handles the cash also writes the books and also does the reconciling, nobody is watching anybody. Split those jobs, reconcile every single month, let a partner sign it off, and chase anything that has sat on the outstanding list too long — that is how a small business catches a hand in the till before it becomes a habit. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: cross the bridge in both directions, and say in your own words why the bank makes such a reliable watchdog.
