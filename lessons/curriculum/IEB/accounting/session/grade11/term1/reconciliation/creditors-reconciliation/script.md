# Part 1 — Expert

The bank reconciliation set our record of cash against an outsider's record. This lesson repeats that discipline on a different account and a different outsider: the supplier. Every month a creditor posts a STATEMENT showing what its books say we owe; our own creditors ledger holds an account for that same supplier showing what our books say we owe. The two will differ. Today: why they differ, how to work through both records, how to build the Creditors Reconciliation Statement, and why this small monthly job protects real money. Amatola Building Supplies — Khumalo and Smit Traders of East London — carries the example, and the supplier is Kei Steel & Roofing Wholesalers.

## Subtopic: Two Records of One Debt

First pin down what each record actually is. The supplier's STATEMENT is an extract from the supplier's own DEBTORS ledger: to Kei Steel, Amatola Building Supplies is a debtor, and the statement lists every invoice raised, every credit note issued, every receipt banked, and the balance the supplier believes is owing. Our CREDITORS LEDGER account for Kei Steel is the mirror image: every credit purchase posted from the Creditors Journal, every return posted from the Creditors Allowances Journal, every payment and discount posted from the Cash Payments Journal, and the balance we believe we owe. One debt, two sets of books, kept by two different businesses that never see each other's entries.

The causes of disagreement fall into the same three families the bank reconciliation taught. TIMING: an invoice dated before month end but delivered to us afterwards; a payment we released on the twenty-sixth that the supplier only processes in the new month. Both records are correct; the paperwork is simply slower than the calendar. OMISSIONS: interest the supplier charged on an overdue balance, or a settlement discount the supplier allowed, neither of which we heard about until the statement arrived. And ERRORS, on either side — and here is the difference from the bank reconciliation, where only the bank could be wrong about a fee. The supplier is another ordinary business with an ordinary bookkeeper, so the statement itself may hold mistakes: an invoice charged to the wrong customer, a return never processed, two digits swapped.

That third family sets the discipline for the entire lesson. Nothing on a supplier's statement is automatically true. Read it as a claim to be tested, never as an instruction to be obeyed. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two records identified, and the three families of difference named with an example of each.

## Subtopic: Reading the Statement Against the Ledger Account

At 28 February 2026 the statement from Kei Steel shows R65 930 owing. Our creditors ledger account for Kei Steel shows R48 660. Comparing them item by item produces six differences.

One: invoice 3187 for R7 200, for roof sheeting delivered on 25 February. The supplier raised it in February; the invoice only reached our office on 4 March, so the Creditors Journal never saw it. This is our omission — the goods were received before month end. Record it: debit Trading stock, credit Creditors control, and post it to the supplier's account in the creditors ledger. Our balance rises by R7 200.

Two: credit note 244 for R4 100, issued for dented sheeting we sent back, was entered in the Creditors Allowances Journal as R1 400. Our error, and the R2 700 difference divides by nine — a transposition. We reduced the debt by too little, so our balance must come down a further R2 700: debit Creditors control, credit Trading stock.

Three: interest of R540 charged by the supplier on the overdue portion of the account. Our omission — nobody told us. Debit Interest expense, credit Creditors control. Our balance rises by R540.

Four: a settlement discount of R950 allowed by the supplier on our early February payment, never recorded by us. Our omission again, working the other way: debit Creditors control, credit Discount received. Our balance falls by R950.

Five: R2 380 of guttering charged on the statement was never delivered to us — the supplier invoiced another customer's order to our account. Their error, and the correction belongs in their books, not ours. Nothing is journalised; the item goes on the reconciliation statement, and we telephone Kei Steel the same day.

Six: our electronic transfer of R10 800, released on 26 February, is missing from the statement. A timing difference: we recorded it correctly, the supplier had not yet processed it. Again nothing is journalised.

Work our balance: R48 660 plus 7 200, less 2 700, plus 540, less 950 — a corrected creditors ledger balance of R52 750. Stop for this section's questions now: each difference classified, and each entry that belongs in our books stated as a debit and a credit.

## Subtopic: Building the Creditors Reconciliation Statement

The corrected ledger balance of R52 750 still does not equal the statement's R65 930, because two items remain that only the supplier can resolve. The Creditors Reconciliation Statement reconciles them, and it runs in one direction: from the balance per the statement received to the balance per our corrected creditors ledger account.

"Creditors Reconciliation Statement of Kei Steel & Roofing Wholesalers on 28 February 2026." Balance per statement received: R65 930. Less the payment of R10 800 in transit, because the supplier will still deduct it when our transfer is processed. Less R2 380 invoiced to us in error, because the supplier will still reverse it. Total: R52 750 — agreeing with our corrected account to the rand.

The direction test is the one the bank reconciliation taught, aimed at a different outsider: what will the SUPPLIER still do to this balance? Still going to deduct it — deduct it. Still going to add it — add it. An invoice the supplier has correctly raised but we have only just recorded does NOT appear here at all, because we have already fixed that in our own books; the reconciliation statement carries only what remains for the supplier to do.

One structural point earns its own marks: this statement reconciles ONE supplier's account, not the whole Creditors Control account. Last year you reconciled the control account against the total of the creditors list — an internal check. This reconciliation is external, one supplier at a time, and the two exercises answer different questions. The questions for this section are in front of you now: build the statement with its full heading, and defend why each remaining item sits where it does.

## Subtopic: Errors, Traps and Control Value

Four traps come round repeatedly. First, adjusting our books for the supplier's error — the learner who journalises the R2 380 quietly pays for guttering another business received. Test every statement line against our own delivery notes and invoices before accepting it. Second, the direction of a credit note. A credit note REDUCES what we owe; understating one, as we did by R2 700, leaves our balance too high, so the correction brings it down. Learners reverse this constantly, and reversing it puts the answer out by double the error. Third, dismissing interest charged because we never agreed to it: we did agree, in the credit terms we signed, so it must be recorded — debit Interest expense, credit Creditors control — and only then queried if it looks wrong. Fourth, muddling the two reconciliations: control-against-list is internal; statement-against-ledger-account is external.

Method marks follow the same architecture as the bank topic. Show the corrected creditors ledger account with the opening balance, each correction on its own line, and the corrected balance. Show the reconciliation statement with a full heading, every item named and bracketed where deducted, and one total. A marker rewards the classification of each item and the direction of each adjustment separately from the final figure.

Then the control value, examined in words. This reconciliation is the only routine check that catches a supplier overcharging, double-invoicing, omitting a credit note, or charging interest the credit terms do not allow — because nobody inside the business is placed to notice. It equally catches OUR failures: unrecorded invoices, understated returns, payments posted to the wrong supplier's account. Sound practice: reconcile every supplier's statement in the month it arrives; have the reconciliation done by someone who does not authorise payments; pay from our own corrected ledger account, never from the statement; investigate old items rather than carrying them forward; and query every difference in writing so a record exists. Paying a statement without reconciling it is the quietest way a small business loses money for years on end. The final questions of this part are with you now: traps named, method defended, and the control argument written out.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same job from behind a spaza counter — because anyone who has ever bought stock on account from a wholesaler has done this reconciliation without ever hearing its name.

## Subtopic: The Spaza Owner and the Wholesaler's Bill

You run a spaza. Every week you fetch stock from the wholesaler and sign for it, and you write in your own exercise book what you took and what you paid. At month end the wholesaler sends a bill: "You owe us R65 930." Your exercise book says R48 660.

Do you simply pay their number? Of course not. That is somebody else's book about your money. Do you refuse to pay and stand on yours? Also no, because your book has holes in it too. What you actually do is sit down with both records and go line by line until you understand every single difference.

And once you sit down, the differences turn out to be of only three kinds, and you know them already from the bank lesson. Things that are late — an invoice still in the post, a payment you made that they have not processed yet. Things you never knew about — they charged you interest for paying late, or gave you a discount for paying early, and nobody phoned you either time. And things that are simply WRONG — and here is the twist that separates this from the bank: the wholesaler can be wrong. A bank fee is a bank fee; but the wholesaler's clerk is a person, and people put one customer's delivery on another customer's account.

So the golden rule of the whole lesson: a supplier's bill is a claim, not a command. Quick check before we carry on — questions on the three kinds of difference are coming to you right now, and especially on who is allowed to be wrong.

## Subtopic: Whose Book Is Wrong?

Take the six differences one at a time and ask just one thing: whose book must change?

The R7 200 of roof sheeting delivered on the twenty-fifth, with the invoice arriving in March. The sheets are stacked in your yard — you have them. So YOUR book is short. Add it. The credit note for the dented sheeting: they credited you R4 100, you wrote R1 400. Yours is wrong by R2 700 — and once again, when a difference divides neatly by nine, you have swapped two digits. You knocked off too little, so you owe less than your book says: bring your balance DOWN by R2 700. The interest of R540 for paying late: irritating, but you signed those terms. Your book is short. Add it. The R950 discount they gave you for paying early in February: a gift you never wrote down, so your book is too high. Take it off. Count: R48 660, plus 7 200, minus 2 700, plus 540, minus 950 — R52 750. That is what you honestly owe.

Now the two that are NOT your problem. The R2 380 of guttering you never received — their clerk's mistake, and you change nothing in your book; you phone them today. And the R10 800 you transferred on the twenty-sixth that has not reached their statement — you did nothing wrong; they are simply behind.

Your questions for this part are up now: for every difference, say out loud whose book must change before you move a single rand.

## Subtopic: Why This Small Job Protects Real Money

Last piece, and it is the part that matters most once you run a real business. Why bother? Because R52 750 and R65 930 are more than thirteen thousand rand apart, and if you had simply paid the bill you would have handed a stranger money you did not owe.

The bridge that proves you right is short. Start where they stand: R65 930. They still have to knock off the R10 800 you already sent — knock it off. They still have to take back the R2 380 that was never yours — take it off. R52 750. Their number and your number now agree, and you can pay with a clear conscience. The same question steers every line, exactly as it did with the bank: what does the OTHER side still have to do to this balance?

And the control point, in plain words. Nobody inside a small business can catch a supplier overcharging, because nobody inside the business sees the supplier's books — except through this statement. Reconcile it the month it arrives. Let the person who checks it not be the person who authorises payments, so nobody can cover their own tracks. Pay from YOUR corrected account, never from their statement. Chase old items instead of dragging them forward year after year. And put every query in writing, so a record exists when the argument comes. Businesses rarely collapse from one big theft; they bleed slowly from bills nobody checked. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: cross the bridge yourself, and say why paying a statement without reconciling it is the most expensive shortcut in the book.
