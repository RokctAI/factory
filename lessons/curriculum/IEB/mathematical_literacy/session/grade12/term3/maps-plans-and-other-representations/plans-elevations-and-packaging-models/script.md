# Part 1 — Expert

Last term the scale work read a whole province off a single page; this block points the same skill at a single BUILDING — and then at the crates it gets packed into. Final exams hand you an architect's drawing of a granny flat or a school storeroom and expect four things: speak the drawing's symbol language, turn plan millimetres into real metres, match the flat views to the walls they describe from every compass direction, and then step into three dimensions — assembly instructions, packing arrangements, and the estimate that tells a builder how many tiles and how many litres of paint to buy. Every skill here is the map skill moved onto a building site: identical scale arithmetic, a much smaller territory.

## Subtopic: Plan Symbols, Scale and Actual Dimensions

A building plan is a bird's-eye view of one storey, drawn as if the roof were lifted away, to a stated scale — most commonly 1 : 100 for a house, where every millimetre on paper stands for 100 mm of real building, so one centimetre stands for one metre. Detail drawings zoom in at 1 : 50; a whole property shrinks onto a site plan at 1 : 200 or 1 : 500. Anchor the 1 : 100 conversion pair — plan millimetres times 100, then divide by 1 000 for metres — and the arithmetic of this entire topic is already in your hands.

The symbol language comes first, because questions ask it directly. WALLS are pairs of thick parallel lines — the gap between the pair is the wall's real thickness. A DOOR is a gap in the wall with a quarter-circle arc showing its swing — the arc is the space the door sweeps through, and questions ask both what the arc means and whether the door opens into the kitchen or into the passage. A WINDOW is a break in the wall drawn with thin double or triple lines. Plans also carry standard abbreviations and fittings: WC for the toilet, WHB for the wash-hand basin, a crossed rectangle for the bath, small circles on the stove symbol for its plates, plus a north arrow and a title block that states the scale.

Now the dimensions. Consider a granny flat going up in Howick. Its lounge measures 50 mm by 40 mm on the 1 : 100 plan: real length 50 × 100 = 5 000 mm = 5 m, real width 40 × 100 = 4 000 mm = 4 m. Floor area: 5 × 4 = 20 m². The reverse question supplies the real object and asks for its plan size — a bookshelf 1,8 m wide appears as 1 800 ÷ 100 = 18 mm on the plan. An 8 mm door gap on the drawing is a real opening of 8 × 100 = 800 mm. And the sanity check never retires: rooms come out between about 2 and 8 metres, so a lounge of 50 metres or of half a metre is not a strange house — it is a conversion slip waving at you.

Note carefully what a dimension line measures BETWEEN: some run wall-to-wall inside a room, others run over the walls across the whole building, and the drawing's arrows say which — read the arrowheads before you assume.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the symbol language, 1 : 100 in both directions, floor area from plan measurements, and the sanity check on room sizes.

## Subtopic: Floor Plans and Elevation Plans

One drawing cannot describe a whole building, so a plan set speaks in VIEWS. The FLOOR PLAN looks straight down — it fixes the room layout, wall lengths and thicknesses, door swings and window positions, but it says nothing about height. The ELEVATIONS look horizontally at each outside face — the EAST ELEVATION shows the wall that faces east, and likewise north, south and west. Elevations reveal everything the floor plan cannot: wall heights, roof shape and pitch, the heights of windows and doors, gutters and chimneys.

The classic task is MATCHING: here is the floor plan, here are two or three elevations — which one is the east elevation? Work it like a detective holding the north arrow. From the floor plan, list what the east-facing wall carries — say, the kitchen door plus one wide window. Now interrogate each candidate elevation: the drawing showing exactly one door with one wide window beside it is the east elevation; the almost blank wall with a single small window belongs to whichever side the plan hides the bathroom on. Count the openings, match their positions, and check the left-right order — because an elevation is seen from OUTSIDE, its features run mirror-wise to the way you read the room from inside, and that mirror is precisely where the trick option waits.

Second task: COMBINING views. The floor plan gives a window's width — 1 500 mm; the elevation gives its height — 1 200 mm; only together do they size the glass: 1,5 × 1,2 = 1,8 m². Any height question — will a 2,3 m bookcase clear a 2,6 m ceiling — belongs to the vertical drawings, never to the floor plan.

Third: DRAWING to scale. Told to draw a 1 : 100 floor plan of a 3 m by 2,5 m laundry with an 800 mm door on the short wall: the outline is 30 mm by 25 mm, the door gap 8 mm with its quarter-circle swing, thin window lines on the stated wall, dimensions labelled, scale written down. The marks live in the ratios being right, the symbols standard and the labelling complete — shading and artistic flourishes earn nothing.

Stop for this section's questions now — what each view can and cannot show, the matching method with its mirror trap, the glass sized by two drawings, and the laundry drawn to scale.

## Subtopic: Assembly Diagrams and Instruction Manuals

From buildings to boxes that become furniture. An ASSEMBLY DIAGRAM is the wordless plan inside every flat-pack: an EXPLODED VIEW that floats the parts apart along the lines where they join, so the eye can see what touches what and in which order. The skill is to read one methodically, and the method transfers to any manual — a bookshelf kit, a gate hinge, a washing machine's filter.

Step one: the PARTS LIST — every component drawn, lettered and counted: two side panels, four shelves, fourteen screws marked S, one small spanner. The professional habit is the INVENTORY CHECK: count the physical parts against the list before assembly begins, because discovering a missing screw at step eight means undoing steps seven back to one. Questions mirror the shop counter too: fourteen screws are needed and screws sell in packets of four — 14 ÷ 4 = 3,5, so four packets must be bought, with two screws spare, because packets are sold whole and buying answers always round UP.

Step two: SEQUENCE. Instructions are numbered because order carries hidden constraints — the middle shelf slides into its grooves before the back board closes the frame; the wall bracket screws on while the panel still lies flat. The diagram encodes order in its geometry: a screw drawn floating on a dotted line passes through the washer, then the shelf, then into the side panel, in exactly that order along the line. Questions probe consequence: what goes wrong if step 4 is done before step 3 — and the full-marks answer names the specific failure, the blocked groove or the unreachable screw hole, never a vague statement that it will not work.

Step three: ORIENTATION cues — arrows for direction, L and R for mirrored parts, THIS SIDE UP, the finished edge facing the room. Mirrored parts are the classic failure: the left and right side panels look like twins until their pre-drilled holes disagree.

Why assessors keep returning to this material: it tests whether you can lift an exact procedure out of a technical picture under real-world constraints — counting, ordering, orienting, and buying in whole packets. Read the list, check the count, follow the numbers, respect the arrows.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the exploded view read along its dotted lines, the inventory habit, whole-packet purchases rounded up, and one out-of-order consequence named precisely.

## Subtopic: Packaging, Models and Material Estimates

The final skill family: fitting things into things, and estimating what a job consumes.

PACKAGING: how many rooibos boxes, 8 cm by 5 cm by 12 cm tall, fit a crate 40 cm by 25 cm by 24 cm? Method: match dimensions axis by axis with the boxes standing upright. Along the crate's 40 cm run 40 ÷ 8 = 5 boxes; along its 25 cm run 25 ÷ 5 = 5; so one layer holds 5 × 5 = 25. Height: 24 ÷ 12 = 2 layers. Total 25 × 2 = 50 boxes. Cross-check with volume: crate 40 × 25 × 24 = 24 000 cm³, box 8 × 5 × 12 = 480 cm³, and 24 000 ÷ 480 = 50 — a perfect fit, no wasted air. The caution that gets tested: volume division alone can overpromise. It gives a ceiling, not always the fit — if the boxes were 9 cm long, 40 ÷ 9 = 4 whole boxes per row with 4 cm left over, and that leftover slice holds nothing; only the axis-by-axis count tells the truth about whole items, and part-items do not ship. When the first orientation leaves big remainders, try the boxes on their sides.

SCALE MODELS run the plan arithmetic into 3D: a spaza-shop counter 3 m long and 1,2 m wide, modelled at 1 : 20 for a design display, becomes 15 cm by 6 cm — divide each real dimension by the scale factor — and remember from measurement work that AREAS scale by the square of the factor, so the model's top is not one-twentieth of the counter's area but one four-hundredth.

MATERIAL ESTIMATES, the money end. Tiles: the 5 m by 4 m lounge floor is 20 m²; one 200 mm by 200 mm tile covers 0,04 m²; 20 ÷ 0,04 = 500 tiles. Add the standard 10 percent for cutting and breakage: 550. Sold in boxes of 20: 550 ÷ 20 = 27,5, so buy 28 boxes — rounding UP again, because no shop splits a box and no floor stops half-tiled. Paint: the lounge walls run 2 × the sum of 5 and 4, which is 18 m around, and stand 2,6 m high: 46,8 m². Subtract the openings — the 0,9 m by 2 m door is 1,8 m², the 1,5 m by 1,2 m window another 1,8 m²: paintable area 43,2 m². Two coats doubles it to 86,4 m²; at a coverage of 9 m² per litre, 86,4 ÷ 9 = 9,6 litres — so 10 litres must be bought, because paint sells by the whole litre. State the chain — area, minus openings, times coats, divided by coverage, rounded up to buyable units — and every link earns its mark.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the axis-by-axis fit against the volume ceiling, the model's dimensions and the squared area rule, and both estimates run through to buyable quantities.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same skills across one Saturday — a neighbour's granny-flat plan on the stoep table, a flat-pack bookshelf in its box, and a run to the hardware store before it closes.

## Subtopic: The Drawing That Speaks Builder

Your neighbour Mrs Khumalo unrolls the plan of the granny flat going up behind her house, and it looks like secret writing. It is — but the language has about ten words, and you can learn them before the rusks run out.

The fat double lines are WALLS — the gap between them is how thick the real wall will be. A gap in a wall with a little quarter-moon is a DOOR, and the moon shows which way it swings — into the lounge, so nothing bulky may stand inside that arc. Thin double lines interrupting a wall are a WINDOW. WC is the toilet, the crossed box is the bath, the circle-topped square is the stove. Roof lifted off, looking straight down: that is all a floor plan is.

The magic is the scale: 1 : 100 — every millimetre of paper is 100 mm of building, which means EVERY CENTIMETRE IS A METRE. Ruler on the lounge: 50 mm by 40 mm. Real room: 5 m by 4 m. Will her 1,8 m bookshelf fit the 4 m wall and still leave the door's quarter-moon clear? Eighteen millimetres of pencil rectangle parks the bookshelf on the plan and answers the question before the bakkie is even loaded. That is the whole point of a plan: rearrange the furniture with a pencil, at no cost to anyone's back.

And run the sense check like a habit: rooms are people-sized — two to eight metres. If your conversion says the new bathroom is 30 m long, the builders did not go mad; a zero slipped somewhere in your working.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the unrolled plan are coming to you right now: the ten-word language, centimetres to metres at 1 : 100, and the bookshelf parked with a pencil.

## Subtopic: Walking Around the Paper House

The floor plan sees the flat the way a hadeda flying over sees it. But a bird's view cannot tell you how tall the windows are — for that, the plan set walks around the outside and stares at each wall like a curious neighbour: four ELEVATIONS — north, south, east and west — each named for the direction its wall FACES.

The favourite matching game: here are the floor plan and two elevations — which is which? Play detective with the north arrow. The plan puts the kitchen door and the one wide window on the east side. So pick up each elevation and count: this drawing has a door with a wide window next to it — east elevation, found. That one is nearly blank with a single tiny window — that must be the bathroom's wall; check the plan for where the bathroom hides. Count the openings, match the pattern — and mind the mirror: standing OUTSIDE looking at a wall, what the plan showed on the left now sits on the right. Think of a taxi coming toward you: its driver sits on the vehicle's right, yet appears on YOUR left. Facing something flips left and right — and that flip is exactly where the trick answer waits. Anchor to the compass instead, because east never moves.

The views also share the work. Plan says the window is 1,5 m wide; elevation says it is 1,2 m tall; only together do they price the glass — 1,8 square metres. Will the 2,3 m bookcase clear the 2,6 m ceiling? Only a vertical drawing knows; the floor plan is silent about UP.

And when a question says DRAW — a 1 : 100 plan of the 3 m by 2,5 m laundry — it is asking for ratios, not for art: a 30 mm by 25 mm rectangle, an 8 mm door gap with its quarter-moon, the window's thin lines, sizes labelled, scale written. Neat and correct earns the marks; shading earns nothing.

Your questions for this part are up now: what only elevations know, the detective method with its mirror, the glass priced by two drawings, and what DRAW actually rewards.

## Subtopic: Crates, Shelves and the Hardware List

Saturday afternoon: the flat-pack bookshelf and the hardware run.

The bookshelf's box holds a picture where the shelf has EXPLODED — every part floating apart, dotted lines showing each screw's flight path home: through the washer, through the shelf, into the side panel, in that exact order. Before anything else, do what the pros do: tip everything out and COUNT it against the parts list. Fourteen screws promised; if thirteen arrived, better to know now than at step eight with the frame half-standing. And if the shop sells screws in packets of four, needing fourteen means FOUR packets — 3,5 packets cannot be bought, so buying always rounds up, and two screws ride home as spares. Then obey the numbers: instructions are numbered because the middle shelf must slide into its grooves BEFORE the back board closes the frame — skip ahead and step eight includes undoing steps one to seven. Watch the L and the R: the two side panels are twins until their drilled holes disagree.

Then the hardware store, with the lounge's numbers in your pocket. Tiles: floor 5 by 4 is 20 square metres; each 200 mm tile covers 0,04; that is 500 tiles — but tiles crack and edges need cutting, so add 10 percent: 550. Boxes of twenty: 550 ÷ 20 = 27,5 — buy 28, because the shop does not split boxes and a floor does not stop at half a box. Paint: walls 18 m around, 2,6 m up — 46,8 square metres; take away the door's 1,8 and the window's 1,8 — 43,2 to paint; two coats — 86,4; the tin promises 9 square metres a litre — 86,4 over 9 is 9,6, so TEN litres go in the trolley. Say the chain out loud in the aisle: area, minus holes, times coats, divide by coverage, round UP to whole tins.

One last crate trick for the drive home: 50 rooibos boxes fit that crate — five along, five across, two layers — and the volume division agrees, 24 000 over 480. But if each box were one centimetre longer, whole rows would vanish while the volume sum barely blinked: real packing counts WHOLE boxes along each side, and the leftover slice ships nothing but air. Divide axis by axis first; let volume only check your answer.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the exploded view read along its dotted line, both hardware estimates rounded to what shops actually sell, and the longer-box trap explained in one sentence. Plans, elevations, manuals, crates — one Saturday, one skill: scale with a purpose.
