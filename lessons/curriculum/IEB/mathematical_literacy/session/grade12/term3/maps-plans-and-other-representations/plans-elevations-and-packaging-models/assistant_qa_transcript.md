**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: that mirror business with the elevations keeps slipping out of everyone's heads. Standing outside the east wall, why does the plan's left become my right — and what habit actually protects the mark?

**Tutor:** Fix it with one everyday picture and one paper habit. The picture: think of a taxi driving toward you. The driver sits on the vehicle's right — every car on our roads does — yet as it approaches, the driver appears on YOUR left. Nothing about the taxi changed; you are facing it, and facing something reverses left and right. That is exactly your situation with the granny flat. Reading the floor plan, you hover above the rooms looking down, sharing the building's own sense of left and right. Standing outside the east wall looking back, you have turned to face the building the way you face that taxi — so the wide window the plan placed toward the northern end of the wall now sits on the opposite side of your view. Now the paper habit that makes the mark safe: never match elevations by left and right at all — match by compass and by neighbour. Say: the plan puts the kitchen door in the middle of the east wall with the wide window on its NORTHERN side; then look at the candidate elevation and ask where north sits when I face this wall from outside — and check that the window sits on that side of the drawn door. Compass directions never flip no matter how many times you turn; left and right flip with every turn. Anchor to the thing that cannot move and the trap has nothing left to catch.

**Mandy:** Next one: in earlier grades everyone learnt to round to the NEAREST number, so 9,6 litres should round to 10 but 27,5 boxes should round to 28 only by luck — half rounds up. What is the actual rule in this topic, and would 27,2 have rounded down?

**Tutor:** No — and this is worth engraving, because it overrides the rounding you learnt for pure arithmetic. In buying contexts the rule is not round to the nearest; it is round UP, always, regardless of the decimal. If the tiles had worked out to 27,2 boxes, the answer is still 28, because 27 boxes leaves part of the floor bare and the job unfinished. The decimal's size is irrelevant; what matters is that shops sell whole units and a job must be completed. So 9,1 litres means 10 litres exactly as surely as 9,6 does. The reasoning earns marks when you state it: the cost of rounding up is a small surplus — a few spare tiles, a little leftover paint, two extra screws — while the cost of rounding down is a second trip to the shop, a possible colour or batch mismatch, and an unfinished wall in the meantime. There is a matching rule on the other side of the till: when a question asks how many complete items you can make FROM a fixed supply — how many 1,2 m curtain lengths from a 5 m roll — the division rounds DOWN, because a partial curtain is not a curtain; five divided by one comma two gives four complete lengths with material left over. So attach the direction to the question, not to the decimal: buying enough rounds up, cutting from stock rounds down, and pure arithmetic rounding to the nearest belongs to neither.

**Mandy:** This one came from homework: the packing method says try another orientation if the remainders look big. When exactly should we bother turning the boxes on their sides, and how do we show waste properly if the fit is not perfect?

**Tutor:** Turn the boxes whenever an axis leaves a large leftover relative to the box dimension — that is the signal that a different pairing of box edge to crate edge might swallow the gap. In our crate the upright fit was perfect: five by five by two gave fifty, and the volume check agreed at fifty exactly, so no other orientation can beat it and one line saying so is enough. But suppose each box were 9 cm long instead of 8. Upright, the 40 cm side takes 40 ÷ 9 = 4 whole boxes with 4 cm left over, and the count drops to 4 × 5 × 2 = 40. Now the volume ceiling is 24 000 ÷ 540, which is about 44 — so the honest answer has three labelled parts. Part one, the headline: the axis-by-axis count, 40 whole boxes, tried in more than one orientation if the remainders invite it. Part two, the check: the volume ceiling, stated AS a ceiling, never as the answer. Part three, the waste: the gap between what fits and what the volume promised — here the 4 cm slice running the crate's full width and height, which you can quantify as 4 × 25 × 24 = 2 400 cm³ of shipped air, a tenth of the crate. Questions about wasted space want exactly that subtraction. What loses marks is writing forty-four boxes because the volumes divide that way — the marking guideline looks for the whole-item count first and penalises a ceiling dressed up as a fit.

**Mandy:** Last one: the assembly diagram questions feel like carpentry, not mathematics. Where are the actual marks hiding in a bookshelf kit?

**Tutor:** The marks are pure Mathematical Literacy wearing sawdust — and there are four hiding places, the same four every time. Hiding place one: structured COUNTING. The parts list says each of the four shelves takes four screws, and the back board takes two more — questions ask for totals across sub-assemblies, which is multiplication and addition with the diagram as your data table: four fours are sixteen, plus two is eighteen. Show the sum, not just the answer. Hiding place two: PACK arithmetic, the same law as tiles and paint. Eighteen screws, packets of four: 18 ÷ 4 = 4,5, buy five packets — and the follow-up is usually the remainder, twenty bought minus eighteen used leaves two, or the cost of those five packets. Whole-packet logic plus remainders is a guaranteed pair of marks. Hiding place three: SEQUENCE reasoning. Why must the middle shelf slide into its grooves before the back board goes on? Because the closed frame blocks the groove — the mark is for naming that specific constraint, access or alignment, in one precise sentence of cause and effect. Hiding place four: ORIENTATION and mirrored parts — the L and R panels, the arrow showing which way the screw travels along its dotted line, which face carries the pre-drilled holes. Swap L and R and the holes face the room instead of the wall; the mark again sits in the named consequence. So harvest every manual question four times: totals from the list, packets and remainders, one named reason for the order, one named consequence for the orientation. None of it needs a toolbox; all of it is reading structured information and calculating on it — which is this subject's whole job description.
