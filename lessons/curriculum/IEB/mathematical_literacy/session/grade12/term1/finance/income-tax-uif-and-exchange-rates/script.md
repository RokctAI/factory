# Part 1 — Expert

Two forces reach into a South African payslip and a South African wallet from opposite directions: the taxman, who takes a share of what you earn, and the exchange rate, which decides what your remaining rands can buy from the rest of the world. Final exams test both. This session follows Karabo, a paramedic at a Bloemfontein ambulance base earning R27 000 a month, from his gross salary through the tax table to his net pay — and then follows his rands abroad, where they are measured against the euro.

## Subtopic: From Gross Salary to Taxable Income

GROSS INCOME is everything earned before anything is subtracted: basic salary, overtime, bonuses, allowances. But personal income tax is not charged on gross income — it is charged on TAXABLE INCOME, which is what remains after removing income the law does not tax and deductions the law allows.

Some money is NON-TAXABLE: inheritances, most bursaries and scholarships, donations between spouses, and the portion of interest income below the annual exemption. Other amounts are DEDUCTIONS that shrink taxable income before the tax table is applied — the most important being contributions to a pension or retirement annuity fund, within limits set by SARS.

Karabo's numbers. His gross salary is R27 000 per month, which is 27 000 × 12 = R324 000 per year. For this lesson he has no deductions, so his taxable income is R324 000. His employer reports all of this to SARS on a certificate called an IRP5, which records the year's income and every cent of tax already deducted — the document every employee should check against their own payslips.

Vocabulary that carries marks: the TAX YEAR for individuals runs from 1 March to the last day of February. PAYE, pay-as-you-earn, is the monthly deduction the employer sends to SARS in advance, so that the year's tax is collected gradually rather than in one impossible lump. The TAX THRESHOLD is the taxable income below which no tax is payable at all — for a person under 65, R95 750 in the 2023/24 year. Earn less than that and the tax table never touches you. The questions for this section are with you now; separate gross, non-taxable and taxable before any table is opened.

## Subtopic: Reading the Tax Table and the Rebate

The income tax table is a STAIRCASE OF RATES: each band of income is taxed at its own percentage, higher bands at higher rates. In the 2023/24 table, the first band runs to R237 100 and is taxed at 18%. The second band, from R237 101 to R370 500, is taxed at 26% — but only the income INSIDE that band. The table prints this as: R42 678 plus 26% of taxable income above R237 100. The R42 678 is the tax on the whole first band, already worked out: 18% of 237 100.

Apply it to Karabo's taxable income of R324 000. He is in the second band, because 324 000 lies between 237 101 and 370 500. Tax before rebates: 42 678 + 26% of the amount above 237 100. The amount above: 324 000 − 237 100 = R86 900. Then 86 900 × 0,26 = R22 594,00. Add the first-band tax: 42 678 + 22 594 = R65 272,00.

Now the REBATE — a discount every taxpayer receives, subtracted from the calculated tax. The primary rebate for under-65s is R17 235. Karabo's annual tax: 65 272 − 17 235 = R48 037,00. Monthly PAYE: 48 037 ÷ 12 = R4 003,08 to the nearest cent.

The classic error is to charge 26% on ALL R324 000, which gives R84 240 — massively wrong, because only the slice above R237 100 is taxed at 26%; everything below kept its 18% rate inside the R42 678. The rebate is also where the threshold comes from: tax only becomes payable once the calculated tax exceeds the rebate. The questions for this section are ready; find the band, tax only the slice above the band's floor, then subtract the rebate.

## Subtopic: UIF and What the Payslip Shows

Beyond PAYE, one more statutory deduction appears on nearly every payslip: UIF, the Unemployment Insurance Fund. The employee contributes 1% of their salary, the employer adds another 1%, and the fund pays benefits during unemployment, maternity leave or illness.

The 1% has a ceiling. Contributions are calculated only on salary up to a maximum — R17 712 per month in this period — so the employee contribution can never exceed 1% of that maximum: R177,12. Karabo earns R27 000, which is above the ceiling, so his UIF is not 1% of 27 000; it is the capped R177,12. A junior colleague earning R9 800, below the ceiling, would pay the straightforward 1%: R98,00.

Now assemble Karabo's payslip. Gross salary: R27 000,00. Deduction one, PAYE: R4 003,08. Deduction two, UIF: R177,12. NET PAY, the amount actually landing in his bank account: 27 000 − 4 003,08 − 177,12 = R22 819,80.

Read the proportions the way exam questions ask. Total deductions: 4 003,08 + 177,12 = R4 180,20. As a percentage of gross: 4 180,20 ÷ 27 000 × 100 = 15,48%, to two decimal places. So roughly fifteen rand in every hundred leave before Karabo sees his salary — a sentence worth writing, because interpretation marks sit at the end of every payslip question.

Note what UIF is not: it is not a savings account, and the employer's matching 1% never appears as a deduction on the employee's slip — it is paid on top. The questions for this section follow now; apply the ceiling before the percentage, and end at net pay in a sentence.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates and Buying Power

An EXCHANGE RATE is the price of one currency measured in another. The quotation R20,40 to the euro means one euro costs twenty rand forty. Written as a rate: R20,40 per euro.

Two directions, one decision. Converting EUROS TO RANDS multiplies, because each euro becomes R20,40 of ours: a German tourist exchanging 380 euros receives 380 × 20,40 = R7 752,00. Converting RANDS TO EUROS divides, because every R20,40 buys just one euro: Karabo saving R6 500 for a trip receives 6 500 ÷ 20,40 = 318,63 euros, to two decimal places.

The reliable test: a euro is worth many rands, so a number in euros must GROW when it becomes rands and SHRINK when rands become euros. If your rand answer is smaller than the euro amount you started with, the operation went the wrong way.

Now the language of movement. If the rate shifts from R20,40 to R21,10 per euro, the euro now costs more rand — the rand has WEAKENED, or depreciated. Imported goods, fuel and overseas travel become more expensive for South Africans: a camera priced at 749 euros rises from 749 × 20,40 = R15 279,60 to 749 × 21,10 = R15 803,90. If the rate falls to R19,60, the rand has STRENGTHENED: imports cheapen, and exporters earn fewer rands for the same euro sales.

That is BUYING POWER: the weaker the rand, the less of the world each rand can buy. Exam questions ask for the calculation and then the consequence — who wins, who loses, in a sentence. The questions for this section are with you now; write the rate as rand per euro, choose multiply or divide by the growth test, and finish with the effect on buying power.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same payslip and the same rands, at the kitchen table with a calculator and a cup of tea.

## Subtopic: The Ladder of Tax Brackets

Here is the fear that lives in every workplace: do not take the increase, it will push you into a higher bracket and you will take home less. That fear rests on a misunderstanding, and one picture cures it.

Income tax is a ladder. The first rung of your income — everything up to R237 100 a year — is taxed at 18 cents in the rand. Only the money ABOVE that line climbs to the next rung, where it is taxed at 26 cents in the rand. Moving into a higher bracket never re-taxes the money on the lower rungs; it only means your newest rands, the ones above the line, pay the higher rate.

Watch it work for Karabo, who earns R324 000 a year. The tax table has already done the first rung for us: tax on the full R237 100 at 18% comes to R42 678 — the table prints that number so nobody recalculates it. His income above the line is 324 000 minus 237 100: R86 900. That slice, and only that slice, pays 26%: R22 594. Add the two rungs: R65 272.

Then comes the good news line: the REBATE. The government gives every taxpayer a discount of R17 235 off the calculated tax. So Karabo truly owes 65 272 minus 17 235: R48 037 for the year, about R4 003 a month, which his employer quietly sends to SARS as PAYE before his salary lands.

And the wrong way? Charging 26% on the whole R324 000 gives R84 240 — nearly R19 000 too much tax. The ladder never does that. Some questions on this section are with you right now; tax the slice above the line, never the whole salary, and remember the discount at the end.

## Subtopic: One Percent for the Rainy Day

There is a small deduction on the payslip that most people never read: UIF. One percent of your salary, matched by another one percent from your employer, into a national fund that pays you when the work stops — retrenchment, maternity leave, long illness. It is insurance, priced at one cent in every rand.

The one wrinkle worth knowing is the ceiling. The fund only counts salary up to R17 712 a month. Earn less than that, and your UIF is a clean one percent — on R9 800 you pay R98. Earn more, like Karabo on R27 000, and the counting stops at the ceiling: his UIF is one percent of 17 712, which is R177,12, and not one percent of 27 000, which would be R270. The high earner and the very high earner pay the same capped amount.

Now put the whole payslip together like a shopping receipt in reverse. Karabo's gross: R27 000. The taxman takes R4 003,08. The rainy-day fund takes R177,12. What actually arrives in the bank — the net pay — is 27 000 minus those two: R22 819,80.

Step back and read it like a person, not a calculator: out of every hundred rand Karabo earns, about fifteen and a half leave before he wakes up on payday. That sentence — deductions are about 15,5% of gross — is exactly the kind of plain-language conclusion that earns the interpretation mark. Questions on this section are coming your way now; check the ceiling first, then subtract to net pay, then say what the payslip means in one sentence.

## Subtopic: Rands per Euro: Which Way to Divide

Exchange rates confuse people for one reason only: they cannot decide whether to multiply or divide. Fix the rate in your head as a price tag and the decision makes itself.

R20,40 per euro means exactly what a price tag means: one euro COSTS R20,40, the same grammar as apples at R20,40 a kilogram. Buying 380 euros is like buying 380 kilograms — you multiply: 380 times 20,40 is R7 752. Turning R6 500 into euros asks how many R20,40 price tags fit into R6 500 — you divide: 6 500 divided by 20,40 is about 318 euros.

Then run the sanity check: rands are the small unit, euros the big one. Rand amounts should always be the bigger number, euro amounts the smaller. If your R6 500 turned into 132 600 euros, the machine ran the wrong way — nobody gets rich at a bureau de change.

Last piece: what it means when the rate moves. From R20,40 to R21,10 per euro, the price tag on a euro went UP — the rand weakened. Everything imported quietly follows: the 749-euro camera climbs from about R15 280 to about R15 800, petrol follows the same arithmetic, and your rand buys a little less of the world. When the rate drops to R19,60, the rand strengthened, and imports breathe out. Weaker rand, expensive imports; stronger rand, cheaper imports — say it with the numbers and the interpretation mark is yours.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now; treat the rate as a price tag, multiply when turning euros into rands, divide when buying euros, and finish with what the movement does to your buying power.
