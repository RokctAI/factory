# Part 1 — Expert

Almost nothing big in South Africa is bought with cash on the counter. The fridge goes on hire purchase, the bakkie on finance with a balloon at the end, the emergency on a personal loan, and the risks of life — the funeral, the crash — are carried by insurance premiums. Every one of these is a deal with a price hiding inside it, and final exams test whether you can find that price. This session follows one street in East London: a fridge bought on instalments, a first bakkie financed with a balloon payment, a R18 000 personal loan, and the policies that pay out on the worst day.

## Subtopic: Hire Purchase: The True Price of Pay-Later

HIRE PURCHASE, written HP, is buying on instalments: pay a DEPOSIT now, take the goods home today, and pay the balance in equal monthly INSTALMENTS — with interest and charges loaded inside them. Until the final instalment, the goods legally still belong to the store.

Mrs Jacobs wants a fridge with a CASH PRICE of R9 499. The HP deal: a 15% deposit and 30 monthly instalments of R379,90. First the deposit: 15% of 9 499 is R1 424,85. Then the instalments: 379,90 × 30 = R11 397,00. The TOTAL HP PRICE is deposit plus all instalments: 1 424,85 + 11 397,00 = R12 821,85.

Now the number the exam always wants: the EXTRA paid for the convenience of paying later. 12 821,85 − 9 499 = R3 322,85 — about 35% more than the cash price. That extra is the interest and fees of the agreement, and the comparison sentence is compulsory: on HP the fridge costs R3 322,85 more than paying cash.

The gentler cousin is LAY-BY: pay the store in instalments with no interest, but the goods stay in the store until the last payment. The same fridge on a five-month lay-by costs exactly R9 499 — about R1 900 a month — but Mrs Jacobs keeps her old fridge limping along until it is paid. HP sells time; lay-by sells patience. The questions for this section are with you now; calculate deposit, total instalments and total HP price separately, because each carries its own mark.

## Subtopic: Car Finance: Deposits, Instalments and the Balloon

A vehicle is usually the biggest HP-style deal a household ever signs, and it comes with a special trap: the BALLOON PAYMENT, also called a RESIDUAL — a large lump sum left over at the END of the agreement, after all the monthly instalments are done.

Sibusiso finances a bakkie priced at R215 000. The agreement: a 10% deposit of R21 500, then 72 monthly repayments of R3 285, and a balloon payment of 35% of the price — R75 250 — due in month seventy-two. The balloon's job is seduction: because R75 250 of the debt is pushed to the end, the monthly instalment looks affordable. Without the balloon, the same loan would need a much higher monthly payment.

Total the deal honestly. Repayments: 3 285 × 72 = R236 520. Add the deposit and the balloon: 21 500 + 236 520 + 75 250 = R333 270. The bakkie costs R333 270 in total — R118 270 more than the price on the windscreen. And the balloon is not optional: in month seventy-two Sibusiso must pay R75 250 in one blow, refinance it into a new loan with new interest, or sell the bakkie to settle it.

The exam skills: compute each component, total the true cost, compare against the cash price, and answer the interpretation question — why do balloon deals advertise such low instalments, and what waits at the end? The questions for this section follow now; list deposit, instalments and balloon as three separate lines before adding.

## Subtopic: Personal Loans and the Total Cost of Credit

A PERSONAL LOAN is borrowed cash: the bank pays out a lump sum, and the borrower repays monthly over a fixed TERM. The advertised monthly repayment is never the whole story, because loans carry FEES: a once-off INITIATION FEE when the loan starts, and a monthly SERVICE FEE for administering it.

Naledi borrows R18 000 over 24 months. The repayment is R939 per month, the initiation fee is R1 050,00, and the service fee is R60 per month. Her true monthly outflow is 939 + 60 = R999. The TOTAL COST OF CREDIT question asks: how much does the loan cost in total, and how much of that is the price of borrowing?

Total paid: the initiation fee plus 24 months of repayment-plus-service-fee: 1 050 + 24 × 999 = 1 050 + 23 976 = R25 026,00. The cost of credit is everything paid minus the cash received: 25 026 − 18 000 = R7 026,00. Borrowing R18 000 cost more than R7 000 — about 39% of the loan itself.

Affordability is the other examinable angle, and it is an income-and-expenditure calculation. Naledi's monthly income is R8 600; her expenses — rent, transport, food, electricity, airtime — total R7 300, leaving a SURPLUS of R1 300. The instalment of R999 fits, but only just: R301 of breathing room, one taxi-fare crisis away from a missed payment. Lenders must run this check by law, and exam questions ask you to run it too. The questions for this section are ready; separate the once-off fee from the monthly ones, and let total paid minus amount borrowed give the cost of credit.

## Subtopic: Insurance: Premiums, Cover and the Excess

INSURANCE is a deal about risk: you pay a small, certain amount every month — the PREMIUM — and the insurer pays a large amount — the COVER — if the insured disaster strikes. The household on our street carries two policies.

The first is FUNERAL COVER: R95 per month for R25 000 of cover. Funeral insurance is the most widely held policy in South Africa because funerals are certain, sudden and expensive. The arithmetic of the deal: R95 × 12 = R1 140 per year in premiums. To pay in R25 000 at that rate would take over 263 months — nearly twenty-two years — yet the full R25 000 pays out even if the policy is four months old. That is the point of insurance: the many who do not claim this year carry the few who must.

The second is VEHICLE INSURANCE at R920 per month — R11 040 a year. Vehicle policies carry an EXCESS: the first part of any claim that the owner must pay personally. Sibusiso's excess is R5 200. When a crash causes R31 400 of damage, the insurer pays 31 400 − 5 200 = R26 200, and Sibusiso pays the R5 200. The excess exists to keep small claims out of the system and premiums lower.

The examinable comparisons: premium versus cover, total premiums over a period versus a claim, and the effect of the excess on a payout. One more reading skill — a vehicle financed with a balloon almost always REQUIRES comprehensive insurance in the contract, because the bank wants its R333 270 protected. The questions for this section are with you now; name the premium, the cover and the excess before calculating anything.

## Subtopic: The Fridge That Cost a Third More

The price tag in the shop window says R9 499. Mrs Jacobs walks out having agreed to pay R12 821,85. Nobody robbed her — she signed for it, and the signing is called hire purchase.

Here is the deal in plain words: pay a slice now — the deposit, R1 424,85 — take the fridge home today, and pay R379,90 a month for two and a half years. Each payment feels small. That is the design. But do the multiplication the shop hopes you skip: 379,90 times 30 is R11 397. Add the deposit back: R12 821,85. The fridge costs R3 322,85 more than the window price — about a third extra — and until the last payment, the fridge still belongs to the store. Miss payments, and it can go back on the truck.

The alternative in the same shop is lay-by: pay about R1 900 a month for five months, pay exactly R9 499 in total, not one cent of interest — but the fridge waits in the storeroom until the money is done. So the real choice is honest: HP buys the fridge today and charges R3 322,85 for the favour; lay-by costs nothing extra but tests your patience for five months.

The exam move, every single time: total everything you would pay — deposit plus every instalment — and stand it next to the cash price. The gap is the price of impatience, and naming that gap in rands is where the marks live. Some questions on this section are with you right now; multiply, add the deposit, compare, and say the difference out loud.

## Subtopic: The Bubble at the End of the Car Loan

Sibusiso's eyes are on the monthly number: R3 285. Tight, but doable. The salesperson smiles, because the number was built to be looked at. The trick is floating seventy-two months away: a balloon payment of R75 250.

A balloon deal is simple once you see the shape. Take the bakkie's price, R215 000. Slice off a deposit, R21 500. Then, instead of spreading ALL the remaining debt over the seventy-two months, push a fat bubble of it — 35% of the price — to the very end. The monthly instalments only carry the middle part, so they look light. The bubble waits.

Add the whole thing up like an accountant, not like a dreamer: deposit R21 500, plus seventy-two payments of R3 285 — that is R236 520 — plus the balloon, R75 250. Total: R333 270, for a bakkie that was priced at R215 000. R118 270 extra. And in month seventy-two, the bubble bursts into a bill: pay R75 250 cash, or borrow AGAIN to cover it — new loan, new interest, the debt getting a second life — or sell the bakkie to settle it and start from nothing.

None of this makes balloon deals evil; it makes them expensive, and the law says the dealer must show you the total. The skill is refusing to judge a deal by its monthly payment. Judge it by one number: everything you will ever pay, added up. Questions on this section are coming your way now; find the three pieces — deposit, instalments, balloon — and total them before any opinion.

## Subtopic: Paying for the Worst Day Before It Comes

Every month, R95 leaves the account for funeral cover, and every month nothing happens. Ouma asks the fair question: is this not money thrown into the river?

Look at what the R95 actually buys: a promise of R25 000 on the family's hardest day. Save R95 a month in a tin instead, and reaching R25 000 takes over 263 months — nearly twenty-two years. The policy pays the full amount even if the worst day comes in month four. That is the machine of insurance: thousands of families each put in a little, and the money flows to the few whose worst day arrived this month. You are not buying a payout; you are buying certainty that the funeral will not bury the family in debt as well as grief.

The vehicle policy runs the same logic with one extra lever: the excess. Sibusiso pays R920 a month, and when the crash comes — R31 400 of damage — the insurer pays R26 200 and he covers the first R5 200 himself. The excess is the deal's way of saying: bring us the disasters, not the scratches. Without it, every tiny claim would push everyone's premiums up.

And the two halves of this lesson lock together: the bank financing Sibusiso's R333 270 bakkie insists on insurance, because until the balloon is paid, the bakkie is the bank's security. A financed bakkie that burns uninsured leaves the debt alive with nothing to show for it — the worst outcome in this whole topic. So the closing habit: premium, cover, excess — name all three on any policy, and weigh the certain small cost against the possible huge one. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; calculate what the insurer pays after the excess, and say in a sentence what the premium is really buying.
