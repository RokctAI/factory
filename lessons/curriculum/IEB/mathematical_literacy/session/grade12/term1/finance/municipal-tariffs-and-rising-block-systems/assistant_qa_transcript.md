**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up every single year: for 420 units, why can't I just multiply all 420 by R2,45, since 420 falls inside block two?

**Tutor:** Because the block a consumption FINISHES in is not the block it was all charged in. Think of the blocks as containers that fill from the bottom. The first 150 units always land in block one and are charged at block one's price, R1,95 each — that is R292,50. Only the units that spill over into block two get block two's price: 420 minus 150 leaves 270 units at R2,45, which is R661,50. The correct total is R954. Multiplying all 420 by R2,45 gives R1 029, and the R75 difference is exactly the discount the tariff gives you on your first 150 units — a discount the wrong method throws away. The tariff table is written as ranges, 0 to 150, 151 to 450, and the range tells you which units it prices, not which customers it applies to. A reliable habit for the exam: before multiplying anything, write down how many units sit in each block — 150 in the first, 270 in the second, zero in the rest — and check that those numbers add back to the total consumption. If your block units do not sum to 420, the bill will be wrong no matter how careful the multiplication is.

**Mandy:** Next one, from the water bill: the household used 26 kilolitres, so why is the middle block charged for 14 kilolitres and not 20?

**Tutor:** Because 20 is where the block ENDS, not how many units it holds. The middle block runs from 6 kl to 20 kl, and its capacity is the difference between those boundaries: 20 minus 6, which is 14 kilolitres. The first 6 kl were already accounted for in the free block, so by the time the household's consumption reaches the middle block, only kilolitres number seven through twenty pass through it — fourteen of them, at R16,80 each, R235,20. Then the remaining 26 minus 20, which is 6 kilolitres, land in the top block at R29,50. This boundary subtraction is where most marks are lost in tariff questions, because the table shows the numbers 6, 20 and 35 so prominently that learners multiply by them directly. Train yourself to convert every range into a capacity first: 0 to 6 holds 6; 6 to 20 holds 14; 20 to 35 holds 15. Then check the used amounts against those capacities — free block full at 6, middle block full at 14, top block holding the last 6 — and confirm that 6 plus 14 plus 6 gives back the 26 kilolitres on the meter. That check takes ten seconds and catches nearly every boundary error.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: someone said prepaid electricity is always cheaper because there is no fixed monthly charge. Is that true?

**Tutor:** It is true only for some households, and the whole point of the comparison skill is to find out which. The fixed charge is a bad deal for small users and a good deal for big ones, because it is the same R250 regardless of usage. A household using 420 units spreads that R250 over 420 units — about 60 cents extra per unit — and for them prepaid wins, R954 against R1 048. But a household using 650 units spreads the same R250 much thinner, about 38 cents per unit, and meanwhile the prepaid staircase is punishing them: their last 200 units cost R2,85 each, while the conventional deal keeps charging a flat R1,90. At 650 units the conventional option costs R1 485 against prepaid's R1 597,50, and prepaid loses by R112,50. So the honest answer is: prepaid is cheaper below the break-even consumption and more expensive above it, and the break-even sits where the two cost lines cross on the graph, somewhere between those two usage levels. In an exam, never answer a comparison question with a general rule. Calculate both options at the consumption the question gives, name the cheaper one, state the saving in rands, and tie your advice to that specific household's usage. The question is testing whether you compare, not whether you memorised a slogan.

**Mandy:** Last one: when does the VAT go on? Someone added 15% to each block separately and someone else added it right at the end, and they got confused about whether it matters.

**Tutor:** Mathematically it gives the same answer either way, because 15% of a sum equals the sum of 15% of the parts — but one of the two methods is far safer, and full-marks working is built around it. Add all the consumption charges and any fixed charge first, write that line down as the subtotal, and apply VAT once, to the whole subtotal. On the water bill: R235,20 plus R177,00 gives R412,20, VAT is 412,20 times 0,15, which is R61,83 to the nearest cent, and the amount payable is R474,03. Doing VAT block by block multiplies your rounding opportunities — each block's VAT gets rounded to a cent, and three small roundings can drift the total by a cent or two away from the expected answer. Doing it once at the end matches the way the real bill is printed: consumption lines, subtotal, VAT line, total due. Two cautions while we are here. First, if a block is free, its VAT is zero — you never add VAT to the free basic water. Second, if a question hands you a total that already includes VAT and asks for the consumption charges, do not subtract 15%: divide by 1,15, because the inclusive total is 115% of the subtotal. The bill reads downward with VAT last; your working should read the same way.
