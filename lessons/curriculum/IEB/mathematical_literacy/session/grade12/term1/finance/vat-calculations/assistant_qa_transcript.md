**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first, because it comes up every year: why is taking 15% off an inclusive price wrong when adding 15% to an exclusive price is right? It feels like the same move in reverse.

**Tutor:** It feels symmetrical, but percentages are not symmetrical, because the two moves take their 15% from different bases. Adding VAT takes 15% of the SMALLER number, the exclusive price: 15% of R340 is R51, and R340 grows to R391. Now try to reverse it by taking 15% of R391 — but R391 is the bigger number, so 15% of it is R58,65, a thicker slice than the R51 that was ever added. Subtract it and you land at R332,35, below the true R340. The genuine reverse of multiplying by 1,15 is dividing by 1,15, the same way the reverse of doubling is halving, not subtracting the same amount you added. The inclusive price is 115% of the original, so dividing by 1,15 recovers the original 100% exactly: 391 divided by 1,15 is 340. And you can always certify your answer in five seconds by running it forwards — 340 times 1,15 must give back the 391 on the shelf. If a backwards answer cannot reproduce the price you started from, it is wrong, no matter how reasonable it looked on the way down.

**Mandy:** Next: a shop is advertising a washing machine at R8 049 and the poster says price includes VAT. The question asked for the VAT amount, and half the class multiplied by 0,15. What is the fastest correct route?

**Tutor:** Two correct routes exist, and both start from the same fact: R8 049 is 115%, not 100%. Route one is the two-step: divide by 1,15 to find the exclusive price — 8 049 divided by 1,15 gives R6 999,13 to the nearest cent — then subtract from the inclusive price: 8 049 minus 6 999,13 gives VAT of R1 049,87. Route two is the direct fraction: VAT is fifteen parts of every 115 parts of an inclusive price, so multiply 8 049 by 15 and divide by 115, which gives the same R1 049,87 in one move. What you may never do is multiply the inclusive price by 0,15, because that computes 15% of a number that already contains the tax — it treats R8 049 as if it were the pre-VAT price, and it inflates the VAT to R1 207,35. The quick mental test that guards you: the VAT amount must always be smaller than 15% of the sticker price, never equal to it, precisely because the sticker is the swollen 115% figure. In the exam, show the fraction 15 over 115 or the divide-by-1,15 step explicitly — that visible step is usually where the method mark lives.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: someone said their whole till slip should have VAT taken out with the 15 over 115 fraction, and someone else said you cannot, because of the rice. Who is right?

**Tutor:** The one who mentioned the rice. The fraction 15 over 115 is perfectly correct — but only on money that actually contains VAT, and a South African trolley is nearly always a mixture. Brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, dried beans, tinned pilchards, cooking oil — these are zero-rated, taxed at 0% by deliberate policy so that basic food stays affordable, and their prices contain not one cent of VAT. Apply 15 over 115 to a total that includes them and you invent tax that was never charged. On our Saturday slip the zero-rated pile came to R214,60 and the starred, standard-rated pile to R356,50; the honest VAT is 15 over 115 of the R356,50 only, which is R46,50, not 15 over 115 of the R571,10 total, which would claim R74,49. So the order of operations for any till-slip question is fixed: sort first, calculate second. And learn the trap pairs that questions love — brown bread zero-rated but white bread taxed, fresh potatoes zero-rated but frozen chips taxed, raw eggs zero-rated but a takeaway omelette taxed, because preparation moves food off the protected list. When a question mentions a till slip, your first pen stroke should be splitting it into the two piles.

**Mandy:** Last one: an electrician's quote said R9 600 excluding VAT, and someone answered the customer pays R9 600, arguing VAT is the electrician's problem. How should we think about who actually pays?

**Tutor:** Follow the money one step at a time and the roles become clear. The electrician is a registered vendor, which makes her a tax COLLECTOR, not the tax payer. When the job is invoiced, the law obliges her to add 15% — R1 440 on that quote — so the customer's bill reads R11 040. The customer pays the full R11 040; the electrician keeps R9 600 for the work and holds the R1 440 in trust for SARS. When she does her VAT return, she pays that R1 440 over, minus any VAT she herself paid on materials — which is why the system is called value-added: each link in the chain pays tax only on the value it adds. But trace the whole chain to its end and every cent of VAT was funded by one person: the final consumer, the one with nobody further down the chain to charge. That is why exam questions phrase it as the amount payable by the customer — and why answering R9 600 loses the marks, because an excluding-VAT price is by definition not what a consumer pays. Practical reading habit: on any quote or invoice, find the VAT line before the total. If the words excl. VAT appear anywhere, the real cost to you is the printed figure times 1,15, and it is worth doing that multiplication before you sign anything.
