# Part 1 — Expert

Money is never standing still. Left in the right account it grows; left under the mattress it silently shrinks, because prices climb while the notes stay the same. Final exams test both movements: interest pulling your money up, and inflation dragging its buying power down, with a bank statement full of fees sitting in the middle. This session follows one family in Mitchells Plain: Mrs Adams with a R12 000 savings-club payout to invest, her niece Ayanda's first bank statement, and the price of a two-litre bottle of milk doing its slow, relentless climb.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest: The Straight-Line Grower

INTEREST is the price of money. When you deposit money, the bank pays you interest for the use of it; when you borrow, you pay the bank. The money you start with is the PRINCIPAL, the percentage per year is the INTEREST RATE, and the time is measured in years.

SIMPLE INTEREST is calculated on the original principal only, every single year. Mrs Adams invests her R12 000 savings-club payout at 6,5% simple interest per year. One year's interest is 6,5% of 12 000: 12 000 × 0,065 = R780,00. And here is the defining fact: it is R780 in year one, R780 in year two, R780 in year three — always calculated on the original R12 000, never on the growing total.

After 3 years the interest is 780 × 3 = R2 340,00, and the investment is worth 12 000 + 2 340 = R14 340,00. The formula says the same thing in one line: final amount equals principal times, in brackets, one plus the rate times the number of years. Check it: 12 000 × (1 + 0,065 × 3) = 12 000 × 1,195 = R14 340,00.

Simple interest grows in a straight line — equal steps of R780, year after year. On a graph it is a straight, tilted line, and that straightness is exactly how an exam asks you to recognise it. Hire purchase agreements and short-term loans often use simple interest, which is why it matters beyond the savings account. The questions for this section are with you now; find the principal, the rate and the years before you multiply anything.

## Subtopic: Compound Interest: Interest That Earns Interest

COMPOUND INTEREST changes one rule and everything else follows: each year's interest is calculated on the CURRENT balance, not the original principal. The interest earns its own interest.

Same R12 000, same 6,5%, but compounded yearly. Year one: 12 000 × 1,065 = R12 780,00. Year two starts from 12 780, not 12 000: 12 780 × 1,065 = R13 610,70. Year three starts from 13 610,70: 13 610,70 × 1,065 = R14 495,40. Compare the two machines after 3 years: simple gave R14 340,00, compound gives R14 495,40 — a R155,40 advantage, earned by the interest itself going to work.

The year-by-year table is the Mathematical Literacy method, and it earns its marks: each row shows the balance being multiplied by 1,065 again. The multiplier logic is the VAT logic wearing a new jersey: 100% of the balance plus 6,5% growth is 106,5%, so each year multiplies by 1,065. Three years of compounding is × 1,065, three times over.

The gap between simple and compound starts small and widens forever. At 5 years, simple reaches R15 900,00 flat, while compound reaches 12 000 × 1,065 to the power five — R16 441,04. On a graph, simple interest is the straight line and compound interest is the upward-bending curve that pulls away from it; every year the curve's lead grows. That is why long-term savings, investments and unfortunately also long-term debts are almost always compound. The questions for this section follow now; build the table year by year, and label each answer as the balance at the end of that year.

## Subtopic: Reading a Bank Statement and Hunting the Fees

A BANK STATEMENT is the diary of an account: every deposit, withdrawal, debit order and fee, in date order, each line updating the BALANCE. Money coming in is a CREDIT to your account; money going out is a DEBIT. A statement question asks three things: explain a line, find the fees, and confirm the closing balance.

Walk through Ayanda's statement for one month. Opening balance: R860,40. Her wages arrive: a credit of R4 200,00, balance R5 060,40. A DEBIT ORDER — a payment the account owner authorised to run automatically each month — takes R329,00 for her phone contract, balance R4 731,40. She draws R600,00 cash at another bank's ATM, balance R4 131,40, and because the machine belonged to another bank, a fee of R10,50 follows, balance R4 120,90. Month end brings the monthly ADMIN FEE of R5,90, closing balance R4 115,00.

Now the fee hunt, because this is where statements earn their exam marks: the bank charged R10,50 plus R5,90, so R16,40 of Ayanda's money left without buying her anything. Banks charge for account administration, for cash withdrawals — especially at other banks' ATMs — for declined debit orders and for paper statements, and the tariff differs between account types. Over a year, R16,40 a month is R196,80.

The verification skill: closing balance must equal opening balance plus all credits minus all debits. Check: 860,40 + 4 200,00 − 329,00 − 600,00 − 10,50 − 5,90 = R4 115,00. If a statement question says find the missing value, this equation is the tool. The questions for this section are ready; read each line as money in or money out, and let the running balance prove your arithmetic.

## Subtopic: Inflation: Rising Prices, Shrinking Buying Power

INFLATION is the general rise in prices over time, quoted as a percentage per year. If inflation is 5%, then what cost R100 this year costs about R105 next year. The arithmetic is compound growth again — the same × 1,05 machine — but now it works against you, because your money buys less each year.

Track a two-litre bottle of milk at R32,00 with inflation at 5%. Next year: 32,00 × 1,05 = R33,60. The year after, the increase compounds: 33,60 × 1,05 = R35,28. Prices inflate on the new price, exactly as compound interest grows on the new balance.

BUYING POWER is the other side of the same coin. Today R200 buys six bottles at R32,00, with change. Two years later, at R35,28 a bottle, the same R200 buys only five, with more change but fewer bottles. The note did not change; what it can do changed. Push it further: at 5% for ten years the multiplier is 1,05 to the power ten, about 1,63 — prices climb by nearly two thirds, and a R200 note keeps only about R122,78 of today's buying power.

This is why keeping cash idle is a slow loss, and why the exam asks the comparison question: a wage of R7 500 per month rises by 3% to R7 725, but keeping up with 5% inflation needed R7 875 — the earner is R150 a month poorer in buying power despite the bigger number. An increase below inflation is a decrease in disguise. Graphs of price over time curve upward like compound interest, and reading values off them is examinable. The questions for this section are with you now; ask of every amount whether it grew faster or slower than prices did.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same money, told at the kitchen table in Mitchells Plain with a calculator and last month's till slips.

## Subtopic: The Two Ways Money Grows

Mrs Adams's savings club paid out R12 000, and the bank offers her two deals. Deal one: 6,5% simple interest. Deal two: 6,5% compound. Same rate, same money — surely the same result? Not even close, and the difference is one small rule.

Simple interest is a worker paid to grow only the ORIGINAL R12 000. Every year he delivers the same R780, because he never looks at the growing pile — only at the R12 000 she started with. Three years, three deliveries: R2 340, and the pot stands at R14 340.

Compound interest is a worker who grows WHATEVER IS IN THE POT. Year one he turns 12 000 into 12 780 — same R780, no difference yet. But year two he works on 12 780 and delivers R830,70. Year three he works on 13 610,70 and delivers R884,70. The pot ends at R14 495,40 — R155,40 ahead, and the lead grows every single year after that.

The picture to keep: simple interest walks in a straight line, same-size step every year; compound interest is a snowball rolling downhill, picking up snow on top of the snow it already picked up. Short race, small gap. Long race, no contest. That is why savings left to compound for years become serious money, and why debts left to compound become serious trouble. Some questions on this section are with you right now; year by year, multiply by 1,065, and write down every year's balance.

## Subtopic: The Statement on the Kitchen Table

Ayanda's first bank statement arrived and she swears the bank is stealing from her: she never bought anything for R5,90. Spread the statement flat and read it like a story, top to bottom.

Every line is money in or money out, and the balance column keeps the score. Wages came in: R4 200 — balance jumps. The phone contract's debit order went out: R329 — that one she authorised months ago, and it will run every month like clockwork until she cancels it. She drew R600 cash. So far, everything is her own spending.

Then the quiet lines. R10,50 — the fee for drawing cash at another bank's machine, when her own bank's ATM around the corner might have cost less than half of that. And the famous R5,90: the monthly admin fee, the rent an account charges just for existing. Nobody stole anything; the tariff was in the fine print all along. Total fees this month: R16,40. That is a loaf and a half of bread, gone. Over a year: R196,80 — a school tracksuit.

The skill that turns a statement from a mystery into a tool: check the closing balance yourself. Start at the opening 860,40, add what came in, subtract every line that went out, and land — to the cent — on 4 115,00. If your total and the bank's total disagree, one of you is wrong, and now you can prove which. Questions on this section are coming your way now; hunt the fees first, then rebuild the closing balance.

## Subtopic: The Bottle of Milk Time Machine

Here is a machine that shows the future, and it runs on milk. A two-litre bottle costs R32,00 today. Inflation is running at 5% — meaning prices as a whole climb 5% a year. Feed the bottle into the machine: next year it comes out at R33,60. Feed that price back in: R35,28 the year after. Same milk, same size — only the number on the shelf grew.

Now watch the R200 note in your pocket. Today it buys six bottles. In two years it buys five. The note never changed — its POWER changed. That is the whole meaning of inflation: money quietly getting weaker while it sits still. Run the machine ten years at 5% and prices multiply by about 1,63 — nearly two thirds more — so the R200 note limps along with roughly R123 of today's strength.

This machine explains two family arguments. First: money under the mattress is not safe — it is melting at the inflation rate, every year, guaranteed. Money earning 6,5% while inflation runs 5% is actually gaining ground; money earning nothing is losing it. Second: the wage increase. An increase of 3% when inflation is 5% sounds like a win — the number went from R7 500 to R7 725 — but keeping up needed R7 875, so the family is R150 a month behind. Any increase below inflation is a polite pay cut.

So the final habit: never judge money by its number; judge it by what it buys, and compare every growth rate against the inflation rate before celebrating. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; run the × 1,05 machine carefully, and say in a sentence who wins — the money or the prices.
