**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The one that comes up a lot: if hire purchase makes a R9 499 fridge cost R12 821,85, why would anyone ever choose it over lay-by?

**Tutor:** Because the two products sell different things, and sometimes the expensive one is genuinely the rational choice. Lay-by sells a price: pay about R1 900 a month for five months, pay exactly R9 499, not a cent of interest — but the fridge lives in the storeroom until the last payment. Hire purchase sells time: the fridge is in your kitchen tonight, and you pay R3 322,85 for that privilege over two and a half years. If the item is a luxury, lay-by wins on pure arithmetic almost every time. But picture the item being a fridge in the middle of summer, or a bed, or the stove a family cooks on — five months of waiting has a real cost too, just not one printed on a receipt. The honest exam answer computes both totals, names the difference in rands, and then matches the deal to the household's situation: HP when the goods are needed now and the budget can carry the instalment; lay-by when patience is affordable and the extra R3 322,85 is better kept. What is never rational is signing an HP agreement without doing the total — deposit plus every instalment — because the monthly figure of R379,90 is designed to feel small, and only the total reveals that the fridge costs about a third more. And remember the ownership clause: on HP the store owns the fridge until the final payment, so missed instalments can take it back off your floor after months of paying.

**Mandy:** Next: the bakkie adverts with tiny instalments and a balloon payment — my cousin says the balloon is just a formality the dealer sorts out later. True or dangerous?

**Tutor:** Dangerous, and the arithmetic shows exactly how dangerous. The balloon is not a formality; it is real debt — 35% of the bakkie's price, R75 250 on our deal — that the agreement simply postpones to month seventy-two. Nothing about postponement makes it smaller; in fact interest arrangements around a residual typically make the whole deal dearer, which is why Sibusiso's R215 000 bakkie totals R333 270 once the deposit, the seventy-two instalments of R3 285 and the balloon are all added. When month seventy-two arrives, exactly three doors exist. Door one: pay R75 250 in cash, which very few households have waiting after six years of instalments. Door two: refinance — borrow again to pay the balloon, meaning new interest on old debt, and the bakkie keeps costing money into its ninth or tenth year. Door three: sell the bakkie to settle the balloon, and start again with no vehicle and no savings, hoping it is still worth more than R75 250 by then. The reason dealers love the structure is the reason buyers should be wary of it: the advertised instalment answers the question can I afford this month, while the balloon quietly changes the answer to can I afford month seventy-two. The law obliges the credit agreement to state the total cost, so the defence is simply to read it: one line, everything you will ever pay, and that line — not the instalment — is the price of the bakkie.

**Mandy:** This one came from a homework argument: the loan advert said R939 per month, but the memo's answer for total cost was thousands higher. Which fees count?

**Tutor:** All of them — that is the whole principle of the total cost of credit, and it is why the advertised instalment is never the finish line. The National Credit Act allows a lender three main charges: interest, which is baked inside the R939 repayment; a once-off initiation fee for setting up the loan, R1 050 on Naledi's agreement; and a monthly service fee for running the account, R60 here. The method is one honest sum: everything paid out, minus everything received. Naledi pays the initiation fee, plus twenty-four months of repayment and service fee together — 939 plus 60 is R999 a month, times 24 is R23 976 — so R25 026 leaves her pocket over the term. She received R18 000. The credit cost her 25 026 minus 18 000, which is R7 026 — about 39% of what she borrowed. Answers earn marks for each component, so list them separately before totalling: repayments, service fees, initiation fee. And the companion skill is affordability, which is an income-and-expenditure statement in miniature: income R8 600, expenses R7 300, surplus R1 300, instalment R999 — it fits, with only R301 to spare, and that thin margin is exactly the sort of interpretive point an exam asks you to comment on. A loan that fits on paper with no room for a single emergency is a loan that fits badly.

**Mandy:** Last one, from Ouma herself: years of premiums, never a single claim. Would the family not be richer just saving the money?

**Tutor:** Run both plans forward and let the worst day arrive in each one — that is the only fair test of insurance, because insurance is bought for the worst day, not the average one. Plan one, the tin: R95 a month saved faithfully. After two years the tin holds R2 280. If the funeral comes then, the family is R22 720 short, and shortfalls like that are exactly how funeral debt starts — often on a personal loan whose cost of credit we have just seen. Plan two, the policy: the same R95 a month, and if the funeral comes in month four or month four hundred, the full R25 000 pays out. The premium does not buy a payout; it buys the removal of a risk the family cannot absorb. The tin would need over 263 months — nearly twenty-two years of perfect, untouched saving — just to match what the policy promises from its first month. Where Ouma's instinct deserves respect is on the details: cover should match real funeral costs, premiums must fit the budget because a lapsed policy can pay nothing, and waiting periods in the fine print matter. The same weighing applies to the bakkie: R920 a month feels heavy until the R31 400 crash, when the insurer's R26 200 — the damage minus the R5 200 excess — is the difference between an inconvenience and a catastrophe, especially on a financed vehicle the bank still effectively owns. Premiums are not wasted money; they are the price of the family never facing its biggest bills alone.
