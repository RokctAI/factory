# Part 1 — Expert

Fifteen percent of almost everything you buy in South Africa is tax. It hides inside the price of airtime, school shoes, an electrician's quote and a plate of hot chips, and it is called VAT — value-added tax. Final exams test VAT in both directions: adding it on and stripping it out, and the second direction is where the marks are lost. This session follows one Saturday of spending in Mamelodi: a supermarket till slip, an electrician's quote for a home repair, and the basket of foods the law leaves untaxed.

## Subtopic: How VAT Works and Who Pays It

VAT, value-added tax, is an INDIRECT TAX of 15% charged on most goods and services sold in South Africa. Indirect means the shop collects it from you inside the price and passes it on to SARS, the South African Revenue Service — you pay the tax without ever filling in a form. A business registered to charge VAT is called a VENDOR, and any business with a turnover above R1 million per year must register.

Two phrases control every VAT calculation. A price EXCLUDING VAT, also written excl. VAT, is the price before the tax goes on — this is how electricians, wholesalers and business quotes usually talk. A price INCLUDING VAT, or incl. VAT, is the final price with the tax already inside — this is what appears on supermarket shelves and till slips, because the law requires consumer prices to be displayed VAT inclusive.

The percentage machinery: the exclusive price is 100%. Add 15% of it and the inclusive price is 115% of the exclusive price. That single sentence — inclusive equals 115% of exclusive — generates every method in this lesson. Multiplying by 1,15 moves you from exclusive to inclusive; dividing by 1,15 moves you back.

Who really carries the tax? Not the shop. The vendor collects VAT on sales, subtracts VAT already paid on its own purchases, and sends the difference to SARS. The final consumer — the person at the till with no one further down the chain — is the one who ultimately pays. That is you, and that is why reading VAT correctly is a life skill and not just an exam skill. The questions for this section are with you now; identify whether each price is inclusive or exclusive before touching anything else.

## Subtopic: Adding VAT: From Exclusive to Inclusive

Adding VAT is the friendly direction. An electrician quotes R9 600 excluding VAT to rewire a kitchen. The VAT is 15% of the quote: 9 600 × 0,15 = R1 440,00. The amount actually payable is 9 600 + 1 440 = R11 040,00.

The one-step version multiplies by 1,15 directly: 9 600 × 1,15 = R11 040,00 — same answer, because 115% of a number is the number times 1,15. If a question asks for the VAT amount AND the total, calculate the 15% separately so both parts are visible in your working.

A second example at till-slip scale. A pair of soccer boots is priced at R620 excluding VAT in a wholesaler's catalogue. VAT: 620 × 0,15 = R93,00. Inclusive price: 620 + 93 = R713,00, or in one step 620 × 1,15 = R713,00.

Two disciplines protect these marks. First, state which price you have been given. Exam questions bury the word excluding in the middle of a paragraph, and learners who miss it add VAT to a price that already contains it — taxing the tax. Second, round only at the end, and to the nearest cent. VAT on R9 599 would be 1 439,85 exactly; write the cents, because careful working carries them.

And always finish in context: the customer pays R11 040,00, of which R1 440,00 goes to SARS. A number with a sentence around it is a Mathematical Literacy answer; a bare number is only arithmetic. The questions for this section are ready; find the exclusive price, take the 15%, and give the total in a sentence.

## Subtopic: Removing VAT: Working Backwards from the Till

Now the direction that fails learners every year: the price INCLUDES VAT, and the question wants the price before VAT, or the VAT amount hiding inside.

A school tracksuit costs R391,00 on the shelf — VAT inclusive. The wrong move is to take 15% off: 15% of 391 is R58,65, and 391 − 58,65 gives R332,35, which is incorrect. Why? Because the 15% was never charged on R391. It was charged on the smaller, pre-VAT price, so taking 15% of the bigger inclusive number strips out too much.

The correct logic: the inclusive price is 115% of the exclusive price. So divide by 1,15 to find the 100%: 391 ÷ 1,15 = R340,00. That is the price before VAT. The VAT inside the price is the difference: 391 − 340 = R51,00. Check it forwards: 340 × 1,15 = 391. The check takes five seconds and certifies the answer.

There is also a direct fraction for the VAT portion of any inclusive price: VAT equals the inclusive price times 15 over 115. On the tracksuit: 391 × 15 ÷ 115 = R51,00, the same answer, because 15 parts of every 115 parts of an inclusive price are tax.

The habit that separates the two directions: before calculating, write down what the given price represents — 100% if exclusive, 115% if inclusive. Multiplying by 1,15 and dividing by 1,15 are one machine running forwards and backwards, and the label tells you which way to run it. The questions for this section follow now; label the price first, divide by 1,15 when the VAT is already inside, and prove your answer by multiplying back.

## Subtopic: Zero-Rated Items and Reading the Till Slip

The law protects the poorest households by charging VAT at 0% on a basket of basic foods. These are ZERO-RATED items: brown bread, maize meal, samp, rice, dried beans, lentils, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, tinned pilchards, cooking oil and a handful of others. Zero-rated is not the same as EXEMPT: exempt supplies, like residential rent and school fees, fall outside the VAT system entirely, but for the consumer at the till the effect is similar — no VAT in the price.

Till slips mark the difference. Items that carry VAT are usually flagged with an asterisk or a letter, and the slip's foot summarises: total, VAT included, zero-rated total.

Read one Saturday slip. The zero-rated goods — rice, samp, milk, eggs, a bag of bananas — total R214,60, containing no VAT at all. The standard-rated goods — dishwashing liquid, toothpaste, white bread, frozen chips... careful, frozen chips are not on the zero-rated list even though fresh potatoes are — total R356,50 including VAT. The VAT hiding in the slip: 356,50 × 15 ÷ 115 = R46,50. The full trolley: 214,60 + 356,50 = R571,10, of which only R46,50 is tax, precisely because more than a third of the trolley was zero-rated basics.

Notice the exam trap in reverse: the VAT is NOT 15% of the whole R571,10, because part of the slip carries no VAT. Always separate the standard-rated subtotal before extracting VAT. White bread carries VAT while brown bread does not — question setters adore that pair. The questions for this section are with you now; sort the basket into zero-rated and standard-rated before any percentage moves.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same Saturday with the till slip flat on the kitchen table and a pen in your hand.

## Subtopic: The Fifteen Percent That Follows You Around

Nearly every time money leaves your pocket, fifteen cents out of every rand of the before-tax price walks off to the government. Airtime: VAT inside. Sneakers: VAT inside. The electrician's bill: VAT on top. It is the quietest tax in the country because nobody ever asks you for it — it is baked into the price like salt into bread.

The whole topic runs on one picture. The price before VAT is the plain loaf: call it 100%. The government adds a 15% slice on top. The price you actually pay is the loaf plus the slice: 115%. Every calculation is just moving between the plain loaf and the loaf-with-slice.

Going forward is easy. The electrician says R9 600 before VAT. The slice is fifteen percent of that: R1 440. You will actually pay R11 040. One shortcut does it in a single move: 9 600 times 1,15. The 1 is the loaf, the 0,15 is the slice, together 1,15.

The thing to check before any calculation: does this price already have the slice on it or not? Shelf prices and till slips: slice already inside, by law. Electricians' quotes, wholesale catalogues, business invoices: usually before the slice, and the small print says excl. VAT. Circle that word on the paper before you calculate anything, because the entire method depends on it. Some questions on this section are with you right now; first say loaf or loaf-with-slice, then calculate.

## Subtopic: The Backwards Trap

Here is the question that catches thousands of matrics every single year. The tracksuit costs R391 with VAT inside. What did it cost before VAT? And almost everyone does the natural thing: take fifteen percent off. Fifteen percent of 391 is 58,65, subtract it, get R332,35. Feels right. Is wrong.

Here is why, in plain language. The 15% slice was cut from the SMALLER loaf — the before-VAT price — not from the R391. When you take 15% of 391, you are slicing the bigger number, so your slice is too thick and your answer lands too low.

The right move: R391 is the loaf plus the slice — 115% of the original. To find the original 100%, divide by 1,15. Three hundred and ninety-one divided by 1,15 is exactly R340. That was the price before VAT, and the VAT inside was 391 minus 340 — R51.

Now prove it to yourself, because this is the habit that makes the method stick: run it forwards. R340 plus fifteen percent of 340 — that is 340 times 1,15 — gives back R391 on the nose. If your backwards answer cannot run forwards to the price on the slip, it is wrong, no argument.

So burn in the rule: VAT going ON, multiply by 1,15. VAT coming OUT, divide by 1,15. Never subtract fifteen percent from an inclusive price — that move has never once been correct. Questions on this section are coming your way now; divide, then multiply back to check.

## Subtopic: Reading the Till Slip Like a Detective

Take tonight's till slip and actually read it. Some lines have a little star or letter next to them, some do not. That mark is the whole story: starred items carry VAT, unmarked ones are zero-rated — the government charges 0% on them on purpose, so that the food that keeps a family alive is not taxed.

The protected basket is worth knowing by heart: brown bread, maize meal, samp, rice, dried beans, lentils, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, tinned pilchards, cooking oil. Notice the sibling rivalry: brown bread free of VAT, white bread taxed. Fresh potatoes free, frozen chips taxed. The exam loves those pairs because they test whether you actually know the list or are just guessing.

Now the detective work on our Saturday slip. Zero-rated pile: rice, samp, milk, eggs, bananas — R214,60, and not one cent of that is tax. Starred pile: dishwashing liquid, toothpaste, white bread, frozen chips — R356,50 with VAT already inside. How much tax is hiding in the starred pile? Fifteen parts out of every 115: 356,50 times 15, divided by 115 — R46,50.

And the trap the slip sets for the careless: the whole trolley came to R571,10, but the VAT is NOT fifteen over one-fifteen of R571,10 — the zero-rated pile carries nothing. Sort first, then calculate on the starred pile only. That is the entire skill: sort, then slice. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; split the slip into its two piles before a single percentage moves, and say in a sentence how much of the trolley was tax.
