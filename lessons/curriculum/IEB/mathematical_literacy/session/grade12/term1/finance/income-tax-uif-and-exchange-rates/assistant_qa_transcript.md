**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! The evergreen one first: my aunt turned down extra shifts because she said the higher tax bracket would make her take home less. The class is split — can extra income ever actually shrink your net pay?

**Tutor:** Under the personal income tax table, no — and the ladder picture shows why. Suppose the extra shifts lift a salary from R310 000 to R320 000 a year, both inside the 26% band: only the extra R10 000 is taxed at 26%, so R2 600 goes to SARS and R7 400 stays in the pocket. Now suppose the increase crosses a bracket line, say from R230 000 into the second band. The money below R237 100 continues to be taxed at 18%, exactly as before — crossing the line never re-taxes a single rand of the lower rungs. Only the few rands above the line pay the new 26 cents each, which still leaves 74 cents of every new rand in the earner's pocket. The worst a bracket can ever do is tax the newest slice at the highest marginal rate, currently topping out at 45% — and even then, 55 cents of each additional rand comes home. The confusion arises because people imagine the bracket rate applies to the whole salary, the same error as charging 26% on all of R324 000 and getting R84 240 instead of R65 272. So the arithmetic verdict is clean: a bigger gross always means a bigger net under this table, and the aunt should take the shifts.

**Mandy:** Next: in the tax table, where does the R42 678 at the start of the second band actually come from? Some of us just accept it, and accepting numbers feels dangerous.

**Tutor:** Never accept a number you can audit — and this one audits beautifully. The first band of the 2023/24 table runs from zero to R237 100, taxed at 18%. If your income fills that whole band, the tax on it is 18% of 237 100: multiply and you get R42 678 exactly. The table's authors have simply pre-calculated the full first rung and printed it, so that anyone in the second band can start from R42 678 and only work out the 26% on their own slice above R237 100. Every band works the same way: the flat amount at the start of each band is the accumulated tax on all the full bands below it. That is also your error alarm in the exam. If you find yourself multiplying your entire taxable income by your band's percentage, stop — the printed flat amount already contains the lower bands at their lower rates, and your job is only the slice. The working for R324 000 therefore has exactly three lines: the excess, 324 000 minus 237 100 giving 86 900; the slice tax, 86 900 times 0,26 giving 22 594; and the total, 42 678 plus 22 594 giving 65 272, before the rebate of 17 235 brings it to R48 037. Three lines, each auditable, no faith required.

**Mandy:** This one caused an argument: two colleagues earn R22 000 and R45 000, and their payslips show exactly the same UIF amount. One of them is convinced the payroll office made a mistake. Did they?

**Tutor:** No mistake — they have both hit the ceiling, and the ceiling is the whole point of the design. UIF contributions are one percent of salary, but only salary up to the maximum of R17 712 per month is counted. The colleague on R22 000 exceeds that maximum, so the fund counts only R17 712 of it: one percent gives R177,12. The colleague on R45 000 also exceeds it, and the fund counts the same R17 712: also R177,12. Identical deductions, by law, because contributions and benefits are both capped — the fund insures ordinary income, not executive income, so it neither collects premiums on the amount above the ceiling nor pays benefits based on it. Where the one percent runs uncapped is below the ceiling: a worker on R8 000 pays R80, a worker on R14 000 pays R140, each exactly one percent. Two more payslip facts settle the usual follow-up arguments. The employer's matching one percent is real money paid to the fund, but it is paid on top of the salary, so it never appears among the employee's deductions. And UIF is not a savings account you can claim back at will — it is insurance, payable when employment income stops for a listed reason: retrenchment, maternity, adoption leave, or prolonged illness.

**Mandy:** Last one: with exchange rates, someone multiplied when they should have divided and turned R6 500 into 132 600 euros. Beyond memorising rules, how do we make the right operation obvious?

**Tutor:** Stop memorising multiply-or-divide and start reading the rate as a price tag — the grammar you already use at the shop. R20,40 per euro says one euro costs R20,40, exactly as apples at R20,40 per kilogram says one kilogram costs R20,40. Nobody at a fruit stall wonders which operation to use: buying 380 kilograms costs 380 times 20,40; asking how many kilograms R6 500 buys means dividing 6 500 by 20,40. Currencies obey the same sentence. Converting 380 euros into rands is pricing 380 items at R20,40 each: multiply, R7 752. Converting R6 500 into euros is asking how many R20,40 price tags fit inside R6 500: divide, 318,63 euros. Then apply the size check, which would have caught that 132 600 instantly: the euro is the expensive unit, so the euro figure must always be the smaller number and the rand figure the bigger one. R6 500 becoming 132 600 euros claims a bureau de change made you twenty times richer — impossible, so the operation ran backwards. Finish every exchange question with the buying-power sentence, because that is where the interpretation mark hides: when the price tag on a euro rises, the rand has weakened and imports cost more; when it falls, the rand has strengthened and your money reaches further abroad.
