# Part 1 — Expert

Every Saturday night, millions of South Africans watch six numbered balls decide whether their R5 ticket becomes a fortune. Every day, insurers price policies, weather services forecast rain, and shop owners judge whether the bread delivery will arrive before the morning rush. All of it is PROBABILITY — the mathematics of uncertainty — and matric examines it from the spinner in a board game to the fine print of a lottery. This session builds the tools: the probability scale, the two ways of measuring chance, the honest arithmetic of gambling, and the diagrams that tame events happening one after another.

## Subtopic: The Language of Chance

An EXPERIMENT is any action with an uncertain result: spinning a spinner, tossing a coin, drawing a card. An OUTCOME is one possible result; the SAMPLE SPACE is the list of all possible outcomes — for a spinner with eight equal sectors, the eight numbers 1 to 8. An EVENT is the outcome or set of outcomes you care about: landing on an even number is the event containing 2, 4, 6 and 8.

PROBABILITY measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 to 1. Zero means impossible: spinning a 9 on that spinner. One means certain: spinning a number between 1 and 8. Everything uncertain lives in between, and the same probability wears three outfits — fraction, decimal, percentage. A fair coin shows heads with probability one half, 0,5, or 50%.

When all outcomes are equally likely, probability is a counting exercise: the number of outcomes in the event, divided by the total number of outcomes. Landing on the 8: one favourable outcome out of eight, so one eighth, which is 0,13 rounded to two decimal places. Landing on an even number: four out of eight, one half. Drawing a heart from a full pack: 13 out of 52, one quarter.

Two guard rails. First, the phrase equally likely is doing real work — a weighted spinner or a bent coin breaks the counting method. Second, probabilities of all outcomes in a sample space add to 1, so the probability that an event does NOT happen is 1 minus the probability that it does: the chance of not landing on the 8 is 1 minus one eighth, seven eighths. The questions for this section are with you now; write the sample space before any fraction, and place every answer on the 0-to-1 scale.

## Subtopic: Theoretical Probability and Relative Frequency

There are two honest ways to attach a number to chance, and exams test the difference sharply.

THEORETICAL PROBABILITY comes from reasoning about the experiment before it happens: a fair coin has two equally likely faces, so heads has probability 0,5. No coin need ever be tossed.

RELATIVE FREQUENCY comes from actually doing the experiment: toss the coin 200 times, count 108 heads, and the relative frequency of heads is 108 out of 200 — 0,54. It is calculated as the number of times the event occurred, divided by the number of trials.

Why do the two disagree — 0,54 against 0,5? Because chance is lumpy in the short run. Toss the coin only ten times and seven heads is unremarkable. But as the number of trials grows, relative frequency drifts toward the theoretical value: after ten thousand tosses of a fair coin, the proportion of heads will sit very close to 0,5. That long-run settling is why casinos and insurers, who play thousands of times, can rely on probabilities that any single gambler cannot.

Relative frequency also earns its keep where theory cannot reach. What is the probability that the bread delivery to a spaza shop arrives late? No symmetry argument exists — you must observe: out of 60 recorded deliveries, 21 were late, giving 21 over 60, which is 0,35. Insurers price young drivers the same way: claim records, not coin logic.

And the FAIRNESS test: a game is fair when each player has an equal probability of winning. If observed frequencies drift far from the theoretical claim over many trials — a spinner landing on 8 in 25 of 50 spins when theory says one eighth — suspect the spinner, not the mathematics. The questions for this section are ready; label each probability as theoretical or observed, and quote the number of trials whenever frequency speaks.

## Subtopic: The Lottery and Other Long Shots

The national lottery asks players to match six numbers drawn from 52. The probability of a single board matching all six is one in 20 358 520 — roughly one in twenty million. Numbers that size defeat intuition, so translate: a player buying one board every week can expect a jackpot about once in nearly four hundred thousand years. Meanwhile the probability of SOME ticket winning somewhere is decent, because millions of boards are sold — which is precisely how the advertising works: the winner is real, visible and photographed; the twenty million losers are not.

Exams require the vocabulary of gambling read honestly. Every game of chance has a price, a prize and a probability, and the three combine into an expected long-run result that always favours the operator — lotteries, casinos and betting shops are businesses, and the mathematics of their profit is printed in their own rules. A R5 ticket with a one-in-twenty-million chance at a typical jackpot returns, on average, a fraction of its price; the rest funds the operator and its causes. Playing for entertainment is a choice; playing as a financial plan is arithmetic error.

INSURANCE is the same mathematics with the roles reversed. The insurer collects a small premium from many clients, and pays large amounts to the few who suffer the insured event. Premiums are set by RISK ASSESSMENT — relative frequencies harvested from data: young drivers claim more often, so their premiums are higher; a car parked behind a locked gate claims less often, so its premium drops. Where the gambler pays small for a tiny chance of gaining big, the insured pays small to cancel a small chance of LOSING big. Same probabilities, opposite purpose.

The examinable core: state the probability, interpret its size in words, and follow the money honestly. The questions for this section follow now; treat every game as price, prize and probability, and say who profits in the long run.

## Subtopic: Compound Events: Tree Diagrams and Two-Way Tables

A COMPOUND EVENT combines two or more simple events: load-shedding AND a late delivery; two coin tosses in a row. Two tools organise the counting.

The TREE DIAGRAM handles events in sequence. In Zinhle's township, the probability of morning load-shedding is 0,4. When the power is off, the probability that her shop's bread delivery runs late is 0,5; on mornings with power it is 0,1. Draw two branches for power off, 0,4, and power on, 0,6 — they must sum to 1. From each, branch again for late and on time. The rule: MULTIPLY along branches, ADD between paths. Probability of power off and late: 0,4 × 0,5 = 0,20. Probability of power on and late: 0,6 × 0,1 = 0,06. Probability the bread is late at all — either path: 0,20 + 0,06 = 0,26, just over a one-in-four chance. Check the machine: the four end-of-path probabilities, 0,20, 0,20, 0,06 and 0,54, sum to exactly 1.

The TWO-WAY TABLE handles two characteristics recorded together. A school surveys 120 learners: 45 walk to school, of whom 27 arrive on time and 18 arrive late; 75 use transport, of whom 69 arrive on time and 6 arrive late. The table's rows are how learners travel, its columns on time or late, and the totals row confirms 96 on time, 24 late, 120 learners.

Read probabilities straight off the counts. Probability a randomly chosen learner arrived late: 24 over 120, 0,2. Probability a randomly chosen WALKER arrived late: now the pool shrinks to the 45 walkers, and 18 over 45 gives 0,4. That shift of pool — from all learners to walkers only — is the heart of two-way table questions: the words given that, or of the walkers, change the denominator, and the marks are decided exactly there.

The questions for this section are with you now; multiply along, add across, and read every denominator out of the question's own words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chances at street level: a board game on the stoep, a lotto kiosk, and a dark Monday morning at the spaza shop.

## Subtopic: A Scale from Impossible to Certain

Chance is a ruler, and every event in the world sits somewhere on it. At the left end, 0: impossible — your eight-sector spinner shows a nine. At the right end, 1: certain — the sun sets this evening. Halfway, 0,5: the fair coin, could go either way. Everything uncertain lives strictly between the ends.

To place an event on the ruler when every outcome has an equal shot, count two things — that is the entire method. Count the ways your event can happen; count all the ways anything can happen; divide. The spinner has eight sectors: landing on the 8 is 1 way out of 8, about 0,13 — close to the impossible end. Landing on an even number is 4 ways out of 8 — dead centre, 0,5. Landing below nine is 8 out of 8 — certain, probability 1.

Now the trick that solves half the exam's probability one-liners: the NOT trick. The whole ruler holds exactly 1 of probability, so the chance an event does NOT happen is 1 minus the chance it does. Chance of missing the 8: 1 minus one eighth — seven eighths. If a weather service says 0,4 for rain, dry sits at 0,6, no further thought required.

One warning before the questions: equal shots is a real condition, not decoration. A cooldrink-cap competition where one cap in ten thousand wins is not a coin; counting sectors only works when the sectors are fair. Some questions on this section are with you right now; place each event on the ruler, and let the NOT trick do the heavy lifting.

## Subtopic: Why the Lotto Is Not a Retirement Plan

Here is the number the adverts never say slowly: one in twenty million, three hundred and fifty-eight thousand, five hundred and twenty. That is the chance that one lotto board matches all six balls. Try to feel its size: hand a ticket to every single person living in Gauteng, and then keep going through every person in Limpopo too — more than twenty million people, every man, woman and baby holding one ticket — and ONE of them wins. You are one face in those two whole provinces.

But someone wins almost every few weeks — is that not proof it works? It is proof that MILLIONS of boards are sold. When twenty million tickets chase a one-in-twenty-million chance, a winner somewhere is unsurprising; a winner being YOU stays as close to impossible as everyday life gets. The camera interviews the one; it cannot interview the twenty million.

Follow the R5. The game keeps roughly half of all money staked for prizes; the rest runs the operator and its funded causes. So the average ticket buys back less than half its price in prize money, meaning every rand staked is, on average, a donation with a lottery attached. Fine as entertainment, honestly priced as a dream — fatal as a financial plan, because the long run is arithmetic, not luck.

And notice insurance is the same machine turned to protect you. With insurance you pay a small premium so that a rare disaster — the taxi crash, the shack fire — cannot destroy you; the many premiums of the lucky pay the claims of the unlucky. Gambler and insured hold the same tiny probability, but one buys risk and the other buys rescue. Questions on this section are coming your way now; check every game by its price, its prize, and its honest chance.

## Subtopic: Two Steps of Chance

Some chances come in sequences: first the power decides, then the delivery van decides. A tree diagram is nothing more than drawing the story before living it.

Monday morning at Zinhle's spaza shop. First fork: power off, 0,4, or power on, 0,6. Notice the two branches share the whole ruler — they add to 1. Now each branch forks again. On the power-off branch: bread late, 0,5, or on time, 0,5. On the power-on branch: late drops to 0,1, on time 0,9.

Two rules run every tree ever drawn. Rule one: multiplying along a path answers AND. Power off and late: 0,4 times 0,5 — 0,20. Why multiply? Because the second chance happens inside the first: late-during-load-shedding only matters on the 4 mornings in 10 that the power is off at all — 5 tenths of those 4 tenths is 20 hundredths. Rule two: adding across paths answers OR. Late can arrive by two roads — off-and-late, 0,20, or on-and-late, 0,06 — and since the roads cannot both happen on one morning, add: 0,26. Zinhle should keep yesterday's stock ready about one Monday in four.

The self-check: all the endpoints of the tree — 0,20, 0,20, 0,06, 0,54 — must add to exactly 1, because the tree contains every possible morning.

Two-way tables are the same idea for counted things, and the only trap is the shrinking pool. Of 120 surveyed learners, 24 arrived late: 0,2. But of the 45 walkers, 18 arrived late: 18 over 45, 0,4. The words of the walkers shrank the pool from 120 to 45 — always ask, out of WHOM, before writing the fraction. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; multiply along the path, add between paths, and check the endpoints swallow the whole 1.
