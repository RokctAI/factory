# Part 1 — Expert

This session walks a full practice paper built for today — one hundred and fifty marks of Finance and Data Handling, written question by question the way a marker reads it. The paper is our own construction, but every skill inside it is the year's work, and the marking plan we wrote for it pays the way markers in this subject generally pay: method first, answer second, stage by stage. Every question carries its mark allocation, and the allocation is the instruction — two marks means a calculation and an answer; four marks means show the chain; six marks means the chain has stages and each stage pays. Learn to read allocations here, and the reading transfers to whatever layout your final exams choose.

## Subtopic: How the Finance Practice Paper Is Built and How Marks Are Won

Our practice paper carries 150 marks in five questions. Question One is worth 30 marks and holds only short, direct, single-step items from Finance and Data Handling on a short context — a deliberate warm-up floor. Question Two is Finance; Question Three is Data Handling; Questions Four and Five mix the two families, and that is where we placed the less familiar contexts. Probability contributes a small handful of marks and hides inside any question it likes. We also spread the difficulty deliberately: roughly a third of the paper is quick recall, a third is routine procedure, and the rest climbs through multi-step work into reasoning — because a balanced practice run must rehearse every register, not just the comfortable one.

Reading the allocation is a skill in itself. A two-mark question pays one mark for method and one for the answer. A three-mark question hides a middle step — usually a conversion or a subtotal. Anything from five marks up is a staged journey, and in our marking plan part-marks flow for every correct stage EVEN IF an earlier slip changed your numbers — the follow-through principle that marking in this subject runs on. The strategy that follows: never leave a staged question blank, always show substitution, and carry wrong numbers forward boldly rather than stopping.

## Subtopic: Question One Walk-through: Warm-up Marks

Question One is the confidence deposit — 30 marks of single-step credit. Four specimens at the pace the paper expects.

First, for two marks: a stationery pack costs R180,00 excluding VAT; calculate the VAT at 15 percent. One line — 180 times 0,15 is R27,00 — and if the question asks the total, R207,00. Second, for two marks: a payslip shows gross salary R14 650,00 and total deductions R2 980,00; calculate the net pay. Subtract: R11 670,00. The knowing mark is knowing WHICH way the arithmetic points — net is what remains.

Third, from the data side, for two marks: the mode of the marks 9, 13, 13, 17, 20 — the value appearing most, 13. And a probability warm-up for two: a spinner has five equal sectors and exactly one is green; the probability of landing on green, written as a fraction — 1 over 5. No simplification drama, no decimals demanded; a warm-up pays for the definition applied cleanly.

The discipline in Question One is speed WITHOUT skipped writing: even two-mark answers show the one line of working, because an arithmetic slip with no working forfeits both marks, while the same slip beside visible method usually keeps one.

## Subtopic: Question 2.1: The Electricity Block Tariff

Question Two opens Finance with a tariff table, and this specimen is worth six marks. The prepaid electricity tariff: the first 100 kilowatt hours each month cost R1,80 per unit; from 101 to 400 units, R2,40 per unit; above 400, R3,10 per unit. The Nkosi household in Mbombela used 460 units. Calculate the total bill including VAT at 15 percent.

Walk the blocks — each stage is a mark. Block one: 100 units at R1,80 is R180,00. Block two: from 101 to 400 is 300 units at R2,40 — R720,00. Block three: 460 minus 400 leaves 60 units at R3,10 — R186,00. Subtotal: 180 plus 720 plus 186 is R1 086,00. VAT step: 1 086 times 1,15 is R1 248,90. Six marks: each block on its own line, the subtotal, the VAT, the final answer with its rand sign and two decimals.

The two mark-killers: charging all 460 units at R3,10 because 460 falls in the top block's range — that invents a bill of R1 426,00 — and forgetting that the cheap first block still absorbs 100 of the 460. Consumption is SPLIT across blocks — the tariff table is a staircase, not a switch.

## Subtopic: Question 2.2: Compound Interest on a Fixed Deposit

The second Finance movement, for four marks: Mr Cele invests R9 500,00 in a fixed deposit at 7,2 percent per year, compounded annually, for three years. Calculate the value at the end of the term.

The safest full-marks route is year by year, because it shows the compounding instead of asserting it. End of year one: 9 500 times 1,072 is R10 184,00. End of year two: 10 184 times 1,072 is R10 917,25 — and keep the full figure on the calculator. End of year three: the full balance times 1,072 gives R11 703,29 — rounded to the cent, stated with its rand sign. Four marks: the multiplier 1,072 built correctly from 7,2 percent, each year's interest applied to the NEW balance, and the final rounding.

The follow-up the paper loves, for two more marks: how much interest did he earn? Final value minus original deposit — 11 703,29 minus 9 500 is R2 203,29. And the reasoning twin, for three: his sister earned simple interest at the same rate — explain who ends with more and why. Simple interest pays 9 500 times 0,072, which is R684,00, every year — R2 052,00 over three years — so compound wins by R151,29, because from year two onward its interest is calculated on a balance that already contains interest. The explanation mark needs that final clause, not just the numbers.

## Subtopic: Question 3: Summarising the Test Marks

Question Three turns to Data Handling. The context: ten learners' test percentages, already arranged — 41, 44, 49, 53, 57, 61, 64, 68, 74, 79.

For two marks, the range: 79 minus 41 is 38. For three marks, the mean: the sum is 590, divided by 10 gives exactly 59,0 — one mark for the sum, one for dividing by the correct count, one for the answer. For three marks, the median: ten is even, so the median averages the fifth and sixth values — 57 and 61 — giving 59,0. The even-count rule IS the question; picking a single middle value forfeits it.

For four marks, the quartiles and interquartile range: the lower half's middle is the third value, so Q1 is 49; the upper half mirrors with its own middle, the eighth value, so Q3 is 68; the interquartile range is 68 minus 49, which is 19 — the spread of the middle half of the class, untouched by the 41 and the 79 at the ends.

Then the reasoning marks, for three: the coach reports the group did well because the mean is 59. Comment, with calculations. The honest answer notes mean 59,0 and median 59,0 sit together, so no outlier is distorting the picture — but a quarter of the group scored below 49, which the single number hides. Marks flow for citing computed values in the argument, not for opinion.

## Subtopic: Question 4: Integrated Finance, Data and Probability

The paper's back end mixes the families and climbs in difficulty. The integrated specimen: the same ten learners sell cans of cold drink to fund their year-end dinner, and the question stitches data to money to chance.

Part one, for two marks: a learner is chosen at random to count the cash box; what is the probability the chosen learner scored below 50 percent on the test? Count from the data — 41, 44 and 49 sit below 50, so 3 out of 10: 3 over 10, or 0,3, or 30 percent — any dialect earns it unless one is demanded.

Part two, for six marks, the budget: the group buys 150 cans at a wholesale price of R7,20 and sells them at R12,00. Costs: 150 times 7,20 is R1 080,00. Income if all sell: 150 times 12 is R1 800,00. Profit: R720,00. Then the twist: only 80 percent of cans sell and the rest are donated to the staff room. Income becomes 120 cans times 12, which is R1 440,00, and profit falls to R360,00. Each recalculated line pays.

Part three, for four marks, the reasoning close: the group needs R950,00 profit to cover the sound-system deposit — advise them, with calculations, whether the 80 percent scenario meets the target, and what selling price would. R360,00 falls R590,00 short; keeping 120 sales, the price must satisfy 120 times price minus 1 080 equals 950, so the price is 2 030 over 120 — 16,9166, rounded UP to R16,92, because rounding down to R16,91 would bank only R2 029,20 and miss the target by eighty cents. Advice questions pay for the verdict PLUS the supporting number, never the verdict alone.

## Subtopic: Memo Habits That Harvest Marks

The closing discipline — the habits that decide borderline scripts, all free of new content.

Habit one: SHOW SUBSTITUTION. A marking plan awards the method mark the moment correct values sit inside a correct structure — 1 086 times 1,15 written down banks a mark even if the multiplication then slips. Habit two: FOLLOW-THROUGH is real — a wrong subtotal carried correctly forward keeps paying, so never abandon a staged question at a suspected slip; finish the chain. Habit three: UNITS AND FORM — rand answers take two decimals and the rand sign; the decimal comma is the convention of this subject; percentages declare their percent sign; a probability may live as fraction, decimal or percentage unless the question chooses. Habit four: ROUNDING serves the context — money to the cent, people and cans UP to whole units when buying and to the honest whole when counting, and premature rounding mid-chain is the quiet killer: keep full figures until the final line. Habit five: ANSWER THE VERB — calculate means numbers, show that means arrive at the given value visibly, comment and advise mean a sentence CONTAINING a number. A script that obeys the five habits typically finds ten marks the same knowledge would otherwise leak — the cheapest ten marks any paper offers.
