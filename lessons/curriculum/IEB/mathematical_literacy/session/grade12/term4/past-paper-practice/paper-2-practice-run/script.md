# Part 1 — Expert

This session walks a second full practice paper built for today — Measurement with Maps and Plans, one hundred and fifty marks — question by question at examination pace, mark allocations named at every turn. This is the practical paper of the pair: it puts tariff-free, real-world objects in front of you — a road, a hall, a drum, a plan — and pays for measured, converted, correctly-united arithmetic. The paper is our own construction, its marking plan is our own, and the walk-through below treats both exactly as a marker would.

## Subtopic: How the Measurement Practice Paper Is Built and How Marks Are Won

Our practice paper carries 150 marks in five questions. Measurement holds the largest share of the paper, Maps and Plans most of the rest, Probability a small handful — and small Finance calculations appear in Questions Four and Five where they connect directly to building, buying or travelling. Question One is worth 30 marks of short knowing-type items from Measurement and Maps — the warm-up floor. Question Two is Maps and Plans; Question Three is Measurement; Questions Four and Five mix them, and the less familiar contexts wait there. The difficulty spread matches the Finance practice paper: a third quick recall, a third routine, the rest climbing into reasoning.

The paper's special demand is UNITS. Nearly every chain crosses at least one conversion — millimetres to metres, cubic metres to litres, decimal hours to minutes — and the marking plan places a mark directly on the crossing. Read each allocation as a recipe: two marks, one operation plus answer; four marks, a conversion lives inside; six or more, a staged build where every stage pays, and a wrong stage carried forward keeps paying under follow-through.

## Subtopic: Question One Walk-through: Warm-up Marks

Thirty marks of single-step credit, taken at pace but never unwritten. Specimens.

For two marks: convert 4 800 metres to kilometres — divide by 1 000, which is 4,8 kilometres. For two marks: a drum holds 0,25 cubic metres of water; how many litres is that? One cubic metre is 1 000 litres, so 250 litres. For two marks: the scale on a furniture plan reads 1 to 50 — explain its meaning. One unit on the plan represents 50 of the same units on the real object; the explanation mark wants SAME UNITS said aloud.

From the maps side, for two marks: name the national route joining Cape Town and Beaufort West shown on the given map — the N1, read straight off the labelling. For two marks: on the plan, thin double lines interrupting a wall — a window, drawn without a swing arc. And a warm-up measurement read, for two: the dimension line on the plan marks the bedroom wall as 2 700 millimetres; write it in metres — 2,7.

Question One's discipline never changes: one visible line each, correct units on every answer, no chain left implied.

## Subtopic: Question 2: The Road Trip on the N2

Question Two belongs to Maps and Plans, and this specimen — worth eleven marks across its parts — plans a Gqeberha to East London trip of 310 kilometres on the N2.

Part one, for three marks: at an average speed of 100 kilometres per hour, calculate the travelling time. Time is distance over speed: 310 over 100 is 3,1 hours. Then the conversion mark: 0,1 hours times 60 is 6, so the time is 3 hours 6 minutes. Writing 3 hours 10 minutes — reading the decimal as minutes — is the single most marked-against error in this question family.

Part two, for four marks, the fuel: the car uses 6,9 litres per 100 kilometres. Litres needed: 310 over 100 times 6,9, which is 21,39 litres. At R22,80 per litre, cost is 21,39 times 22,80 — R487,69 to the cent. Two conversions of thinking, each carrying a mark: kilometres into hundred-kilometre blocks, litres into rands — the Finance link this paper explicitly allows.

Part three, for four marks, the map work: the final approach is measured on a 1 to 200 000 strip map as 4 centimetres. Real distance: 4 times 200 000 is 800 000 centimetres — 8 kilometres after the unit ladder. Then the reasoning close: the family wants to arrive before 13:00 and plans a 50 minute lunch stop — working backwards from 13:00 through the stop and the 3 hours 6 minutes of driving, departure must be no later than 09:04. Reverse timelines pay a mark per correct subtraction.

## Subtopic: Question 3.1: Painting the Youth Centre

Question Three is Measurement's centrepiece, and painting is its classic staged build — this specimen worth eight marks.

A youth centre activity room is 10 metres long, 7 metres wide, 3 metres high. All four walls get two coats. Subtract two doors of 0,9 metres by 2,0 metres and six windows of 1,5 metres by 1,0 metre. Paint covers 8 square metres per litre and is sold only in 10 litre tins. How many tins, and is the given budget of R2 500,00 enough?

Stage one: wall area. Perimeter is 2 times the sum of 10 and 7, which is 34 metres; times the height 3 gives 102 square metres. Stage two: openings. The doors are 2 times 0,9 times 2,0 — 3,6 square metres; the windows are 6 times 1,5 times 1,0 — 9 square metres. Paintable area: 102 minus 3,6 minus 9 is 89,4 square metres. Stage three: coats. Two coats double the work: 178,8 square metres. Stage four: litres. 178,8 over 8 is 22,35 litres. Stage five: tins. 22,35 over 10 is 2,235 — and tins are bought whole, so THREE tins, rounded UP, never to the nearest. Stage six, the Finance link: at R899,00 per tin, the paint costs 3 times 899 — R2 697,00 — and the verdict compares that against the R2 500,00 budget in a sentence containing both numbers: the budget falls R197,00 short.

Every stage is a mark or two, and the round-up at tins is the one the marking plan watches: 2 tins leaves wall unpainted; 2,235 tins cannot be bought.

## Subtopic: Question 3.2: The Oil Drum and the Flour Boxes

Measurement's second movement pairs volume with packing — nine marks across the parts.

The drum, for five marks: a bakery in Kroonstad stores cooking oil in a cylindrical drum with radius 0,4 metres and height 0,9 metres; using 3,142 for pi, calculate its capacity in litres. Substitution first — volume is 3,142 times 0,4 squared times 0,9. In stages: 0,4 squared is 0,16; times 3,142 is 0,502 72 to five decimals; times 0,9 gives 0,452 cubic metres rounded to three. The conversion mark: times 1 000 is about 452 litres. The radius-diameter trap guards the door: a question giving the DIAMETER as 0,8 metres expects you to halve it first, and the marking plan's first mark sits exactly there.

The boxes, for four marks: flour boxes measuring 25 centimetres by 20 centimetres by 10 centimetres are packed into a storage crate of 100 by 60 by 50 centimetres. How many fit? Axis by axis: 100 over 25 is 4 along the length; 60 over 20 is 3 across; 50 over 10 is 5 layers high. Total: 4 times 3 times 5 — 60 boxes. The volume check — crate 300 000 cubic centimetres over box 5 000 — agrees at 60, worth stating as confirmation, but the counting method is the answer: only WHOLE boxes ship, and volume division alone is merely a ceiling.

## Subtopic: Question 4: The Plan, the Budget and the Carpenter

The integrated back end, twelve marks, where plans meet money.

A carpenter holds a 1 to 50 floor plan of a new reading room for a crèche in Mahikeng. Part one, for three marks: the longest wall measures 96 millimetres on the plan; calculate its real length. 96 times 50 is 4 800 millimetres — 4,8 metres after the ladder — and the sanity check earns its keep: rooms live between about 2 and 8 metres, so 4,8 rings true where 48 would ring alarm.

Part two, for three marks: the floor of the room is 4,8 metres by 3,5 metres; calculate the area to be covered with vinyl tiles — 16,8 square metres. Each box of tiles covers 2,2 square metres: 16,8 over 2,2 is 7,64, so EIGHT boxes, bought whole, rounded up.

Part three, for four marks, the elevation match: the plan places the door and two windows on the east wall, a solid wall on the west. Of the two elevation drawings supplied, the one showing three openings is the east elevation — the answer earns full marks only with the counting reason attached, anchored to compass directions, never to left and right, which flip when the viewer steps outside.

Part four, for two marks, the Finance cameo: tiles cost R215,00 per box; the flooring budget line is 8 times 215 — R1 720,00. This paper pays for exactly this kind of costing whenever it touches a plan.

## Subtopic: Probability Moments and Memo Habits

Probability's marks arrive inside other contexts. The crèche holds a raffle at the reading room's opening: 240 tickets sold, and the carpenter bought 6. For two marks: the probability the carpenter wins — 6 over 240, which is 1 over 40, or 0,025, or 2,5 percent, any dialect accepted unless one is demanded. For two more, the complement: probability he does NOT win is 39 over 40 — 0,975 — because the two outcomes share the whole. And the reasoning mark the paper loves: buying 6 tickets in THIS raffle genuinely multiplies his chance by six, but last month's loss changes nothing about this month's draw — each raffle starts fresh.

Then the closing habits, measurement edition. Habit one: UNITS ON EVERY LINE — square metres for area, cubic metres for volume, litres after the times-1 000 crossing; a correct number in a wrong unit is a lost answer mark. Habit two: conversions are mark-bearing steps — write them, never absorb them silently. Habit three: rounding obeys the context — tins, boxes and tiles round UP because shops sell whole things; measurements round as instructed, and never mid-chain. Habit four: substitution before evaluation — 3,142 times 0,4 squared times 0,9 on paper banks the method mark whatever the calculator then does. Habit five: verdict questions end in a sentence with a number inside it. A script running these habits typically recovers the same ten quiet marks as its Finance twin — and the practice run is how they become reflex.
