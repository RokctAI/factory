# Part 1 — Expert

Measurement, Maps and Plans, and Probability are the practical half of the subject — the skills that price a building job, plan a journey and weigh a forecast — and small Finance calculations ride along wherever they connect to building, buying or travelling. This revision block runs the four essentials in a deliberate order: conversions with perimeter, area and volume; scale and travel planning on maps; plans, elevations and models; and probability in the language a marking plan rewards. The numbers are chosen the way good questions choose them — realistic, South African, and mark-bearing at every line.

## Subtopic: Conversions, Perimeter, Area and Volume

Conversions are the toll gates of the practical topics — nearly every question passes through one. The metric ladders: 10 millimetres to a centimetre, 100 centimetres to a metre, 1 000 metres to a kilometre; 1 000 grams to a kilogram; 1 000 millilitres to a litre. So 3,2 kilograms of mealie meal is 3 200 grams, and a 500 millilitre bottle holds 0,5 litres. The heavyweight conversion, printed on the formula sheet but tested for understanding: one cubic metre is 1 000 litres.

Perimeter, area, volume — three different questions about the same object. A chicken run 7 metres by 4 metres: PERIMETER is the walk around the edge, 2 times the sum of 7 and 4, which is 22 metres of fencing. AREA is the surface covered, 7 times 4, which is 28 square metres of ground. The units police themselves: metres for distance around, square metres for surface, cubic metres for space inside.

Volume earns its marks on the cylinder. A rainwater drum with radius 0,6 metres and height 1,2 metres, using pi as 3,142: volume is 3,142 times 0,6 squared times 1,2. Work it in steps for the substitution marks — 0,6 squared is 0,36; times 3,142 is 1,131 12; times 1,2 gives 1,357 cubic metres, rounded to three decimals. Then the conversion finishes the job: 1,357 cubic metres times 1 000 is about 1 357 litres of water. Two classic errors guard this question: using the diameter where the radius belongs, and forgetting that the answer in cubic metres is useless to a household that thinks in litres.

Questions on this section reach you now: the ladder conversions, fencing against ground cover on the chicken run, and the drum walked from formula to litres.

## Subtopic: Scale, Maps and Travel Planning

A scale of 1 to 25 000 means one unit on the map is 25 000 of the same units in the world. Measure 9 centimetres between two picnic spots on such a hiking map: real distance is 9 times 25 000, which is 225 000 centimetres — divide by 100 for metres, 2 250, then by 1 000 for kilometres: 2,25 kilometres. Always run the unit chain to kilometres; an answer left in centimetres is a method mark stranded short of its answer mark.

The distance, speed and time triangle carries the travel questions: distance equals speed times time, so time is distance divided by speed. A 264 kilometre trip at an average of 80 kilometres per hour takes 264 over 80, which is 3,3 hours. The decimal trap sits right here: 3,3 hours is NOT 3 hours 30 minutes. The 0,3 is three tenths of an hour — 0,3 times 60 is 18 — so the trip takes 3 hours 18 minutes. Converting decimal hours through 60 is one of the most reliably examined single steps in the subject.

Map-reading itself is mark-bearing: national routes carry N labels — the N1, the N2 — and questions ask which route joins two cities, which towns line the way, and what the distance chart says. A distance chart is read like a multiplication table: run along the row of one town to the column of the other; where they cross sits the kilometres. Elevation charts for a cycle race add a vertical story: climbs where the line rises, and reasoning marks for statements like the steepest section is where the graph is steepest, not highest.

This section's questions arrive now: the scale chain to kilometres, decimal hours converted honestly, and the chart-reading habits.

## Subtopic: Plans, Elevations and Models

A floor plan is the roofless view from above, drawn to scale — 1 to 50 for a small building, where every millimetre of paper is 50 millimetres of building. A wall measuring 88 millimetres on such a plan is really 88 times 50, which is 4 400 millimetres — 4,4 metres. The sanity check never retires: rooms live between about 2 and 8 metres; an answer of 44 metres announces a dropped conversion.

The plan's symbol language is directly examinable: paired thick lines are walls, a gap with a quarter-circle arc is a door and its swing, thin double lines are windows, WC marks the toilet. What the floor plan cannot show is UP — heights belong to the ELEVATIONS, the views a neighbour gets of each outside wall, named for the compass direction the wall faces. The matching question — which drawing is the south elevation — is solved by counting: list the doors and windows the plan puts on the south face, then find the elevation showing that pattern. Anchor to compass directions, never to left and right, because left and right flip when you step outside the wall.

Models shrink buildings to desks. A community library of 8 metres by 5 metres modelled at 1 to 40 becomes 20 centimetres by 12,5 centimetres — divide each dimension by the scale factor. The reasoning trap: AREAS do not shrink by 40 but by 40 squared, so the model's roof is one one-thousand-six-hundredth of the real one. Packaging closes the family: count whole boxes along each axis of a carton and multiply — volume division alone is only a ceiling, because part of a box fits nothing.

Questions for this section reach you now: the plan measurement converted, the elevation matched by counting, and the model with its squared-area rule.

## Subtopic: Probability Essentials

Probability's marks are woven into other questions, and the subject speaks three dialects: fraction, decimal, percentage. A spinner with eight equal sectors and one red sector: the probability of landing on red is 1 over 8, which is 0,125, or 12,5 percent. A marking plan accepts any dialect unless the question names one — then it must be obeyed.

A coin tossed with a die rolled produces twelve equally likely outcomes — two faces times six numbers — so the probability of a head together with a six is 1 over 12. Listing ALL outcomes first is the method mark; the answer mark follows for free.

Contingency tables carry the data-flavoured marks. A school surveys 60 learners: 36 girls and 24 boys; 21 girls and 18 boys walk to school. Probability that a randomly chosen learner walks: 21 plus 18 is 39, over 60, which is 0,65. Probability of choosing a boy who walks: 18 over 60, which is 0,3. The denominator discipline decides everything — out of the WHOLE 60 unless the question narrows the group: the probability that a chosen BOY walks is 18 over 24, which is 0,75, but by a different road.

The complement closes the toolkit: if the weather service says 40 percent chance of rain, the chance of no rain is 60 percent — the two must sum to 1. And the reasoning classics: a 40 percent chance does not mean rain for 40 percent of the day, and lottery numbers played every week do not grow more likely — each draw starts fresh.

The final questions of this part arrive now: three dialects of one spinner, the coin-and-die outcomes listed, and the table read with denominator discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same skills carry one family event — a reunion at a farm shed outside Kimberley, with a shed to set up, an umkhulu to transport, and a sky to worry about.

## Subtopic: Setting Up the Shed

The shed floor measures 18 metres by 8 metres — an area of 18 times 8, which is 144 square metres. The plan is trestle tables, each needing a zone of floor 12 square metres so benches can slide and uncles can pass: divide the floor, 144 over 12, and 12 tables fit. Eight guests per table: 12 times 8 seats 96 people — and suddenly the invitation argument has a number to respect.

The bunting runs the edge of the shed: perimeter is 2 times the sum of 18 and 8, which is 52 metres — a different question from area, answered in plain metres, because edging and covering are different jobs.

Juice after the welcome: the cooler holds 25 litres and a cup takes 200 millilitres. Convert first — 25 litres is 25 000 millilitres — then divide: 25 000 over 200 gives 125 cups per cooler. With 96 guests, one cooler carries the day: 125 cups against 96 people leaves 29 spare. That last step is the exam's favourite shape: calculate, then DECIDE — is it enough, and by how much. The answer states the number and the verdict: 125 cups is more than 96 guests, so yes, with 29 to spare.

Questions on the shed setup reach you now: tables from floor area, bunting from perimeter, and the cooler verdict with its reason.

## Subtopic: Fetching Umkhulu in Time

Umkhulu lives 240 kilometres from the farm, and the lunch starts at 12:00. His daughter drives at an average of 80 kilometres per hour: time is distance over speed, 240 over 80, which is exactly 3 hours. But averages hide stops — a petrol stop and a stretch for his hip add three quarters of an hour of not-driving. Working backwards from an 11:30 arrival, comfortably before the 12:00 start: subtract the 3 hours of driving to 08:30, then the 45 minutes of stops to 07:45. Departure 07:45, stated as the answer with its chain of reasons — that reverse timeline is a full question in any travel context, marks at every subtraction.

The last stretch to the farm is on a district map with scale 1 to 100 000. The road from the tar turnoff measures 7 centimetres: real distance 7 times 100 000, which is 700 000 centimetres — 7 kilometres of gravel. At a careful 60 kilometres per hour, that is 7 over 60 hours — times 60 gives 7 minutes. Small number, same discipline: through the units, through the 60, never guessing.

And the petrol money is the Finance cameo the practical topics allow: the bakkie drinks 9 litres per 100 kilometres, so 240 kilometres uses 240 over 100 times 9, which is 21,6 litres; at R22,40 per litre the one-way trip costs 21,6 times 22,40 — R483,84. The reunion budget line just wrote itself.

Questions on the trip arrive now: the reverse timeline to 07:45, the gravel road off the map, and the petrol cost to the cent.

## Subtopic: Watching the Weekend Sky

The weather app says 40 percent chance of rain on the reunion day. Three honest readings of one number: as a fraction it is 40 over 100, which simplifies to 2 over 5; as a decimal, 0,4; and its COMPLEMENT — the chance of staying dry — is 60 percent, because rain and no-rain must share the whole 100.

What 40 percent does NOT mean: it will rain for 40 percent of the day, or over 40 percent of the district. It means that on days like this one, rain has historically arrived about 4 times in 10. So the family checks history themselves: of the last 24 years, this weekend saw rain 6 times. Experimental probability: 6 over 24, which is 0,25 — 25 percent, close to the forecast and calculated from lived data. The exam names both kinds: the app's figure is a modelled prediction; the family's figure is experimental, and more years of data would make it steadier.

The decision layer carries the reasoning marks: with a 40 percent rain chance, does the family hire the marquee for R1 200? There is no single right answer — the marks sit in the argument. Sixty percent says the money is wasted; 40 percent says 96 guests could stand soaked. A justified answer weighs the cost of the marquee against the cost of being wrong, and states the choice with its reason — exactly the shape of every probability reasoning question this subject asks.

The last questions of the lesson reach you now: the three dialects and the complement, the 24-year check against the forecast, and the marquee argued properly.
