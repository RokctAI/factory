# Part 1 — Expert

Finance and Data Handling are two of the hardest-working families in this subject — most of the money questions, and most of the number-summary questions, in any final assessment grow from them. Term four is not new work; it is the year's work put under exam pressure. This session runs the Finance and Data essentials in a deliberate order: tariffs, interest and inflation first, then VAT, income tax and exchange rates, then the data toolkit of averages and quartiles, and finally graphs — reading them, choosing them, and catching the ones designed to mislead. Every worked number here is the kind a marking plan rewards line by line.

## Subtopic: Tariff Systems, Interest and Inflation

Rising block tariffs first, because a tariff question opens almost every Finance section. The design principle: the more you consume, the more each unit costs. A municipal water tariff might charge R9,00 per kilolitre for the first 10 kilolitres and R16,50 for every kilolitre above that. A household using 18 kilolitres does NOT pay 18 times one rate. Split the consumption across the blocks: the first 10 kilolitres cost 10 times R9,00, which is R90,00; the remaining 8 kilolitres sit in block two at R16,50, giving R132,00. Subtotal R222,00. Then the almost-always-present final step: add VAT at 15 percent — R222,00 times 1,15 equals R255,30. The classic lost mark is charging ALL kilolitres at the higher rate; the block boundary protects the first 10.

Interest next, and the split that organises everything: SIMPLE interest is calculated on the original amount only, every year the same; COMPOUND interest is calculated on the growing balance, so interest earns interest. Take R8 000 invested at 5 percent for three years. Simple: 8 000 times 0,05 is R400 per year, times three years is R1 200, final value R9 200,00. Compound: 8 000 times 1,05 to the power three — year by year if the formula feels slippery — gives R9 261,00. The gap of R61,00 is interest earned on interest, and a reasoning question loves asking you to explain exactly that sentence.

Inflation is compound growth wearing shop prices. A pair of school shoes costing R450,00 rising at 6 percent per year for two years reaches 450 times 1,06 squared, which is R505,62. Not R504,00: adding 6 percent twice to the ORIGINAL is the simple-interest error in disguise. Each year's increase works on the new, higher price.

Questions on this section reach you now: the block split on 18 kilolitres, simple against compound on the same deposit, and the two-year inflation chain.

## Subtopic: VAT, Income Tax and Exchange Rates

VAT is 15 percent, and every paper tests both directions. Forward: a school backpack priced at R320,00 excluding VAT carries VAT of 320 times 0,15, which is R48,00, for a total of R368,00. Backward — the direction that costs marks — a till slip total of R368,00 INCLUDES the VAT, so the exclusive price is 368,00 DIVIDED by 1,15, which lands back on R320,00. Taking 15 percent of the inclusive price and subtracting is wrong, because the 15 percent was charged on the smaller, exclusive amount.

Income tax runs on a bracket table, and the method is pure substitution. A payslip first: gross salary is everything earned; deductions — tax, UIF at 1 percent, pension, medical aid — come off; net pay is what reaches the bank. The tax table works on ANNUAL taxable income. Say Nandi's taxable income is R285 000 per year, and the bracket printed on the question reads: R38 916 plus 26 percent of the amount above R226 000. The amount above is 285 000 minus 226 000, which is R59 000; 26 percent of that is R15 340,00; add the bracket's base R38 916 for R54 256,00. Then subtract the rebate the question supplies — R16 425, the discount the table grants every taxpayer in her age group — leaving annual tax of R37 831,00, which is R3 152,58 per month. Four operations, four marks, and the rebate step is the one most often forgotten.

Exchange rates close the set. At R19,20 to one euro, a 95 euro imported jacket costs 95 times 19,20, which is R1 824,00. Banks charge for the service: a 2,5 percent transaction fee lifts the cost to 1 824 times 1,025, which is R1 869,60. Direction check before any conversion: rands per euro means MULTIPLY when going from euros to rands, divide coming back.

This section's questions are with you now: VAT run both directions, the bracket-plus-rebate chain, and the fee-loaded conversion.

## Subtopic: Summarising Data: Mean, Median and Quartiles

The data toolkit compresses a list of numbers into a handful of honest summaries. Here is a marked set — nine learners' test percentages, already arranged: 36, 43, 48, 52, 57, 63, 69, 74, 82.

MEAN: add and divide. The sum is 524, divided by 9 gives 58,2 — the balancing point of the data. MEDIAN: the middle value of ARRANGED data. With nine values the middle position is position five — add one to the count and halve it — so the median is 57. RANGE: maximum minus minimum, 82 minus 36, which is 46 — one number describing the spread's full width.

Quartiles cut the arranged data into quarters. The lower quartile is the middle of the bottom half: positions one to four have no single middle, so average the second and third values — 43 and 48 — giving Q1 of 45,5. The upper quartile mirrors it: positions six to nine average the seventh and eighth values — 69 and 74 — so Q3 is 71,5. The INTERQUARTILE RANGE is Q3 minus Q1, which is 26 — the width of the middle half of the class, immune to the extremes at either end.

The reasoning layer, where the deepest marks live: which average represents the data best? The mean uses every value but is dragged by outliers; the median ignores how extreme the extremes are. If one learner had scored zero, the mean would drop sharply while the median barely moved — so for data with outliers, the median is the honest spokesperson, and full marks require saying WHY, not just naming it. Growth charts belong here too: a baby on the 60th percentile for height is taller than 60 percent of babies that age — a percentile is a position in a population, not a score.

Questions for this section arrive now: mean, median, both quartiles and the interquartile range on the nine marks, and one justified choice of average.

## Subtopic: Representing Data and Spotting Misleading Graphs

Choosing the graph is itself examinable. Categories compare on a BAR graph; shares of a whole belong on a PIE chart; change over time runs on a LINE graph; continuous grouped data fills a HISTOGRAM, bars touching; and the five-number summary — minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum — draws a BOX-AND-WHISKER plot. Two box plots on the same scale are the exam's favourite comparison tool: if Class A and Class B share a median of 63 but A's box is narrow and B's is wide, the classes are equally strong at the centre while A is more CONSISTENT — same middle, smaller interquartile range. Write both halves of that sentence; the comparison mark needs both.

Then the misuse questions, worth easy marks to a suspicious reader. Trap one: the axis that starts at 60 instead of zero, making a rise from 65 to 70 look like a doubling — the picture exaggerates because the bars show only their tips. Trap two: a three-dimensional pie chart tilted so the front slice looks fatter than its percentage. Trap three: the biased sample — a survey about school-bus reliability conducted only among learners dropped off by car cannot speak for bus riders, whatever its graph claims. The exam verb is CRITIQUE: name the flaw, explain what impression it creates, and state what an honest version would show. Three sentences, three marks, no calculation required.

The final questions of this part reach you now: matching data types to graphs, the two-box-plot comparison in full, and one misleading graph dismantled properly.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same machinery on one Sunday at a Pietermaritzburg farmers' market — a vetkoek stall's ledger, a malume's savings decision, and a homework book on the kitchen table.

## Subtopic: The Vetkoek Stall Ledger

Zanele runs a Sunday vetkoek stall, and her pricing IS the Finance section. The ingredients for each vetkoek cost her R4,00 and she sells each for R7,00. Profit per vetkoek: R3,00. But the exam word is MARK-UP, and mark-up is measured against what SHE paid: 3,00 divided by 4,00, times 100, gives 75 percent. Not 42,9 percent — the comparison base is the cost price, the R4,00, because mark-up answers the question how much did the price grow from what it cost her.

Then the supplier invoice arrives reading R180,00 excluding VAT for a bag of flour. The rule of the till: excluding means the tax is still coming. VAT is 15 percent of 180 — R27,00 — so R207,00 leaves her account. Going backwards works too, and this is the one that catches people: her till slip from the wholesaler shows a TOTAL of R207,00, VAT already inside. To find the price before tax, divide by 1,15 — back to R180,00 exactly. Never take 15 percent OF the total and subtract: the tax was charged on the smaller number, so only division unwinds it.

Why it matters beyond marks: a vendor who mistakes 75 percent for 42,9, or forgets which prices contain VAT, is quietly mispricing every vetkoek on the table.

Quick questions on the stall reach you now: the mark-up measured off the right base, and VAT walked in both directions.

## Subtopic: Stokvel Rands and Bank Interest

Zanele's malume Sipho pays R150,00 every month into the street's stokvel. Twelve months, twelve payments: R1 800,00 saved by December — safe, social, but the money does not GROW; a stokvel payout returns what the members put in.

The bank across the road offers growth. Malume has R6 000,00 from a work bonus and two choices for two years. Choice one, a simple-interest account at 5 percent: interest is 6 000 times 0,05, which is R300 every year — the same R300, because simple interest only ever looks at the original deposit. Two years, R600,00, final balance R6 600,00. Choice two, a fixed deposit at 5 percent COMPOUND: year one ends at 6 000 times 1,05, which is R6 300,00; year two charges interest on the NEW balance — 6 300 times 1,05 is R6 615,00. The extra R15,00 over simple interest is year two's interest earned on year one's interest. Small now; the gap widens every added year, which is the whole argument for starting early.

Inflation is the same engine running against him: if prices rise 6 percent a year, the R450,00 pair of school shoes costs 450 times 1,06 twice over — R505,62 — in two years. Money standing still in a tin loses the race that compound interest is trying to win.

Questions on malume's choices arrive now: the two-year balance under each option, and the one-sentence reason compound wins.

## Subtopic: Scores in the Homework Book

Zanele's brother Katlego has five spelling-test percentages in his homework book: 68, 64, 75, 71 — and one disaster, 22, from the week the power was out every evening.

His mean: the sum is 300, divided by 5 gives 60,0. His median: arrange first — 22, 64, 68, 71, 75 — and the middle of five values is the third, so 68. Now the family argument the exam loves to stage: his aunt says he is a 60 learner; the homework book median says 68. Who is honest? Remove the blackout mark and the mean of the remaining four is 69,5 — the 22 alone dragged the mean down nine and a half points, while the median never flinched, because the median only cares about position, not how BAD the bad mark was. For data carrying an outlier, the median represents the typical performance; the mean must be reported with its story attached. That is the full reasoning answer: name the average, name the outlier, say which way it pulls.

One graph habit closes the Sunday: a phone-shop poster shows data-bundle sales bars climbing steeply — but the money axis starts at R300, not zero, so a small rise poses as a boom. Cover the axis numbers with a thumb and the picture tells no story at all; that thumb test is the fastest lie detector in the subject.

The last questions of the lesson reach you now: mean against median on the homework marks, the outlier's pull explained in one sentence, and the poster caught by its axis.
