# Part 1 — Expert

Lerato runs a guesthouse with a small coffee corner in Clarens, and this month everything is measurement. A British baking book wants pounds and Fahrenheit; the new rainwater tank needs its capacity checked against the supplier's promise; the tank must be painted; and the supply run to Bloemfontein must be timed and costed. Final exams build their measurement questions from exactly this kind of month — conversions, volume, surface area and rates, always in context, always with a decision at the end.

## Subtopic: Conversions: Metric, Imperial and the Oven Dial

The metric system moves in tens: 10 millimetres to a centimetre, 100 centimetres to a metre, 1 000 metres to a kilometre; 1 000 millilitres to a litre, 1 000 litres to a kilolitre; 1 000 grams to a kilogram, 1 000 kilograms to a tonne. Converting inside the metric family is only shifting the comma: 3 200 g is 3,2 kg; 0,45 m is 45 cm.

Between metric and IMPERIAL units — the older system British and American recipes and some tools still use — there is no comma-shifting, only CONVERSION FACTORS supplied in a table: 1 inch is 2,54 cm; 1 foot is 30,48 cm; 1 mile is 1,609 km; 1 pound is 0,454 kg, so 1 kg is 2,205 pounds; 1 gallon is 3,785 litres. The method is one honest multiplication or division, guided by direction: going FROM the named unit, multiply by its factor; going TO it, divide. The recipe's 5 pounds of sugar: 5 ÷ 2,205 = 2,268 kg, so Lerato weighs off about 2 268 g.

Temperature is the exception — Celsius and Fahrenheit are related by a formula, not a factor, because their zeros sit in different places. Fahrenheit equals 1,8 times Celsius, plus 32. The recipe says bake at 400 °F: work backwards, 400 − 32 = 368, then 368 ÷ 1,8 = 204,4 °C, so the oven dial goes to 200 °C, the nearest setting. Checking forwards: a 30 °C summer day is 1,8 × 30 + 32 = 86 °F.

The mark-earning habits: quote the conversion factor you used, keep units written at every line, and round only at the end — and sensibly, because an oven cannot be set to 204,4 degrees. The questions for this section are with you now; write the factor, state the direction, and carry the units.

## Subtopic: Volume: How Much the Tank Holds

VOLUME measures the space inside a three-dimensional object, and CAPACITY expresses that volume as liquid: one cubic metre holds exactly 1 000 litres; one cubic centimetre holds one millilitre.

Lerato's new rainwater tank is a cylinder: radius 1,1 m, height 1,5 m. The volume of a cylinder is pi times the radius squared times the height, with pi taken as 3,142. Substitute in order: radius squared is 1,1 × 1,1 = 1,21. Multiply by the height: 1,21 × 1,5 = 1,815. Multiply by pi: 3,142 × 1,815 = 5,703 m³ to three decimal places. Convert to capacity: 5,703 × 1 000 = 5 703 litres. The supplier's brochure promised roughly 5 500 litres — the promise checks out, and saying so IS the answer: a Mathematical Literacy calculation ends in a sentence about the context.

Her storage chest in the pantry is a rectangular prism: 1,5 m long, 0,6 m wide, 0,8 m deep. Volume of a prism is length times width times height: 1,5 × 0,6 × 0,8 = 0,72 m³, which is 720 litres.

Two disciplines carry the marks. First, the radius is HALF the diameter — a tank advertised as 2,2 m wide has a radius of 1,1 m, and substituting the diameter quadruples the answer, since the radius gets squared. Second, keep units consistent before multiplying: mixing metres and centimetres in one formula produces nonsense. If a measurement arrives in centimetres, convert it before substitution, not after. The questions for this section are ready; write the formula, substitute with units, convert to litres at the end.

## Subtopic: Surface Area: How Much Paint

Volume fills an object; SURFACE AREA wraps it. Painting, tiling, wrapping and rust-proofing are all surface area problems, and confusing the two is the classic measurement error.

The tank's cylinder must be painted on its curved wall and its flat top — not the base, which stands on a concrete plinth. The curved wall unrolls into a rectangle whose length is the circle's circumference, 2 × pi × radius, and whose width is the height: 2 × 3,142 × 1,1 × 1,5. Step by step: 2 × 3,142 = 6,284; times 1,1 gives 6,912 to three decimals; times 1,5 gives 10,369 m². The top is a circle: pi times radius squared, 3,142 × 1,21 = 3,802 m². Total to paint: 10,369 + 3,802 = 14,171 m², call it 14,17 m².

Now the buying decision, where rounding discipline decides real money. The paint tin says one litre covers 8 m². Litres needed: 14,17 ÷ 8 = 1,77 litres. Paint sells in whole litres, so Lerato buys 2 litres — and here is the rule: quantities you BUY round UP, always, regardless of the decimal. Rounding 1,77 down to 1 litre leaves nearly half the tank bare; the mathematics of the shop is the ceiling, not the nearest whole number.

Notice what was NOT painted: the base. Real surface area questions specify which faces count — an open box has no lid, a room's floor is tiled but its ceiling is not — and the marks follow the reading as much as the formula. Area is measured in square units, volume in cubic units; an answer in the wrong unit family signals the wrong concept entirely. The questions for this section follow now; list the faces first, add their areas, and round the shopping upward.

## Subtopic: Rates: Speed, Cost and Filling Time

A RATE joins two different units into one number: kilometres per hour, rands per kilogram, litres per minute. Every rate question is a division waiting to be read correctly — the unit itself says what to divide by what.

Speed is distance over time. The supply bakkie covers the 360 km to Bloemfontein and back in 4 hours: 360 ÷ 4 = 90 km/h on average. Rearranged, time is distance over speed, and distance is speed times time — one triangle of relationships, three questions an assessment can ask.

Filling time runs on the same logic with litres. The municipal pipe fills the tank at 18 litres per minute; the tank holds 5 703 litres: 5 703 ÷ 18 = 316,83 minutes. Translate for a human: 316,83 ÷ 60 is 5 hours and about 17 minutes, since 5 hours accounts for 300 minutes and 16,83 remain. Opening the tap at 07:30 means a full tank a little before 12:50 — the sentence, again, is the answer.

Cost rates decide value for money. Coffee beans for the guesthouse: a 1 kg bag at R220,00 or a 2,5 kg bag at R500,00. Convert both to rands per kilogram: 220 ÷ 1 = R220,00 per kg; 500 ÷ 2,5 = R200,00 per kg. The bigger bag is the better buy, saving twenty rand on every kilogram — but only if 2,5 kg will actually be used while the beans are still fresh; the marks reward the calculation AND the judgement.

The discipline: write every rate with its full unit, rands per kilogram, litres per minute, and the division writes itself — the unit's top goes on top, its bottom on the bottom. The questions for this section are with you now; divide as the unit instructs, then turn the number back into a time, a cost or a choice.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same month in the kitchen and the yard, measuring with your hands before any formula.

## Subtopic: Changing Units Without Fear

Metric units are a family staircase: millimetre, centimetre, metre, kilometre — each step a jump of ten or a hundred or a thousand. Walking down to smaller units, the number grows: 3,2 kg becomes 3 200 g. Walking up to bigger units, the number shrinks: 45 cm becomes 0,45 m. Ask yourself one question before every conversion: should my answer get bigger or smaller? Grams are smaller than kilograms, so the same sugar needs MORE of them — if your converted number moved the wrong way, the comma jumped the wrong direction.

Imperial units — pounds, inches, gallons, miles — are the overseas cousins, and you are never expected to know them by heart. The question gives a little dictionary: 1 kg is 2,205 pounds, 1 inch is 2,54 cm, 1 gallon is 3,785 litres. Using the dictionary is one multiplication or one division, and the bigger-or-smaller question picks between them. The recipe wants 5 pounds of sugar. A pound is less than half a kilogram, so 5 pounds must be UNDER two and a half kilograms: 5 divided by 2,205 gives 2,268 kg — about 2 268 g. Had you multiplied, 11,03 kg would be screaming that it moved the wrong way.

Ovens are the special case: 400 °F is not 400 × anything — Fahrenheit starts counting from a different zero, so there is a two-step recipe instead of a factor. Off with the 32, then divide by 1,8: 400 becomes 368 becomes about 204, so set the dial to 200 °C.

Estimate first, calculate second, and let the size of the answer confirm the direction. Some questions on this section are with you right now; before each one, say out loud whether the number should grow or shrink.

## Subtopic: Filling the Tank

How much water fits in the new tank? Before formulas, understand what volume is: counting how many litre-boxes stack inside a shape. A box one metre by one metre by one metre — a cubic metre — swallows exactly 1 000 litres. Every volume answer in cubic metres is secretly counting thousands of litres.

The tank is a cylinder — a circle wearing height. Its recipe: area of the circular floor, times how tall the water stands. The floor: pi — call it 3,142 — times the radius squared. The tank is 2,2 m across, so the radius, centre to edge, is HALF of that: 1,1. Radius squared: 1,21. Floor area: about 3,802 square metres. Now stack the water 1,5 m high: 3,802 × 1,5 gives about 5,703 cubic metres. Thousands of litres: 5 703 litres. The brochure said 5 500 — the supplier told the truth, more or less.

The pantry chest is easier — a straight box: 1,5 × 0,6 × 0,8 = 0,72 cubic metres, 720 litres of flour tubs and frozen stock.

Two potholes swallow most marks here. Pothole one: using the full width, 2,2, as the radius — squaring doubles the damage and the tank magically holds four times too much. Whenever a question says wide or diameter or across, halve it before anything else. Pothole two: mixing units — centimetres in one slot, metres in another. Convert everything to the same unit BEFORE the formula, and the formula behaves. Questions on this section are coming your way now; halve the diameter, match the units, and turn cubic metres into litres by the thousand.

## Subtopic: Covering the Outside

Volume is what a shape holds; surface area is what a shape WEARS. Paint, wrapping paper, tiles, plaster — whenever material lies on a surface, you are adding up faces, not filling space.

The tank needs painting: the curved side and the lid, not the bottom pressed against the concrete plinth. The curved side is sneaky until you imagine peeling it: slice the label off a tin of beans and it flattens into a rectangle. One side of that rectangle is the tank's height, 1,5 m. The other is the distance AROUND the tank — the circumference, 2 × 3,142 × 1,1, about 6,91 m. So the peeled wall is 6,91 × 1,5 — about 10,37 square metres. The lid is a flat circle: 3,142 × 1,21 — about 3,80 m². Wall plus lid: roughly 14,17 square metres of steel wanting paint.

Now the shop maths, and this rule saves real money and real marks: the tin covers 8 m² per litre, and 14,17 ÷ 8 is 1,77 litres — but nobody sells 1,77 litres. Shopping ALWAYS rounds up: buy 2 litres. Round down and the job stops just past halfway, with the hardware store closed on Sunday. The same ceiling rule runs through tiles, bricks, fence poles and paint: real objects come in whole units, and running short costs more than the leftover.

Last habit: read WHICH faces the job touches. An open tank has no lid; a room gets paint on walls and ceiling but tiles on the floor; a box wrapped as a gift needs all six faces. List the faces on paper, one line each, before any formula — the list is where the marks live. The final questions of the lesson are with you now; peel the label, list the faces, and round the shopping up.
