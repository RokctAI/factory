# Part 1 — Expert

Every school holiday, cars stream out of Cape Town along the N2 toward the forests and lagoons of the Garden Route. This session plans that journey the way a Grade 12 question does — on a regional road map where one centimetre swallows fifteen kilometres — and then leaves the tar altogether, into the Outeniqua mountains, where the map's brown curves warn you how steep a trail will be before your boots find out. Four skills carry the work: number scales at province size, building and choosing scales of your own, grids and compass directions, and the Grade 12 headline skill — reading slope from a flat page.

## Subtopic: Number Scales on the Country's Road Map

A regional road map carries a scale like 1 : 1 500 000 — one unit on paper stands for one and a half million of the same unit on the ground. Convert once and carry the fact everywhere: 1 500 000 cm is 15 km, so on this map EVERY CENTIMETRE IS 15 KILOMETRES. Working that out before the ruler touches the page is the professional habit; it turns every later question into a single multiplication.

The route from Cape Town to Knysna along the N2 measures 33 cm on the map. Distance: 33 × 15 = 495 km. Check against sense: the route is signposted at about 489 km, so a map answer of 495 is exactly the accuracy a ruler deserves — map measurement is honest estimation, and a marking guideline allows a small range for it.

Now the reverse direction, the one that catches the divide-or-multiply confusion. Swellendam lies about 225 km from Cape Town by road; how far apart on this map? Ground to paper means shrink, so divide: 225 ÷ 15 = 15 cm. Run the sense test both ways, every time — paper to ground must give a big number, ground to paper a small one. An answer of 3 375 cm for a map distance means the operations swapped.

One more habit for full marks: units stated and consistent. The safest chain is centimetres times scale gives centimetres, then divide by 100 000 for kilometres — 33 × 1 500 000 = 49 500 000 cm; ÷ 100 000 = 495 km, the same answer with the conversion shown. Markers reward the method line, so write it. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the one-centimetre fact worked out first, the trip forwards, the trip backwards, and the sense test on both.

## Subtopic: Bar Scales and Making a Scale of Your Own

The map's corner also carries a BAR SCALE — a printed bar in segments, each labelled with real distance. Reading it is a two-step: measure the bar, price the centimetre. If one segment is 3 cm long and labelled 30 km, then 1 cm is worth 10 km; a mountain pass road measuring 6 cm is 6 × 10 = 60 km. The bar's superpower, tested year after year: it survives resizing. Photocopy or zoom the map and the printed ratio becomes a lie, but the bar shrinks and stretches WITH the roads, so bar measurements stay true on any copy. Its weakness is your ruler and your eye — a number scale is exact at original size; a bar scale is only as good as your measuring.

Grade 12 adds the reverse skill: DETERMINING a scale when nobody printed one. The rule is one honest fraction: scale equals drawn length over real length, in the same units, simplified to 1 : something. A netball court is really 30 m long; its drawing in the sports-day programme is 12 cm. Same units first: 30 m is 3 000 cm. Scale = 12 : 3 000 = 1 : 250. Say it in words to bank the understanding: every centimetre of drawing is 250 cm — two and a half metres — of real court.

Then CHOOSING a suitable scale, the design version of the same fraction. The school's new swimming-pool complex is 45 m long and its plan must fit a display board 90 cm wide. Try 1 : 100 — the plan comes out at 4 500 ÷ 100 = 45 cm: fits the board with room for labels. Try 1 : 50 and the plan is 90 cm — it fills the board edge to edge with no margin at all; try 1 : 500 and it is 9 cm — fits, but wastes the board and hides every detail. A suitable scale makes the drawing AS LARGE AS THE SPACE ALLOWS: large enough to read, small enough to fit with a margin, and preferably a round number others can work with. Stop for this section's questions now — pricing a bar's centimetre, resizing survival, building 1 : 250 from the court, and defending a chosen scale.

## Subtopic: Grid References and Compass Directions

Distance answers how far; the map's grid and compass answer WHERE and WHICH WAY.

The GRID: letters run across the top of the map, numbers down the side, cutting it into blocks. The reference works column-first: Ladismith in C4 sits where column C crosses row 4. A grid reference names a BLOCK, not a pinpoint — the index gets you to the block and your eye does the rest. Questions run both directions: given a town, state its block; given a block, find and name what is there.

The COMPASS: eight directions the question expects — north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west, north-west. On most maps north is straight up the sheet. From Cape Town, Knysna lies to the EAST — along the coast to the right of the page; Saldanha Bay lies to the NORTH-WEST; Beaufort West to the NORTH-EAST. The marker wants the compass word, and wants it from the right viewpoint: direction is always FROM somewhere TO somewhere, and swapping the towns reverses the answer — Cape Town from Knysna is west.

Compass words are fixed by the world; LEFT and RIGHT travel with the traveller — so route descriptions mix both. Driving east along the N2 toward Knysna, the Outeniqua mountains rise on your left, which is north; the same peaks are to the RIGHT of the driver heading home at the end of the holiday. Orient before answering: face the map's north away from you in imagination, or physically turn the map, and only then translate between compass and body.

Complete the toolkit with the DISTANCE TABLE, the triangle chart printed with road atlases: find the row for one town, the column for the other, and read the crossing cell — Cape Town row, Knysna column, 489. The table is measured along ROADS, which is why it beats ruler-and-scale for planning: your wheels cannot take the straight line the ruler takes. The questions on this section are in front of you now — block references both directions, eight compass points from the correct viewpoint, left against north on the eastbound road, and the crossing cell of a distance table.

## Subtopic: Slope on Maps: Contours and Gradient

Now the Grade 12 headline skill: a flat page telling you about steep ground.

Mountain maps draw CONTOUR LINES — brown curves joining all points at the same height above sea level. Walk along a contour and you neither climb nor drop; step across contours and you are climbing or descending. Each map states its CONTOUR INTERVAL, the height difference between neighbouring lines — say 10 m. Cross twelve lines and you have climbed 12 × 10 = 120 m, whatever the path looked like on the ground.

The reading rule is spacing: contours CLOSE TOGETHER mean the ground rises fast — steep; contours FAR APART mean gentle. A ring of contours closing on itself is a summit; lines bunched tight into a V pointing uphill trace a valley with its stream. Before any calculation, the question asks exactly this comparison: which path is steeper? The one whose contours crowd.

Then the number, GRADIENT: vertical rise over horizontal distance, expressed as 1 : something. An Outeniqua trail climbs from the 400 m contour to the 520 m contour — rise 120 m — while its map length is 4,8 cm on a 1 : 25 000 map: horizontal distance 4,8 × 25 000 = 120 000 cm = 1 200 m. Gradient = 120 : 1 200 = 1 : 10 — for every 10 m you walk forward, you climb 1 m. A second trail gains the same 120 m in only 360 m of walking: gradient 1 : 3. Now the reading that catches half of every class: 1 : 3 is STEEPER than 1 : 10 — the smaller the second number, the steeper the slope, because the same climb is packed into less distance. Say it as a picture: 1 : 3 climbs a metre every three steps; 1 : 10 spreads that metre over ten.

Finish with the planner's use of slope: time. Flat-ground walking manages about 4 km/h, so 7 km of contour path is 1,75 hours — 105 minutes; a steep gradient slows the party and adds rest stops, so a route card always allows extra for the crowded-contour sections. Distance, direction, slope, time — one map answered all four. The final questions of this part are with you now: rise counted from contour crossings, gradient built as 1 : n, the steeper-is-smaller comparison, and minutes from kilometres at walking pace.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four skills the way a family actually uses them in one school holiday — a lounge floor, a road atlas, and an older brother who trusts his ruler.

## Subtopic: Fifteen Kilometres in Every Centimetre

The atlas is open on the lounge floor, Cape Town to Knysna, and the scale in the corner says 1 : 1 500 000. Scary number, friendly meaning: everything real got shrunk one and a half million times. Do the one clever move FIRST: one and a half million centimetres is fifteen kilometres, so on this page, every centimetre is 15 km. Write that on your hand and the whole map becomes a times table.

Big brother Wesley lays his ruler along the N2: 33 cm. Times 15: 495 km. The road signs say 489 — the ruler is telling the truth to within a small town, which is all a ruler can promise, and all any question expects.

Ouma asks how far Swellendam is from Cape Town on the page, knowing the road is about 225 km. Going from real world to paper, things SHRINK: 225 divided by 15 is 15 cm — about a hand-span. If you ever get a map answer of thousands of centimetres, you multiplied where you should have divided; the sense test catches it before the marker does.

And when the youngest zooms the same map on a phone, the printed 1 : 1 500 000 stops being true — the picture grew, the promise did not. Only the little striped bar in the corner keeps telling the truth, because it grew along with the roads. Ruler on the bar, price one centimetre, and measure with that. On paper, trust the ratio; on any copy or screen, trust the bar. Quick check before we carry on — questions on the lounge-floor map are coming to you right now: the one clever move, forwards times, backwards divide, and which scale survives the zoom.

## Subtopic: Where It Is and Which Way It Lies

Finding a small town in a big atlas without help is like finding one shell on a beach — so the atlas cuts the beach into blocks. Letters across the top, numbers down the side. The index says Ladismith, C4: run your finger down column C, across row 4, and search only that block. The grid takes you to the block; your eyes finish the job.

Directions come in two languages, and the trick is knowing which one the question speaks. The compass language never moves: north is up the page, east right, south down, west left, and the four in-betweens. Knysna from Cape Town: along the coast to the right — EAST. Saldanha Bay: up and left — NORTH-WEST. Always check which town the question stands in, because direction is a view from somewhere: Cape Town seen FROM Knysna is west, the exact reverse.

The body language — left, right, straight — travels with the traveller. Driving east toward the lagoons, the Outeniqua peaks are on your LEFT; driving home at the end of the holiday, the same peaks are on your RIGHT. Same mountains, different answer, because YOU turned around. When a question mixes the languages, orient first: point the map's north where north really is, stand yourself on the road in imagination, and then translate.

And for planning, skip the ruler entirely: the atlas's triangle table of distances already drove every road for you. Cape Town row, Knysna column, 489 — road kilometres, not crow kilometres. The ruler's straight line is always shorter than the road; the table knows the bends. Your questions for this part are up now: block first, compass words from the right town, left against north both ways along the N2, and the table that knows the bends.

## Subtopic: Reading the Hills Without Climbing Them

After the lagoon days, the family laces up boots for an Outeniqua trail, and the trail map looks like someone dropped brown spaghetti on the page. Those curves are the mountain, flattened: each brown line joins ground at the SAME height, and the map says neighbouring lines are 10 m apart in height. Walk along a line, stay level. Cut across lines, climb — cross twelve and you have climbed 120 m, guaranteed, however the path winds.

Read steepness with your eyes only: where the lines crowd together, the ground rears up; where they spread, it strolls. Rings closing into a ring is a peak; tight lines folding into a V is a valley with a stream in its crease. Wesley compares two trails to the viewpoint: the short one crosses the same twelve lines in a thumb of map, the long one in a full hand. The short one is the steep one — same climb, squeezed into less walking.

The number version is gradient, and it is one fraction: climb over distance, tidied to 1 : something. Trail one: 120 m of climb over 1 200 m of walking — 1 : 10, a metre up for every ten along. Trail two: 120 m over 360 — 1 : 3, a metre up every three steps. Here is the sneaky part, and say it aloud: ONE-TO-THREE IS STEEPER THAN ONE-TO-TEN. Small second number, steep path — the whole climb crammed into a few strides. If you remember it as ladders against a wall, the 1 : 3 ladder stands nearly upright.

Last, turn map into morning: the gentle trail is 7 km, and legs do about 4 km each hour, so 7 ÷ 4 = 1,75 hours — 105 minutes, plus water stops, plus extra for any stretch where the spaghetti bunches. That is the whole art: one flat page told you how far, which way, how steep and how long — before a single boot touched the mountain. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: contour crossings counted into metres, the crowding rule, both gradients built and compared the right way round, and the 105 honest minutes. Next block, the same scale skills walk indoors — onto floor plans and the boxes and buildings of the third term.
