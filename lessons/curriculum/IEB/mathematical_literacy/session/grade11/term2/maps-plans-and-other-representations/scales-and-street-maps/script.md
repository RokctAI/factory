# Part 1 — Expert

A map is a contract: everything on this sheet is smaller than reality by exactly the same factor. The scale declares that factor, and every skill this topic tests — distances, directions, locations, routes, travel times, fuel costs — flows from reading it correctly. This session works with two maps of one journey: a street map of Mbombela for finding the bus depot and the library, and a regional road map for the trip to an uncle in White River.

## Subtopic: Number Scales: One Centimetre Standing for Thousands

A NUMBER SCALE is written as a ratio: 1 : 25 000. It means one unit on the map stands for twenty-five thousand of the same unit on the ground — one centimetre of paper is 25 000 centimetres of real Mbombela. The ratio owns no units of its own, and that is its power: it works in centimetres, millimetres, anything, provided both sides speak the same unit.

The method is three steps, always in this order. Measure the map distance with a ruler. Multiply by the scale number. Convert into sensible units. The route from the bus depot to the library measures 8 cm on the 1 : 25 000 street map: 8 × 25 000 = 200 000 cm. Convert: divide by 100 for metres, 2 000 m, then by 1 000 for kilometres, 2 km.

Conversion is where the marks drain away, so nail down two facts: 100 cm in a metre, 1 000 m in a kilometre — hence 100 000 cm in a kilometre. A shortcut worth verifying rather than memorising: on a 1 : 25 000 map, every centimetre is a quarter of a kilometre.

The calculation reverses cleanly. How long on the map is a 6 km road? 6 km is 600 000 cm; divide by 25 000 and the road takes up 24 cm of paper. Dividing where you should multiply manufactures absurdities — a suburb crossing measured in millimetres — so always test the answer against sense: map to ground grows, ground to map shrinks. The questions for this section are with you now; measure, multiply, convert, and sense-check the size.

## Subtopic: Bar Scales and the Photocopier Problem

A BAR SCALE says the same thing as a drawing: a printed bar cut into segments, each labelled with the real distance it represents. To use it, lay your ruler against the bar and price one centimetre of map. On the regional map, the bar shows 3 cm representing 12 km, so every centimetre is worth 4 km.

Using it is a proportion. The road from Mbombela to the White River turn-off measures 7 cm: at 4 km per centimetre, that is 7 × 4 = 28 km. No ruler at hand? Tick the route onto the straight edge of any scrap of paper, then step that ticked length along the bar, counting segments — a field trick older than any of us.

Now the insight assessments love: why do maps carry a bar scale when the number scale is more precise? Photocopy a map at 80% and every printed distance shrinks — yet the number scale on the copy still claims 1 : 25 000, which has become a lie. The bar scale, being a drawing, shrinks in step with the roads, so the proportion between bar and route survives any enlargement or reduction. A bar scale stays honest on every copy; a number scale is honest only at original size. A comparison question expects that exact point, and the answer must mention resizing.

The weakness cuts the other way: reading a bar scale means measuring the bar itself, so the result inherits your ruler and your eyesight, whereas a number scale is mathematically exact. Precision against robustness — name the trade-off and the mark is yours. Your questions for this section are ready; price one centimetre before measuring anything.

## Subtopic: Directions, Grids and Finding the Place

Maps answer where as well as how far. A street map carries a GRID: letters across the top, numbers down the side, slicing the sheet into blocks. The library in block D2 sits where column D crosses row 2 — and a grid reference finds the block, not the exact doorstep, which is why an index reads "Brown Street, D2" and trusts your eye to finish the search.

Directions arrive in two languages, and questions use both. COMPASS directions are fixed by the planet: north, south, east, west, and the in-betweens like north-east. On most maps north is the top of the sheet, and the rest follow: east right, south bottom, west left. White River lies north-east of Mbombela; the answer "up and to the right" earns nothing, because the marker needs the compass word.

RELATIVE directions — left, right, straight on — depend on where the traveller is facing, and they are the language of route descriptions: from the bus depot, travel east along Henshall Street, turn left into Brown Street, and the library is on your right after three blocks. Writing such a description is itself an assessed task, and the marks hide in the details: street names, turn directions, and a landmark to confirm arrival.

One habit stitches the skills together: orient the map first. Find the north arrow, rotate the map in your mind until its north matches the world's, and only then translate between "left" and "east". A traveller heading south along Henshall Street turns RIGHT, not left, to head west — the classic trap whenever the journey runs down the page. The questions for this section follow now; check the north arrow before answering anything about direction.

## Subtopic: From Map Measurement to Time, Fuel and Rands

The final skill chains everything to the questions a real trip asks: how long, and how much?

Distance first. On the 1 : 200 000 road map, the full route from Mbombela to the uncle's gate measures 11 cm: 11 × 200 000 = 2 200 000 cm = 22 km.

Time next, from the relationship: time equals distance divided by speed. Averaging 55 km/h through the hills, 22 ÷ 55 = 0,4 hours. Convert honestly: 0,4 × 60 = 24 minutes. The trap is reading 0,4 hours as 40 minutes — decimal hours are not minutes, and questions choose their numbers to expose exactly that.

Fuel is a proportion. The family car burns 7,5 ℓ of petrol per 100 km. Over 22 km: 22 ÷ 100 × 7,5 = 1,65 ℓ. At R22,80 per litre, the one-way fuel bill is 1,65 × 22,80 = R37,62 — and the question usually wants the round trip, R75,24, so read whether the journey comes home.

Compare with the minibus: R30 per person each way. One person returning pays R60; the car's return fuel is R75,24, but four people in the car still pay R75,24 together against R240 by minibus. Cost per person is the comparison that decides, and stating it in a sentence — "for four travellers the car is cheaper by R164,76" — is the expected finish.

Every link in this chain — measure, scale, convert, divide by speed, multiply by consumption and price — carries its own mark, so write every link. The last questions of this part are with you now: map to kilometres, kilometres to minutes and rands.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same two maps pinned to the depot noticeboard, read the way a traveller actually reads them.

## Subtopic: The Map Shrinks the World Evenly

A map is the world fed through a shrinking machine, and the scale is the dial setting. 1 : 25 000 means everything came out twenty-five thousand times smaller — every street, every block, all by the same amount. That evenness is the entire trick: measure anything on the paper, multiply back up, and you hold the real thing.

So the routine is measure, multiply, tidy up. The library route is 8 cm on the map. Multiply by 25 000: 200 000 cm. Nobody describes a trip across town in centimetres, so tidy: 100 cm make a metre — 2 000 m; 1 000 m make a kilometre — 2 km. A twenty-minute walk, or one quick bus hop.

Going the other way, shrink instead of grow: a 6 km stretch of road, written in centimetres — 600 000 — divided by 25 000, becomes 24 cm of paper.

And always run the sense test. Paper to world: the number must balloon. World to paper: it must collapse. If your answer claims the library is 20 km away or the road fits into 2 mm, you divided where you should have multiplied — no shame, just repair it before it costs you. Some questions on this section are with you right now: ruler first, multiply second, tidy the units last.

## Subtopic: The Little Bar That Survives the Photocopier

In the corner of the map lives a striped bar, and it is the most honest object on the sheet. The label says each stripe is 12 km. Lay your ruler on it: the stripe spans 3 cm. So the map is speaking plainly — every centimetre means 4 km.

Read distances with that single fact. The road to the turn-off is 7 cm: seven times four, 28 km. No ruler? Lay the straight edge of any paper along the route, tick the start and the end, then hold the ticks against the bar and count stripes. The bar does not mind what you measure with.

Here is why the bar earns its corner. Feed the map through a photocopier and shrink it to pocket size. Every road just got smaller — and the printed ratio 1 : 25 000 is now wrong, because one centimetre of the small copy no longer covers twenty-five thousand real ones. But the bar shrank WITH the roads. Seven of its centimetres still match the same stretch of tar, stripe for stripe. The bar keeps its promise on any copy; the ratio only tells the truth at original size.

The bar's weakness is your eyesight — you must measure the bar itself, so a slightly skew ruler gives a slightly skew answer, while the printed ratio is exact. Exact but fragile, or rough but robust: that is the choice, and saying it in one sentence is worth a mark. Questions on this section are coming your way now — find the bar, price one centimetre, then count.

## Subtopic: Getting There: Blocks, Turns and Petrol Money

Finding the place, describing the way, and paying for the trip — the last three jobs of any map.

Finding: the grid. Letters across the top, numbers down the side, and "library, D2" means the block where column D meets row 2. The grid delivers you to the block; your eyes do the final hundred metres. Directions come in two flavours: compass words — north at the top of the sheet, east to the right — and body words — left, right, straight on. The compass never moves; left and right travel with you. Driving south down Henshall Street, west sits on your RIGHT — turn the map around in your head before you answer, or the down-the-page journey will flip every turn.

Describing: name the streets, name the turns, end on a landmark. From the depot: east along Henshall, left into Brown, library on the right after three blocks. That sentence earns marks exactly as written.

Paying: chain the map to your wallet. The road map puts the uncle 11 cm away at 1 : 200 000 — 22 km. At 55 km/h that is 22 divided by 55, which is 0,4 of an hour — and 0,4 of 60 minutes is 24 minutes, not 40. The car sips 7,5 litres every 100 km, so 22 km costs 1,65 litres; at R22,80 a litre, R37,62 there and R75,24 there-and-back. The minibus wants R60 for one person returning — cheaper if you travel alone. Load the car with four people and the R75,24 splits to about R18,81 each against the minibus's R60 — the car wins by R164,76 altogether. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: block, turns, kilometres, minutes, rands — in that order, every time.
