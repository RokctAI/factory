Hey, it's Bianca — maps can feel like geography sneaking into your maths lesson, but every map question is the same three-step dance, and you already know all the steps.

Step one: measure. Ruler on the map, honest centimetres. Step two: multiply by the scale — 1 : 25 000 means times 25 000. Step three: tidy the units in two small hops, never one giant leap. Divide by 100 for metres, then by 1 000 for kilometres. 8 cm becomes 200 000 cm becomes 2 000 m becomes 2 km. Write all three hops and the conversion can never betray you.

Then run the sense test, your free safety net: paper to world must balloon, world to paper must collapse. If a route across town comes out as 20 km, the conversion slipped — catch it yourself, smile, fix it.

Bar scales: measure the bar, price one centimetre, count. And keep the comparison sentence loaded: the bar survives photocopying because it shrinks with the roads; the printed ratio is exact but only true at original size.

Directions: find the north arrow FIRST. North top, east right — but left and right belong to the traveller, so a journey heading down the page flips every turn. Rotate the map in your head before you answer.

And the money questions are just a chain: kilometres, divided by speed — decimals of an hour times 60 for minutes, 0,4 hours is 24 minutes, never 40 — then litres per 100 km, then rands per litre. One link at a time, each link a mark.

Measure, multiply, tidy, sense-check. That is every map ever printed. Now go take on the practice questions — you've got this!
