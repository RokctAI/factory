Hey, it's Bianca — probability feels like betting your marks on luck, but it is honestly the most predictable topic you will meet. Every question is one of four moves, and you can name the move before touching a single number.

Move one: counting chances. Ways to win, over ways in total — two faces greater than four out of six, one WIN slice out of ten. Keep the answer between 0 and 1, and hold the ruler in mind: 0 never, 1 definitely, 0,5 coin-toss country. If your answer says 1,3, that is not a strong chance, it is a broken fraction — go fix the denominator.

Move two: watching. No symmetry, no formula — minibuses, rain and power cuts get counted instead: full on 18 of 50 mornings is 0,36, and it is an ESTIMATE, one that grows truer the longer you watch. Coins get theory; minibuses get tallies. Always say which one you used.

Move three: the tree, for one-thing-then-another. Branch, branch again, multiply along the journey. The tree's whole job is catching the journey your head drops — tails-heads, the missing twin of heads-tails. Draw it every time; the drawing itself earns marks. And when you hear "at least one", flip it: 1 minus the probability of NONE. One line instead of three branches.

Move four: the grid, for crowds with two labels. Fill the cells, add the totals, check they agree. Then the words choose your denominator: "a random visitor" means the grand total; "given that" or "of the learners" shrinks the world to one row. Underline the phrase, ask who is still in the room, divide.

Count, watch, branch, or grid — name the move, draw the picture, keep it between 0 and 1. Now go claim those practice questions. You've got this!
