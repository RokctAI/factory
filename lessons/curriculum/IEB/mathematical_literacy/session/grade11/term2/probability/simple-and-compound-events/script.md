# Part 1 — Expert

Uncertainty threads through ordinary South African life: whether the morning minibus has a seat left, whether the power stays on for tonight's homework, whether the lucky-draw wheel at the school fair ever pays for its ticket. Probability is the arithmetic of that uncertainty, and grade 11 wants four skills from you: the scale that measures chance, the split between calculating outcomes and watching them, and the two machines for double events — tree diagrams and two-way tables. Our setting is one fundraising fair in Polokwane, with a coin game, a lucky-draw wheel, and a gate survey of who arrives by minibus.

## Subtopic: The Scale from Impossible to Certain

An OUTCOME is one possible result of an activity: a coin showing tails, a die showing 5. An EVENT is the set of outcomes you care about: "greater than four" bundles the outcomes 5 and 6. PROBABILITY measures how likely the event is, on a scale running from 0 to 1 and absolutely nowhere beyond.

Zero means IMPOSSIBLE — rolling an 8 on an ordinary die. One means CERTAIN — rolling some number from 1 to 6. Every real chance lives between: 0,5 is an even chance, under 0,5 unlikely, over 0,5 likely. One probability wears three outfits — fraction, decimal, percentage: a half, 0,5, 50% — and questions change outfits mid-sentence, so converting between them must be reflex.

For equally likely outcomes, theoretical probability is a ratio: outcomes inside the event, divided by all possible outcomes. A die has six faces, two of them greater than four, so the probability is two sixths — one third. The fair's lucky-draw wheel has ten equal sectors, one marked WIN, so the chance of winning is one tenth: 0,1, or 10%.

Two disciplines hold the whole topic up. No probability may exceed 1 or dip below 0 — an answer of 1,3 or a negative is wrong regardless of the working. And the probability of an event NOT happening is 1 minus the probability that it does: the wheel loses with probability nine tenths, because one tenth and nine tenths must total exactly 1. The questions for this section are with you now; place every answer on the 0-to-1 scale and confirm it belongs there.

## Subtopic: Watching versus Calculating: Relative Frequency

Theoretical probability is worked out before anything happens, from the symmetry of the equipment: a fair coin grants heads a half, because two identical faces split the certainty. But minibuses, weather and power cuts own no symmetry — their chances must be estimated by WATCHING.

RELATIVE FREQUENCY is the fraction of trials in which the event actually happened: occurrences divided by trials. Karabo logs the 06:45 minibus for 50 school mornings; it arrives full on 18 of them. The relative frequency of a full minibus is 18 over 50 = 0,36, and 0,36 becomes her working estimate of tomorrow's risk.

Relative frequency wobbles. Ten flips of a fair coin can produce eight heads — relative frequency 0,8 — without the coin being loaded. The law underneath the topic says: as trials multiply, relative frequency settles toward the true probability. A thousand flips landing 800 heads convicts the coin; ten flips landing 8 is just a short sample being a short sample. More trials, better estimate — and an answer comparing the two must say so.

The two ideas partition the world. Symmetric equipment — coins, dice, wheels, cards — takes theoretical probability. Real-world processes — rain, breakdowns, library books returned late — take relative frequency built from records. The classic task hands you a coin flipped 250 times with 110 heads and wants both numbers: theoretical 0,5, relative frequency 110 over 250 = 0,44, plus a sentence noting the coin looks fair because 0,44 sits near 0,5 and 250 flips is a respectable test. Your questions for this section are ready; decide first whether you are calculating from symmetry or estimating from data.

## Subtopic: Tree Diagrams: Chance in Two Steps

A COMPOUND event is two simple events in sequence: two coin tosses, a wheel spun twice, a route chosen and then the weather arriving. The TREE DIAGRAM is the machine that lists every combined outcome and never drops one.

Build the fair's coin game: toss a coin twice, win a koeksister for two tails. From the start, two branches — heads and tails, each carrying a half. From EACH of those, two more branches for the second toss, again a half each. The tree finishes with four end-points: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails — four equally likely journeys through the game.

Read events off the ends. Two tails is one journey of four: probability one quarter. Exactly one tail is two journeys — heads-tails and tails-heads — so two quarters, a half. At least one tail is three journeys, three quarters; and the shortcut goes through the complement, 1 minus the probability of no tails at all: 1 minus a quarter, three quarters again.

The multiplication rule also drives unequal trees: multiply along the branches. The food stand runs out of boerewors rolls one day in ten — probability 0,1 — and the days behave independently. Rolls available two days running: 0,9 × 0,9 = 0,81. Sold out both days: 0,1 × 0,1 = 0,01. Whatever remains is one day stocked, one day not: 0,18 — and 0,81 + 0,01 + 0,18 = 1, the check that the tree missed nothing.

Draw the tree even when the answer feels obvious; the diagram itself collects method marks, and the forgotten journey — tails-heads, dropped by everyone counting in their head — is precisely what it exists to catch. The questions for this section follow now; label every branch with its probability and multiply along the journey.

## Subtopic: Two-Way Tables: Chance in a Grid

When each person carries TWO facts at once — learner or adult, minibus or on foot — the TWO-WAY TABLE sorts the crowd into a grid, and probability becomes reading that grid.

The gate survey asked 150 fair visitors two questions: learner or adult, and arrived by minibus or walked. The table: learners by minibus, 36; learners who walked, 54; adults by minibus, 24; adults who walked, 36. Row totals: 90 learners, 60 adults. Column totals: 60 by minibus, 90 walked. Grand total: 150 — and every row and column must add to its total, the built-in check a marker expects you to run before anything else.

Now the grid answers questions. The probability that a random visitor is a learner: 90 over 150, which simplifies to three fifths — 0,6. That a random visitor is an adult who came by minibus: the single cell 24, over the grand total 150 — four twenty-fifths, 0,16. Compound events read straight from cells; no tree required.

The subtle question is the CONDITIONAL one: given that a visitor is a learner, what is the chance they came by minibus? The words "given that" shrink the world: only the 90 learners remain, and 36 of them rode, so 36 over 90 = 0,4. Compare minibus use among adults: 24 over 60 = 0,4 as well — identical rates, and noticing that is itself worth marks: in this crowd, transport habit is independent of being a learner or an adult.

The entire skill is choosing the correct denominator: the grand total for "a random visitor", a row or column total whenever the wording says "given" or "of the learners". Read the words, pick the world, then divide. The last questions of this part are with you now: totals first, cells second, denominator picked by the wording.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same fair at ground level, where chance is a coin in your palm and a crowd filing through a gate.

## Subtopic: How Sure Is Sure

Every chance on earth fits one short ruler: 0 — never — up to 1 — definitely. Tomorrow's sunrise sits at 1. Rolling an 8 on a normal die sits at 0. Everything else lives between, and the middle, 0,5, is coin-toss country: could go either way.

One chance speaks three languages: one half, 0,5 and 50% are a single statement in fraction, decimal and percent. The fair's wheel has ten equal slices with one WIN: the chance is one in ten — one tenth, 0,1, 10%. Three spellings, one size of hope.

Counting chances, when everything is equally likely, is counting twice: ways to win, out of ways in total. A roll greater than four: two faces out of six — one third. Drawing a red sweet blind from a bag of 4 red and 16 green: 4 out of 20 — one fifth.

Two rules keep answers legal. Nothing outranks certainty: a probability of 1,3 is not a strong chance, it is a broken answer. And happening plus not-happening always fills the whole ruler: if WIN is one tenth, LOSE is nine tenths, because together they must make exactly 1. Some questions on this section are with you right now — count the ways, count the total, and check that your answer fits on the ruler.

## Subtopic: The Minibus You Watched for Fifty Mornings

A coin announces its chances before you touch it: two faces, equal weight, a half each. The 06:45 minibus announces nothing — there is no rulebook for minibuses. So you watch.

Karabo watched for 50 school mornings and counted 18 mornings when the minibus pulled up full. Her estimate of the chance: 18 out of 50, which is 0,36 — call it a thirty-six percent risk of watching a full minibus drive past. Watch, count, divide: that is relative frequency, and it is how the real world's chances get measured — rain, breakdowns, late deliveries, all of them.

But hold a short watch loosely. Flip a fair coin ten times and eight heads is no scandal — the coin is honest, the sample is just tiny. Flip it a thousand times and eight hundred heads means that coin has a problem. The rule: the longer you watch, the closer the count creeps to the truth. Fifty mornings is a solid watch; five mornings would be gossip.

So when a question serves you both worlds — a coin flipped 250 times showing 110 heads — give both numbers and let them talk: theory says 0,5, the watching says 0,44, and the sentence that earns the mark says they sit close together, so the coin behaves fairly and 250 flips is a test worth trusting. Questions on this section are coming your way now — theory for coins and wheels, watching for minibuses and weather, and always name which one you used.

## Subtopic: Branches and Grids: Handling Two Things at Once

Two events at once is where heads start spinning — so stop juggling in your head and build one of two pictures.

Picture one: the tree, for things happening in sequence. The coin game: first toss splits into heads and tails, a half each. Each branch splits again for the second toss. Four journeys through: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads, tails-tails, each a quarter — multiply along the journey, a half times a half. Two tails takes the koeksister: one journey, one quarter. Exactly one tail: TWO journeys — and here waits the trap. Juggling mentally, you spot heads-tails and lose tails-heads; the tree lays both in front of your eyes, and that lost branch is exactly the mark the rest of the room drops.

Picture two: the grid, for a crowd wearing two labels each. At the gate, 150 visitors: learner or adult, minibus or walked. Fill the four cells — 36 learner-minibus, 54 learner-walk, 24 adult-minibus, 36 adult-walk — then the totals: 90 learners, 60 adults, 60 minibus, 90 walked, 150 in all. Rows and columns must agree with their totals; if they refuse, a cell is lying, and catching it now costs nothing.

Then every question is one division, and the wording picks the bottom number. "A random visitor is a learner": 90 out of 150. "A random visitor is an adult who came by minibus": that single cell, 24 out of 150. But "GIVEN that a visitor is a learner, did they ride?" — the words shrink the world to the 90 learners only: 36 out of 90, which is 0,4. Spot "given that" or "of the learners", and the denominator stops being 150.

Journeys for sequences, cells for crowds, and the denominator chosen by the words. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — draw the picture first, and let the picture do the counting.
