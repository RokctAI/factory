# Part 1 — Expert

Four kinds of numbers quietly run every South African household budget: the VAT folded into every shop price, the UIF line that shrinks every payslip, the tariff tables that price a phone call or a unit of electricity, and the exchange rate that prices the rand itself. This session follows one household in Makhanda through all four: a till slip from the Saturday shop, a payslip from the bakery where Naledi works, two cellphone deals side by side in the shop window, and an aunt in Dublin who sends euros home.

## Subtopic: VAT: the Fifteen Percent on Almost Everything

VALUE-ADDED TAX is a 15% tax that the government collects on most goods and services, gathered for it by the shops. A price EXCLUDING VAT is the amount before the tax is added; a price INCLUDING VAT is what leaves your wallet. The whole skill is moving between those two prices, and each direction has exactly one legal move.

Going up — adding VAT: take 15% of the excluding price, or jump straight to the total by multiplying by 1,15. A kettle listed at R360 excluding VAT carries tax of 360 × 0,15 = R54, so the till charges R414 — or in one step, 360 × 1,15 = R414.

Coming down — removing VAT — is where careless answers are born. The R414 already contains the tax, which means it stands for 115% of the pre-tax price. The only way back is to divide by 1,15: 414 ÷ 1,15 = R360. The seductive wrong move is to knock 15% off the R414. That gives 414 × 0,85 = R351,90 — out by R8,10, because it computes fifteen percent of the larger, already-taxed figure instead of the original.

Not everything is taxed. ZERO-RATED items are the protected basic foods: brown bread, maize meal, rice, dried beans, lentils, fresh fruit and vegetables, eggs, milk. Till slips flag them, and a common task hands you a slip where only some lines carry VAT — you tax the taxable lines and leave the protected basket untouched. The questions for this section are with you now; before touching a calculator, state whether the price in front of you already includes the tax.

## Subtopic: UIF: One Percent from Each Side

The UNEMPLOYMENT INSURANCE FUND exists to pay a worker a temporary income during retrenchment, maternity leave or serious illness. It is fed by a contribution of 1% of the worker's earnings — collected twice over: 1% deducted from the WORKER's pay, and a matching 1% paid in by the EMPLOYER on top of the salary.

Naledi earns a gross salary of R9 200 a month at the bakery. Her UIF deduction is 9 200 × 0,01 = R92. The bakery pays a further R92 to the fund from its own account. The fund therefore banks R184 for her every month, yet only R92 ever appears on her payslip. Assessment questions live inside exactly that gap: what the worker pays, what the employer pays, and what the fund receives are three different answers built from the same salary.

On the payslip itself, UIF sits among the DEDUCTIONS — the amounts stripped away between GROSS pay, the full amount earned, and NET pay, the amount actually deposited. Naledi's gross is R9 200; after UIF of R92 and other deductions of R648, her net pay is 9 200 − 92 − 648 = R8 460.

Two refinements each worth a mark. UIF is always calculated on gross earnings, before the other deductions are considered — never on net pay. And the rate never varies with income: 1% for the packer and 1% for the branch manager alike, though the law does set an earnings ceiling, and when a question quotes that ceiling, a high earner contributes one percent of the ceiling rather than of the full salary. Your questions for this section are ready; one percent, taken from both sides, always on the gross.

## Subtopic: Exchange Rates: the Price of the Rand

An EXCHANGE RATE is nothing more than the price of one currency quoted in another. R19,80 to the euro means every euro costs nineteen rand eighty. Every conversion is one of two opposite journeys, and picking the right direction is the entire skill.

Buying the foreign currency: multiply. An online course billed at €150 costs 150 × 19,80 = R2 970. Turning rands into the foreign currency: divide. A traveller holding R6 000 receives 6 000 ÷ 19,80 = €303,03.

Here is a direction test that never lets you down: the euro is the expensive item, priced at R19,80 apiece. Travelling from the expensive unit into rands, multiply — many rands per euro. Travelling from rands into the expensive unit, divide — your rands buy fewer euros than their face value suggests.

STRONGER and WEAKER describe how the rate moves. If the quote climbs from R19,80 to R20,60 per euro, each euro now demands more rand, so the rand has WEAKENED — imported goods, fuel and overseas holidays all cost more. If the quote drops to R19,10, the rand has STRENGTHENED. The aunt in Dublin sends €250 home each month: at R19,80 the family banks 250 × 19,80 = R4 950; if the rand weakens to R20,60, the same €250 lands as R5 150. A weaker rand stings at the petrol pump yet delights a family receiving money from abroad — and questions love asking you to argue both sides of that coin. The questions for this section follow now; speak the rate as a sentence — one euro costs R19,80 — before you choose to multiply or divide.

## Subtopic: Tariff Systems: Which Option Is Cheaper for You

A TARIFF SYSTEM is any charging rule that depends on how much you use: municipal water and electricity blocks, cellphone contracts, taxi fares, bank fee menus. The grade eleven task is nearly always the same — put TWO options next to each other and establish where each one wins.

The household is weighing up two cellphone deals. Option P: a fixed monthly charge of R120 plus R0,60 per minute of calls. Option Q: no monthly charge at all, but R1,40 per minute. First job, always: turn each deal into a formula. Cost of P in rand = 120 + 0,60 × minutes. Cost of Q = 1,40 × minutes.

Now test honest usage figures. At 100 minutes: P costs 120 + 60 = R180, while Q costs R140 — Q wins. At 250 minutes: P costs 120 + 150 = R270, while Q costs R350 — P wins. Somewhere between those figures the deals must swap. Set the formulae equal: 120 + 0,60 × m = 1,40 × m, so 120 = 0,80 × m, and m = 150 minutes exactly. Below 150 minutes a month the light talker should sign for Q; above it the heavy talker should sign for P. That swap point is the BREAK-EVEN point of the comparison, and on a graph it is literally where the two cost lines cross — Q launching from zero but climbing steeply, P starting at R120 but rising gently.

The identical machinery settles bank-account questions: a bundled account with a monthly fee and cheap transactions against a pay-as-you-transact account, with the crossing measured in transactions rather than minutes. The method is fixed: a formula per option, test values either side, solve for the crossing, then answer as advice in words — who should take which option, and why. The last questions of this part are with you now: never compare deals before both formulae are written down.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same till, same payslip, same shop window — this time with bare hands and no formula sheet.

## Subtopic: The Tax Inside the Till Slip

Almost every price you meet is carrying a stowaway: fifteen percent for the government, tucked inside the number on the sticker. Your first question at any price is whether the stowaway is already aboard.

Price WITHOUT the tax, and you need the till total? Add the fifteen percent: the R360 kettle picks up R54 of tax and rings up at R414. The one-move shortcut: multiply by 1,15 and you are done.

Price WITH the tax, and you need the original? This is the trap that catches somebody every single time. Do not peel fifteen percent off. The R414 is the original PLUS fifteen percent of the original — it is 115 percent of something. Divide by 1,15 and the something reappears: R360 on the nose. Peel fifteen percent off instead and you land on R351,90 — close enough to feel safe, wrong enough to score nothing. Adding fifteen percent and then removing fifteen percent never brings you home, because the second fifteen percent is measured on a bigger number than the first.

And keep the protected basket in your head: brown bread, maize meal, rice, beans, vegetables, fruit, eggs, milk. Zero-rated — no stowaway at all. On a mixed till slip, only the taxable lines pay. Some questions on this section are with you right now — interrogate every price first: is the fifteen percent already inside you?

## Subtopic: The Two R92s on the Payslip

Hold Naledi's payslip up to the light. Top line, gross pay: R9 200 — what the month's work earned. Bottom line, net pay: what the bank actually shows. Between them sit the deductions, and one row reads UIF: R92.

That R92 is precisely one percent of her gross, and it buys insurance — if the bakery closes, the fund pays her a temporary income while she hunts for the next job. One percent is the gentlest percentage there is: slide the decimal comma two places left. R9 200 becomes R92. A gross of R14 350 would give R143,50. No calculator required, ever.

Now the half that stays invisible: the bakery pays a second R92 into the fund, over and above her salary, out of its own pocket. The fund collects R184 a month for Naledi, but her payslip only ever admits to R92. So when a question asks "how much does Naledi contribute?", the answer is R92. "How much does the fund receive?" — R184. Both numbers will be lounging in the options, waiting for the student who did not read the question.

Then close off the payslip: gross R9 200, minus UIF R92, minus other deductions R648, leaves R8 460 net. Gross minus deductions equals net — every payslip, every time — and the one percent is always taken on the gross, before anything else is subtracted. Questions on this section are coming your way now — slide the comma, then check whether the worker's side, the employer's side, or the total is being asked.

## Subtopic: Rands Abroad and the Cheaper Phone Deal

Two everyday decisions close the lesson: moving money across a border, and choosing between two deals.

Money first. A euro is simply an item with a price tag: R19,80 each. Paying €150 for the online course? That is buying 150 of the items: 150 times 19,80 — R2 970. Turning R6 000 INTO euros? Now you are asking how many R19,80 items your money can afford: 6 000 divided by 19,80 — just over 303 euros. Multiply on the way to rands, divide on the way to the foreign money. And when the quote rises from R19,80 to R20,60, euros became dearer — meaning the rand became weaker. Weaker rand: fuel and imports bite harder, but the aunt's €250 from Dublin swells to R5 150 at home. One movement, two opposite faces.

Now the shop window. Deal P: R120 rent every month, then only 60 cents a minute. Deal Q: no rent, but R1,40 a minute. Never guess — test. A quiet talker at 100 minutes: P totals 120 plus 60, R180; Q totals R140. Q wins by R40. A busy talker at 250 minutes: P totals R270, Q totals R350. P wins by R80. The deals change places at exactly 150 minutes, because P charges rent but sells cheap minutes, while Q charges no rent but sells dear ones.

That is every tariff comparison compressed into one idea: the fixed-charge option rewards the heavy user, the pay-as-you-go option rewards the light user, and the break-even point is where they trade crowns. Work both totals at the usage you are given, then answer like a consultant: "at 100 minutes a month, take Q and keep R40." The final questions of the lesson are with you now — run both options honestly and let the arithmetic sign the contract.
