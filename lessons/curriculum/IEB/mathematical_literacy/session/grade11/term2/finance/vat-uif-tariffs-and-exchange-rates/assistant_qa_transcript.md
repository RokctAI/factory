**Bianca:** Class questions before we wrap up! The one I hear the most: why does dividing by 1,15 remove VAT? People memorise the rule but never quite believe it, and the moment the pressure is on they slide back into taking fifteen percent off.

**Tutor:** Belief comes from seeing what the inclusive price is made of. When the shop added VAT to the kettle, it took the original R360 — call that one whole, one hundred percent — and stacked fifteen percent OF THAT ORIGINAL on top. So the till price of R414 is one hundred and fifteen percent of the original. Recovering the original therefore means undoing a multiplication by 1,15 — and the undo button for multiplication is division: 414 ÷ 1,15 = R360. Now watch the popular method break: fifteen percent OF R414 is R62,10, a bigger slice than the R54 that was ever added, because R414 is a bigger number than R360. Subtract it and you get R351,90 — you removed too much, R8,10 too much. The deep idea is that percentage increases and decreases are not symmetrical: adding fifteen percent and then subtracting fifteen percent never lands you back at the start, because the two fifteens are percentages of different numbers. If you keep one sentence from tonight, keep this one: the inclusive price is 115%, so divide by 1,15 to find the 100%.

**Bianca:** Next: the two R92s confused a lot of people. If the bakery also pays R92 UIF for Naledi, why doesn't her payslip show R184 — and is the employer's share secretly coming out of her salary?

**Tutor:** The employer's share never comes near her salary — it is an extra cost of employing her, paid from the bakery's own account, over and above the R9 200. Picture the law charging both sides of the employment relationship one percent each for the same insurance policy: the worker insures her income, and the employer contributes to the pool that keeps the insurance solvent. A payslip only records what happens to the worker's own earnings, so it shows her gross of R9 200, her own deduction of R92, and stays silent about the employer's matching payment. That silence is exactly what questions probe. "What is deducted from the worker's pay?" — R92. "What does the employer contribute?" — R92, separately. "What does the fund receive in total?" — R184. Three questions, three answers, one salary. And one refinement for full marks: the one percent is always calculated on gross earnings, before tax and other deductions — and if a question quotes the legislated earnings ceiling, a worker earning above it contributes one percent of the ceiling, not of the full salary. If the ceiling matters, the question will hand you the figure.

**Bianca:** This one caused genuine panic: multiply or divide with exchange rates. Everyone knows the rate is R19,80 per euro, and then half the class multiplies where they should divide.

**Tutor:** One sentence cures the panic permanently: say the rate as a price. "One euro COSTS nineteen rand eighty." A euro is now just an expensive item on a shelf. Buying a known number of euros — the €150 course fee — is ordinary shopping: quantity times price, 150 times 19,80, R2 970. Notice the answer lands in rands and is BIGGER than the euro figure, because each euro costs many rands. Holding rands and asking how many euros they buy — R6 000 in the account — is the opposite trip: how many R19,80 items fit into the money? Six thousand divided by 19,80, just over 303 euros. The answer lands in euros and is SMALLER than the rand figure. That size check is your seatbelt: rands converted to a stronger currency must shrink; a stronger currency converted to rands must grow. If R6 000 ever becomes 118 800 of anything, you multiplied where you should have divided — and the seatbelt catches it before the marker does.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the phone-deal comparison, someone asked why the break-even point matters at all. If the question already says the person talks 100 minutes a month, can't we just work out both totals and pick the cheaper?

**Tutor:** For that one person, do exactly that — P costs R180 at 100 minutes, Q costs R140, so Q is the advice, and the saving of R40 belongs in your final sentence. The break-even point answers a bigger question: not "which deal is cheaper for THIS caller" but "for WHOM is each deal cheaper". At 150 minutes the two deals cost the same; below it, every caller on earth is better off on Q; above it, every caller is better off on P. That single number upgrades you from calculator to consultant — you can advise any customer who walks into the shop without redoing the sums. Assessments test it from both ends: a graph question asks you to read off where the lines cross, and an advice question describes two or three different users and expects each placed on the correct side of 150. There is also a scoring reason to care: the equation 120 plus 0,60 m equals 1,40 m, solved cleanly to m equals 150, earns its own method marks even if a later step wobbles. So do both jobs — answer the given caller with both totals, and let the break-even point make your advice general. And keep the edge case honest: at exactly 150 minutes the deals tie, so neither choice is wrong, and a careful answer says so.
