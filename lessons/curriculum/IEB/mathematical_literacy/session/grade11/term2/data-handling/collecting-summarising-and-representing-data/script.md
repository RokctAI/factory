# Part 1 — Expert

Every statistics task in this subject retells the same short story: somebody needed to know something, asked a group of people, recorded the answers, compressed them into a handful of honest numbers, and finished with a picture. The syllabus names the stages — developing questions, collecting, classifying, summarising and representing — and assessment visits every stage, especially the quiet ways each one can go wrong. This session runs one investigation end to end: a grade 11 class in Kimberley studying what learners at their school spend on tuck-shop snacks and cool drinks each week, to decide whether the tuck shop should launch a R25 lunchtime combo.

## Subtopic: The Question, the Population and the Sample

An investigation starts with a QUESTION sharp enough for arithmetic to answer. "Do learners spend too much at the tuck shop?" cannot be investigated — "too much" is a judgement, not a measurement. "How many rand does a learner at our school spend at the tuck shop in a week?" can be. Writing the sharp version is itself an assessed skill: the question must name the group, the quantity and the unit.

The POPULATION is the whole group the question speaks about — all 900 learners at the school. Asking every single one is a CENSUS: perfectly accurate, painfully slow, and expensive. The practical alternative is a SAMPLE — a smaller group selected to stand in for everyone. The class decides to survey 50 learners.

The entire investigation now hangs on HOW those 50 are chosen. A sample must be REPRESENTATIVE — the school in miniature. Grabbing 50 grade 11 classmates fails immediately: seniors tend to carry more spending money than grade 8s, so the sample is BIASED — tilted away from the truth in a predictable direction. The repair is a RANDOM sample, where every learner has an equal chance of selection: 50 names drawn blind from the full register, or every eighteenth name down the alphabetical list.

Finally, the collection INSTRUMENT: a questionnaire, an interview, direct observation, or existing records. Questionnaires reach many respondents cheaply but come back with blanks; interviews dig deeper but devour time and can steer the answers. A question will often ask you to pick an instrument AND justify the pick in one sentence. The questions for this section are with you now; name the population, weigh the sample, and sniff out the bias.

## Subtopic: Tallies, Frequencies and Sensible Groups

Fifty raw answers are noise: 30, 45, 10, 55, 25, 70, in no particular order. The first organising tool is the TALLY: read each value exactly once, put a stroke beside its group, and lay every fifth stroke across the previous four. The stroke count for a group is its FREQUENCY, and the completed grid is a FREQUENCY TABLE.

With rand amounts scattered from R0 to R100, tallying each separate value achieves nothing — nearly every value would score a single stroke. So the data is packed into CLASS INTERVALS: R0 to under R20, R20 to under R40, R40 to under R60, and onward. Intervals may never overlap — a learner spending exactly R40 must have exactly one home, which is why "R20 to R40" followed by "R40 to R60" is a defect any marker will pounce on. Intervals should also share one width, and there should be few enough to expose a shape: five to eight groups is the working zone.

The class's finished table reads: R0 to under R20, frequency 6; R20 to under R40, frequency 14; R40 to under R60, frequency 18; R60 to under R80, frequency 8; R80 and more, frequency 4. Always verify the total: 6 + 14 + 18 + 8 + 4 = 50 — every learner counted once, nobody counted twice. That single addition earns its own mark.

The table now says what the raw pile could not: spending gathers between R20 and R60, with a thin tail of big spenders. But grouping charges a fee — the individual values vanish, so the table cannot reveal the single largest amount. Naming that exchange, detail traded for shape, is a favourite two-mark question. Your questions for this section are ready; tally once per slip, confirm the total, and guard the interval boundaries.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median, Mode and the Story of the Range

A SUMMARY STATISTIC crushes a whole dataset into one number, and grade 11 works with four of them. Start small — eleven learners' weekly tuck-shop spending in rand, already sorted: 10, 15, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 100.

The MEAN is the share-out number: add everything, divide by the count. The values total 385, so the mean is 385 ÷ 11 = R35. The MEDIAN is the middle of the SORTED list: eleven values, so the sixth — R30 — with five below and five above. The MODE is the most frequent value: R15, appearing twice. The RANGE measures spread rather than centre: largest minus smallest, 100 − 10 = R90.

Now the insight that earns reasoning marks: mean and median disagree, R35 against R30, and the culprit is the learner spending R100 — an OUTLIER, far beyond the rest. The mean is hauled upward by extremes because every rand joins the addition; the median responds only to position, so the outlier cannot budge it. Delete the R100 and the mean sinks to 285 ÷ 10 = R28,50, while the median scarcely moves. Whenever data carries outliers — salaries, house prices, spending with a few big spenders — the median is the honest "typical" value, and explaining WHY is where the marks sit.

Questions also drive the machine in reverse: if eleven values have a mean of R35, their total must be 11 × 35 = R385, and one missing value emerges by subtracting the known ten from that total. Rehearse both directions. The questions for this section follow now; sort before you take the median, and point at the outlier whenever the averages quarrel.

## Subtopic: Graphs That Tell the Truth — and Graphs That Lie

Data finishes as a picture, and grade 11 must both draw pictures and interrogate them. Three forms carry this term. A MULTIPLE BAR GRAPH stands two bars side by side per category — boys' and girls' average tuck-shop spending per grade — comparing two datasets category by category; it demands a key, labelled axes and equal bar widths. A LINE GRAPH threads points through time to show trend — the tuck shop's weekly turnover across the term; a BROKEN-LINE graph is the same idea where values jump instead of flow. A SCATTER PLOT drops one dot per person — snack spending against pocket money — and exposes whether two quantities travel together: dots rising to the right hint that more pocket money accompanies more spending.

Reading any graph is a three-step drill: title and axis labels first, scale second — establish what one gridline is worth — and only then the values. Nearly all reading errors are scale errors.

Suspicion is an assessed skill, because a graph can mislead without one false number. The classic device: a vertical axis opening at R25 instead of R0 makes a bar of R32 loom over a bar of R28, dressing a R4 difference as a canyon. Also watch axes with uneven jumps, picture symbols whose AREA balloons when only the value grew, and line graphs with absent months shoved together. The defence never changes: read the numbers off the axes and quote them against the image — "the gap is R4, though the drawing makes it look more than double."

Honestly graphed, the tuck-shop decision writes itself: weekly spending clusters from R20 to R60, so a R25 combo will sell. The last questions of this part are before you now: establish the scale before the bars, and check where the axis begins.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same survey, seen from a desk in the Kimberley classroom, one careful step at a time.

## Subtopic: Asking the Right People the Right Question

Begin with a question a calculator could settle. "Do learners waste money at the tuck shop?" — no. Waste, says who? "How many rand a week does a learner here spend at the tuck shop?" — yes. Group, quantity, unit: all present.

Who gets asked? The dream is everybody — all 900 learners. That is a census, and it is flawless and completely impractical before the deadline. So you ask a sample, a small crowd standing in for the school — and the one rule of the game is that the small crowd must be the school shrunk down. Every grade, both sexes, boarders and day scholars.

The lazy trap is asking whoever happens to be close — your own grade 11 circle at break. Grade 11s hold more money than grade 8s, so every answer leans rich. That lean has a name: bias — the sample whispering a different number than the population would. The remedy is chance: pull 50 names blind from the register, or walk the alphabetical list taking every eighteenth learner. Random means each learner had an equal shot, and nobody's convenience did the choosing.

Then pick the asking tool. A questionnaire: cheap, covers all 50, but a few forms return empty or silly. An interview: you can clarify the question, but it burns time and your tone can push answers around. Choose the tool AND state the reason — the reason is the mark. Some questions on this section are with you right now: find who was asked, then ask who was left out.

## Subtopic: Fifty Answers into One Small Table

Fifty slips return and it is confetti — R30, R45, R10, R55, R25 — no order, no message. The tally table digests the confetti one slip at a time.

Because amounts run from zero to R100, you do not tally individual amounts — you build bins. R0 to under R20. R20 to under R40. R40 to under R60. R60 to under R80. R80 and up. Read each slip ONCE, stroke its bin, and lay every fifth stroke across the previous four so the later counting is counting parcels of five.

Two rules keep bins trustworthy. Every amount needs exactly one address — that is why a bin says "to under R40" while the next opens AT R40; write "R20 to R40" beside "R40 to R60" and the R40 spender owns two addresses, the exact slip markers hunt for. And keep every bin the same width, like matching drawers in one cabinet.

The class's strokes settle at 6, 14, 18, 8 and 4. First move, always: add them — 50. All fifty slips landed, none lost, none doubled. That small sum is a mark sitting on the table.

Now the table speaks. Most of the school spends between R20 and R60 a week; only a few pass R80. The pile of slips could never say that. The fee you paid: exact numbers are gone — the table knows four learners spent R80 or more, not that one of them spent R100. Shape bought, detail sold; give both halves when asked. Questions on this section are coming your way now — bins that never overlap, and a total that lands on 50.

## Subtopic: Three Middles, One Spread, and a Graph with a Trick

Eleven friends, sorted spending: 10, 15, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 100. Three different "typical" numbers are hiding in that line.

The mean is the share-out: pool every rand — R385 — and deal it out evenly: R35 each. The median is the queue: stand everyone in order and tap the middle shoulder — the sixth person, R30. The mode is the crowd's favourite: R15, the only amount seen twice. And the range is no middle at all — it is the distance from smallest to biggest, 100 minus 10, R90 across.

Why do mean and median fall out? Look at the R100 friend. The mean shook hands with that R100 — every rand enters the pot, so one lavish spender lifts the share-out. The median never met the R100; it counts positions in the queue, and the queue's midpoint is indifferent to how rich the last person is. So when a big outlier lurks, the median tells the truer "typical" story — and WRITING that reason, not merely the numbers, is where the marks live.

Final stop: the pictures. Bars standing together compare groups; a line tells a story through time; drifting dots reveal partnerships between quantities. Before trusting any of them, run the axis check: where does the vertical axis START? A graph opening at R25 makes R32 dwarf R28 like a giraffe over a goat — a R4 gap staged as a rout. Read the true numbers off the axis and say them aloud; a picture cannot fool a reader who quotes it. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: sort, share, queue, and inspect the bottom of every axis before believing the top.
