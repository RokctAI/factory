**Bianca:** Class questions before we close! First, the evergreen one: when do we use the mean and when do we use the median? Everyone can calculate both, and then the moment a question asks which one 'best represents' the data, the room goes quiet.

**Tutor:** The deciding question is always the same: does this dataset carry outliers — a few values far from the pack? If it does, the median is the better representative, because the median is selected by position in the sorted queue and no extreme value can shift it; the mean, on the other hand, pours every rand into one pot, so a single enormous value drags it toward itself. Our eleven spenders demonstrate it precisely: mean R35, median R30, and the entire quarrel is manufactured by the lone R100 spender. Take that one learner out and the mean tumbles to R28,50 while the median barely stirs — that steadiness is what "resistant to outliers" means. When the data sits tight and symmetrical with no extremes, the two averages nearly coincide and the mean is a fine choice — it uses every value, a genuine virtue when every value can be trusted. In an assessment, never answer with a bare name. The full answer is the name PLUS the mechanism: "the median, because the R100 outlier inflates the mean, and the median is unaffected by extreme values." Salaries, house prices and spending data almost always drag a long tail of large values behind them — which is exactly why serious reports quote median income rather than mean income.

**Bianca:** Next: bias keeps costing marks because people describe it vaguely — they write 'the sample is unfair' and score nothing. What does a complete bias answer look like?

**Tutor:** A complete bias answer names three things: WHO is over- or under-represented, WHY the collection method caused it, and WHICH DIRECTION the result tilts. "Unfair" earns nothing because it is a feeling, not a mechanism. Compare: "surveying grade 11 classmates at break over-represents seniors, who carry more spending money than grade 8s, so the survey will OVERSTATE the school's average weekly spending." Every clause is earning its keep: the group, the cause, the direction. Learn the standard bias machines by sight. Convenience sampling — asking whoever is within reach — over-represents people who resemble the asker. Voluntary response — a suggestion box in the foyer — over-represents the passionate, because the indifferent never walk to boxes. Timing bias — surveying outside the tuck shop at break — nets spenders and misses savers. Leading questions — "how much do you WASTE on snacks?" — push answers down, because nobody confesses to waste. The repair always comes from one family: define the population, list its members, and let chance choose — a blind draw from the register, or every eighteenth name. When asked to improve a survey, diagnose the specific bias first and then prescribe the specific repair; a repair without a diagnosis is half marks at best.

**Bianca:** This one started an argument at the tally table: why must the intervals say 'R20 to under R40' instead of just 'R20 to R40'? It feels like fussy wording for the same thing.

**Tutor:** It is the difference between a table that works and a table that double-counts, and the test case is one learner: the one who spends exactly R40. Under sloppy intervals — "R20 to R40" next to "R40 to R60" — that learner qualifies for both rows. Two people tallying the same fifty slips would produce different tables; the frequencies might total 51 with someone counted twice, or the learner might vanish entirely while each tallier assumes the other row claimed them. "R20 to under R40" shuts the door properly: R39,99 lives here, R40,00 lives next door, and no value on earth holds two addresses or none. That is also why your first act after tallying is the total check — 6 plus 14 plus 18 plus 8 plus 4 must land exactly on 50, and if it lands on 49 or 51, an overlap or a gap in the intervals is the first suspect. The principle carries names worth writing in an answer: intervals must be mutually exclusive — no value fits two — and exhaustive — every value fits one. Deliver those two words with the R40 example attached and both the wording mark and the reasoning mark arrive together.

**Bianca:** Last one: the misleading-graph questions feel like a riddle — the graph IS the data, so how can it lie? People are unsure what they are actually being asked to do.

**Tutor:** The numbers are true while the impression is false — that is the whole trick, and your task is to separate the two in writing. The commonest weapon is the truncated axis: open the vertical scale at R25 instead of zero, and a bar for R32 stands seven units tall while a bar for R28 stands three. The eye announces "more than double"; the data says "R4 apart, about fourteen percent." Your answer performs that exact translation: read the true values off the axis, state the honest difference, and name the device — "the vertical axis begins at R25, which inflates a R4 difference into a visual ratio of seven to three." Keep a mental checklist of the other regulars. Scales that jump unevenly — gridlines stepping 10, 20, 50. Picture graphs where the symbol grows in height AND width, so doubling the value quadruples the printed area. Line graphs with missing months squeezed together, making a slow drift look like a plunge. The follow-up task is usually two-part: explain how the graph misleads, then present the data honestly — and the honest presentation is always the same move, an axis from zero with equal steps, or a plain sentence quoting the real figures. Data cannot defend itself; your quoting of the axes is its defence.
