**Bianca:** Class questions before we wrap up! The one that came up most: on the municipal statement, does VAT get calculated before or after the basic charge goes on? Two people got two different totals.

**Tutor:** VAT always comes last, and it applies to everything the municipality is charging for the service — consumption charge and basic charge together. So the order never changes: consumption times tariff first, then add the basic charge for the subtotal, and only then take fifteen percent of that entire subtotal. In our worked example, 285 units at R2,40 gave R684, the basic charge lifted it to R844, and the VAT of R126,60 was fifteen percent of the full R844, not of the R684 on its own. If you tax the consumption alone and bolt the basic charge on afterwards, the basic charge has quietly escaped the tax and your total lands R24 short. A dependable habit is to rule a line beneath everything the supplier charges, label it subtotal, and let VAT act on that line only. The one variation to watch is a document showing a figure already marked VAT inclusive — the fifteen percent is inside it already, and adding VAT a second time is double taxation, a mistake markers spot instantly.

**Bianca:** Next one: fixed versus variable caused arguments, because taxi fare feels completely fixed — the same R16,00 every single trip. Why does the lesson insist it is variable?

**Tutor:** Because the test is never whether the expense repeats — it is whether the month's total is guaranteed before the month starts. Rent is R4 500 in a 28-day month and in a 31-day month, home or away — the landlord's figure never moves, so it is fixed. The taxi charges R16,00 a trip, true, but nobody promises you the number of trips: a public holiday deletes two, a week of flu deletes ten, a lift from a colleague deletes two more, and April's total looks nothing like October's. The final amount depends on how the month actually unfolds, and that dependence is the definition of variable. The same logic sorts income: a salary is fixed because the contract names the amount; wages are variable because the hours shift; commission is variable because the sales shift. Occasional is the third drawer, for anything with no schedule — a uniform, a funeral contribution, a gift. In an assessment, attach the reason to the label: variable, because the amount depends on the number of trips actually taken. The label by itself is usually only half the mark.

**Bianca:** This one split the class: for the washing-machine loan, someone calculated fourteen percent of R6 500, got R910, and submitted that as the answer for the whole two-year loan. Where does the times two come in, and why is that not charging twice?

**Tutor:** The R910 is the rent on the money for a single year. Simple interest charges that identical rent again for every additional year the debt survives, because the calculation always looks back at the original R6 500 and never at anything accumulated. Year one costs R910, year two costs another R910 — two years of borrowing, two payments of rent, so 910 times 2 gives R1 820. That is not charging twice, because each R910 buys a different year of the lender's patience. Charging twice would be working year two on R7 410 instead of R6 500 — interest on interest — and that is a different arrangement called compound interest, which you will study properly later in the year. Simple interest never does it. So the working always carries three factors: principal, rate as a decimal, time in years — 6 500 times 0,14 times 2. Drop the time and you have priced one year only; skip the decimal conversion and you have priced nonsense. Then add the interest back onto the principal, because the favourite closing question is the total repaid, R8 320, not the interest on its own.

**Bianca:** Last question: someone's budget expenses came out bigger than their income, so they wrote the negative number and moved on. What does a full-marks answer to a deficit actually contain?

**Tutor:** Three ingredients: the number, the name, and the consequence. First show the subtraction honestly — income of R12 500 minus expenditure of R13 150 gives negative R650, and recording it as negative is correct arithmetic. Second, name it in words: the household has a deficit of R650, because deficit and surplus are the exact terminology being assessed. Third — the ingredient that separates full marks — explain what the deficit means and what can be done: a deficit means the month cannot be funded from the month's income, so the gap must come out of savings or out of borrowing, and borrowing stacks interest on top. Then point at the budget lines that can genuinely move. Fixed expenses will not budge this month — the rent does not negotiate — so the suggestion must come from the variable lines: cut airtime, trim the grocery basket, walk one of the taxi legs. Writing simply earn a bigger salary is not a budget adjustment and earns nothing. What is being tested is whether you can read a budget the way a household must: not as a subtraction exercise, but as a decision about which numbers sit within your own power to change.
