# Part 1 — Expert

Money leaves a paper trail through every South African home: the municipal statement in the letterbox, a payslip on the twenty-fifth, a till slip folded into a pocket, a credit agreement with terms nobody reads twice. This session teaches you to read that trail properly — naming the terminology, auditing the arithmetic line by line, separating income from expenditure — and then walks one step further, to the simple interest that reveals what borrowed money truly costs. Our setting is a single household in Mamelodi: a salary, part-time wages, a jewellery side hustle, a municipal statement and a loan for a washing machine.

## Subtopic: Reading a Municipal Account

A FINANCIAL DOCUMENT is any record, on paper or on a screen, of money owed, paid or earned: municipal statements, till slips, payslips, bank statements, invoices and quotations. Two skills are assessed here — recognising the vocabulary on the document, and verifying its calculations one line at a time.

The municipal account carries the richest vocabulary, so we start there. The ACCOUNT NUMBER identifies the property being billed. The BILLING PERIOD is the month the charges cover. CONSUMPTION is the quantity of electricity or water used, calculated by subtracting the previous meter reading from the current reading. The TARIFF is the price of one unit — one kilowatt-hour of electricity, one kilolitre of water. ARREARS are amounts left unpaid from previous months, and the DUE DATE is the final day to pay before interest or disconnection follows.

Now audit this month's electricity charge. The household consumed 285 kWh at a tariff of R2,40 per kWh: 285 × 2,40 = R684,00. Add the basic charge of R160 — a fixed fee owed even in a month of zero consumption — and the subtotal reaches R844,00. Then VAT, value-added tax, at 15%: 844 × 0,15 = R126,60. The final amount due is 844 + 126,60 = R970,60.

That skeleton — consumption times tariff, plus fixed charge, plus 15% VAT — is the skeleton of nearly every utility account in the country, and questions routinely blank out one line and ask you to restore it. Work down the statement in order, and when a question supplies only the VAT-inclusive total, divide by 1,15 to travel back to the pre-VAT amount: 970,60 ÷ 1,15 = R844,00. The questions for this section are with you now; find each line on the document first, then let the calculator confirm it.

## Subtopic: Income: Fixed, Variable and Occasional

INCOME is money flowing in, and it is classified three ways. FIXED income arrives in an identical amount at regular intervals: a monthly SALARY, rent collected from a tenant, a social grant. VARIABLE income arrives regularly but in shifting amounts: WAGES paid by the hour or the day, COMMISSION earned as a percentage of sales, the takings of a small business. OCCASIONAL income has no timetable at all: a gift, a bonus, an inheritance.

Our household demonstrates all three. Sipho earns a fixed salary of R11 200 a month as a warehouse supervisor. Naledi works part-time at R32,00 per hour; a 38-hour week pays 32,00 × 38 = R1 216, but a shorter week pays less — the moving total is exactly what variable means. She also strings beaded jewellery and receives commission of 4% on whatever a craft market stall sells for her: on December sales of R38 500, commission = 0,04 × 38 500 = R1 540. In March the stall sold nothing and the commission was R0 — variable again, and a caution against building a budget on the best month ever recorded.

A business sorts its income into the same three drawers under different names: SALES are the main income and usually variable; DONATIONS and GRANTS are fixed if pledged monthly, occasional if once-off.

Two habits earn the marks. First, classify before you calculate — the label plus a reason is what the question rewards. Second, when totalling a month's income, enter variable income at a realistic level, never at its record high. The questions for this section are ready now; place every income source in its drawer and defend the placement in a phrase.

## Subtopic: Expenditure and the Household Budget

EXPENDITURE is money flowing out, and the same three categories apply. FIXED expenses repeat in the same amount: rent, an insurance premium, a stokvel contribution, school fees. VARIABLE expenses repeat but change size: groceries, electricity, airtime, taxi fares. OCCASIONAL expenses appear irregularly: a January school uniform, a dentist's bill, a funeral contribution.

A BUDGET is a written plan setting expected income against expected expenditure. Build the household's month. Income: Sipho's salary R11 200 plus Naledi's earnings averaging R2 800 — total R14 000. Expenditure: rent R4 500; taxi fares at R16,00 a trip, two trips a day across 21 working days, which is 16,00 × 42 = R672; groceries R3 200; electricity R950; airtime and data R380; stokvel contribution R600; school costs R750. Total expenditure = 4 500 + 672 + 3 200 + 950 + 380 + 600 + 750 = R11 052.

Income minus expenditure: 14 000 − 11 052 = R2 948. A positive result is a SURPLUS — money available for saving or for paying down debt. A negative result is a DEFICIT, and a deficit budget is a written plan to borrow, which is why questions ask you to nominate a variable expense to trim.

Watch the taxi line closely: it repeats every working day and feels fixed, yet it is variable — one public holiday or one lift from a colleague moves the total. The test for fixed is whether the AMOUNT is guaranteed in advance, not whether the expense recurs. Answer budget questions in full sentences: the household runs a surplus of R2 948, so a washing-machine instalment near R347 is affordable. Your questions for this section arrive now; total with care, label every line, and write surplus or deficit in words.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest: the Price of Borrowed Money

Borrowed money always carries a price, and the plainest version of that price is SIMPLE INTEREST: interest computed each year on the ORIGINAL amount alone. That original amount is the PRINCIPAL. In words, the formula reads: one year's interest equals principal times the rate; total simple interest equals principal times rate times the number of years.

The household finances a washing machine with a loan of R6 500 at 14% per year simple interest, repaid over 2 years. One year's interest: 6 500 × 0,14 = R910. Two years: 910 × 2 = R1 820. The full repayment is principal plus interest: 6 500 + 1 820 = R8 320. Divided into 24 equal monthly instalments: 8 320 ÷ 24 = R346,67 a month, with the last instalment trimmed by a few cents. A machine priced at R6 500 genuinely costs R8 320 — the interest is the fee for spending money you do not yet have.

Simple interest also pays the saver. Naledi deposits R4 000 into an account earning 7,5% per year simple interest: each year adds 4 000 × 0,075 = R300, so three years add R900 and the balance stands at R4 900.

Three disciplines protect the marks. Convert the percentage before multiplying — 14% becomes 0,14, and writing 6 500 × 14 earns nothing. Match the rate's time unit: a rate PER YEAR demands time in years, so 30 months is 2,5 years, never 30. And close with the total, principal plus interest, stated in a sentence — because the real question behind every loan is the whole cost. The last questions of this part are with you now: principal times rate times time, then the honest total.

## Subtopic: The Envelope from the Municipality

The municipal statement intimidates because it is busy, but strip it down and it only ever answers three questions: how much you used, what that usage costs, and by when you must pay.

Begin with usage. The meter was read last month and read again this month; the gap between the readings is your consumption — the electricity that actually ran through the wires. This month that was 285 units. Every unit costs R2,40, so 285 times 2,40 is R684. Then comes the basic charge of R160, the fee for the connection itself — switch everything off for a month and it still arrives. Together that makes R844.

Then the taxman's slice: VAT, fifteen percent, added to almost everything you buy. Fifteen percent of R844 is R126,60, and the bottom line lands on R970,60. When a question hands you only that bottom line and asks for the amount before VAT, resist subtracting fifteen percent — divide by 1,15 instead, and R970,60 returns to R844.

Two last words and the document holds no secrets. Arrears is last month's unpaid balance hitching a ride on this month's statement — settle it first, because it attracts interest. Due date is the final safe day; after it, the account starts billing you for lateness. Some questions on this section reach you right now — locate every line on the document before any calculation begins.

## Subtopic: Money In, Money Out

Picture the household as a water tank. Income flows in through the inlet; expenses drip out through the taps. A budget is nothing more than an honest reading of both flows, taken before the month begins.

The inflow: Sipho's salary is the same R11 200 every month — fixed, solid ground to build on. Naledi's wages follow her hours, and her jewellery commission paid R1 540 in December and nothing in March — variable, so plan around an ordinary month, never the best one. A birthday gift from an aunt is occasional — welcome, but never something to budget on.

The outflow sorts identically. Rent, stokvel, school fees: fixed, the same figure every month. Groceries, electricity, airtime, taxi fare: variable — the taxi runs every workday and feels fixed, but one public holiday shifts the total, so variable it is. The new uniform in January: occasional, and it catches every family that never set money aside for it.

Now the honest arithmetic. In: R14 000. Out: rent 4 500, taxis 672, groceries 3 200, electricity 950, airtime 380, stokvel 600, school 750 — R11 052. The difference is 14 000 minus 11 052: R2 948. Money left over is a surplus; falling short is a deficit, and a deficit means borrowing, which always costs extra. Questions on this section are coming your way now — add up the taps, compare with the inlet, and say in a sentence whether the tank is rising or falling.

## Subtopic: What the Loan Really Costs

The washing machine is R6 500 cash, and the household does not have R6 500 cash. So they borrow, and borrowing carries a price with a name: simple interest.

Simple means the interest is measured against the original R6 500 every single year — the same rent charged on the same borrowed amount, year after year. Fourteen percent of 6 500 is 910. That is year one's interest. Year two: another 910. Two years of borrowing costs 2 times 910, which is R1 820.

So the lender gets back the original R6 500 plus the R1 820, which is R8 320. Split across the 24 months of the agreement, the instalment is about R347 a month. Carry both numbers into the shop: the sticker says R6 500, the truth says R8 320. That gap of R1 820 is what fourteen percent per year over two years means in rands, and judging whether the machine is worth it means judging the truth, not the sticker.

Two traps before your questions. The rate must turn into a decimal before it multiplies — fourteen percent is 0,14. And the time must be in years when the rate is per year — a loan of 30 months is 2,5 years. The same machinery runs in your favour when you are the saver: R4 000 banked at 7,5% earns R300 a year, and after three years the bank owes you R4 900. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — principal, times rate, times years, then add it back on and state the full cost out loud.
