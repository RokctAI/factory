# Part 1 — Expert

A number pattern is a list of numbers changing by a rule, and Mathematical Literacy asks one thing above everything: find the rule, then use it. Tariffs climbing by a fixed amount, shares tripling, stacked rows growing by one more each time — assessments dress patterns in real situations and reward you for seeing straight through the costume. This session builds the three families of pattern you will meet, the difference-row test that identifies them in seconds, and the stacked-rows question that reliably separates prepared candidates from the rest in final exams: rows of objects growing by one each row, and the total demanded under time pressure.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Number Patterns

Every pattern question in this subject belongs to one of three families, and naming the family is half the marks.

Family one: constant difference. Each term comes from adding the same amount to the term before. The list 17; 25; 33; 41 climbs by 8 at every step. Real life supplies these endlessly: a shuttle fee growing by the same amount per kilometre, a savings account fed an identical deposit every month, a pile of crates where each crate adds the same height.

Family two: constant ratio. Each term comes from multiplying the term before by the same factor. The list 4; 12; 36; 108 triples at every step — the ratio between consecutive terms is 3. Real life again: a video reaching three times as many viewers each day, bacteria multiplying, a chain message forwarded to the same number of friends by every receiver.

Family three: neither. The steps themselves change — but they change with their own smaller pattern. The classic example is 1; 3; 6; 10, where the steps run 2, then 3, then 4: a growing step. These are the patterns of triangles and stacks: one object in the first row, two in the second, three in the third, and the running total climbing row by row.

The vocabulary you are expected to command: the numbers in the list are terms; a term's position matters — first term, second term and so on; and consecutive terms means terms standing side by side. A question saying describe the pattern in words wants the family plus the number: add 8 to each term; multiply each term by 3; the amount added grows by one each time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three families, an everyday example of each, and the meaning of term and consecutive.

## Subtopic: The Difference Row — Your First Test

Here is the method that settles most pattern questions before panic gets a word in. Write the terms in a row with space between them. Under each gap, write the difference: second term minus first, third minus second, onwards. That difference row is a diagnosis.

Run it on 17; 25; 33; 41. The differences: 25 minus 17 is 8; 33 minus 25 is 8; 41 minus 33 is 8. A constant difference of 8 — family one confirmed. Continuing is now mechanical: 41 plus 8 gives 49; 49 plus 8 gives 57. A missing middle term is no harder: if the list reads 17; 25; blank; 41, the blank must be 25 plus 8, which is 33 — then check it from the far side, 41 minus 8 is 33. Both directions agree, so the answer stands.

Assessments rarely leave a pattern naked; they wrap it in a tariff. An airport shuttle charges R17 for the first kilometre and R8 for every kilometre after that, and the list 17; 25; 33; 41 becomes the cost of one, two, three, four kilometres. The rule in words: each extra kilometre adds R8. The question then wants the cost of six kilometres, so extend the row: 49; 57. Six kilometres costs R57. Two habits protect these marks. First, answer inside the story — R57 with the rand sign, never a naked 57. Second, count positions on your fingers as you extend: extending perfectly and stopping one term early is a mark thrown away.

When the difference row is not constant, never force it. That is the signal to test the other two families — and that test comes next.

Work this section's questions before moving on: the difference row on a clean list, the missing middle term checked from both sides, and the tariff wrapped around the pattern.

## Subtopic: Constant Ratio — When Patterns Multiply

Some lists fail the difference test on purpose. Run it on 4; 12; 36; 108: the differences are 8, then 24, then 72 — not constant, and themselves tripling. That behaviour in the difference row is the fingerprint of family two. Confirm with the ratio test: divide each term by the one before. 12 divided by 4 is 3; 36 divided by 12 is 3; 108 divided by 36 is 3. A constant ratio of 3.

Continuing a ratio pattern is multiplication: 108 times 3 gives 324; 324 times 3 gives 972. In words: multiply each term by 3 to get the next term. Growth stories live here. A dance clip is watched by 4 people on day one, and each day the audience triples: by day four, 108 new viewers that day; by day five, 324. Or algae on a dam triples its patch every week — the drama being how sleepy it looks early and how violently it finishes. From 4 to 108 took three triplings; two more take it past 900. Multiplication patterns start on their knees and end through the roof — a sentence worth writing in any interpretation answer.

Hold the two families apart with one contrast: constant difference grows by the same amount each step; constant ratio grows by the same factor. A tariff adding R8 per kilometre will never explode; a chain that triples will never stay polite. When a question asks which grows faster in the long run, the ratio pattern always wins eventually — and writing eventually is what earns the reasoning mark, because over the first few terms the adding pattern may well be in front.

Take the questions for this section now: the ratio test performed, the pattern continued by multiplying, and the same-amount against same-factor contrast.

## Subtopic: Growing Patterns and the Stacked-Rows Question

Now the family that final exams use to separate the prepared from the hopeful. A display is built from rows of cups: one cup in the first row, and each row below holds one more cup than the row above. Eight rows in all. How many cups altogether?

Diagnose first. Cups per row run 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6; 7; 8 — constant difference of 1, easy. But the question wants the total, and the running totals run 1; 3; 6; 10; 15; 21; 28; 36. Difference row on the totals: 2; 3; 4; 5; 6; 7; 8 — growing by one each time. Family three: the steps themselves form a pattern. These totals are the triangular numbers, the numbers stacking always produces.

The safe method is the table. Two columns: row number, and total so far. Row one, total 1. Row two, 1 plus 2 is 3. Row three, 3 plus 3 is 6. Row four, 6 plus 4 is 10. Row five, 10 plus 5 is 15. Row six, 15 plus 6 is 21. Row seven, 21 plus 7 is 28. Row eight, 28 plus 8 is 36. The display uses 36 cups. The table is slower than a formula and immune to nerves — every line is one small addition, and the final line carries the answer with its position attached.

There is also an elegant shortcut for adding one up to eight, worth knowing as a check. Pair the ends: 1 plus 8 is 9; 2 plus 7 is 9; 3 plus 6 is 9; 4 plus 5 is 9. Four pairs of nine, 4 × 9 = 36. Same answer down a different road — which is exactly what a checking habit looks like.

Answer in the story's units: 36 cups. And read what the question counts: cups in the eighth row is 8; cups in all eight rows is 36. The single biggest mark-loser on this question type is answering the row when the total was asked.

Pause for this section's questions: the two lists told apart, the table built to row eight, the pairing check, and the row-against-total trap.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same three families again, from the taxi rank, the group chat and the shop shelf — no new maths, only familiar clothes.

## Subtopic: The Same Step Every Time

Picture climbing a staircase where every step is the same height. After the first step you know the whole staircase: each step lifts you the same amount, so climbing is just adding, over and over. That is a constant difference pattern — the steady staircase of numbers.

Money climbs this staircase all day long. An airport shuttle wants R17 for the first kilometre and R8 more for each one after: 17; 25; 33; 41. A bottle deposit refunded per bottle, a phone paid off weekly in equal amounts, loyalty points dripping in equally — steady staircases, every one. The test is a single move: subtract neighbours. Twenty-five minus seventeen, eight. Thirty-three minus twenty-five, eight. The moment the gaps agree you own the step, and the future writes itself: 41 plus 8 is 49, then 57.

Two street-smart habits close the deal. Habit one: when a term is missing in the middle, walk to it from both sides — up from the term before, down from the term after — and trust only the answer both walks agree on. Habit two: keep count of your steps out loud. If the question wants the sixth term, count six positions on your fingers as you go. The pattern never betrays you; miscounting does.

And keep the story's units. If the numbers are rands, the answer is R57, written with its sign. An answer without units is a staircase with no floor at the top.

The questions for this section are up now: the staircase test, the both-sides walk to a missing term, and the finger-count to term six. Take them gently — this is the friendliest family of the three.

## Subtopic: The Pattern That Doubles Like a Rumour

Now a different creature. A dance clip reaches 4 people on the first day. Each viewer shows enough friends that the next day reaches triple: 12, then 36, then 108. Nobody is adding here — the clip multiplies. Divide any day's number by the day before and the answer is always 3. That fixed multiplier is the constant ratio, the second family's signature.

Feel how differently it moves. The staircase pattern added 8 forever, politely. The clip started smaller than the staircase — 4 against 17 — and for a while it trails. But tripling compounds: 324, then 972. The staircase is still trudging through 49, 57, 65 while the clip has left the province. Whenever a question asks which plan overtakes, which spreads faster, which grows fastest in the long run — the multiplying pattern wins in the end, and your written reason is exactly that: it multiplies by the same factor each step, while the other only adds the same amount.

The test order to run under pressure: subtract neighbours first. Gaps equal? Constant difference; done. Gaps unequal but swelling suspiciously? Divide neighbours. Answers equal? Constant ratio; done. The division is the same one you use to check data bundles — how many times does the old number fit into the new — and the moment it repeats, the multiplier is caught.

Your questions for this section are ready: the clip's ratio caught by division, the slow-start-fast-finish behaviour, and the test order that never leaves you guessing.

## Subtopic: Cans at the Shop and the Growing Step

Picture the pyramid of cans stacked at the supermarket entrance. One can on top. Two in the row below. Then three, then four — every row one can wider than the row above. Eight rows down, the display is finished. The stacker's question is the assessment's question: how many cans came off the storeroom trolley?

The rows themselves are simple — 1; 2; 3; 4; 5; 6; 7; 8. But the total is a different list: after one row, 1 can; after two rows, 3; after three, 6; then 10; 15; 21; 28; 36. Look at the gaps between the totals: 2, then 3, then 4, then 5 — the step grows by one every time, because every new row is one can longer. This is the third family: not a broken pattern, a growing one. The steps stand on their own little staircase.

The everyday method is the running total, exactly as the stacker counts. Say it while you build: one. Plus two, three. Plus three, six. Plus four, ten. Plus five, fifteen. Plus six, twenty-one. Plus seven, twenty-eight. Plus eight, thirty-six. Thirty-six cans off the trolley. Written as a two-column table — row number, cans so far — it is bulletproof, and the marker can watch every mark being earned.

Want the show-off check? Pair the rows from the outside in: the top row of 1 with the bottom row of 8 makes 9; rows 2 and 7 make 9; rows 3 and 6 make 9; rows 4 and 5 make 9. Four pairs of 9: 36 again. Two roads, one answer — that is certainty, not luck.

Last warning, and it is the one that costs real marks: the eighth row holds 8 cans, but the display holds 36. Read the question's final line slowly — a row, or the whole stack? — and give the number the story asked for, with its unit: 36 cans.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the growing step spotted in the gaps, the running total said out loud to row eight, the pairs-of-nine check, and the row-or-stack decision. Answer them at your own pace, and this family joins the other two in your pocket.
