# Part 1 — Expert

Every tariff sheet, wage slip and price list in this subject conceals a rule. This session takes a table of real rand amounts, digs the rule out of it, writes that rule as a formula, draws it as a graph, and then uses it to answer the question a real person is actually asking: what will this cost me, and what can I afford? Grade 11 works with four relationships — constant difference, inverse proportion, constant ratio, and combinations of these — and the marks go to the learner who can name which one is on the page and show the substitution that follows.

## Subtopic: Naming the Variables and Finding the Rule

Start with the two variables, because every later step leans on telling them apart. The INDEPENDENT variable is the one you choose: hours booked, litres bought, kilometres driven, people in the vehicle. The DEPENDENT variable is the one that follows from the choice — almost always the amount you pay. Say it as a sentence before touching a calculator: the cost depends on the number of hours. On a graph the independent variable takes the horizontal axis and the dependent variable the vertical axis. This gets asked directly, because swapping them plots the entire graph the wrong way round.

Now a real table. A plumber working in Bloemfontein charges a call-out fee of R400 and then R260 for every hour on site. One hour costs R660, two hours R920, three hours R1 180, four hours R1 440.

Test the table the way every table gets tested: subtract each value from the one after it. R920 minus R660 is R260. R1 180 minus R920 is R260. R1 180 to R1 440 is R260 again. The difference is CONSTANT, so the relationship is linear and its graph is a straight line. A constant difference always means the same amount added for each extra unit — here, the hourly rate.

Two numbers now assemble the formula. The constant difference R260 is the rate per hour. The starting value is the cost of zero hours — not zero rand, but R400, the call-out fee, charged before a single spanner turns. So: Cost in rand equals 400 plus 260 multiplied by the number of hours. Substitute to check: three hours gives 400 plus 260 times 3, which is 400 plus 780, which is R1 180 — the table agrees, so the formula is sound.

Three habits earn marks here and cost nothing: write the formula down before substituting, keep the rand sign and the unit hours attached to the numbers, and finish with a sentence — the plumber's bill for three hours is R1 180. The questions for this section are with you now; give attention to which variable is independent, and to testing the differences before naming the relationship.

## Subtopic: When the Difference Is Not Constant

Not every table adds the same amount. When the differences refuse to settle, do not abandon the table — run two more tests, for a constant PRODUCT and for a constant RATIO.

First, inverse proportion. Six friends hire a bakkie and driver from Queenstown for R2 400 for the day and split the cost equally. One person pays R2 400. Two pay R1 200 each. Three pay R800. Four pay R600. Five pay R480. Six pay R400. The differences run R1 200, R400, R200, R120, R80 — nothing constant there. But multiply each pair: 1 times 2 400, 2 times 1 200, 3 times 800, 4 times 600 — every product is 2 400. That constant product is the signature of INVERSE PROPORTION: as one quantity rises, the other falls in exact proportion. The formula is cost per person equals 2 400 divided by the number of people. Its graph is a smooth curve dropping steeply then flattening, approaching the axes without ever touching them — because however many people climb in, each still pays something above zero.

Second, constant ratio between consecutive terms. A stokvel in Botshabelo holds R5 000 in an account paying 6% a year, left untouched. After one year the balance is R5 300. After two years R5 618. After three years R5 955,08. The differences grow — R300, then R318, then R337,08 — so it is not linear. But divide each value by the one before: 5 300 divided by 5 000 is 1,06; 5 618 divided by 5 300 is 1,06 again. The RATIO is constant, and the rule is multiply by 1,06 each year. Constant-ratio relationships climb slowly at first and then steeply — that upward curve is why money left alone for years grows far faster in the later years than the earlier ones.

Build the tests into a routine you can run under pressure. Differences constant? Linear. Products constant? Inverse proportion. Ratios constant? Constant ratio. None of the three? Hunt for a combination, which the fourth section handles. Your questions for this section are ready; keep the three tests in that order, and justify the choice with actual arithmetic, never a guess from the shape.

## Subtopic: One Relationship, Three Representations

A relationship wears three costumes, and questions switch between them without warning. The FORMULA or equation is the rule in symbols or words. The TABLE is a list of matched input and output values. The GRAPH is the picture. All three describe the same relationship, and a complete answer can travel from any one to the others.

From formula to table: substitute chosen inputs. From the bakkie rule, eight passengers give 2 400 divided by 8, which is R300 each. From table to graph: plot each pair as a point, independent variable across, dependent variable up, then join the points only if the quantity between them means something. That is the distinction between DISCRETE and CONTINUOUS data, and it is assessed. Passengers in a bakkie are discrete — there is no such thing as 3,5 people — so the points are plotted but never joined. Hours of plumbing are continuous, since a plumber can work an hour and a half, so those points are joined with a straight line.

From graph back to numbers: to read a value, go up from the horizontal axis to the line, then straight across to the vertical axis, and read the amount. Always state the units you read. And label everything — both axes with their quantity and unit, plus a heading — because axis labels carry marks of their own.

The shapes deserve memorising, because they let you audit yourself. A constant-difference relationship is a straight line, lifted above the origin by any fixed starting charge. An inverse proportion is a falling curve that flattens. A constant-ratio relationship is a rising curve that steepens. If your plotted points bend while your table claimed constant differences, the picture has just exposed an arithmetic slip. Turn to the questions for this section now, with special care on discrete against continuous and on reading a graph value correctly.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards on a Combination Tariff

Most real charges combine relationships, and the commonest combination is a fixed charge plus a rate. A municipality in Limpopo bills water at a fixed basic charge of R160,00 per month plus R24,50 for every kilolitre used. The rule: monthly bill equals 160 plus 24,50 times the kilolitres used.

Forwards is routine. A household using 12 kℓ pays 160 plus 24,50 times 12, which is 160 plus 294, giving R454,00. A household using 20 kℓ pays 160 plus 490, giving R650,00.

Backwards is where the marks hide. Suppose the bill reads R699,00 and the household wants to know how much water was used. Undo the rule in reverse order. First remove the fixed charge: 699,00 minus 160,00 leaves R539,00 — the part that actually paid for water. Then divide by the rate: 539,00 divided by 24,50 gives 22. The household used 22 kilolitres. Check by substituting forwards: 160 plus 24,50 times 22 is 160 plus 539, which is R699,00 — confirmed.

That reversal generalises to every combination tariff you will meet: subtract the fixed part first, then divide by the rate. Marks die when the whole bill is divided by the rate — 699 divided by 24,50 gives about 28,5 kℓ, wrong by six and a half kilolitres because the basic charge was never removed.

Two further disciplines. Round only at the end: carry the full value through the working, then round sensibly — to cents for money, to a whole unit where fractions make no sense. And answer in a sentence with its unit: the household used 22 kilolitres of water in that month. A bare number at the foot of a page of correct work is an answer no marker can credit in full. The last questions of this part are in front of you now: expect a combination tariff read forwards, then read backwards, with the fixed charge handled apart from the rate.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rules from the kitchen table, where a price list is not a pattern exercise but a bill somebody must pay.

## Subtopic: The Plumber Who Charges Twice

The moment the plumber steps into your yard, you are already paying. That is the call-out — R400 just for arriving, whether the job takes five minutes or five hours. Then the clock starts, at R260 an hour. So one hour costs R400 plus R260, which is R660. Two hours: R920. Three hours: R1 180. Four: R1 440.

Watch what changes from row to row. R660 to R920 is R260 up. R920 to R1 180 is R260 up. Every single step, the same R260. When a table climbs by the same amount every time, you are looking at the simplest kind of rule: something fixed at the start, then something extra for each unit. Write it the way you would explain it to your aunt: four hundred rand, plus two hundred and sixty rand for every hour.

Those are two different jobs in one sentence, and they behave differently. The R400 sits still. The R260 multiplies. So whenever a price list appears, ask two questions: what do I pay before anything happens, and what does each extra unit add? Answer those and the rule is yours — plumber, taxi, water bill or phone contract.

One more thing worth naming: the hours are what you choose, and the money is what follows. That is why hours run along the bottom of a graph and rands climb the side, never the other way round. Some questions on this section are with you right now; hunt for the fixed part and the per-unit part in every price list.

## Subtopic: Splitting the Bakkie, Growing the Stokvel

Two more rules, and neither adds the same amount each time.

First, the bakkie. Hiring it costs R2 400 for the day, and everyone aboard shares that cost. Alone, you carry the whole R2 400. Bring a friend: R1 200 each. Three of you: R800. Four: R600. Five: R480. Six: R400. Notice how the price per person plunges early and then barely moves. Going from one person to two saves you R1 200; going from five to six saves only R80. The bakkie never gets cheaper — it only gets shared further. Here is the trick that proves it: multiply the people by the price every time — one times 2 400, two times 1 200, three times 800 — and you always land back on R2 400, because the bakkie's price never moved. That is inverse proportion: one goes up, the other comes down, and their product stays put.

Second, the stokvel money. Five thousand rand sits in an account earning 6% a year and nobody touches it. Year one: R5 300. Year two: R5 618. Year three: R5 955,08. The jumps get BIGGER each year — R300, then R318, then R337,08. Why? Because in year two the interest is earned on the R5 300, not just the original R5 000. The rule is not add something — it is multiply by 1,06. The same multiplier, every year, which is why money left alone quietly accelerates.

So when a table refuses to add nicely, do not panic. Try multiplying across each pair and see whether the same total keeps appearing, like the bakkie. Then try dividing each number by the one before it and see whether the same multiplier keeps appearing, like the stokvel. One of the three tests almost always answers. Questions on these two patterns are coming to you now — test with the arithmetic, never with your eyes.

## Subtopic: Reading the Rule Backwards

The last move is the one people find hardest, and it is really just untying your shoes in the right order.

The municipality charges R160 every month before you open a single tap, then R24,50 for every kilolitre — that is a thousand litres. Use 12 kℓ and your bill is R160 plus twelve lots of R24,50, which is R160 plus R294, so R454. Easy enough. Now flip it. The bill arrives at R699 and you want to know how much water the family actually used — because that is the number you can change.

Take the fixed part off first: R699 minus R160 leaves R539. That R539 is pure water. Now share it out at R24,50 per kilolitre: R539 divided by R24,50 gives 22. Twenty-two kilolitres. And check yourself by going forwards again: R160 plus twenty-two lots of R24,50 is R160 plus R539, which is R699 exactly. When the check lands on the number you started with, the worrying can stop.

The mistake to dodge is dividing the whole R699 by R24,50, because that pretends the basic charge bought water. It did not — it bought the pipes, the meter and the connection. Take it off first, every time.

And finish like a person, not a calculator. Do not write 22 and stop. Write: the household used 22 kilolitres of water that month. Keep the rands with the rands, the kilolitres with the kilolitres, and round only at the very end, once the work is done. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — expect a bill read forwards and then, the interesting way, backwards.
