**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how do we actually know which variable is independent when the question does not say? In the plumber example it felt obvious, but on a strange context we froze.

**Tutor:** Use the dependency sentence, and say it out loud in both directions to hear which one is nonsense. The cost depends on the number of hours makes sense; the number of hours depends on the cost does not, because the pipe takes as long as it takes regardless of what you are willing to pay. The one that follows is dependent; the one that is chosen or set is independent. A second cue is control: whoever is doing the buying, driving or using controls the independent variable, and the till or the meter reports the dependent one. A third cue is the formula itself — the variable you substitute INTO the rule is independent, and the value that comes out is dependent. In almost every context this subject offers, the money is dependent: rands come out at the end. Once decided, hold the decision through the whole question, because the graph's axes, the table's columns and your final sentence all rest on it. And when you plot, the independent variable always runs horizontal, the dependent one vertical.

**Bianca:** Next: our table's differences were nearly constant — R260, R260, then R261 in one row. Do we call it linear or not?

**Tutor:** Check your arithmetic first, because in a designed question table the differences are constructed to be exactly constant, and a value one rand out is far more likely a subtraction slip than a real feature of the data. Redo that single subtraction. If it genuinely is one rand out and the table came from real collected data — a household's actual bills, a spaza's actual takings — the honest description is that the relationship is approximately linear, and you say so: the difference is constant at about R260 per unit, so the data lies very close to a straight line. Real-world data is allowed to be slightly untidy; a designed pattern question is not. The distinction matters because your next move differs: for a designed table you may confidently write a formula and predict any value, while for real data you write the formula as a model and treat predictions as estimates. Markers reward that caution when the context is genuine measurement, and reward exactness when the pattern is clean.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: someone said inverse proportion and constant ratio are the same thing because both graphs are curves. Are they?

**Tutor:** They are genuinely different, and the arithmetic separates them cleanly even when the pictures look vaguely alike. In inverse proportion, one quantity climbs while the other falls, and the PRODUCT of the pair stays fixed: six people at R400 and three people at R800 both multiply to R2 400, because the total being shared never changed. Its graph falls, flattening as it goes, and never reaches zero — sharing among more and more people makes each share tiny, but never nothing. In a constant-ratio relationship both input and output rise, and it is the QUOTIENT of consecutive outputs that stays fixed: each year's balance divided by the previous year's gives 1,06 every time. Its graph rises, and rises faster and faster, because each multiplication acts on a bigger number than the last. So one is a fixed total being divided up; the other is a fixed multiplier applied again and again. Test with multiplication for the first and division for the second, and write down which test you used — that sentence is often worth a mark by itself.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: why bother with the graph at all if the formula gives the exact answer anyway?

**Tutor:** Three reasons, and each earns marks. First, a graph answers comparison questions faster and more convincingly than arithmetic: when two tariffs are drawn on the same axes, the crossing point is the usage at which they cost the same, and whichever line sits lower on either side of it tells you instantly which option suits a light user and which a heavy one. Second, the graph audits your working. If your table claimed constant differences but your plotted points bend, one value is wrong and the picture just caught it — free error-detection. Third, questions run in both directions: sometimes you are handed a graph and asked for a value, sometimes handed a formula and asked for a sketch, so the traffic must flow both ways. When you read a value off a graph, go up from the horizontal axis to the line and straight across to the vertical axis, then state the reading with its unit. And label both axes with quantity and unit and give the graph a heading — those labels carry their own marks, and they are the easiest marks in the topic to collect.
