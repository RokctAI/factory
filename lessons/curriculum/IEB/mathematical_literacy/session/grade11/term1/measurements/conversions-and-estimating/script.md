# Part 1 — Expert

A grade 11 measurement question almost never asks for a bare conversion. It hands you a cake recipe written in Fahrenheit, a plumber's fitting sized in inches, a paint tin promising so many square metres per litre, or a bus schedule in the twenty-four-hour clock, and it asks what a person should DO with it. This session builds the four conversion skills that carry this topic — moving within the metric system, crossing between metric and imperial, converting temperature and time, and scaling context rates such as paint coverage and medicine doses — along with the estimating that keeps every answer honest.

## Subtopic: The Metric Ladder and the Marks in Units

The whole metric system stands on one number: a thousand. One metre is 1 000 millimetres; one kilometre is 1 000 metres; one kilogram is 1 000 grams; one tonne is 1 000 kilograms; one litre is 1 000 millilitres; one kilolitre is 1 000 litres. The centimetre is the one exception to file separately: 1 metre is 100 centimetres, and 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres.

Settle the direction before the calculator comes out. Converting to a SMALLER unit means you end up with more of them, so you multiply; converting to a LARGER unit means fewer of them, so you divide. A fun-run route of 3,6 km is 3,6 × 1 000 = 3 600 metres. A 250 g slab of butter is 250 ÷ 1 000 = 0,25 kg. A household billed for 24 kℓ of water used 24 × 1 000 = 24 000 litres. If your converted number moved the wrong way — the kilometre figure coming out larger than the metre figure — you divided where you should have multiplied, and one glance exposes it.

Two assessed habits sit on top of the ladder. First, convert BEFORE you calculate, never in the middle. A room given as 3,8 m by 260 cm must become 3,8 m by 2,6 m before anything is multiplied; mixing units inside one calculation produces answers wrong by factors of a hundred. Second, write the unit on every line of working: a correctly converted value earns credit even when the final answer goes wrong later, but only if the marker can see which unit each number carries.

Estimating lives here too, because it is the free error-check. Keep a handful of anchors: a small fingernail is about a centimetre across, one walking stride is close to a metre, a large cool drink bottle holds two litres, a bag of mealie meal weighs 10 kg or 12,5 kg. A kettle holding 4 000 litres or a schoolbag of 300 kg is an instruction to recheck the working, not a result to submit. The questions for this section are with you now; hold the direction rule — smaller unit means multiply — and keep every unit attached.

## Subtopic: Crossing Into Imperial: Inches, Pounds and Miles

South Africa runs on metric, yet imperial units refuse to leave: television screens and pipe fittings in inches, height in feet, imported groceries labelled in pounds, overseas road signs in miles. Questions always print the conversion factors you need, so the skill being tested is never memory — it is applying the factor in the correct direction and rounding sensibly at the end.

The factors you will meet most often: 1 inch is 2,54 cm; 1 foot is 12 inches, which is 30,48 cm; 1 mile is about 1,609 km; 1 kilogram is about 2,2 pounds; 1 ounce is about 28,35 g.

One example in each direction. A 25 kg bag of maize meal weighs 25 × 2,2 = 55 pounds. Coming back, an imported bag of dog food marked 44 pounds is 44 ÷ 2,2 = 20 kg. A woman described as five foot ten is 70 inches, and 70 × 2,54 = 177,8 cm — call it 1,78 m, a believable height, which is the sense check earning its keep. And a road sign abroad reading 45 miles per hour is 45 × 1,609, which is about 72,4 km/h — a town-driving speed, which again makes sense.

The direction is the entire difficulty. Ask which unit is BIGGER. A kilogram outweighs a pound, so a mass in kilograms becomes a larger NUMBER of pounds: multiply. A mile outstretches a kilometre, so miles become a larger number of kilometres: multiply. Turn either around and you divide. Write the reasoning in one line — one kilogram is more than one pound, so the pounds number must grow — and round only at the very end, to a figure the context can actually use. Your questions for this section are ready now; declare the direction of every conversion before touching it.

## Subtopic: Temperature, Time and Timetables

Temperature refuses a single multiplier, because the two scales do not begin at the same place. Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by 9, divide by 5, then add 32. Fahrenheit to Celsius: subtract the 32 first, then multiply by 5 and divide by 9.

An overseas recipe wants the oven at 400 °F. Subtract 32 to get 368. Multiply by 5 to get 1 840. Divide by 9 to get 204,44 °C — so a South African oven goes to 200 °C, the closest setting on the dial. In the other direction, a feverish child at 39 °C is, in Fahrenheit, 39 × 9 = 351, divided by 5 = 70,2, plus 32 = 102,2 °F. The order is fixed: moving the 32 to the wrong end of the calculation is the most common error in this whole section, and it produces answers that are absurdly wrong.

Time obeys its own grammar. The twenty-four-hour clock runs 00:00 to 23:59, so 15:10 is ten past three in the afternoon. Duration is counted forwards — whole hours first, then the leftover minutes — never subtracted as if times were decimals. From 13:35 to 15:10: one hour lands on 14:35, then thirty-five more minutes reach 15:10, so one hour thirty-five. Punching 15,10 minus 13,35 into a calculator returns 1,75, which is not a time at all.

Timetables combine the two. A long-distance bus pulls out of Polokwane at 06:50 and the trip takes five hours twenty-five. Add the whole hours: 06:50 becomes 11:50. Add the remainder: twenty-five past 11:50 is 12:15, so the bus arrives at 12:15. Notice the minutes rolled over the hour — 50 plus 25 is 75, which is one hour and 15 — and that rollover is precisely where the marks leak. Take the questions for this section now, watching the order of the temperature steps and counting every duration forwards.

## Subtopic: Proportional Conversions: Coverage, Doses and Recipes

The fourth family of conversions comes with no table at all. The context hands you a rate — this much of one quantity matches that much of another — and you scale it. The classic carriers are a paint tin that promises coverage and a teaspoon that holds about 5 mℓ.

Paint first. The label states: 5 ℓ covers 30 m². Reduce to a unit rate: 30 ÷ 5 = 6, so one litre covers 6 m². A wall measures 51 m² and needs two coats, so the true area is 51 × 2 = 102 m². Paint required: 102 ÷ 6 = 17 ℓ. Now the step everyone skips: no shop sells 17 litres. It sells 5 ℓ tins at R465 and 20 ℓ drums at R1 680. Four tins give 20 ℓ for 4 × 465 = R1 860; one drum gives the same 20 ℓ for R1 680. The drum saves R180, and the answer to what should be bought is the drum, stated in a sentence with the price.

Medicine next. A syrup is prescribed at 10 mℓ twice a day for seven days. Total needed: 10 × 2 × 7 = 140 mℓ, so a single 120 mℓ bottle cannot finish the course and two must be bought. With no medicine spoon at home, convert to what the kitchen has: at about 5 mℓ per teaspoon, 10 mℓ is two teaspoons.

Recipes scale the same way. A dish serving 6 must feed 27, so every quantity multiplies by 27 ÷ 6 = 4,5. The 250 mℓ of milk becomes 1 125 mℓ, which is 1,125 ℓ. The 3 eggs become 13,5, and since half an egg is not for sale, 14 eggs. That last move is the moral of the section: rounding obeys the context. Paint and eggs round UP, because running short halts the job; money rounds to cents; a medicine dose is never rounded at all. The final questions of this part are in front of you now: expect a coverage rate, a scaling step, and a buying decision.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same conversions again, but from the kitchen counter and the hardware aisle, where a wrong answer costs a second taxi trip and money that was never in the plan.

## Subtopic: Moving the Decimal Point

Everything metric runs on a thousand, and once that clicks, most of the fear evaporates. A thousand millimetres make a metre. A thousand metres make a kilometre. A thousand grams make a kilogram. A thousand millilitres make a litre. A thousand litres make a kilolitre. The same number, again and again. Only the centimetre breaks the beat: a hundred of them to a metre.

Which way, though? Drop the rules for a moment and think about size. A kilometre is a long walk; a metre is one stride. So a kilometre must contain LOTS of metres — 3,6 km becomes 3 600 m. Coming back, there must be FEWER kilometres than metres, so divide. Smaller unit, bigger number. Bigger unit, smaller number. If your answer breaks that pattern, you went the wrong way — and you can catch it yourself before anyone marks it.

Then the check nobody writes down but everybody needs: does the answer fit the real world? Your small fingernail is about a centimetre. One stride is roughly a metre. The big cool drink bottle holds two litres. A bag of mealie meal is ten or twelve and a half kilograms. So if your page says the kettle holds four thousand litres, or your schoolbag weighs three hundred kilograms, stop — the slip happened a few lines back and it is findable.

One more small habit that pays: if one measurement arrives in centimetres and another in metres, fix that FIRST, before anything gets multiplied. Questions on this section are with you now — check the direction, then check the size against something you can picture.

## Subtopic: Granny's Recipe in Fahrenheit

Some measurements arrive from another world. The old family recipe book says bake at 400 degrees — Fahrenheit, though your oven thinks in Celsius. The tape measure has inches down one edge. Your aunt says she is five foot ten. The imported dog food is labelled in pounds.

For length and weight it is a single multiply or divide, and the question supplies the number. A 25 kg bag of maize meal: 25 times 2,2 is 55 pounds. The imported bag stamped 44 pounds: 44 divided by 2,2 is 20 kg. Five foot ten is 70 inches, and 70 times 2,54 is 177,8 cm — about 1,78 m, a perfectly believable height, so the answer passes the sense test.

Which way, again? Ask which unit is bigger. A kilogram is heavier than a pound, so one kilogram must become SEVERAL pounds — multiply. It is the same logic as rands and cents: the bigger unit always breaks into more of the smaller one.

Temperature is the one demanding care, because the scales start in different places. Celsius calls freezing 0; Fahrenheit calls it 32. So coming down from Fahrenheit, remove that 32 FIRST, then times 5, then divide by 9. The recipe's 400 degrees: 400 take away 32 is 368; times 5 is 1 840; divide by 9 is 204,44 — set the oven to 200 and the bake will behave. Going up for a feverish child at 39 °C: times 9 is 351, divide by 5 is 70,2, add 32 is 102,2 °F. Take the 32 off first coming down; put it on last going up. Some questions on this section reach you now — walk slowly through the order of the steps.

## Subtopic: How Much Paint, How Much Medicine

The last kind of conversion carries no table. The label itself announces the rate, and your job is the sharing.

The tin says 5 litres covers 30 square metres. Cut it down to one litre: 30 divided by 5 is 6, so each litre covers 6 m². Your wall is 51 m² and wants two coats, so you are really covering 51 times 2, which is 102 m². Paint needed: 102 divided by 6, which is 17 litres.

And now real life walks in. No shop sells 17 litres. It sells 5 ℓ tins at R465 and a 20 ℓ drum at R1 680. Four tins make 20 litres for R1 860. The drum is the same 20 litres for R1 680. Same paint, R180 apart — and that R180 is why this is a mathematical literacy question and not a plain arithmetic drill. The answer is not seventeen litres. It is: buy the 20 ℓ drum at R1 680, which is R180 cheaper than four tins.

The same thinking runs the medicine cabinet. The dose is 10 mℓ, twice a day, for seven days: 10 times 2 is 20 mℓ a day, times 7 days is 140 mℓ. The bottle holds 120 mℓ, so one bottle cannot finish the course — buy two. No medicine spoon in the drawer? A teaspoon holds about 5 mℓ, so 10 mℓ is two level teaspoons.

Cooking for a crowd is the identical move. The recipe feeds 6, the church lunch needs 27, so everything multiplies by 27 divided by 6, which is 4,5. The 250 mℓ of milk becomes 1 125 mℓ — a litre and a bit. The 3 eggs become 13,5, and since nobody sells half an egg, you buy 14. Round paint and eggs UP, round money to cents, never round a dose. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — read the rate off the label first, then scale it, then decide what a person actually carries to the till.
