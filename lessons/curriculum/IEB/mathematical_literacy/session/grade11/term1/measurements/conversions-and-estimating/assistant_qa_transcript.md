**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the paint question worked out to 17 litres, but the model answer was about buying a 20-litre drum. A few of us wrote 17 and felt robbed. Why is 17 not the full answer?

**Tutor:** The 17 is not wrong — it is unfinished, because the question holds two questions inside it. How much paint does the wall need? Seventeen litres, and you should write that down, since it carries its own mark. What must the painter actually buy? That is answered by the shop's packaging, and the tin sizes and prices are printed in the question precisely so that you will use them — in mathematical literacy nothing in a question is decoration. So the complete answer names the purchase and its cost: one 20 ℓ drum at R1 680, and it goes one better by comparing that with four tins at R1 860 and stating the R180 saving. A habit worth building is to reread the question once you have a number and ask what a real person would still need to know before walking into the shop. If your answer does not let them act, it is not yet an answer.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why can temperature not use one multiplier the way kilograms and pounds do? The extra formula feels like an unnecessary complication.

**Tutor:** Because the two temperature scales measure from different starting points, and a lone multiplier can only stretch a scale — it cannot slide it. Mass and length share a true zero: zero kilograms is zero pounds, zero metres is zero feet, so converting is pure stretching and one factor does everything. Temperature is not like that. Water freezes at zero on the Celsius ruler but at thirty-two on the Fahrenheit ruler, so the rulers are offset before any stretching begins. That is why the conversion needs two operations: the 32 deals with the offset, and the nine-fifths deals with the stretch. The order falls straight out of the logic. Coming down from Fahrenheit you remove the offset first, then unstretch: subtract 32, multiply by 5, divide by 9. Going up you stretch first, then add the offset: multiply by 9, divide by 5, add 32. Once you can explain why the 32 exists, you will never park it at the wrong end — and you also get the party trick that minus forty reads the same on both scales, the one point where offset and stretch cancel exactly.

**Bianca:** This one caused a debate: an answer came out at 13,5 eggs and someone rounded it to 13, because ordinary rounding says a half can go down. Was that unfair marking?

**Tutor:** It is a real and painful loss, and the rule to internalise is that in this subject the CONTEXT decides the rounding, never the digit after the decimal comma. Ordinary rounding is a convention for numbers on paper; rounding in a situation is a decision with consequences. Buy 13 eggs when the batter needs 13,5 and the final tray never gets baked, so the honest answer is 14. The same reasoning governs paint, tiles, bricks, cement, minibuses for an outing and packets of anything: whenever running short stops the job, round up. Money rounds to two decimals because cents exist. Time rounds to whole minutes or hours according to what a schedule can use. And a medicine dose is never rounded at all, because it is a medical instruction, not an estimate. The safest habit is to attach a short sentence to the rounded value explaining the choice — fourteen eggs, since half an egg cannot be bought — because that sentence shows the marker the rounding was deliberate.

**Bianca:** Last one: how much of the estimating part actually gets assessed? It feels like the soft skill nobody bothers to test.

**Tutor:** It is assessed in two distinct ways, and both carry marks. The first is direct: questions ask you to estimate a length, a mass or a volume from a photograph or a description, or to select the most reasonable value from a list, and the expected method is reasoning from a known reference — a doorway is about two metres, a bag of mealie meal is ten or twelve and a half kilograms, a cool drink bottle holds two litres, a walking stride is close to a metre. Naming your reference in the answer is what upgrades a guess into an estimate. The second way is indirect and far more valuable: estimating is the error-check that patrols your entire answer script. Every finished calculation can be held against the world for a second — is this kettle, this wall, this journey, this bill the right SIZE? A learner who checks like that catches shifted decimal points, wrong-direction conversions and mixed units before the marker ever sees them, and those three are the most common errors in the whole measurement section. Estimating is not the decorative part of the topic. It is the guard standing over everything else you calculate.
