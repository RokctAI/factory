Hey, it's Bianca — conversions look like a mountain of numbers to memorise, and that is exactly why the topic feels heavy. Here is the relief: the conversion table is given to you in the question. Nobody is testing whether you remember 2,54 or 1,609. They are testing whether you know which way to point them.

So learn one question instead of twenty numbers: which unit is BIGGER? A kilometre is bigger than a metre, so kilometres explode into lots of metres — multiply. A kilogram is heavier than a pound, so kilograms become more pounds — multiply. Going the other way, divide. One question, every conversion, every time.

Temperature is the only conversion with a real order to it, and the order is the whole mark. Coming down from Fahrenheit: take the 32 off first, then times five, then divide by nine. Going up to Fahrenheit: times nine, divide by five, add the 32 last. Write those two lines inside the front cover of your book and read them once a day this week.

Your mark-protecting habit is small and boring and it works: get everything into the same unit BEFORE you calculate, and write the unit next to every number in your working. Half the marks lost in measurement die when a metre and a centimetre meet inside one multiplication. The other half die on an answer with no unit.

And let common sense patrol your page. A kettle does not hold four thousand litres. A schoolbag does not weigh three hundred kilograms. If the answer is the wrong size for the world, go back — the slip will show itself in ten seconds.

One question about size, two lines about temperature, units on everything. That is the entire topic — now go take it apart in the practice questions. You've got this!
