# Part 1 — Expert

Nothing tests measurement like a building project. Someone must fence a yard, pave it, tile a floor and paint the walls, and each of those decisions starts as a length or an area and finishes as a rand amount. This session prices one project in Tembisa — a rectangular yard and the room that opens onto it — moving through perimeter, area, surface area and the costing chain, using the standard formulae for rectangles, triangles and circles and the rounding rules that a hardware shop enforces whether you like them or not.

## Subtopic: Perimeter: the Line Around the Job

PERIMETER is the total distance around the outside of a two-dimensional shape, measured in ordinary length units — millimetres, centimetres, metres. It answers every question about things that run along an edge: fencing, skirting board, curtain rail, a row of border bricks, a strip of beading.

For a rectangle the formula is P = 2 × (length + breadth). The yard measures 14 m by 9 m, so P = 2 × (14 + 9) = 2 × 23 = 46 m. A circle's perimeter carries its own name, CIRCUMFERENCE, and its own formula: C = 2 × π × radius, equivalently π × diameter. A circular herb bed of radius 1,2 m has C = 2 × 3,14 × 1,2 = 7,54 m of edging, rounded to two decimals.

For any other shape, perimeter is nothing more than the sum of the sides — no formula, only the discipline of walking the outline without skipping a side. In a composite shape, an inward-facing side still counts whenever the edging actually runs along it.

Now the step that turns a maths answer into a useful one. The yard's perimeter is 46 m, but the fence is not 46 m, because a 2 m gate sits on that line and the gate comes off a different shelf. Fencing needed = 46 − 2 = 44 m. At R315 per metre, the fencing costs 44 × 315 = R13 860. Fence the full 46 m and you have burned R630 on material standing where the gate must swing.

That subtraction is the assessed habit: scan the context for openings, gates and gaps before multiplying by any rate — almost every perimeter question at this level hides one. The questions for this section are with you now; apply the formula, then interrogate the picture for what must come off the line.

## Subtopic: Area: Rectangles, Triangles and Circles

AREA is the amount of flat surface a shape covers, measured in SQUARE units — square metres, square centimetres. Three formulae do the work this year: rectangle A = length × breadth; triangle A = ½ × base × perpendicular height; circle A = π × radius squared.

The yard again: A = 14 × 9 = 126 m². The circular herb bed inside it: A = 3,14 × 1,2² = 3,14 × 1,44 = 4,52 m² to two decimals. A triangular corner bed with base 3 m and perpendicular height 2 m: A = ½ × 3 × 2 = 3 m².

COMPOSITE shapes are the grade 11 step up, and the method is adding or subtracting whole areas. The paving must cover the yard but not the herb bed, so the paved area is 126 − 4,52 = 121,48 m². Where a shape forms an L or a T, slice it into rectangles, calculate each piece, and add. Sketch the cut and label the pieces — labelled working lets a marker follow the method and credit it even when one multiplication slips.

Two cautions. First, a triangle's height must be PERPENDICULAR to its base — the straight drop, never the slanted side. Second, area units are squared, and converting them is not like converting lengths. Since 1 m is 100 cm, one square metre is 100 × 100 = 10 000 cm². Dividing an area in square centimetres by 100 instead of 10 000 lands you out by a factor of a hundred.

Write the formula, substitute with units, and give the answer in square metres. Turn to the questions for this section now, watching the composite shapes and the perpendicular height.

## Subtopic: Walls, Doors and Windows

Painting a room requires the area of its walls — a SURFACE AREA calculation, since walls are the standing faces of a box. A shortcut is worth owning: the total wall area of a rectangular room equals the PERIMETER of the floor multiplied by the wall height. It works because the four walls, unrolled flat, form one long rectangle whose length is that perimeter.

The room measures 3,9 m by 3,2 m, with walls 2,6 m high. Floor perimeter = 2 × (3,9 + 3,2) = 14,2 m. Wall area = 14,2 × 2,6 = 36,92 m².

Now remove what never gets painted. A door of 0,8 m by 2,0 m has an area of 1,6 m². A window of 1,4 m by 1,0 m has an area of 1,4 m². Together they subtract 3,0 m², leaving a paintable area of 36,92 − 3,0 = 33,92 m².

The specification calls for two coats, and each coat is a complete covering, so the area the paint must cover is 33,92 × 2 = 67,84 m². This is the most commonly skipped step in the whole chain, and skipping it at the shop instead of on the page doubles the paint bill.

The same reasoning covers any rectangular prism you may be given — a storage trunk, a water tank, a carton. Total surface area = 2 × (length × breadth) + 2 × (length × height) + 2 × (breadth × height). Take each face in turn, name it, and add. When a face is absent — an open-topped box, a tank without a lid — leave that face out and state in words that you have. Your questions for this section are ready; expect a room with openings to subtract and a number of coats to apply.

## Subtopic: From Square Metres to Rands

The closing move converts area into money, and it runs on three gears: the coverage rate, the wastage allowance, and the packaging.

Coverage first. Paint spreading 7 m² per litre must cover 67,84 m², so 67,84 ÷ 7 = 9,69 ℓ. The shop stocks 5 ℓ tins at R560, so two tins are needed, giving 10 ℓ for 2 × 560 = R1 120. No shop measures out 9,69 litres.

Wastage next. Tiles and paving get cut at edges and some crack, so the trade orders about 10% extra. The floor is 3,9 × 3,2 = 12,48 m². With 10% wastage, order 12,48 × 1,10 = 13,73 m². Each box of tiles covers 1,2 m², so 13,73 ÷ 1,2 = 11,44 boxes — round UP to 12 boxes, because 11 boxes leaves bare screed. At R265 a box, the tiles cost 12 × 265 = R3 180.

Paving the yard repeats the logic at scale. The paved area was 121,48 m²; adding 10% gives 133,63 m², and paving sold by the square metre means ordering 134 m². At R130 per square metre, the paving costs 134 × 130 = R17 420.

Then total the project honestly: fencing R13 860, paving R17 420, tiles R3 180, paint R1 120 — R35 580 in materials, before a cent of labour. That total answers the question the household actually asked.

Three disciplines guard these marks. Round only at the end of each independent calculation, never midway. Round UP whenever running short would stop the job, and note the reason in a short phrase. And answer in a sentence carrying the rand amount, because "12" is not an answer while "12 boxes of tiles, costing R3 180" is. The last questions of this part are in front of you now: coverage rates, wastage and packaging, each converting an area into things to buy.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same project again, seen from the front gate, with a borrowed tape measure and a budget that has to survive.

## Subtopic: Walking the Fence

Perimeter is the walk around the edge. Start at a corner of the yard, walk the whole boundary back to where you began — that distance is the perimeter, and it is what you pay for when you buy fencing, skirting or a line of border bricks.

The yard runs 14 m one way and 9 m the other. Walk it: 14 plus 9 plus 14 plus 9 makes 46 m. Or take the formula's shortcut — one long side plus one short side, doubled: 14 plus 9 is 23, doubled is 46 m. Same walk, less ink.

Round shapes play the same game under a different name. The distance around a circle is the circumference: π — call it 3,14 — times the full width across the middle. The herb bed reaches 1,2 m from centre to edge, so it is 2,4 m across, and 3,14 times 2,4 is 7,54 m of edging.

Now the trap that catches nearly everyone. The fence is not 46 m, because a 2 m gate sits in that line, and gates are bought separately. So the fencing is 46 minus 2, which is 44 m. At R315 a metre, that is R13 860. Order the full 46 and you have thrown away R630 on fence that would block your own gateway.

So before any length meets a price, look at the drawing again and ask what is not fence: a gate, a driveway, a stretch where the house wall already closes the boundary. Some questions on this section are with you right now — walk the full outline, then remove what is not being bought.

## Subtopic: Covering the Floor and the Walls

Area and perimeter are different jobs, and the difference is one sentence: perimeter is the walk around, area is the covering over. Fencing is perimeter. Tiles, paint, paving, grass — all coverings, all area.

For a rectangle, multiply the two sides. The yard is 14 by 9, so 126 square metres. The room floor is 3,9 by 3,2, so 12,48 square metres. A triangle is half a rectangle, so half of base times height — the corner bed 3 m along the bottom and 2 m up is half of 6, which is 3 square metres. A circle is 3,14 times the radius times the radius again — the herb bed at 1,2 m radius is 3,14 times 1,44, which is 4,52 square metres.

When a shape is not one tidy rectangle, cut it into pieces you recognise, work each piece, then add. And when something must be left out — the herb bed sitting in the paving — find the big area and subtract the small one: 126 minus 4,52 leaves 121,48 square metres to pave.

Walls are simply rectangles standing upright, and here is the time-saver: walk the floor's edge for the perimeter, then multiply by the wall height. The room's walk-around is 14,2 m, the walls stand 2,6 m, so 14,2 times 2,6 is 36,92 square metres of wall. Then off comes the door, 0,8 by 2, which is 1,6, and the window, 1,4 by 1, which is 1,4. Three square metres gone, leaving 33,92 to paint. And the spec says two coats — so the paint really covers 33,92 twice, which is 67,84 square metres. Questions on this section are coming your way now; keep perimeter and area in separate pockets, and remove the door and window before buying anything.

## Subtopic: The Shop Does Not Sell Half a Box

The final step turns square metres into a shopping list, and the shop writes its own rules.

Paint first. The tin covers 7 square metres per litre and 67,84 must be covered, so 67,84 divided by 7 is 9,69 litres. Now go and ask the counter for 9,69 litres of paint. They will stare at you. They sell 5 ℓ tins at R560, so you take two — 10 litres, R1 120 — and the leftover handles the touch-ups.

Tiles next, and tiles introduce WASTAGE. Edge tiles get cut, a couple crack, offcuts go in the skip. So the trade orders about 10% extra. The floor is 12,48 square metres; add 10% and you order 13,73. Each box covers 1,2, so 13,73 divided by 1,2 is 11,44 boxes. Round UP to 12 — always up — because eleven boxes means a bare strip of floor and another taxi to the shop. Twelve boxes at R265 is R3 180.

The paving repeats the story at full scale: 121,48 square metres to cover, plus 10% is 133,63, so order 134 square metres, and at R130 each that comes to R17 420.

Then add it up the way a person planning a real job must: fence R13 860, paving R17 420, tiles R3 180, paint R1 120 — thirty-five thousand five hundred and eighty rand in materials, before anyone is paid to lift a spade.

Two rules to take home. Round UP whenever running out would stop the work, and write the reason beside it in a few words. And close with a sentence someone could act on: she must buy 12 boxes of tiles, which will cost R3 180. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — turn each area into tins, boxes or square metres, and each of those into rands.
