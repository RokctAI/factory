# Part 1 — Expert

Every small venture stands or falls on four money questions: what does one item cost to make, what should it sell for, how many must be sold before the operation stops losing, and what happens to the money afterwards — in the bank, against a loan, and against prices that never stop climbing. This session runs all four questions through a single venture: a sports-day fundraising stall in Pietermaritzburg selling boerewors rolls, with the takings banked, part of the profit invested, and next season's plans priced against inflation.

## Subtopic: Cost Price, Selling Price and Percentage Profit

The COST PRICE of an item is what it costs to produce: the ingredients, plus a fair share of whatever else the production consumes. The stall's batch of 80 boerewors rolls uses bread rolls at R150, boerewors at R250, onions and sauce at R50 and gas at R30 — total production cost 150 + 250 + 50 + 30 = R480, so the cost price per roll is 480 ÷ 80 = R6,00.

The SELLING PRICE is what the customer hands over, and it is a decision, not a calculation — but a decision steered by three numbers: the cost price, what nearby sellers charge, and what the crowd will pay. The stall settles on R10. PROFIT per item is selling price minus cost price: 10 − 6,00 = R4,00.

PERCENTAGE PROFIT measures that margin against the cost price: profit divided by cost price, times 100. Here: 4,00 ÷ 6,00 × 100 = 66,7%. The denominator is where marks are won and lost: percentage profit is reckoned on COST, not on selling price — 4,00 against 10 would give 40%, a different figure answering a different question. Always state the base you used.

Organisations of any size run this same arithmetic, with two extra habits. They compare YEAR against YEAR: takings of R9 180 this season against R8 500 last season is an increase of 680 ÷ 8 500 × 100 = 8%. And they compare PROJECTED against ACTUAL: a budget that projected R550 for ingredients against an actual spend of R480 shows a saving of R70, and writing one sentence on the difference — under budget, and why — is the analysis the question is really after. The questions for this section are with you now; keep cost price and selling price in separate columns and name the base of every percentage.

## Subtopic: Break-even: Where Loss Turns into Profit

Some costs ignore how many rolls are sold. The stall pays R290 for its place at the sports day — a FIXED COST, gone before the first customer appears. The ingredients are VARIABLE COSTS: R6,00 more for every roll assembled. Total cost for n rolls: 290 + 6,00 × n. Income at R10 each: 10 × n.

The BREAK-EVEN point is where income first equals total cost — the moment the stall has lost nothing and gained nothing. Set the two expressions equal: 10 × n = 290 + 6,00 × n. Each roll contributes 10 − 6,00 = R4,00 toward the fixed cost, so the number needed is 290 ÷ 4,00 = 72,5. Round UP: 73 rolls, because at 72 the stall is still R2 short — income 720 against costs 722 — while at 73 it sits R2 ahead.

On a graph the whole story is visible: the income line climbs from zero at R10 per unit; the cost line starts at R290 and climbs more gently at R6,00 per unit. They cross at the break-even point; left of the crossing the cost line rides higher and the gap is loss, right of it the income line rides higher and the gap is profit. Questions hand you the graph and ask you to read off the crossing, the loss at a given quantity, or the profit at full capacity — every one of them a vertical gap between the two lines.

Break-even also prices decisions. A cheaper site at R200 drops the break-even to 200 ÷ 4,00 = 50 rolls; nudging the price to R10,50 lifts the contribution to R4,50 and drops it to 290 ÷ 4,50 = 64,4, so 65. Every change in price or cost moves the crossing, and saying WHICH way it moves is the understanding being tested. Your questions for this section are ready; identify fixed against variable first, and round break-even answers up with a reason.

## Subtopic: Interest and Banking, Year by Year

The stall banks its money, and at this level you handle the bank's arithmetic WITHOUT formulae — building the growth year by year in a table, which is both what the questions require and the honest way to see what compounding actually is.

The fundraising committee invests R8 000 of profit in a FIXED DEPOSIT at 6% per year, interest added to the account each year. Year one: interest is 8 000 × 0,06 = R480, balance 8 480. Year two: the 6% now works on 8 480 — interest 508,80, balance 8 988,80. Year three: 6% of 8 988,80 is 539,33, balance R9 528,13. Each year's interest is larger than the last because the base keeps growing: that is COMPOUND interest, interest earning interest.

Compare simple interest at the same rate: R480 a year, every year, totalling 1 440 over three years — final amount R9 440. Compounding beat it by 9 528,13 − 9 440 = R88,13. Small over three years; decisive over twenty.

The same table runs in reverse for a LOAN: borrow R8 000 at 6% compounded, repay nothing, and the debt walks the identical staircase — 8 480, then 8 988,80, then 9 528,13. What delights the saver terrifies the borrower.

Banking vocabulary rounds off the section. A SAVINGS account earns modest interest and allows withdrawals. A CHEQUE or CURRENT account is for daily transactions, earns little or nothing, and charges fees. A FIXED DEPOSIT locks money away for a term at a higher rate. A DEBIT account or card spends your own money; a CREDIT account or card spends the bank's, which must be repaid — with interest if late. Bank FEES — monthly account fees, per-transaction charges — are expenses in any budget a question sets. The questions for this section follow now; build the table one year at a time and mark whose money grows.

## Subtopic: Inflation: When the Same Trolley Costs More

INFLATION is the general rise of prices over time, quoted as a percentage per year. Inflation of 5% means that what cost R100 a year ago typically costs R105 now — the same goods, the same trolley, more rand.

The calculation is a percentage increase. Next year's price equals this year's price × 1 plus the rate. The stall's ingredient basket costs R2 400 today; at 5% inflation, next year it costs 2 400 × 1,05 = R2 520. A committee budgeting next season's fundraiser at this season's prices will be R120 short before a single roll is buttered.

The reverse skill: given two prices, find the rate. A litre of milk rose from R22,50 to R24,30: the increase is 1,80, and 1,80 ÷ 22,50 × 100 = 8% — the milk inflated by eight percent. The base is always the OLD price; dividing by the new one is the classic error and gives 7,4%, which is wrong.

Inflation compounds, exactly like the fixed deposit. Two years at 5% multiplies prices by 1,05 × 1,05 = 1,1025, not by 1,10: the R2 400 basket costs 2 400 × 1,1025 = R2 646 in two years, and adding 5% twice to the original — R2 640 — undercounts, because the second year's rise works on the first year's higher prices.

The sting in the tail ties the whole session together: money in the bank grows, but so do the prices it must chase. A fixed deposit earning 6% while inflation runs 5% grows its BUYING power by only the sliver between them; savings under a mattress earning nothing LOSE 5% of their power each year. Rising prices also remind you that a rate of increase can be a rate of DECREASE in disguise — a price falling from R25 to R22 is a decrease of 3 ÷ 25 × 100 = 12%, same method, opposite direction. The last questions of this part are with you now: old price as the base, compound over multiple years, and one sentence on what the number means for the budget.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same stall from behind the braai, where every number smells of woodsmoke and counts in singles.

## Subtopic: Pricing the Boerewors Stall

Before the stall can charge anything, it must know what one roll costs to make. Add up the whole batch: bread rolls 150, boerewors 250, onions and sauce 50, gas 30 — R480 for 80 rolls. So each one costs R6,00 before it earns a cent. That is the cost price, and it is the floor: sell below it and every sale digs the hole deeper.

The selling price is your call. Too close to R6,00 and the profit is crumbs; too high and the queue drifts to the cooldrink table. The committee lands on R10 — cheaper than the tuck shop burger, comfortably above the cost. Each sale clears 10 minus 6,00: R4,00 of profit.

How good is R4,00? Measure it against what you spent, not what you charged: 4,00 out of 6,00 is 66,7%. That is the percentage profit, and the base matters — measured against the R10 selling price it would look like 40%, a different number answering a different question. The default is profit on COST unless the question says otherwise; write the base next to the answer and nobody can quarrel with it.

And once the braai cools, the committee compares like a business: this season took R9 180 against last season's R8 500 — up 8%. The budget projected R550 of ingredients; the till slip says R480 — R70 under, because the boerewors was on special. One sentence per comparison: what changed, which way, and by how much. Some questions on this section are with you right now — cost floor first, price decision second, percentage on the cost.

## Subtopic: How Many Before We Stop Losing

Here is the stall's uncomfortable truth at eight in the morning: before one customer arrives, it is already R290 down — the site fee, paid whether the day goes well or badly. The first roll sold does not make the stall rich; it makes the stall slightly less poor.

Watch the crawl back to zero. Each roll sells for R10 but costs R6,00 to make, so each one pushes R4,00 against that R290 hole. How many pushes fill it? 290 divided by 4,00: 72,5. And here you round UP — at 72 sold, the stall has taken 720 but spent 722, still two rand down; the 73rd roll tips it R2 into profit. Number 73 is the break-even roll: before it, every sale shrinks a loss; after it, every sale IS profit, the full R4,00 a time.

Picture the graph like a race: the income line starts at zero and climbs R10 a step; the cost line starts R290 up the wall and climbs R6,00 a step. Income climbs steeper, so it catches up — the crossing is break-even, and the gap between the lines at any point is that day's loss or profit. Read the gap, not the lines.

The crossing moves when the numbers move. Cheaper site at R200? Break-even falls to 50. Charge R10,50 instead of R10? Each sale pushes R4,50, and 65 rolls clears it. Every business decision — rent, price, recipe — is secretly a question about where the crossing sits. Questions on this section are coming your way now — fixed cost in the hole, contribution per item, divide, round up, and say why you rounded up.

## Subtopic: The Bank's Staircase and the Trolley That Grows

The fundraiser banked R8 000 in a fixed deposit at 6%, and the balance climbs a staircase. First step: 6% of 8 000 is R480 — balance 8 480. Second step: the bank pays 6% on the NEW balance, 8 480, which is R508,80 — balance 8 988,80. Third step: R539,33 — balance R9 528,13. Each step is taller than the last, because interest starts earning its own interest. That is compounding, and you build it exactly like this: one year at a time, in a table, no formula required.

Compare the flat staircase: simple interest pays R480 every year, full stop — R9 440 after three years. Compounding beat it by R88,13. Three years, small gap; twenty years, a canyon. And the staircase has a dark twin: borrow R8 000 at 6% compound and repay nothing, and your DEBT climbs the same steps — 8 480, 8 988,80, 9 528,13. The machine does not care whose money is in it.

Meanwhile, outside the bank, the trolley is climbing its own staircase. Inflation at 5% means this year's R2 400 basket of stall ingredients rings up at 2 400 times 1,05 next year: R2 520. Budget next season on this season's prices and you arrive R120 short. Two years out, the multiplier is 1,05 times 1,05 — 1,1025 — because the second rise stands on the first: R2 646.

Spot the rate from two prices by always dividing by the OLD one: milk from R22,50 to R24,30 is a rise of 1,80, and 1,80 over 22,50 is 8%. Old price on the bottom, every time. And put the two staircases side by side for the final truth: money earning 6% while prices climb 5% is only truly growing by the sliver between; cash in a drawer, earning nothing, quietly shrinks 5% a year in what it can buy. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — build the bank's table year by year, grow the trolley by 1,05, and check which staircase is climbing faster.
