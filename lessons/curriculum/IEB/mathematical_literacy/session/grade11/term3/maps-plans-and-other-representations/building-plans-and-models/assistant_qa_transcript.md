**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that derails people before any calculating starts: on the plan, do we measure walls from the inside or the outside? Different learners got different room sizes from the same drawing.

**Tutor:** Both measurements are real, and the question decides which one it wants — your job is to read the wording and then say which lines you used. Inside face to inside face gives the USABLE room: the floor you can cover, the space a desk occupies, the walls you will paint. Outside face to outside face gives the building's FOOTPRINT: what the structure covers on the stand, which matters for site plans and municipal approval. The gap between the two answers is precisely the thickness of the walls — typically 220 to 280 real millimetres of brick, four to five millimetres on a 1 : 50 drawing — so the two versions of a 4 m room can differ by half a metre across the pair of walls. When a question says the area of the study floor, it wants inside; the area the extension adds to the house's footprint wants outside. If the wording is genuinely ambiguous, state your choice in a phrase — measured between inside wall faces — and proceed; a declared assumption protects the method marks, while silent measuring leaves the marker guessing which walls you meant. And measure to the LINE, not to the shading: the thick double line's inner edge is the inside face.

**Bianca:** Next: the mirror thing with elevations. Everyone accepts that the north elevation is the face seen from the north, but the left-right flip still catches half the class. Is there a foolproof way to hold it?

**Tutor:** Draw yourself on the plan — literally a small arrow or stick figure standing off the north edge, facing the house. That figure is facing SOUTH. Now trace its gaze: the plan's west side falls on the figure's right hand, the east side on its left. That is the whole mirror, and it stops feeling like a trick the moment the figure is on the page, because you are no longer imagining a rotation in your head — you are reading one off the paper. The reason it exists: a floor plan is seen from above with north conventionally up, but an elevation is seen horizontally, and turning from a bird's view to a person's view reverses one axis. The practical drill for matching questions goes: choose the face; draw the observer arrow; list the openings along that wall in the order the OBSERVER meets them, right to left or left to right as the arrow dictates; then hunt the elevation with that exact sequence and spacing. Combine it with counting openings first and locating tall landmarks — the chimney on the west end must appear on the observer's right in the north elevation — and matching becomes mechanical. One more comfort: the south elevation flips the flip, so a feature on the plan's west reappears on the viewer's left there. If you ever feel lost, redraw the arrow; never trust the unaided imagination with mirrors.

**Bianca:** This one cost real money in the practice questions: for the skirting, someone bought exactly 13,5 metres' worth — four and a half lengths — and someone else said five lengths. The shop question feels different from the maths question. Which is right?

**Tutor:** The shop question IS the maths question in this subject, and five lengths is the answer. The arithmetic says the room needs 13,5 running metres after the doorway is excused. The shop sells skirting only in fixed 3 m lengths — there is no such purchase as 4,5 lengths. Four lengths supply 12 m, which is 1,5 m short: a whole wall's worth of skirting missing beside the door, and no offcut in the pile long enough to fill it honestly. So the builder buys five lengths, 15 m, uses 13,5, and accepts 1,5 m of offcuts as the price of packaging reality — R475 instead of a theoretical R427,50. The examinable structure is a two-part discipline: first compute the true requirement — that earns the measurement marks — then convert to purchasable units by rounding UP, with a written reason: five lengths, since skirting is sold in whole 3 m lengths and four would leave a shortfall. Notice the contrast with the packing questions, where fractions round DOWN because a fifth of a can cannot enter a box. The direction of rounding always follows the physical consequence: short materials stop a job, so buying rounds up; overhanging objects cannot exist, so packing rounds down. Ask what happens in the real world if I round this way, and the direction chooses itself.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the can-packing problem, someone divided the carton's volume by the can's volume — 28 800 over about 283 — got roughly 101, and answered that the box holds 101 cans. The tree of divisions gave 80. Why is the volume method so far off?

**Tutor:** Because volume division imagines the cans melted and poured. A hundred and one cans' worth of liquid would indeed fit in that carton — liquid flows into every corner. But cans are rigid cylinders: they refuse to squash into the four corners of each layer, they cannot lie diagonally in the leftover slivers, and they will not split themselves between layers. The packing count must respect geometry direction by direction: 48 across takes 8 can-widths exactly; 30 takes 5; the layer holds 40; the 20 cm height stacks 2 layers of 10; total 80. The missing twenty-one cans of the volume method are phantoms living in the air gaps — the 21,5% of the carton that circles-in-rectangles always waste — plus any slack the dimensions leave. That is also why the per-dimension division must be FLOORED: a height giving 2,3 layers still stacks only 2, because the 0,3 is a headspace no can fits. The volume calculation is not useless — it answers the different question of wasted space, exactly as we used it — but how many fit is always the three floored divisions multiplied. And test orientations before finalising: cans lying down change every division, and sometimes lying beats standing. The tree of divisions is the model; the volume quotient is the upper limit that packing can never reach.
