# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the finance revision sweep for the final examination — the whole year's money work, reorganised around two paper trails: Naledi's payslip from the packaging plant in Gqeberha, and the books of her uncle Sipho's boerewors-roll stand. Documents and deductions first, then VAT and the percentages that ride on a payslip, then tariffs and exchange rates, then the business mathematics of profit, break-even, interest and inflation. Nothing here is new; everything here is the exam-day version — each calculation compressed to its one deciding move, each answer closed with the sentence that completes it.

## Subtopic: Financial Documents, Income and Deductions

Every finance question begins with a document, and the first skill is naming its parts before a calculator gets involved.

Naledi's payslip. Gross salary: R11 200 — the earnings before anything leaves. Then the deductions. UIF — the Unemployment Insurance Fund — takes 1 percent: 0,01 times 11 200 gives R112, matched rand for rand by her employer behind the scenes. Pension: 7,5 percent — 0,075 times 11 200 — R840. Income tax as deducted here: R1 498. Net salary is what survives: 11 200 minus 112, minus 840, minus 1 498 — R8 750. Gross minus deductions equals net — the most-tested line in the whole section, and the subtraction must be shown, not just the survivor announced.

The vocabulary distinctions that carry credit: income against expenditure; fixed income like a salary against variable income like weekend overtime; fixed expenses like rent against variable ones like electricity and data. A household-budget question is a sorting exercise before it is a calculating one — place every line in its column, total the columns, compare.

Documents beyond the payslip run on the same reading discipline. A till slip: find the VAT line, verify the addition. A municipal account: opening balance, current charges, payments received, closing balance — and the classic ask, explain a line item, wants words rather than numbers: the opening balance is what was still owed from last month.

The habits: read the document twice, label what each number IS before using it, and quote line names inside answers. A finance answer that names its lines audits itself.

This section's questions are with you now — one payslip stripped to its net, one budget sorted and explained.

## Subtopic: VAT, Payslip Percentages and the Reverse Gear

VAT is 15 percent, and it is tested in both gears.

Forward gear: a school blazer costs R1 240 before VAT. The VAT is 0,15 times 1 240 — R186 — so the till price is R1 426; or in one move, 1 240 times 1,15. Reverse gear, where most answers go wrong: a tag of R1 426 already includes VAT, and the question wants the price without it. The tag is 115 percent of the true price, so divide by 1,15: R1 240, carrying R186 of tax. Subtracting 15 percent of the tag takes 15 percent of the wrong base and lands on the wrong answer — division is the only honest way back. The check costs nothing: 1 240 times 1,15 returns exactly 1 426.

Zero-rated items complicate the trolley, and questions know it: brown bread, maize meal, rice and other staples carry no VAT, so a till-slip question may hand you a basket where only SOME items attract the 15 percent. Sort before calculating: subtotal the standard-rated items, apply VAT to that subtotal only, then add everything together.

Back to the payslip percentages, because they are the same machine. UIF at 1 percent is the gentlest calculation of the year — slide the decimal: 1 percent of R11 200 is R112. The employer contributes the same again, a fact the interpretation questions love to probe. Pension at 7,5 percent: 0,075 times gross. And any percentage-increase question — a salary rising 5 percent — is one multiplication by 1,05, with the new gross of R11 760 feeding new deductions, which is exactly how the multi-step questions chain.

The gear check before any percentage move: is the given amount the base, or the base plus the percentage already added? Words like including, after the increase and now costs all warn that the reverse gear — division — is the honest route.

Take this section's questions now — both gears run, one mixed trolley sorted, one payslip percentage chained.

## Subtopic: Tariffs, Stepped Charges and Exchange Rates

A tariff is a price per unit of something metered, and this year's upgrade is the STEPPED tariff, where the price climbs as usage climbs.

The municipal electricity structure: the first 150 units cost R1,95 each; every unit after that costs R2,85. A household uses 260 units. The forbidden move: 260 times either single rate. The honest move walks the steps: the first 150 units cost R292,50; the remaining 110 units — 260 minus 150 — cost 110 times 2,85, which is R313,50. Total: R606. Stepped-tariff questions are laddered subtractions and multiplications, and each rung earns its own credit — first block, remaining units, block cost, total. Show the ladder.

Water tariffs run the same ladder in kilolitres; a cell contract with free minutes and then a per-minute rate is the same ladder in disguise. Whenever a tariff table appears, ask two things: where do the steps change, and how many units fall inside each step? The graph of a stepped tariff bends upward at every step — reading the bend is reading the table.

Exchange rates convert one currency's units into another's, and the rate is just a price: R20,25 per euro means each euro costs twenty rand twenty-five. Buying 80 euros for a trip: 80 times 20,25 — R1 620. Converting back: how many euros does R1 215 buy? Divide: 1 215 over 20,25 — 60 euros. The direction rule: multiply by the rate to turn euros into rand, divide to turn rand into euros — and sanity-check with strength: the rand amount is always the bigger number while the rate sits above one rand per unit.

Interpretation completes the skill: if the rate moves from R20,25 to R19,60 per euro, the rand has strengthened — each euro costs fewer rand — and imported goods trend cheaper. One sentence, no calculator, full credit.

Work through this section's questions — one ladder climbed, both conversion directions run, one strengthening explained.

## Subtopic: Profit, Break-even, Interest and Inflation

Sipho's boerewors-roll stand carries the business half of the year.

His numbers: rent for the corner site and the gas equipment, R1 800 a month — the fixed cost, owed even in a week of rain. Ingredients per roll: R16 — the variable cost, paid once per item sold. Selling price: R31. Each sale therefore contributes 31 minus 16 — R15 — toward the fixed cost. Break-even asks: how many sales until the stand stops losing? Divide the fixed cost by the contribution: 1 800 over 15 — 120 rolls. The check: 120 sales bring in 120 times 31 — R3 720 — and cost 1 800 plus 120 times 16 — also R3 720. Income equals total cost: the definition of break-even, and the sentence sales above 120 make profit; below 120 make a loss is the interpretation half of the answer. On a graph, break-even is where the income line crosses the total-cost line.

Interest, the two species. Simple interest grows on the original amount only: R8 000 at 10 percent for 3 years earns 800 each year — R2 400 — closing on R10 400. Compound interest grows on the growing total: year one ends at 8 800; year two grows 10 percent of 8 800 — R880 — ending at 9 680; year three adds R968, closing at 10 648. Same rate, same years, R248 apart — compounding earns interest ON interest, and the gap widens every year. The expected working is the year-by-year table for compound growth, not a memorised formula, plus the comparison sentence naming the winner and the gap.

Inflation is compound growth applied to prices: at 5 percent, a R320 school jersey costs 320 times 1,05 — R336 — next year. Inflation questions are percentage-increase questions wearing an economics jacket, and the interpretation twist — your money buys less as prices compound upward — earns the closing sentence.

The final questions of this part are with you now — one break-even found and checked, both interests grown, one price inflated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision from behind the boerewors stand and around the kitchen table — the arithmetic of an ordinary money month.

## Subtopic: The Payslip Is a Story of Subtraction

A payslip tells one story: money arrives big and leaves smaller, and every line between is a named subtraction.

Naledi earns R11 200 gross. Before it reaches her, three hands dip in. UIF takes 1 percent — R112 — small, and it is insurance: lose the job, and the fund pays out BECAUSE these deductions happened. Pension takes 7,5 percent — R840 — that is her own future self, paid first. Tax takes R1 498 — the schools-roads-clinics subscription. What lands in the account: R8 750. The story in one line: 11 200 in, 2 450 named and explained, 8 750 out.

Why it gets tested: every deduction question is one of three askables. Calculate a line — one percentage multiplication. Explain a line — one sentence saying what the money buys. Recalculate after a change — a 5 percent raise makes the gross 11 200 times 1,05, which is R11 760, and every percentage deduction resizes with it. That is the chained question's whole trick: change the gross first, then rerun the lines.

The same subtraction story runs the household. Money in: salaries, a grant, the back room rented to a cousin. Money out: fixed things that ignore your choices — rent, the funeral policy — and variable things that obey them — electricity, food, data. Surplus or deficit is one subtraction, and advice questions always point at the variable pile.

Read any money document as a story with named characters, and no line on it can ambush you.

Your questions for this section are ready — one line calculated, one line explained, one raise chained through.

## Subtopic: The Stall That Teaches Break-even

Stand behind Sipho's gas griddle for one month and break-even stops being a formula.

The corner site costs R1 800 whether he grills or sleeps. That is the hole the month starts in. Each roll sells for R31 but eats R16 of ingredients — so each sale throws R15 into the hole. The only question that matters in week one: how many R15 shovelfuls fill a R1 800 hole? 1 800 over 15 — 120. Roll number 120 lands exactly at ground level: income R3 720, costs R3 720, nothing lost, nothing gained. Roll 121 is the first one he actually profits from — R15 clean.

Hear the three numbers inside every break-even question: the hole — fixed cost; the shovel — selling price minus cost per item; the count — hole over shovel. Questions dress them as car washes, tuck shops, salon chairs and craft stalls, but the three numbers never change costume convincingly. On the graph version, the income line starts at nought and climbs at R31 per sale; the cost line starts at R1 800 and climbs at R16; they cross at 120 — the crossing IS the filled hole.

And the what-if questions are just new shovels: ingredients rise to R19? The shovel shrinks to R12, and the hole needs 150 sales. Raise the price to R34? Shovel R18, count 100 — but will the taxi rank still buy at R34? That last sentence — the realism check — completes the answer, because mathematics that ignores the customer is bookkeeping, not business.

Here are this section's questions — name the hole, size the shovel, count the shovelfuls, then question the price.

## Subtopic: Slow Money — Interest, Inflation and the Long Game

The last pieces of finance move slowly, which is exactly why they are misunderstood — and why the final examination checks who truly gets them.

Two savings accounts, both starting with R8 000 at 10 percent. The simple account pays interest on the original only: R800 a year, like a salary that never gets a raise — after three years, R10 400. The compound account pays interest on everything so far: 8 800 after year one, then 10 percent of 8 800 — R880 — ending year two at 9 680, then R968 more, closing year three at 10 648. Same rate, same years — R248 extra, earned by interest working on interest. Stretch the years and the gap stops being polite: compounding is a snowball, and the hill is time. The expected working is the table, year by year, and the sentence naming compound as the better saver WITH the gap quoted.

Inflation is the same snowball rolling against you. At 5 percent, the R320 jersey costs R336 next year, and the year after that the 5 percent bites the NEW price. Your money stands still; prices compound past it. That is why a salary that never rises is quietly a salary that shrinks — and why interpretation questions ask what inflation does to buying power. The answer: the same rand buys less each year, because prices grow compound.

The slow-money habits for exam day: build the year-by-year table — the visible method survives an arithmetic slip; never apply a multi-year percentage in one lazy multiplication unless the question says simple; and end with the comparison sentence — which option, by how much, shown by the table.

Read the last questions of the lesson now — grow both accounts, inflate one price honestly, and let the tables do the talking.
