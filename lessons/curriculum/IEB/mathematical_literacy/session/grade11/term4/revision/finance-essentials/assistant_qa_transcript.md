**Bianca:** Revision questions from the class! First: multi-step payslip questions — a raise changes the gross, and then I lose track of which numbers to recalculate. What is the safe order?

**Tutor:** Work the payslip top-down, exactly as it is printed, and let the gross drive everything below it. Step one: apply the change to the gross alone — a 5 percent raise turns R11 200 into 11 200 times 1,05, which is R11 760. Step two: rerun every PERCENTAGE deduction on the new gross, because percentages resize automatically — UIF becomes 1 percent of 11 760, R117,60, and pension becomes 7,5 percent of 11 760, R882. Step three: leave rand-amount deductions alone unless the question changes them — a fixed union fee stays fixed; tax usually changes, but only if the question hands you the new figure or a tax table. Step four: only now subtract for the new net. The classic error is subtracting the old deductions from the new gross — stitching two different payslips into one. The defence is a two-column layout: old payslip on the left, new on the right, every line recalculated or consciously carried across. That layout IS the visible method, and it turns the final comparison sentence — how much better off she is — into a one-line subtraction between the columns.

***

**Bianca:** Next: stepped tariffs. I understand the blocks, but the wording first 150 units, thereafter, tangles my subtraction. How do I set it up without slipping?

**Tutor:** Build the ladder as a small table before touching the calculator, one row per block, three columns: how many units the block holds, the rate, and the block cost. Read the tariff sentence and fill the capacity column first: the first block holds 150 units at R1,95; the thereafter block holds whatever remains. Then place the household's usage: 260 units fills the first block completely — write 150, not 260 — and spills 260 minus 150, so 110 units, into the second row. Now multiply across each row: 150 times 1,95 gives R292,50; 110 times 2,85 gives R313,50. Total the last column: R606. The table defends you against both classic slips: charging all 260 units at one rate, and forgetting to subtract the first block before pricing the second. It also scales — a three-step water tariff simply adds a row. And when a question flips direction, giving you the bill and asking for the usage, the same table runs backward: subtract the first block's R292,50 from the bill, divide the remainder by the second rate, and add the 150 back. One structure, both directions.

***

**Bianca:** Third: exchange rates. Multiply or divide — I second-guess myself every single time. Is there a check that settles it?

**Tutor:** Treat the rate as a price tag and let size do the checking. R20,25 per euro means one euro COSTS R20,25 — the euro is the item, the rand is the money. Buying 80 euros is buying 80 items: 80 times 20,25 gives R1 620 — multiplication, exactly like buying 80 loaves of bread. Asking how many euros R1 215 buys is asking how many items your money affords: 1 215 divided by 20,25 gives 60 — division, like counting loaves. Then run the size check, which catches every inversion: while a euro costs more than a rand, the rand figure in any correct conversion must be the BIGGER number. If your working says 80 euros becomes R3,95, the direction was wrong, and the check caught it before anyone else did. The interpretation half needs one more anchor: when the rate falls from R20,25 to R19,60, each euro now costs fewer rand, so the rand has strengthened — and imported goods, priced in euros, tend to become cheaper locally. Rate down means rand up; say it with the word because and both numbers, and the interpretation sentence is complete.

**Bianca:** Last one: the compound interest table. Why is year-by-year working expected when one calculation could jump straight to the answer?

**Tutor:** Because the table proves you understand the mechanism, and the mechanism is what Grade 11 assesses — compound growth as interest earning interest, not as a formula recalled under pressure. Watch what the table exposes: R8 000 at 10 percent ends year one at R8 800. Year two's interest is 10 percent of 8 800 — R880, already bigger than last year's R800 — closing at R9 680. Year three earns R968, closing at R10 648. Each row's interest grows because the base grew, and THAT visible growth is the understanding being assessed. The table also carries three practical protections. Visible method survives arithmetic slips — anyone following your rows can credit correct structure even where one multiplication stumbled. Comparison questions become trivial — simple interest's flat R800 rows against compound's climbing rows, gap R248 after three years, read straight off the page. And interpretation questions — why does the gap widen every year — are answered by pointing at your own table: because each year's interest is calculated on a larger amount than the year before. One calculation gives an answer; the table gives an argument, and final examinations reward arguments.
