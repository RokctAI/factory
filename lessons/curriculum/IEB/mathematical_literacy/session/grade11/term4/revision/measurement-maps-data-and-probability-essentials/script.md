# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the space-shape-and-chance revision sweep for the final examination — the year's measurement, maps, data and probability work, rebuilt around one project: Lesedi's family fixing up the granny flat behind their house in Mamelodi, and the choices the fix-up forces. Tiles to buy by the box, paint by the litre, a builder's plan to read at 1 to 50, the street map to the hardware store, the quotes to compare — and, threading through it all, the data and probability tools that turn measurements and surveys into defendable decisions.

## Subtopic: Conversions, Area and the Cost of Materials

Measurement at Grade 11 level is costing: every length and area ends in a rand amount, and the path always runs convert, calculate, round UP, price.

Conversions first, because they gatekeep everything. The metric ladder moves by tens: 4,7 kilometres is 4 700 metres; 60 millimetres is 6 centimetres; and for volume, one cubic metre holds 1 000 litres. The renovation rule: all measurements into the SAME unit before any multiplication, and into the unit the PRICE uses before any costing.

Now the flat's main room: 5,2 metres by 3,6 — an area of 18,72 square metres. Tiles sell by the box, each box covering 1,6 square metres: 18,72 over 1,6 gives 11,7 boxes. Shops do not sell 0,7 of a box, and the floor cannot go short — so round UP: 12 boxes, at R210 each, costing R2 520. Rounding up here is not a rounding preference; it is a real-world constraint, and an answer of 11,7 boxes, or a rounded-down 11, fails the floor. The same logic buys paint: a wall face of 5,4 metres by 2,5 is 13,5 square metres; two coats doubles the job to 27; coverage of 9 square metres per litre demands 3 litres — and if tins hold 5 litres, one tin, with the reasoning written out.

Estimation is the companion skill: before calculating, a one-line estimate — the floor is roughly 5 by 3,5, call it 18 or so square metres — guards against calculator slips, and estimate-then-check is exactly the behaviour long questions reward.

The four-beat rhythm to rehearse: same units, formula and calculation, round up to whole purchasable units, multiply by price. Every materials question you meet is those four beats in some order.

This section's questions are with you now — one ladder climbed, one floor tiled, one wall painted, everything priced.

## Subtopic: Scales, Street Maps and Building Plans

Two scales rule the year: the map scale that shrinks a suburb onto a page, and the plan scale that shrinks a house.

The street map to the hardware store is drawn at 1 to 25 000. The route measures 6,4 centimetres: multiply out — 160 000 centimetres — then tame the number: 1,6 kilometres. Map skills stack around that calculation: compass directions — the store sits south-east of the flat; grid references to pin down a street; and route-description questions, which want the turns and landmarks in order, read off the map like spoken instructions.

The builder's plan uses 1 to 50: every 1 millimetre on paper is 50 in the room. A wall drawn 84 millimetres long is really 4 200 millimetres — 4,2 metres. Reverse gear: a wardrobe 1,6 metres wide, 1 600 millimetres, appears on the plan as 1 600 over 50 — 32 millimetres. Plans carry their own literacy: the symbol conventions for doors, windows and fittings; floor plans for layout and widths; elevations for heights and window positions; and the bar scale, which survives photocopying when a number scale silently lies — measure the bar, compare it to its labelled length, and the true scale emerges.

The favourite integrated move: plan to materials. Read the bedroom's dimensions off the 1 to 50 plan — say 50 millimetres by 64 becomes 2,5 metres by 3,2 — then cost its flooring at 8 square metres. One question, two topics, and the scale conversion feeding the area calculation is where the credit concentrates.

Models and assembly diagrams complete the family: a scale model shrinks all three dimensions by the same factor, and an instruction diagram is a plan of ORDER — parts counted against the list before building, sequence respected during it.

Take this section's questions now — one map distanced, one plan read in both directions, one room costed from paper.

## Subtopic: Data — Summarising, Comparing and Choosing the Honest Middle

Grade 11 data work compresses a list into summary numbers, then asks which summary tells the truth.

The family collected seven quotes for waterproofing the flat's roof, in rand hundreds: 13, 17, 17, 20, 23, 24, 26. The mean: total 140 over 7 — 20. The median: the middle of the ordered list, the fourth value — 20. The mode: 17, the repeated quote. The range: 26 minus 13 — 13, the spread. When mean and median agree, as here, the data is balanced; when they split, an outlier is pulling the mean, and the median becomes the honest middle. That judgement — which measure represents the data, and why — is the section's signature question, and it always wants the reason alongside the choice.

Comparison is the second signature: two data sets, one verdict. Contractors A and B both average 20, but A's quotes range across 13 while B's huddle within 5. Same centre, different spread — and spread is reliability: B's price is predictable, A's is a gamble. Comparing sets means comparing BOTH a centre and a spread, and saying what each comparison means in context.

Representation ties the year together: bar graphs and pie charts for categories, histograms for grouped data, broken-line graphs for change over time — each with the reading disciplines of axis, scale and units. And the classification beneath it all: categorical data like brands and colours takes a mode but never a mean; numerical data takes the full toolkit. Choosing an impossible summary — the average favourite colour — is a classification error that questions plant deliberately.

The defence habit: every summary quoted with its working, every comparison sentence naming both statistics, every graph read scale-first.

Work through this section's questions — one set summarised, two sets compared, one impossible average refused.

## Subtopic: Probability — Simple Events, Compound Events and Real Decisions

Probability quantifies uncertainty on the scale from 0 to 1, and Grade 11's upgrade is the COMPOUND event — two things happening together.

Simple events first, as revision: one spin, one draw, one bus. Favourable outcomes over total outcomes, written as a fraction, decimal or percentage. The estimated version from data: relative frequency, steadying as observations grow.

Compound events need the full outcome space, and two tools draw it. Tool one: the two-way table. The 50 learners in Lesedi's class: 30 come by minibus and 20 walk; of the minibus riders, 15 are girls; of the walkers, 10 are. The table's margins total everything, and probabilities read straight off it: the chance a randomly chosen learner rides the minibus is 30 over 50 — 0,6. The chance of girl AND minibus — the joint event — is the inner cell over the grand total: 15 over 50, three tenths. Two-way tables turn AND questions into cell-reading.

Tool two: the tree diagram, for events in sequence. Load-shedding this evening has probability 0,4; if the power goes, supper is late with probability 0,5. The branch power-off-then-late carries 0,4 times 0,5 — 0,2. Multiplying along branches computes the compound chance; adding across branches collects alternatives. A spinner with four equal sectors, one of them red, draws the simplest single-event picture: one favourable sector in four — 0,25.

The interpretation duties that finish the topic: probabilities never promise — 0,2 means late-supper-after-load-shedding in roughly 20 of 100 such evenings, not a schedule; complementary events split the certainty of 1, so power-stays-on carries 0,6; and games or promotions described in a question should be judged fair or unfair by comparing the computed chances, with the verdict written out.

The final questions of this part are with you now — one table read, one tree multiplied, one fairness verdict delivered.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same granny flat, same street — now walked through with a shopping list, a folded plan and a healthy suspicion of promises.

## Subtopic: Buy Enough, Not Too Much

Every materials question is the same kitchen-table worry: buy too little and the job stalls; buy too much and the money is wasted. The mathematics is the referee.

The main room's floor: 5,2 by 3,6 — 18,72 square metres of tiling to do. Boxes cover 1,6 square metres each, so the job needs 11,7 boxes. And here is the whole topic in one decision: you cannot buy 0,7 of a box, and 11 boxes leaves bare screed in front of the window. Twelve boxes. Always UP, never nearest — the shop rounds your change in its favour; you round the boxes up in the floor's favour. Twelve at R210: R2 520 on the list.

Paint tells the same story with one extra step: the wall is 5,4 by 2,5 — 13,5 square metres — but two coats means the roller travels 27. At 9 square metres per litre, that is 3 litres, and if the shop sells 5-litre tins, one tin does it with two litres left for scuff marks. The extra step — coats — is exactly the kind of quiet detail questions bury in the middle of a sentence, and finding it is the reading half of the answer.

Units guard the whole list: prices per square metre want areas in square metres; a room measured in millimetres off a plan must become metres BEFORE meeting the price. And before any calculator work, the estimate: floor about 5 by 3,5, so around 18 square metres — if the calculator then says 187,2, a decimal slipped somewhere.

Measure, match units, round up to what the shop actually sells, price it. That list wins hardware-store arguments and exam answers with the same four moves.

The questions for this piece are with you now — tile one floor, paint one wall, and catch one buried detail.

## Subtopic: The Plan Is the House, Folded Small

A builder's plan is the house photocopied smaller by an exact factor, and 1 to 50 just names the fold: everything real is 50 times the paper.

Reading it is un-folding. The wall drawn at 84 millimetres? Un-fold: 84 times 50 — 4 200 millimetres — 4,2 metres of real brick. Planning where the new wardrobe goes runs the fold forward: 1,6 metres of wardrobe, 1 600 millimetres, folds down to 32 on the page. Multiply to un-fold into reality, divide to fold reality onto paper — and the sanity check never fails: reality is always the big number.

The map is the suburb folded smaller still — 1 to 25 000 — and the route to the hardware store un-folds from 6,4 centimetres to 1,6 kilometres. Maps add their own language on top: north at the top of the sheet, the store south-east of the flat, and route questions wanting the turns in order — up the avenue, right at the garage, second left past the taxi rank. Reading a map aloud as instructions is exactly what the questions ask for in words.

Two drawings, two jobs: the floor plan answers across questions — will the bed fit, how wide is the doorway; the elevation answers up questions — window heights, the roofline. And when a plan meets a shopping list, the topics shake hands: read the bedroom as 50 by 64 millimetres, un-fold to 2,5 by 3,2 metres, and suddenly it is an 8 square metre flooring purchase — the previous subtopic wearing plan clothing.

One suspicion completes the skill: photocopiers shrink number scales into liars, but a bar scale shrinks along with the drawing and keeps telling the truth. Trust the bar.

Your questions for this section are up — un-fold one wall, fold one wardrobe, and route one trip aloud.

## Subtopic: Quotes, Chances and Promises — Deciding with Numbers

The fix-up ends where all mathematics ends: deciding whom to trust, and by how much.

Seven waterproofing quotes came in, hundreds of rand: 13, 17, 17, 20, 23, 24, 26. The mean says 20; the median, fourth in the queue, also 20; the mode, 17, is just the popular guess. Balanced data, honest mean. But suppose an eighth quote arrives at 52 — some chancer trying his luck. The mean jumps to 24; the median creeps to 21,5. When one wild number joins, the median is the truthful middle, and saying so WITH the two values is the standard defence.

Choosing between contractors is centres AND spreads: both average 20, but one scatters across 13 while the other huddles within 5. Same expected price, different risk — and the huddled contractor is the safer promise. Spread is trust, written as a number.

Probability judges the promises directly. The neighbours say load-shedding always ruins supper. How often does that actually bite? Power off this evening: 0,4. Supper late, given power off: 0,5. Both together: 0,4 times 0,5 — 0,2 — call it 20 evenings in a hundred. Real, but not the nightly disaster the complaint made it sound. The two-way table does the same for claims about people: of 50 learners, 30 ride the minibus, 15 of them girls — so girl-and-minibus is 15 over 50, three tenths, read straight from a cell, no argument possible.

And the tuck shop's scratch-card promotion — one winning card in every four? A four-sector spinner models it: four equally likely outcomes, one prize, 0,25. If the winners the class actually sees come far rarer than a quarter, the data has voted against the promise, and the numbers — not the poster — deserve the trust.

Read the last questions of the lesson now — one wild quote absorbed, one risk compared, one promise audited. Numbers first, decisions second, every time.
