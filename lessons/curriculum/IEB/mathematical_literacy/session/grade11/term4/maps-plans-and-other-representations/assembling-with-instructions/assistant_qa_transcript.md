**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one comes up every time this topic appears: it feels like common sense rather than mathematics — what is a question actually testing when it shows a flat-pack diagram?

**Tutor:** Four genuinely mathematical skills, wearing cardboard. First, spatial interpretation: an exploded diagram is a three-dimensional object encoded on flat paper, and working out which part enters which hole from which side is the same representational skill as reading a floor plan or an elevation — that is why the topic lives among maps and plans. Second, sequencing: the numbered steps form a strict order, and questions ask you to re-order shuffled steps, justify why one must come before another, or predict what a violation breaks — logical reasoning with a physical consequence. Third, quantities and proportion: a parts list is counting, scaling a kit is multiplication, a shortfall is subtraction, and screws-per-learner across a class is ratio work that ends in rounding up to whole boxes. Fourth, cost analysis: self-assembly against paid assembly is a complete comparison-of-options question with fixed amounts, a rate per half hour and a percentage or two. So treat every assembly question as one of those four in disguise, name which one you are doing, and show the working the disguise was hiding. The everyday setting is deliberate — the point is proving you can carry mathematics out of the classroom and onto a lounge floor covered in parts.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the sequencing questions where the steps arrive shuffled and we must restore the order. If I have never built the thing in the picture, how do I find the order?

**Tutor:** By logic, not memory — every sequence question carries its own evidence, and three tests extract it. Test one: dependency. For each pair of steps, ask which one physically requires the other to be finished. A cam cannot lock before the bolt it grips is seated; a plug cover cannot close before the cores beneath it are connected. Dependencies chain steps together on their own. Test two: access. Ask what each step closes off. Nailing on the back panel seals the carcass's only open face, so anything that must enter through that face — the shelves — has to come earlier; in general, steps that close, cover or seal drift toward the end. Test three: reversibility. Fragile or finishing operations — standing the unit upright, hanging doors, pressing on the felt pads — belong last, because doing them early guarantees undoing them. Run the three tests and a shuffled list mostly sorts itself; where two steps genuinely commute, say so, because instructions sometimes allow parallel branches and a justified 'either order works' is a legitimate answer. And write the reason beside each placement — 'back panel after the shelves, because it seals their entry face' — since the reasoning is usually assessed alongside the order itself.

**Bianca:** This one always causes a stir: if a plug with brown and blue swapped still heats the iron, how would anyone ever know — and why do questions care so much about a mistake nobody notices?

**Tutor:** They care precisely BECAUSE nobody notices — the question is probing whether you understand that correct function is not proof of correct assembly. Follow the electricity and the danger appears. Wired correctly, the appliance's switch sits on the live core: switch off, and the current is stopped at the entrance — everything beyond it is dead. With brown and blue swapped, the switch sits on the neutral instead: switch off, and the flow still stops, so the iron behaves identically — but the element remains CONNECTED to the live side the whole time, energised and waiting. Someone who trusts the off position and touches the plate's workings, or a fault that lets that live path reach the casing, meets full mains voltage from an appliance that reads 'off'. In practice the fault is discovered only three ways: a deliberate visual check against the colour code before the cover closes, a tester screwdriver or multimeter, or the accident itself. That is why the code is regulation and why the checking habit — brown right to live, blue left to neutral, green-and-yellow to the tall earth pin, grip on the sheath — is assessed as knowledge rather than assumed as common sense. The transferable idea runs through the whole topic: 'it works' and 'it is right' are different claims, and only the second one protects people.

**Bianca:** Last one: in the cost comparison, some of us chose the warehouse's R450 assembly and then worried we had picked the 'wrong' answer, since self-assembly is cheapest. Is the cheapest option always the expected answer?

**Tutor:** No — the expected answer is the DEFENDED option, and assessment of these questions rewards the defence rather than the pick. The table itself is compulsory: self-assembly R2 799, warehouse R3 249, handyman R3 339, and the arithmetic lives there. But the final ask is a recommendation with a reason, and several reasons are acceptable when they engage the numbers. Choosing self-assembly: it saves R450, the kit includes the only tool needed, and two people are available for step 8 — a complete argument. Choosing the warehouse: the R450 buys skilled hands for a step that risks a R520 door panel, and for a household with no second builder, insurance costing less than the part it protects is rational — also complete. What earns nothing is a naked choice: 'the warehouse, because it is easier' engages no number, and 'self, because it is cheapest' ignores the risk the question planted on purpose. Notice how the question is built: the saving, R450, sits just below the failure cost, R520, precisely so that both directions are defensible and only the reasoning separates answers. When a promotion complicates things — the free-assembly threshold at R3 000 — the same rule holds: compute the effective cost of the added lamp, R100, then say whether the household wanted a lamp at all. Numbers first, then judgement, then one sentence containing both. That sentence is the real answer; the table is its evidence.
