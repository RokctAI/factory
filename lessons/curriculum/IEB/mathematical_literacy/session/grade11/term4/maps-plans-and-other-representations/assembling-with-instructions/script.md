# Part 1 — Expert

A cupboard that arrives as two flat boxes, a plug that must go onto the cord of an iron, a bicycle carrier in forty pieces: sooner or later every household meets a heap of parts and a wordless folded sheet, and the sheet expects to be obeyed. The closing skill of the maps-and-plans work is exactly this — reading assembly instructions and diagrams accurately enough to build the object in the right order, with every part accounted for. This session builds two things from Pietermaritzburg lounge-floor reality: a flat-pack double-door cupboard from a furniture warehouse, and a three-pin plug wired onto an iron's cord.

## Subtopic: The Language of Instruction Diagrams

An instruction sheet is written for every language at once, which is why it uses almost none: the pictures carry the meaning. Its centrepiece is the EXPLODED DIAGRAM — the cupboard drawn as if it had gently burst apart at every joint, each panel and fitting hanging in mid-air exactly where it belongs, with thin lines tracing each dowel to its hole and each bolt to its socket. To read it, travel the lines: every line is a sentence saying this part, into this hole, from this side.

Around the big drawing sits a fixed grammar of symbols. Step numbers dictate SEQUENCE. A circle beside a joint is a magnified close-up asking for extra care. Arrows are the verbs — slide, rotate, push home. A crossed-out mallet forbids force. A screwdriver drawn next to a screw names the tool for the step, and a double-headed arrow with a figure fixes a gap that must be left. Where a part can be placed two ways, the sheet draws both, the wrong way crossed out — a mistake answered before it happens.

Orientation marks carry the most weight. THIS WAY UP arrows, a shaded face marking the front, and the letters L and R on parts that look like twins: the cupboard's two side panels are drilled as mirror images, and if they trade places, the shelf-peg holes end up facing the room instead of each other. The drawing tells them apart; a rushed glance does not.

Good instructions ask to be read twice — once completely, hands still, to learn where the build is going; then step by step with the parts in hand. The assessable skills are matching a written step to its picture, stating what a symbol commands, and predicting what breaks when it is ignored. The questions for this section are with you now — treat every diagram as a sentence with a part, a position, a movement and a warning.

## Subtopic: The Parts List and the Count Before Building

The first page of every kit is a PARTS LIST: each component drawn, coded and counted. The cupboard's list reads: side panels, code A, 2; doors, code B, 2; shelves, code C, 3; back panel, code D, 1; cam locks, 12; cam bolts, 12; wooden dowels, 10; hinges, 4; screws, 20; Allen key, 1.

Job one is an audit: count every item against that list BEFORE any assembly. The logic is the arithmetic of wasted time — a shortage discovered at step 10 costs the whole afternoon and possibly a partial dismantling, while the same shortage found before step 1 costs one phone call to the warehouse. Assessment turns this into counting with a sting: the box holds only 9 cam locks against a required 12 — how many are missing, and what can still go ahead? Three missing; each shelf locks with 4, so two shelves can be fully secured while the third must wait.

The list also drives proportion work. Three shelves at 4 cam locks each consume all 12 — the kit ships not one spare, so a single lock rolling under the couch halts the third shelf as surely as a factory error. Scaling questions build on the same list: two cupboards double every quantity except the lone Allen key; a technology class of 25 learners using 18 screws each needs 450 screws, and with screws sold in boxes of 40, that is 11,25 boxes — so 12 boxes are bought, because eleven boxes deliver only 440 and leave the class ten screws short.

The checking routine is itself part of the content: tick each component on the list as it is verified, keep small fittings sealed in their packet until their step arrives, and lay the large parts out in step order. Method credit sits in the audit, the subtraction of the shortfall, and the sentence naming what the shortage stops. Your questions for this section are ready — count first, build second.

## Subtopic: Order Matters: Following the Sequence

Steps are numbered because assembly runs in ONE direction: many steps become impossible once the steps after them are done. The cupboard's sequence: press dowels into the side panels; screw cam bolts into the shelf edges; hang the shelves on one side panel and lock those cams; add the second side panel and lock again; square the frame; nail on the back panel; screw the hinges and hang the doors; stand the cupboard upright.

Violate the order and the kit punishes you in kind. Nail the back panel on early and the shelves lose their only entry route — the back seals the open face they slide through. Lock the cams before the second panel arrives and the bolts cannot find their sockets. Hang the doors before the frame is squared and neither door lines up with its partner, and the hinges strain until the chipboard tears. Stand the carcass up before the back is nailed and it leans into a parallelogram, because that thin back sheet is what holds the corners at ninety degrees. Every out-of-order move must be reversed, step by step, back to the mistake — which is why the shortcut is always the longest route through the box.

Wiring a plug onto the iron's cord teaches the same lesson with safety attached. The sequence: open the plug; slacken the cord grip; strip the outer sheath; strip and twist each core; seat each core in its terminal; clamp the cord grip onto the SHEATH, never the thin cores; close the cover. South Africa's colour code is regulation, not taste: BROWN to the LIVE terminal on the right, BLUE to NEUTRAL on the left, GREEN-AND-YELLOW to EARTH at the top, the tallest pin. A plug with brown and blue swapped will still heat the iron — and that is precisely the danger: the switch now sits on the neutral, so the element remains live even when the iron is switched off.

The assessable forms: re-order shuffled steps, justify why one step must precede another, and state the concrete consequence of a named violation — never "it won't work", always "the back panel seals the face the shelves enter through". The questions for this section follow now — sequence, reason, consequence.

## Subtopic: Time, Cost and the Do-It-Yourself Decision

Assembly is also a money decision, and it must be priced. The cupboard costs R2 499 flat-packed. Delivery is R300. The warehouse offers an assembly service at R450, and a local handyman charges R180 per half hour and quotes three half-hours. The only tool the build requires is the Allen key in the box, so self-assembly costs nothing in rands and an afternoon in time.

The options table falls out directly. Option one, self-assembly: 2 499 plus 300 gives R2 799. Option two, warehouse assembly: 2 499 plus 300 plus 450 gives R3 249. Option three, handyman: 2 499 plus 300 plus 3 times 180 — that is 540 — gives R3 339. Self-assembly saves R450 against the warehouse and R540 against the handyman, provided the build succeeds.

That proviso is where the judgement questions live. The sheet rates the cupboard three spanners out of five and prints two figures beside step 8: the second side panel needs four hands. One builder attempting it alone risks splitting a door panel, and a replacement door costs R520 plus a three-week wait — so the R450 saving is guarded by a R520 risk. A defensible answer weighs exactly that: who is available on the day, what a failure costs, and whether the saving covers the risk.

Percentages close the arithmetic. The R450 assembly service on a R2 499 cupboard is 450 divided by 2 499 times 100 — 18,0% of the item's price. And a warehouse promotion of free assembly on purchases over R3 000 sets a classic trap-question: adding a R550 desk lamp lifts the basket to R3 049, unlocks the R450 service for nothing, and the lamp effectively costs 550 minus 450 — R100. Whether that is a bargain depends entirely on whether anyone wanted a lamp, and saying so is part of the answer. The last questions of this part are with you now — build the options table, then defend one choice in a single sentence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same two boxes, same lounge floor — opened now the way a patient builder opens them.

## Subtopic: Pictures That Give Orders

There is not one full sentence on the instruction sheet, and it needs none, because every drawing is a command. The large picture of parts hanging apart in mid-air is the exploded view: the cupboard pulled open along its joints, each piece floating exactly where it will live, thin lines running from every dowel and bolt to its hole. Trace a line with your finger and the sheet has already answered what goes where.

The small marks are the grammar around it. Numbers are the order — step 4 waits for step 3, always. A circle hovering next to a joint is a magnifying glass: look closer here. Arrows are the verbs — slide, turn, press home. The crossed-out mallet says nothing gets hammered. And the paired tick-and-cross drawings show the same part seated rightly and wrongly, the wrong one struck through — your mistake, forbidden in advance.

The letters do the finest work on parts that look identical. The cupboard's two side panels are twins to the eye, but one wears an L and one an R, and their peg holes are drilled as mirror images. Swap them, and at step 7 you discover shelf holes gazing out at the room with nothing for the shelves to grip. The sheet knew all along; hurry didn't ask.

So the reading happens twice: once end to end with the parts untouched, to see where the whole build is heading, and then step by step as the pieces come together. Some questions on this section reach you now — say what each symbol commands, and what disobeying it would cost.

## Subtopic: Count Everything Before You Build Anything

Box open — and before anything clicks together, roll call. The parts list is the class register: every piece drawn, coded, counted. Two side panels. Two doors. Three shelves. One back panel. Twelve cam locks, twelve cam bolts, ten dowels, four hinges, twenty screws, one Allen key. Tick each one as it answers.

The strictness has a reason. A missing part found NOW costs a phone call; the same part missing at step 10 costs the afternoon and maybe some careful unbuilding. Ten minutes of counting insures the entire build.

The count also earns marks directly. Nine cam locks in the box, twelve on the list: three missing. Each shelf locks with four, so two shelves can be fully secured and the third stands empty until the warehouse sends the rest — that sentence, the shortfall plus what it stops, is the complete answer. And notice there are no spares: twelve locks for exactly twelve lockings, so one lock lost under the couch freezes the third shelf just as thoroughly as the factory shorting you.

Scaling keeps the same list honest. Two cupboards: double everything except the Allen key, which serves both without complaint. A technology class of 25 learners using 18 screws each needs 450; the hardware shop sells boxes of 40, so 11,25 boxes — buy 12, because eleven boxes arrive ten screws short and somebody's project stalls. Keep the small fittings sealed until their step, lay the panels out in step order, and the build starts half-won. Questions on this section are coming your way — audit, subtract, and name what the shortage stops.

## Subtopic: One Step at a Time, and What a Shortcut Costs

The step numbers are law inside the box. The cupboard goes: dowels into the sides, cam bolts into the shelves, shelves onto ONE side panel, lock, second panel on, lock again, square the frame, nail the back, hang the doors, stand it up. Each step stands on the shoulders of the one before.

Take the tempting shortcut and the kit teaches you manners. Nail that neat back panel on early and the shelves have no face left to slide in through — the back closes the only door. Hang the doors before the frame is squared and they meet at a slant, hinges straining. Stand it up before the back is on and the whole carcass leans into a parallelogram, because that flimsy-looking back sheet is what keeps the corners square. And every wrong-order move gets undone in reverse, step by step, before the build may continue — the shortcut is the longest road in the box.

The iron's plug lifts the stakes from furniture to safety. The colours are regulation: brown to live on the right, blue to neutral on the left, green-and-yellow to earth at the top, the tallest pin. Strip, twist, seat each core, then clamp the cord grip on the thick outer sheath — never on the thin cores, which the grip would bite through. Now the unsettling part: swap brown and blue and the iron still heats. Everything seems fine. But the switch now sits on the wrong wire, so the element stays live with the switch off — an invisible fault, waiting patiently. That is why the colour code gets checked against the sheet, never assumed from a working appliance.

Finally, the money. Cupboard R2 499, delivery R300. Build it yourself: R2 799. Warehouse assembles: add R450 — R3 249. Handyman: three half-hours at R180 — R540 — lands on R3 339. Doing it yourself saves R450 or more — IF step 8's four-handed lift actually has four hands, because a split door panel costs R520 and the saving vanishes with interest. Weigh the saving against the risk, choose, and say the reason with the numbers in it. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — steps in order, colours by regulation, and a decision defended in rands.
