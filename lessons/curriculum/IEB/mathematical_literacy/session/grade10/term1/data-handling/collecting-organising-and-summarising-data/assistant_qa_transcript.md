**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: our group got a mean of 36 minutes and a median of 30. Which one do we write down as "the answer"? Surely one of them has to be wrong.

**Tutor:** Neither is wrong — they answer different questions, and knowing which question is on the table is the skill being tested. The mean shares the total out equally: 540 minutes of queueing spread across 15 commuters gives 36 minutes each. That makes the mean the right tool whenever the TOTAL matters, because mean multiplied by the number of values rebuilds it — 36 times 15 hands back the full 540 minutes the queue consumed. The median ignores totals and reports position: line the fifteen commuters up, shortest wait to longest, and the eighth one stood for 30 minutes. That makes the median the right tool for the TYPICAL experience, because a few extreme values cannot move it far. Here three commuters waited 60, 72 and 96 minutes; they add plenty to the total, so they lift the mean, but they shift the median by nothing at all. So write both, then declare which one you trust and why: "The median wait was 30 minutes; the mean of 36 is higher because three unusually long waits inflate the total." That sentence earns marks on its own.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why can't we just group the times into intervals of nought to twenty, twenty to forty, forty to sixty? It looks so much tidier than nought to nineteen.

**Tutor:** Tidier to the eye and useless to the table, because the boundaries live in two intervals at once. A commuter who queued exactly twenty minutes fits "nought to twenty" and also fits "twenty to forty", and nothing tells you which row to use. Two people organising the same data would then produce two different tables — and the whole point of organising is that anyone following your rules lands on your counts. So intervals must cover every possible value and overlap nowhere. Two clean ways exist. For data recorded in whole minutes, use nought to nineteen, twenty to thirty-nine, forty to fifty-nine, and so on — every whole number has exactly one home. For data that can take any value, decimals included, use the "from, up to but not including" style: twenty up to but not including forty. Either convention works; mixing them does not. And whichever you pick, finish by totalling the frequency column against your sample size. Our five intervals hold four, six, two, two and one — fifteen altogether, and fifteen commuters were timed, so nothing was lost and nothing counted twice.

**Thandi:** This one confused the whole row: our teacher said the mode is sometimes useless and sometimes the only thing that works. How can both be true?

**Tutor:** Both are true because the mode's value depends entirely on the kind of data in front of it. The mode is the value occurring most often, and it is the ONLY measure of centre available for categorical data — labels rather than numbers. You cannot add "town, Bellville, Wynberg" and divide by three; there is no mean destination and no middle destination. But you can name the destination requested most often, and that is exactly what a route planner needs before deciding where to send the extra taxi, or a tuckshop needs before deciding which roll to stock deepest. Now the useless case. For continuous measurements — queue times, masses, distances — repeated values are often accidents of rounding. Had our fifteen waits all differed, there would be no mode at all; had two values tied, there would be two modes and no single answer. Our data delivered a clean mode of 30 minutes only because times were recorded to whole minutes and the queue moves in taxi-loads. So the rule: for categories, reach for the mode first; for measurements, treat it as a bonus observation and lead with the median or the mean. Writing that reasoning down is exactly the analysis step the marks sit on.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is really about our own project: how many commuters do we actually need, and does it matter WHO we time if we are going to average everything anyway?

**Tutor:** The who matters more than the how-many, and that surprises most people. A sample is a small group standing in for the whole population, and it stands in honestly only if every part of the population had a fair chance of being included. Time only the five o'clock rush and you capture the crush while missing the easy nine o'clock queue — your median then describes one hour of the day, not the morning. Time only the people who come to complain at the marshal's window and you have handpicked the longest waits; the data are guaranteed to look dreadful no matter what the rank does. Averaging repairs neither problem — the arithmetic faithfully summarises whatever you feed it, bias included. So spread the timing across the whole morning and take commuters as they come, not as you choose. On size: fifteen is enough for a school investigation, and small enough that one unusual commuter visibly moves the mean — which is exactly why you also report the median and the range. More values steady the picture, but a small fair sample beats a big crooked one every single time. Put all of that in the report; explaining how you guarded against bias earns real marks.
