# Part 1 — Expert

The queue marshal at a minibus-taxi rank in Khayelitsha asks a Grade 10 group to find out how long commuters really wait in the morning queue before boarding. The taxi association can pay for one extra taxi on the route at peak time, a second loading bay, or nothing at all — and it will decide on the evidence the class delivers. This session runs the DATA CYCLE end to end: posing a question worth asking, collecting and classifying the data, organising it into a tally and a frequency table, summarising it with the mean, median, mode and range — then choosing which summary actually answers the marshal's question.

## Subtopic: The Data Cycle and the Question Worth Asking

The data cycle has a fixed order, and strong answers name its stages: pose a question; collect the data; organise and classify it; summarise and represent it; analyse and interpret; and report an answer. Skip a stage and whatever conclusion follows is standing on air.

Stage one carries the most weight. "Is the queue bad?" is not a usable question — nobody can measure "bad". "How many minutes does a commuter stand in the queue between joining it and boarding a taxi, on a weekday morning?" is usable: it names what is measured, in which unit, on whom and when.

Classify the data before collecting it, because the classification steers everything downstream. NUMERICAL data are counts or measurements: DISCRETE when only separate whole values are possible, like the number of taxis waiting on the rank; CONTINUOUS when the value can land anywhere on a scale, like a queueing time of 23,5 minutes. CATEGORICAL data are labels, not numbers: the destination of the trip, the way a commuter pays, whether someone is a regular on the route.

Then the source. PRIMARY data are collected by you, for your question — the class at the rank with a stopwatch and a clipboard. SECONDARY data already exist, recorded by someone else, like the association's departure log book: cheap to use, but shaped by another purpose.

Finally, guard against BIAS. Timing only the five o'clock rush, or only the commuters who come to complain at the marshal's window, produces a sample that does not represent the population — every commuter the rank serves. A leading question — "Don't you agree the queue is a disaster?" — begs for the answer it wants. So the class times 15 commuters spread across a Thursday morning, reading joining and boarding times off their own stopwatch record rather than asking people to guess.

The questions for this section are with you now: name the stages in order, sort numerical from categorical, and say which sampling choices would have sunk the study.

## Subtopic: Tallies, Frequency Tables and Class Intervals

Fifteen queueing times come back, in the order recorded, in minutes: 30, 72, 15, 45, 24, 30, 12, 60, 20, 54, 30, 6, 28, 96, 18. Raw data in collection order say almost nothing, so the first act of organising is to ORDER them: 6, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 28, 30, 30, 30, 45, 54, 60, 72, 96.

A TALLY turns the list into counts: pass through the data once, striking a mark for each value in its group, and cross the fifth mark through the previous four so the marks read themselves in fives. The FREQUENCY is the total of the marks in that row.

Because queueing time is continuous, the values are grouped into CLASS INTERVALS of equal width that cover every value and never overlap: 0 to 19 minutes, 20 to 39, 40 to 59, 60 to 79, and 80 to 99. Now count. Zero to 19 holds 6, 12, 15 and 18 — frequency 4. Twenty to 39 holds 20, 24, 28, 30, 30 and 30 — frequency 6. Forty to 59 holds 45 and 54 — frequency 2. Sixty to 79 holds 60 and 72 — frequency 2. And 80 to 99 holds only 96 — frequency 1.

Check the table before trusting it: 4 plus 6 plus 2 plus 2 plus 1 equals 15, the number of commuters timed. A frequency column that does not total the sample size means a value was double-counted or dropped.

Two traps lurk here. Overlapping intervals — writing 0 to 20 and 20 to 40 — leave a commuter who waited exactly 20 minutes with two homes and no rule for choosing. And once data are grouped, the individual values are gone: the table remembers that six commuters waited between 20 and 39 minutes, but not that three of them waited exactly 30. Keep the ordered list; the median will need it.

Work through this section's questions now, and give the interval boundaries your full attention — that is where marks are quietly won and lost.

## Subtopic: Mean, Median, Mode and Range

Four summaries, four definitions, four calculations. Three measure CENTRE — one value standing for the whole group — and the fourth measures SPREAD.

The MEAN is the total divided by the number of values. Add the fifteen queueing times: 6 plus 12 plus 15 plus 18 plus 20 plus 24 plus 28 plus 30 plus 30 plus 30 plus 45 plus 54 plus 60 plus 72 plus 96 gives 540 minutes. Divide by 15 commuters: the mean is 36 minutes. In words: if the waiting had been shared out perfectly evenly, every commuter would have stood for 36 minutes.

The MEDIAN is the middle value of the ORDERED list — and ordering first is not optional. With 15 values, the middle position comes from the number of values plus one, divided by two: 16 divided by 2 gives position 8. Count to the eighth value in the ordered list: 6, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 28, and the eighth is 30. The median is 30 minutes. When the count is EVEN, the middle position falls between two values, and the median is the mean of those two.

The MODE is the value appearing most often. Thirty appears three times, more than anything else, so the mode is 30 minutes. The mode is the only measure of centre that also works for CATEGORICAL data — you cannot average "town, Bellville, Wynberg", but you can name the most common destination.

The RANGE is the largest value minus the smallest: 96 minus 6 gives 90 minutes. It measures how stretched the data are, not where they sit.

Three habits protect these marks. Show the substitution — write 540 divided by 15, not just 36. Keep the unit — the answer is 36 minutes, not 36. And round only at the end, to a sensible degree: a queueing time reported as 35,6667 minutes is arithmetic showing off, not information.

Take on this section's questions now: compute all four summaries, and be ready to state the median rule for an even number of values.

## Subtopic: Which Summary Answers Which Question

The class holds four numbers: mean 36 minutes, median 30, mode 30, range 90. They disagree, and the disagreement IS the finding.

The mean sits six minutes above the median because three commuters waited 60, 72 and 96 minutes. Those OUTLIERS drag the mean upward; the median barely feels them, because it only cares which value stands in the middle. So when data carry a few extreme values, the MEDIAN describes the typical experience better than the mean. That is exactly why income is reported as a median: a handful of very large salaries hoists the mean above what most people actually earn.

Match the summary to the question. If the marshal asks what a commuter can typically expect, quote the median: 30 minutes. If the association asks how much commuter time the queue swallows in total, use the mean, because mean times number of values rebuilds the total — 36 times 15 returns the full 540 minutes. If the route planner asks which destination to serve most, that is a mode question. And if the complaint is about unpredictability, the range answers it: 90 minutes between the luckiest and unluckiest commuter on one ordinary morning. State the range's weakness too — it is built from only two values, so a single unusual commuter can triple it.

Now report properly — in a sentence that answers what was asked. "On a Thursday morning the median queue time was 30 minutes, but waits ran from 6 to 96 minutes, with three commuters standing for an hour or more. The rank's problem is not the typical wait but the long tail, so the extra taxi should be placed in the slot where those long waits pile up rather than spread across the day." Numbers, then interpretation, then a recommendation the marshal can act on. That is a full-mark answer.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: justify a choice of summary in words, and explain what an outlier does to the mean but not to the median.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rank study from the kitchen table — asking a question you can actually answer, sorting the answers into piles, and finding the number that speaks for the whole queue.

## Subtopic: Asking a Question the Numbers Can Answer

Say you want to know whether your household spends too much getting around. "Do we spend too much on transport?" wins you an argument, not an answer, because nobody can measure "too much".

Now sharpen it: "How many rands did each person in this house spend on taxis and buses last month?" That one you can answer, because it pins down WHAT you are counting (rands), on WHOM (each person here) and WHEN (last month). Sharpening the question is where most projects live or die.

Next, know what kind of answers are coming back. Some are numbers you can add — rands spent, minutes queued, taxis counted. Others are only labels — the company someone rides with, the destination they queue for. You can add rands; you cannot add "MyCiTi plus Golden Arrow".

Then, who do you ask? Ask only the cousin with the office job in town and of course the transport bill looks huge — you handpicked the answer. Same at the rank: stand there only at five in the morning and you catch the crush while missing the quiet nine o'clock queue. Spread who you ask across the whole group, and never let the question lean — "Don't you agree the queue is a disaster?" is fishing, not asking.

And two ways to get numbers: collect them yourself with a stopwatch, or borrow numbers somebody already wrote down, like the association's log book. Yours fit your question exactly but cost you a morning; theirs are free but were written for someone else's reasons.

Questions on all of that are with you right now — open each one by asking whether the question could actually be settled with a number.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Washing

You never fold washing straight from the basket. You sort into piles first — shirts here, socks there — and only then do you know what you own. Data behave the same way.

The class came back with fifteen queue times, jumbled: 30, 72, 15, 45, 24, 30, 12, 60, 20, 54, 30, 6, 28, 96, 18. Useless in that order. So do the washing thing — line them up, smallest to biggest: 6, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 28, 30, 30, 30, 45, 54, 60, 72, 96. Already the story peeks out: a crowd of short-to-middling waits, and three that ran away completely.

Now the piles. Under twenty minutes: 6, 12, 15, 18 — 4 people. Twenty to just under forty: 20, 24, 28, 30, 30, 30 — 6 people, the big pile. Then two in the forties-and-fifties, two more at 60 and 72, and one lonely 96. As you sort, mark each value with a stroke, and let every fifth stroke lie across the four before it so the marks count themselves in fives. That is a tally, and the number each row ends on is the frequency.

Two rules keep the piles honest. Piles must not overlap — if one pile is "0 to 20" and the next is "20 to 40", where does a 20-minute wait live? Nowhere and everywhere at once. And add your frequencies when you finish: 4 plus 6 plus 2 plus 2 plus 1 must give 15, because 15 commuters were timed. If it does not, a sock has gone missing.

Small questions on sorting and counting are here now. Order first, then pile, then add the piles back up.

## Subtopic: The Middle, the Most, the Average and the Stretch

Four ways of answering "so how long do people wait?", and each says something different.

The AVERAGE is the sharing-out number: sweep all the minutes into one heap and share them equally. Total the fifteen waits and you get 540 minutes; share those among 15 people and each receives 36. So the average wait was 36 minutes — what everybody would have stood if the waiting were spread perfectly fairly.

The MIDDLE is whoever stands at the centre when everyone lines up shortest wait to longest. Fifteen people, so the eighth one is the middle — seven ahead, seven behind. Count along: 6, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 28, and there stands the eighth — 30 minutes. With an even number of people nobody is exactly in the middle, so you take the two middle ones and split the difference.

The MOST-OFTEN is the wait that turns up more than any other: 30 appears three times. This is the only one of the three that also works for words instead of numbers — you cannot average "town, Bellville, Wynberg", but you can say which destination came up most.

The STRETCH is the gap between the longest and the shortest: 96 take away 6 is 90 minutes. An hour and a half between the luckiest commuter and the unluckiest, on one ordinary morning.

Now the part that matters. Why is the average 36 but the middle only 30? Because three people stood for 60, 72 and 96 minutes, and those long waits haul the sharing-out number upward — the same way one cousin with a big contract phone hauls up the family's "average airtime spend" while everyone else buys R30 vouchers. The middle number does not get hauled anywhere, so it tells you what an ordinary commuter can expect. The textbooks call these the mean, the median, the mode and the range — and a full answer says WHICH one you used and WHY.

Your last questions are with you now. Say the number, hang the minutes or the rands on it, and close with one plain sentence about what the rank should actually do — because a number without a decision is only half the work.
