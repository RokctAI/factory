Hey, it's Thandi — four different graphs, each with its own rules, and a question that hands you one and says "interpret". If that feels like four topics wearing one name, I get it. But it's really one topic with four outfits.

Start with the choosing rule, because it settles nearly everything. Names or labels along the bottom? Bar graph, bars with gaps. Numbers grouped into bands along the bottom? Histogram, bars touching. Parts of one single whole? Pie chart. Months or days across the bottom? Line graph. That's the entire decision, made with one glance at the horizontal axis.

Pie charts carry exactly two calculations, and both fit in your pocket. Percentage to angle: multiply by 3,6, because 100 percent must fill 360 degrees. Angle to amount: put the angle over 360, then take that fraction of the total. And the trap that catches everyone — a pie chart on its own gives shares, never rands. Find the total on the page before you convert anything.

For line graphs, describe before you calculate. Say out loud what the line does — flat, rising, steepest here, highest there — and only then chase the numbers behind it. Described trends earn credit on their own, and describing first stops you reading the wrong month off the wrong axis.

Now the move that wins the most marks for the least work: criticising a graph. Three steps, in order. Name the feature — the axis starts at 390, not 0. Say what it does — it makes a small increase look huge. Give the honest number — the rise from 410 to 450 units is about 9,8 percent. Feature, effect, figure. Practise those three on any graph you pass in a newspaper.

One glance at the bottom axis, two pie formulas, describe before you calculate, and three moves for a criticism. That's the whole topic — now go at the practice questions. You've got this!
