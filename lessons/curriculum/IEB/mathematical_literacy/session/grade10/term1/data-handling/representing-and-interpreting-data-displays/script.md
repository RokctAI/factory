# Part 1 — Expert

A community newspaper in Kimberley lays out four pictures on one page: a bar graph of how learners get to school, a histogram of what they spend on data, a pie chart of a household's monthly income, and a line graph of one family's electricity use through the winter. Four displays, four different jobs — and one of them, before the page goes to print, will be quietly redrawn to make a small change look enormous. This session builds the four displays, the rule for choosing between them, and the checklist that catches a misleading graph before it catches you.

## Subtopic: Bar Graphs and Histograms — Cousins, Not Twins

A BAR GRAPH displays CATEGORICAL data — labels, not measurements. Each bar stands for one category, the bars are separated by GAPS, and the bars can be shuffled into any order without changing the meaning.

The transport survey covered 240 learners: 96 walk, 84 take a taxi, 36 take a bus, and 24 come by car. The bars stand at heights 96, 84, 36 and 24 on a vertical axis labelled "Number of learners", above a horizontal axis labelled "Mode of transport". Check the total: 96 plus 84 plus 36 plus 24 gives 240.

Read it properly. The tallest bar names the MODAL category — walking. Converting to a percentage makes it quotable: 96 out of 240 is 96 divided by 240, which is 0,4, so 40 percent of learners walk. The taxi accounts for 84 over 240, which is 35 percent.

A HISTOGRAM looks similar and is a different creature. It displays CONTINUOUS numerical data grouped into class intervals, so the bars TOUCH — because the intervals themselves touch — and the order is fixed, since the horizontal axis is a number line.

The data-spend survey of 80 learners gives: R0 up to R50, twelve learners; R50 up to R100, eighteen; R100 up to R150, twenty-six; R150 up to R200, sixteen; R200 up to R250, eight. Total: 12 plus 18 plus 26 plus 16 plus 8 gives 80. The tallest bar identifies the MODAL CLASS — R100 up to R150 — and mind the wording: with grouped data you may name the modal class, never a single modal value, because the individual amounts have left the picture.

One more reading skill: combining intervals. How many learners spend less than R100? Twelve plus eighteen gives 30 learners, which is 30 over 80 — 37,5 percent.

The first questions of the session are with you now — decide bar graph or histogram by asking whether the horizontal axis carries labels or numbers.

## Subtopic: Pie Charts — Reading Shares, Not Amounts

A PIE CHART shows how one whole is divided into parts. Each SECTOR is a fraction of the circle, and because a full revolution is 360 degrees, any sector's size follows from its percentage: percentage divided by 100, times 360 — which is the same as multiplying the percentage by 3,6.

A household with a monthly income of R15 000 divides it: food 30 percent, housing 20 percent, transport 15 percent, electricity and water 12 percent, school costs 10 percent, savings 8 percent, and airtime and data 5 percent. The percentages total 100, as they must.

Convert to angles. Food: 30 times 3,6 gives 108 degrees. Housing: 20 times 3,6 gives 72 degrees. Transport: 15 times 3,6 gives 54 degrees. Electricity and water: 12 times 3,6 gives 43,2 degrees. School: 10 times 3,6 gives 36 degrees. Savings: 8 times 3,6 gives 28,8 degrees. Airtime and data: 5 times 3,6 gives 18 degrees. Add them: 108 plus 72 plus 54 plus 43,2 plus 36 plus 28,8 plus 18 gives exactly 360 degrees. That check is compulsory.

Now run it the other way, because questions ask that direction more often. A sector measures 54 degrees; what does it cost the household? Its share is 54 over 360, which is 0,15 — so 15 percent of R15 000, which is R2 250 on transport. Food at 108 degrees is 108 over 360, or 0,30 — R4 500.

Two limitations must be stated. First, a pie chart shows PROPORTIONS only: unless the R15 000 total is printed somewhere, no sector can be turned into rands. Second, two pie charts cannot be compared slice for slice unless their totals match — a 30 percent food slice of R15 000 is R4 500, while a 30 percent food slice of R7 500 is only R2 250, and the two slices look identical.

Turn to this section's questions now: convert both ways, and always confirm your angles land on a full revolution.

## Subtopic: Line and Broken-Line Graphs — Change Over Time

A LINE GRAPH puts time on the horizontal axis and shows how a quantity travels across it. Where the quantity is genuinely continuous — temperature through a day — a solid line means something at every point. Where readings exist only at separate moments — a monthly purchase record — the points are joined by a BROKEN-LINE graph, and the joining lines show the trend rather than claiming real values in between.

A household in Bethlehem in the Free State buys prepaid electricity each month: January 250 units, February 230, March 260, April 320, May 410, June 450.

Read off the four things a line graph is always asked for. The MAXIMUM: 450 units in June. The MINIMUM: 230 units in February. The TREND: roughly level from January to March, then climbing hard from April into winter as heaters and early darkness arrive. And the STEEPEST SECTION, where change is fastest — April to May rises by 410 minus 320, which is 90 units, against May to June's 450 minus 410, only 40 units. April to May is the steepest climb, even though June is the highest point.

Then quantify. Total units across the six months: 250 plus 230 plus 260 plus 320 plus 410 plus 450 gives 1 920 units. The increase from January to June is 450 minus 250, which is 200 units — as a percentage of January, 200 divided by 250 gives 0,8, an 80 percent increase. And at a tariff of R2,60 per unit, June's electricity cost 450 times R2,60, which is R1 170,00.

That last step is the whole point of the display: the graph shows the shape, the arithmetic prices it, and the household now knows June will cost nearly double what January did.

The questions for this section are ready — describe the trend in words first, then back the description with a calculated figure.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Display — and Catching a Misleading One

Choose by asking what the data ARE and what the question wants. Categories to compare — bar graph. Continuous measurements grouped into intervals — histogram. Parts of one whole, where shares matter more than amounts — pie chart. A quantity tracked over time — line or broken-line graph.

Every honest display carries the same five things: a title, a label on each axis, the unit of measurement, an evenly spaced scale, and a source. Any one missing is a legitimate criticism to raise in an answer.

Now the newspaper's trick. The electricity figures for May and June — 410 and 450 units — are redrawn on a vertical axis that starts at 390 instead of 0. June's bar now stands three times as tall above the axis as May's, and the headline reads "Winter electricity use triples". The real increase is 40 units on 410, which is 40 divided by 410 — about 9,8 percent. Nothing tripled. A TRUNCATED VERTICAL AXIS is the single most common way of misleading with a graph, and naming it earns the mark.

The rest of the checklist. Uneven spacing on an axis, so equal gaps stand for unequal amounts. Three-dimensional effects, where a tilted pie makes the front slice look bigger than an equal slice at the back. Pictograms where an image doubles in height AND width, so a doubling reads as a quadrupling in area. A time window trimmed to end at a convenient month. Percentages quoted without the total, so "a 100 percent increase" hides a rise from two learners to four. And any axis with no label at all — that is not a graph, only a shape.

Answer the way full credit is earned: identify the feature, explain the distortion it creates, and supply the honest figure. "The vertical axis begins at 390 rather than 0, which exaggerates the difference between the bars. The actual increase from 410 to 450 units is about 9,8 percent, not a tripling." Feature, effect, correct number.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: choose the right display with a reason, and criticise a graph by naming the feature and giving the honest figure.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same four pictures from the kitchen table — because a graph is only a way of showing numbers so a person can see them without counting.

## Subtopic: Bars With Gaps, Bars That Touch

Think of bars as piles. You are counting how the learners at your school arrive, so you build a pile for walkers, a pile for taxi riders, a pile for the bus, a pile for cars. Ninety-six walk, 84 take a taxi, 36 ride the bus, 24 come by car — and adding those gives 240, everybody counted once.

Draw each pile as a bar, and leave a GAP between bars. The gaps carry meaning: walkers and taxi riders are different kinds of people, not different amounts of the same thing, so their bars must not touch. You could swap the walkers' bar with the bus bar and the picture would still be true — which is exactly how you know these are separate categories.

The tallest bar says what most people do: walk. As a percentage? 96 out of 240 — that is 96 shared over 240, which is 0,4, so 40 out of every 100 learners. Forty percent.

Now the cousin that looks the same and is not. Ask 80 learners what they spend on data in a month and you get rands back, not names. Rands run in a line — R49 and R51 are next-door neighbours, not different tribes. So you group them into bands: under R50, R50 to R100, R100 to R150, and so on, and the bars TOUCH, because the bands touch. Twelve, eighteen, twenty-six, sixteen and eight learners — adding to 80. The busiest band is R100 to R150.

The one-second test: look along the bottom. Words down there mean gaps. Numbers running in order down there mean the bars touch.

Some quick questions on the two kinds of bars are here for you now — check the bottom axis first, every time.

## Subtopic: Slices of the Same Plate

A pie chart is a plate of pap. There is only ever ONE plateful, and the whole question is how it gets shared.

A family brings in R15 000 a month. The biggest slice, nearly a third, is food: 30 out of every 100 rands, which is R4 500. A fifth of the plate goes to the roof over their heads — 20 percent, R3 000. Transport takes 15 percent, R2 250. Lights and water, 12 percent, R1 800. School things, 10 percent, R1 500. Savings, 8 percent, R1 200. Airtime and data, 5 percent, R750. Add the rands: they come back to R15 000. Add the percentages: they come back to 100. If either total misses, a slice was measured wrong.

Turning a slice into an angle is one small step. A full plate spun all the way round is 360 degrees, and 100 percent is the whole plate, so each 1 percent is 3,6 degrees. Food at 30 percent is 30 lots of 3,6 — 108 degrees. Housing at 20 percent is 72 degrees — a fifth of the way round. All the angles together must land on 360.

And here is the catch that costs marks. A pie chart never tells you rands by itself — only shares. If your neighbour's food slice is also 30 percent but she brings in R7 500, her slice looks exactly the same size as this family's and buys half the groceries. Same picture, half the food. So before converting any slice into money, hunt for the total written somewhere on the page.

Questions on slices are with you right now. Percentage times 3,6 for the angle; percentage of the total for the rands.

## Subtopic: The Winter Line, and the Graph That Lies

Last picture: a line walking across the months. A family in Bethlehem buys prepaid electricity — 250 units in January, 230 in February, 260 in March, then 320, then 410, then 450 in June. Plot each month as a dot, join the dots, and the line does something you can feel: flat through summer, then climbing hard into the Free State winter.

Read it like a road. The lowest point is February, 230 units. The highest is June, 450. The steepest climb is April to May, where the line jumps 90 units in a single month — steeper than May to June's 40. And the money follows the line: at R2,60 a unit, June cost 450 times R2,60 — R1 170,00. From January to June, use climbed from 250 to 450 units — an extra 200, which is 80 percent more. That is a household that must start putting money aside in March, not in June.

Now the ugly part, and it happens in real newspapers. Take only May and June — 410 and 450 — and draw the bars on an axis that starts at 390 instead of 0. Suddenly May is a stump and June towers over it, and someone types "electricity use triples". It did not. It rose 40 units on 410, which is about 10 out of every 100 — roughly 10 percent. The picture lied by chopping the bottom off the axis.

So before trusting any graph, run the same checks you would run on a till slip: where does the scale START, are the gaps on the axis even, does every axis carry a label and a unit, and is the total given? A tilted three-dimensional pie makes the front slice look fatter. A picture drawn twice as tall AND twice as wide looks four times as big. It is all one trick — making your eyes do a calculation the numbers refuse to support.

Your last questions are with you now. Name the feature that misleads, say what it does to the reader, then give the honest number — those three moves together are the full answer.
