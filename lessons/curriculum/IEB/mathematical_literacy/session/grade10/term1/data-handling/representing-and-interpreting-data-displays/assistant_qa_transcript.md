**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: a bar graph and a histogram look identical to me apart from the little gaps. Does it honestly cost marks if I draw the bars touching when they should have gaps?

**Tutor:** It does, and the reason matters more than the mark. The gaps are not decoration; they are a statement about the kind of data on display. A bar graph shows categories — walk, taxi, bus, car — and there is nothing between "taxi" and "bus", no halfway mode of transport, so the space between bars honestly pictures that emptiness. You could swap any two bars and the information would not change, which is another sign that the horizontal axis is a list, not a scale. A histogram shows measurements grouped into bands — R0 up to R50, R50 up to R100 — and there is nothing between those bands, because every rand in between already has a home. The bars touch because the intervals touch, and their order is fixed, because the axis is a number line. So when a marker sees touching bars above category names, the message received is that you did not recognise which kind of data you were handed. Glance along the bottom axis before drawing the first bar: words mean gaps, numbers in order mean touching.

**Thandi:** Next, and this one caused an argument: someone claimed the pie chart is always the clearest graph because you see the shares instantly. True?

**Tutor:** True for one narrow job and misleading everywhere else. A pie chart answers exactly one question well: how is a single whole divided? For the household earning R15 000 a month, seeing that food takes nearly a third and housing a fifth is instant understanding — no other display delivers it that fast. Now the weaknesses. Human eyes judge angles badly: an 18 percent slice and a 21 percent slice look like twins, while two bars of those heights are clearly different, so pie charts lose precision exactly where bar graphs keep it. Pie charts cannot show change over time — you would need a fresh pie for every month, and comparing pies is unreliable unless every total matches, which household incomes never do. They cannot show more than one group at once. And they collapse under many small categories, because a dozen thin slivers can be neither read nor labelled. Add the standing trap: a pie chart carries no amounts at all — without the R15 000 printed on the page, no slice converts to rands. So use a pie when the question is shares of one whole; reach for a bar graph to compare sizes, and a line graph to show change.

**Thandi:** This one worries me for tests: how do I answer "comment on the trend" without just writing "it goes up"? Surely that's worth more than one mark.

**Tutor:** It is, and there is a structure that gathers all of it. Describe in four layers. Layer one, the overall direction: use rose overall from January to June. Layer two, the shape of the rise, because trends are rarely smooth: broadly level from January to March, then climbing steeply from April onward. Layer three, the named extremes with their values: the lowest was 230 units in February, the highest 450 units in June. Layer four, the steepest section — not the same thing as the highest point, and the layer most people miss: from April to May use rose 90 units, more than any other month-to-month change, including May to June's 40. Then quantify the whole movement — 450 minus 250 is an extra 200 units, which is 200 divided by 250, an 80 percent increase on January. Finally give the reason the context offers, because this subject always wants the story: the climb matches the arrival of winter, with heaters running and darkness falling earlier. Direction, shape, extremes, steepest section, a calculated figure, and a plausible reason. Write those six and a one-line "it goes up" becomes a full-credit paragraph.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it's a bit cheeky: if a truncated axis is so misleading, why do real newspapers and even science textbooks use one? Is it always dishonest?

**Tutor:** Not always — and seeing the difference is what separates a thoughtful reader from a merely suspicious one. Sometimes starting at zero HIDES the information. Body temperature across a week sits between 36 and 38 degrees; drawn from zero, the line lies flat and says nothing, while the two degrees that matter medically vanish. Exchange rates, rainfall and blood pressure share the problem: the interesting variation is tiny next to the size of the numbers. So a truncated axis is a legitimate tool when the small variation IS the subject and the graph announces the truncation — a clearly labelled axis, often with a break symbol showing where the scale jumps. Dishonesty needs three ingredients together: the truncation is unsignalled, the display is a BAR graph, where the eye reads the whole bar as the quantity, and the words alongside claim a change the numbers cannot support. That is our newspaper exactly: bars rising from 390, no break shown, and a headline shouting "triples" at what is really about a 9,8 percent rise. So in a test, never write "the axis does not start at zero" as though that alone were a crime. Say what the truncation does to the reader, and give the honest figure. That is the answer of someone who understands graphs, not someone reciting a rule.
