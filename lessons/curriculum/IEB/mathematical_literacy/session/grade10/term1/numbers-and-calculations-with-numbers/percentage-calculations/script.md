# Part 1 — Expert

Percentage calculations are the hardest-working tool in Mathematical Literacy: they power VAT, discounts, interest, inflation and tariffs, and they will follow you through every finance question from now until your final exams. This session builds the method as one connected line — percentage as a decimal, the single multiplier, two changes chained on one item, the single overall percentage the whole chain amounts to, and finally the chain run in reverse to recover a price before VAT. The case study looks harmless and hides a famous surprise: a pair of sneakers priced at R800 is discounted by 15 percent, and then 15 percent VAT is added to the discounted price. What is the final price — and what single percentage change from the original does the whole journey represent?

## Subtopic: What a Percentage Really Is — and the Decimal Trick

A percentage is nothing more than a fraction whose denominator is locked at 100. Fifteen percent means fifteen out of every hundred — 15 over 100 — and as a decimal that is 0,15. The instant you translate a percentage into decimal form, every calculation opens up: divide by 100, then multiply by the amount in front of you.

So 15 percent OF R800 is 0,15 times 800. Break it down: 0,1 times 800 is 80; 0,05 times 800 is 40; together, R120. That is our discount — the money the shop surrenders.

Now the upgrade that marks out confident work: the multiplier method. Rather than finding the percentage amount and then adding or subtracting it, compress the entire change into ONE multiplication.

A 15 percent decrease leaves 85 percent of the original standing — 100 minus 15. So multiply by 0,85, once. A 15 percent increase hands you 115 percent of the original — multiply by 1,15. Decrease: one minus the decimal. Increase: one plus the decimal.

Test it against the long route. Long route: 800 minus 120 leaves 680. Multiplier route: 800 times 0,85 — 800 times 0,8 is 640, plus 800 times 0,05 which is 40 — gives 680. Same rands, half the keystrokes, and, as the next section shows, the multiplier is the only comfortable vehicle for chains of changes.

The questions for this section are with you now. Hold three things steady while you answer: the decimal form of a percentage, the multiplier that performs a decrease, and the multiplier that performs an increase. The rest of the session stands on those three.

## Subtopic: The Discount Step, Then the VAT Step

Back to the sneakers, working the chain in the order the words demand. The iron rule of chained percentages: every step acts on the RESULT of the step before it, never on the original. The question says VAT is added to the discounted price — that sentence defines the chain.

Step one, the discount. Marked price R800, discount 15 percent. One multiplier: R800 times 0,85 equals R680. That is the reduced shelf price, before tax.

Step two, the VAT. VAT — value-added tax — runs at 15 percent in South Africa, and it is ADDED. Increase multiplier: 1,15, applied to the discounted price, not the original: R680 times 1,15.

Compute it: 680 times 1 is 680. 680 times 0,15: one tenth of 680 is 68; half of that is 34; together 102. Add: 680 plus 102 gives 782. This chain happens to land exactly on the cent — the final price of the sneakers is R782,00.

Before celebrating, stand on the classic error until it cannot bite you. Many learners take 15 percent of the ORIGINAL R800 for both steps: minus R120, plus R120 — and announce a final price of R800, "because the fifteens cancel". They do not cancel. The discount acted on R800, but the VAT acted on the smaller R680 — so the VAT put back only R102 where the discount removed R120. Equal rates on unequal bases are unequal amounts of money. That one insight will guard you in every interest, inflation and mark-up question for the next three years.

Work this section's questions before moving on. They probe the order of the two steps, the base each one acts on, and the reason the two fifteens leave R782 on the till slip rather than R800.

## Subtopic: The Single Percentage Change — One Multiplier for the Whole Journey

Now the second half of the question: what single percentage change carries you from the original R800 to the final R782?

The elegant road first: multiply the multipliers. The journey was times 0,85, then times 1,15, so the combined effect is 0,85 times 1,15. Work it: 0,85 times 1 is 0,85; 0,85 times 0,15 is 0,1275; total 0,9775.

Read that combined multiplier exactly the way you built the single ones. A multiplier of 0,9775 says the final price is 97,75 percent of the original. And 100 minus 97,75 is 2,25 — the whole journey is a 2,25 percent DECREASE.

Confirm along the direct road, because two roads agreeing is verification. Percentage change equals the change divided by the ORIGINAL, times 100. The change: 800 minus 782 is 18. Divide by the original: 18 over 800 is 0,0225. Times 100: a 2,25 percent decrease. Both roads meet.

Hold the headline: minus 15 percent followed by plus 15 percent is NOT zero — it is minus 2,25 percent. Down then up at the same rate always strands you slightly below the start, because the increase works on a smaller base. Retailers understand this; from today, so do you.

The direct formula deserves its own frame, because it answers every "by what percentage did it change?" question you will ever meet: new minus original, divided by original, times 100. The original is always the denominator — the reference point. Divide by the new value instead and the answer drifts; change is always measured from where you started.

This section's questions are in front of you. Keep both roads open — combined multiplier and direct formula — and give every percentage-change answer the first check any marker applies: that R800, the original, sits in the denominator.

## Subtopic: Reverse Percentages — and the Full Toolkit

One final weapon completes the kit: driving the chain backwards. A new question: a price tag reads R782 INCLUDING 15 percent VAT — what was the price before VAT?

The trap answer subtracts 15 percent: 782 times 0,85 gives R664,70. Wrong. Why? Because the VAT was calculated as 15 percent of the BEFORE-VAT price, not of the final price — subtracting 15 percent of the wrong base is our earlier error wearing a disguise.

The correct move: the final price IS the before-VAT price times 1,15. So run that multiplication in reverse — divide. R782 divided by 1,15 equals R680, and there is the discounted price again, recovered exactly. To undo a multiplier, divide by it. That single sentence is the entire theory of reverse percentages.

Now assemble the complete toolkit — five moves that answer any percentage question. Move one: percentage OF an amount — decimal times amount. Move two: increase or decrease — one multiplier, 1 plus or 1 minus the decimal. Move three: chained changes — multiply the multipliers, each step acting on the previous result. Move four: percentage change — difference over ORIGINAL, times 100. Move five: reverse percentage — divide by the multiplier to recover the earlier value.

Close the loop on the whole sneaker problem, as strong answers always do: 800 times 0,85 is 680; times 1,15 is 782; the combined multiplier 0,9775 confirms an overall 2,25 percent decrease; and 782 divided by 1,15 lands back on 680. Every arrow runs forwards and backwards without contradiction. That closed loop is what mastery feels like, and in finance questions from tariffs to inflation to interest you will run it again and again.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They cover the reverse step, the trap of subtracting fifteen percent from a VAT-inclusive price, and the five moves — for each question, name the move before you press a single key.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same sneakers, same R800, same two fifteens, same final answer — but now worked out the way you would do it standing in the queue at the till.

## Subtopic: Taking Off and Putting On — the One-Button Way

Picture the price as a chocolate slab moulded into a hundred equal blocks. R800 is the whole slab, and "percent" only ever means "out of a hundred". A percentage is nothing deeper than counting blocks.

The shop announces fifteen percent off. Fifteen blocks leave the slab. How many blocks remain? A hundred take away fifteen — eighty-five. That is the entire idea, and it is not clever, it is counting: fifteen off means eighty-five left.

Most people do this as two jobs. Job one: price the fifteen blocks — 0,15 times R800 is R120. Job two: remove them — R800 minus R120 is R680. Honest, correct, and double the work.

The one-button way does one job: keep the eighty-five percent. Eighty-five percent as a decimal is 0,85, so R800 times 0,85 delivers R680 in a single press. Same rands, one step — and, crucially for later, no leftover number on your screen to mislay.

Going up is the same trick in the other direction. Say your R150 airtime voucher carries a twenty percent bonus. You keep your full hundred percent and gain twenty more, landing on a hundred and twenty percent: R150 times 1,20 is R180 of airtime. Down puts you under one; up puts you over one.

The number you multiply by — the 0,85, the 1,20, the 1,15 — is called the multiplier, and every single idea in this lesson is that one number doing different jobs.

Right — the questions for this piece are with you now, and they are all the same move: turn the change into one number, then multiply. Say the number out loud before you press anything.

## Subtopic: Two Fifteens That Refuse to Cancel

Here comes the part that fools nearly everyone, including adults with payslips.

Two friends each ask for a fifth of your hot chips. The first takes a fifth of a full parcel — a proper handful. The second asks for the very same fraction, a fifth, but the parcel has shrunk by then, so her handful is smaller. Same fraction, less food. Nobody cheated; the parcel changed size in between.

The sneakers are exactly that story. Fifteen percent of R800 is R120 — the discount, scooped off the full parcel. Then VAT arrives wanting fifteen percent, but the parcel is now R680, and fifteen percent of R680 is only R102. Same fifteen percent, eighteen rand less money. Add it on: R680 plus R102 is R782.

So the price fell by R120 and climbed back by only R102. Of course it does not land where it began. It lands R18 short of R800, at R782 — and the shop is not swindling anyone; the arithmetic simply cannot behave any other way.

Now pin a percentage on the whole trip. Down then up, both moves in one go: 0,85 times 1,15 is 0,9775. That says the final price is 97,75 percent of the original — just shy of the full hundred. A hundred minus 97,75 leaves 2,25, so the sneakers finished 2,25 percent cheaper than they started.

Check it the plain way and watch the same number surface. The price dropped R18 from a starting R800. Eighteen divided by 800 is 0,0225, and times a hundred is 2,25 percent. Two roads, one answer. The plain road has a name — percentage change: the difference, divided by where you started, times a hundred. Where you started goes on the bottom, always.

Your questions on this bit are up now. Give extra care to the one asking which price the VAT was worked on, because whoever can answer that owns the whole lesson.

## Subtopic: Winding the Till Backwards

Last piece, and it is the one with real-life money in it. A till slip reads R782 including VAT. The shop owner asks: what were those sneakers worth before VAT?

The tempting move is to peel off fifteen percent: R782 times 0,85 gives R664,70. It feels fair, and it is wrong by R15,30. Here is why. The VAT actually charged was fifteen percent of the smaller price, R680 — which is R102. But peeling fifteen percent off the final price removes fifteen percent of the bigger number — R117,30 — which is more VAT than anyone ever paid. You are refunding money that was never collected.

The honest move asks how the VAT climbed aboard in the first place. It got there through a multiplication by 1,15. The only thing that undoes a multiplication is a division. So divide: R782 divided by 1,15 gives R680 — the price before VAT, precisely where the chain stood before the tax.

Think of a turnstile at the stadium: you entered through that gate, so you leave through the same gate, walking backwards. Multiply in, divide out.

Prove it with the slip's own numbers: R680 before VAT, R102 of VAT stacked on top, total R782. Every till slip in the country is printed on exactly that arithmetic, and now you can audit yours.

So the whole lesson in five plain moves. Finding a percentage of something: decimal times the amount. Making something bigger or smaller: one number — over one for up, under one for down. Two changes in a row: multiply the two numbers, because the second change works on what the first one left behind. Asking how much something changed: difference over where you started, times a hundred. Going backwards: divide by the number that got you there.

And here are the last questions of the lesson. Once you can undo a multiplier by dividing, you can read the VAT off any slip in South Africa — go prove it on the next one you're handed.
