# Part 1 — Expert

Three friends run a vetkoek stall at a Saturday market in Katlehong and must split R3 600 fairly. A shopper in the rice aisle weighs a 2 kg bag against a 10 kg bag. A builder must turn a heap of mix into the right loads of cement, sand and stone. All three are one mathematics wearing different overalls: RATIO, RATE and PROPORTION. This session builds four moves — sharing in a ratio, calculating a unit rate, telling direct from indirect proportion, and comparing two prices honestly.

## Subtopic: Ratio — Sharing Without Argument

A RATIO compares quantities of the SAME kind, measured in the SAME unit. It is written with a colon — 4 : 3 : 2 — and it carries no unit of its own, because when like is compared with like the units cancel out.

Two rules are tested before anything else. First, ORDER matters: if the ratio of boys to girls is 4 : 5, then 5 : 4 says something different and loses the mark. Second, both quantities must sit in the same unit BEFORE you simplify. A recipe calling for 400 g of sugar to 2 kg of flour is not 400 : 2. Convert first: 400 g : 2 000 g, then divide both sides by 400 to reach 1 : 5.

Now the sharing method — the most examined ratio skill of all. Naledi, Karabo and Anele put in different hours at the vetkoek stall, so they split the day's R3 600 in the ratio 4 : 3 : 2.

Step one: add the parts. 4 plus 3 plus 2 gives 9 parts. Step two: find the value of ONE part. R3 600 divided by 9 equals R400 per part. Step three: multiply each person's parts by that value. Naledi takes 4 times R400, which is R1 600; Karabo takes 3 times R400, which is R1 200; Anele takes 2 times R400, which is R800. Step four — never skip it — check the total: R1 600 plus R1 200 plus R800 equals R3 600. The shares rebuild the whole, so the working is sound.

The same three steps pour a foundation. A concrete mix is specified as cement to sand to stone in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4, and the job calls for 36 wheelbarrows of mix. Parts total 2 plus 3 plus 4, which is 9. One part is 36 divided by 9, which is 4 wheelbarrows. So the mix takes 8 wheelbarrows of cement, 12 of sand and 16 of stone — and 8 plus 12 plus 16 confirms 36.

The first set of questions for this session is with you now. Guard against the two mark-eaters: the order of the ratio, and converting to a common unit before you simplify.

## Subtopic: Rate — A Comparison That Carries Its Units

A RATE compares quantities of DIFFERENT kinds, and unlike a ratio it keeps its units. Rands per kilogram, kilometres per litre, cents per kilowatt-hour, rands per hour — the word "per" is the flag, and "per" always means "for every one".

A UNIT RATE is a rate expressed for exactly one of the second quantity, and division is how you reach it.

Worked example one, fuel consumption. A car covers 560 km on 40 litres of petrol: consumption equals distance divided by fuel, so 560 divided by 40 gives 14 km per litre. Now put the rate to work — a planned 350 km trip needs 350 divided by 14, which is 25 litres; at R22,80 per litre the trip's fuel comes to R570,00.

Worked example two, speed — with a trap coiled inside it. A minibus covers 54 km in 45 minutes. Speed equals distance divided by time — but the answer is wanted in kilometres per HOUR, so the time must become hours first. 45 minutes is 45 over 60 of an hour: 0,75 of an hour. Speed equals 54 divided by 0,75, which is 72 km per hour. Divide 54 by 45 instead and you get 1,2 — a "1,2 km/h" minibus that loses a race against a stroller. Units first, then arithmetic.

Worked example three, a tariff rate. A municipality charges R16,80 per kilolitre of water, so a household using 15 kℓ pays 15 times R16,80, which is R252,00. The rate is the price of one unit; the cost is the rate times the number of units — the engine inside every tariff question in this subject.

Take this section's questions on now, and check each answer twice: once for the number, once for the unit hanging on it — a rate without its units is an unfinished answer.

## Subtopic: Direct and Indirect Proportion

Two quantities are in DIRECT PROPORTION when multiplying one multiplies the other by the same factor — double the muffins, double the bill. The test: dividing one by the other always returns the SAME constant.

A bakery sells 5 muffins for R85,00. Divide for the constant: 85 divided by 5 equals R17,00 per muffin — the unit rate again, doubling as the constant of proportionality. Nine muffins therefore cost 9 times R17,00, which is R153,00. That is the UNITARY METHOD: drop down to one, then climb to the number you need.

Two quantities are in INDIRECT PROPORTION — also called inverse proportion — when multiplying one DIVIDES the other by the same factor. More of this means less of that, and the test is that MULTIPLYING the pair always returns the same constant.

Six workers take 20 days to tile a community hall. Multiply the pair: 6 times 20 equals 120 worker-days — the fixed size of the job. With 8 workers, the days shrink to 120 divided by 8, which is 15 days. The workers rose by a factor of four thirds; the days fell by exactly that factor.

A second inverse case: a minibus hired for a flat R1 080. Split among 12 learners, each pays 1 080 divided by 12, which is R90,00; split among 18, each pays R60,00. Multiply each pair — 12 times 90 is 1 080, and 18 times 60 is 1 080 — the constant confirms the relationship is inverse.

Deciding which shape you are facing earns marks on its own. Ask what the second quantity does when the first increases. Up together — divide to find the constant. One up, the other down — multiply to find the constant.

This section's questions are ready — name the type of proportion in words before your fingers touch the calculator.

## Subtopic: Best Buy — Making the Comparison Fair

Two bags of rice share a shelf. The 2 kg bag costs R56,99; the 10 kg bag costs R249,99. Neither sticker settles anything, because the bags hold different amounts. The only honest comparison is a UNIT RATE: what ONE kilogram costs in each case.

Small bag: R56,99 divided by 2 kg. That is R28,495 per kilogram, which rounds to R28,50 per kg. Big bag: R249,99 divided by 10 kg, which is R24,999 per kilogram — R25,00 per kg. The 10 kg bag costs about R3,50 less per kilogram, so it is the better buy — and taking home 10 kg in one big bag instead of five small ones saves roughly R35,00.

Now the written discipline that turns a right calculation into full credit. Show the SUBSTITUTION — put R56,99 divided by 2 kg on the page, not only the answer, because the method earns marks even when a keystroke slips. Keep the UNITS on every line, because "28,50" is a number while "R28,50 per kg" is an answer. Round only at the END; flatten R28,495 to R28 before comparing and you discard the very cents the comparison turns on. And answer what was ASKED, in a sentence: "The 10 kg bag is the better buy at R25,00 per kg compared with R28,50 per kg."

Then add the judgement Mathematical Literacy always wants beside the number. Better value per kilogram is not automatically the better decision: a shopper with R80,00 in her purse cannot reach the 10 kg bag at any unit price, and a household without a sealed container may lose half the big bag to damp or pests. Cheaper per unit assumes the outlay is affordable and the food gets used in time. State the mathematics, then state the condition — that pairing is what earns the full tick.

The final questions of this part are with you now: compare per unit, keep the units on the page, and close with a sentence that names the better buy and says why.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three ideas from the kitchen table — sharing food fairly, knowing the price of one, and working out whether more hands means more days or fewer.

## Subtopic: Cutting the Loaf Into Shares

Picture a loaf of bread and three cousins whose efforts were not equal — one kneaded, one minded the fire, one turned up late with the jam. Nobody wants equal slices; everybody wants fair slices. So they agree: four slices' worth to the first, three to the second, two to the third.

Count all the slices first: four plus three plus two is nine. Now cut the loaf into nine equal slices — and the whole topic hides in that sentence. Cut into the TOTAL number of parts first, then hand the parts out.

Money obeys the same knife. The vetkoek stall banked R3 600 on Saturday. Nine parts, so one part is R3 600 shared into nine equal piles — R400 a pile. Deal them: four piles is R1 600, three piles is R1 200, two piles is R800. Now stack them back — R1 600 plus R1 200 is R2 800, plus R800 is R3 600, on the nose. That little re-adding check has rescued more marks than any other habit in this topic.

One kitchen warning: never compare a cup with a bucket until both stand in the same container. If a recipe wants 400 grams of sugar to two kilograms of flour, do not write 400 to 2. Turn the kilograms into 2 000 grams first, and the comparison reads 400 to 2 000 — one to five, one part sugar for every five parts flour.

That style of comparing — same kind of thing, same unit, written with a colon — is what the textbooks call a RATIO. Short questions on it are with you right now; open every one by adding the parts.

## Subtopic: What One Costs, What One Does

Now the second idea — the one your gogo runs in every shop without ever calling it mathematics. She wants one thing: what does ONE cost? The little word "per" means "for every one". Rands per kilogram. Kilometres per litre. Rands per hour. And "one" is always reached by dividing.

The car went 560 kilometres on 40 litres. Share the kilometres into 40 equal parcels — one parcel per litre — and each litre carried the car 14 kilometres. Planning a 350 kilometre trip? That is 350 divided by 14, which is 25 litres in the tank. At R22,80 a litre, that is 25 lots of R22,80 — R570,00 for the journey. One division, one multiplication, and the trip is budgeted.

Mind the clock, because minutes ambush people. A minibus does 54 kilometres in 45 minutes, and the question wants the speed in kilometres per HOUR. You cannot divide by 45, because 45 lives in minutes and the answer must live in hours. Forty-five minutes is three-quarters of an hour — 0,75. So 54 divided by 0,75 gives 72 kilometres per hour. Believable for a minibus. Whenever an answer says "per hour", move your time into hours before you divide.

And the municipal version: water at R16,80 per kilolitre means each kilolitre costs R16,80, so 15 of them cost 15 lots of R16,80 — R252,00. Price of one, times how many. That is the entire rule.

Those "per" numbers are RATES, and your questions on them are up. Keep the words welded on — rands per kilogram, kilometres per hour — because a bare number is only half an answer.

## Subtopic: More Hands, Fewer Days — and Finding the Real Price

Two shapes of relationship, and an ordinary morning shows you both.

First shape: more muffins, more money. Five muffins cost R85,00, so one muffin is R85 shared five ways — R17,00. Nine muffins are then nine lots of R17,00 — R153,00. Up together, in step. Drop to one, then climb to what you need — that path never fails, and the textbooks call it DIRECT proportion.

The second shape runs the other way. Six workers take 20 days to tile a community hall; add workers and the days SHRINK. How long for eight? First ask how big the job actually is: 6 workers for 20 days is 120 worker-days of tiling. Eight workers chew through 120 worker-days in 120 divided by 8 — 15 days. More hands, fewer days: INDIRECT proportion, and its constant comes from MULTIPLYING the pair instead of dividing.

You have lived the money version of it. A minibus to the sports day costs R1 080 whether it runs full or half-empty. Twelve learners going means R1 080 split twelve ways — R90 each. Eighteen going means R60 each. More people, lighter share. Every stokvel, every lift club, every group gift runs on this rule.

Last stop: the shelf. A 2 kg bag of rice is R56,99 and a 10 kg bag is R249,99, and your eyes cannot referee this, because the bags are different sizes. Do what gogo does — bring both down to one kilogram. R56,99 shared over 2 kilograms is about R28,50 a kilogram. R249,99 shared over 10 kilograms is R25,00 a kilogram. The big bag wins by roughly R3,50 every kilogram — about R35 saved across ten kilograms.

Then the grown-up sentence, the one that sounds like a real shopper. Cheaper per kilogram only helps if you HAVE the R249,99 today, and if the rice is eaten before damp or weevils reach it. Say the number, then say the condition, in a full sentence — that answer is complete, and complete answers are the ones that get ticked.

Your last questions are here now. Price of one, then compare; and round only at the very end, because the cents you throw away early are exactly the cents the answer turns on.
