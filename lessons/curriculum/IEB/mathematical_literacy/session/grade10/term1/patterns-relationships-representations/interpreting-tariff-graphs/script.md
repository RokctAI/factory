# Part 1 — Expert

In Mathematical Literacy a graph is never decoration; it is a decision tool with money riding on it. This session trains that reading skill on a choice thousands of South African families make every year: two prepaid electricity tariffs drawn on the same set of axes. Option A charges nothing up front and R2,50 for every kilowatt-hour. Option B asks a fixed R200 each month and then only R1,70 per kilowatt-hour. From those two lines we will lift the fixed charge off the vertical intercept, the price per unit off the gradient, and the break-even usage off the crossing point — each reading confirmed by calculation — and then settle the question that decides the bill: which option is cheaper for a household using 400 kilowatt-hours a month, and by how many rands.

## Subtopic: Meeting the Graph — Axes, Lines and the Story They Tell

Start with the situation itself. The electricity supplier offers two tariffs. On Option A there is no monthly charge at all — every kilowatt-hour you draw costs R2,50, and that is the whole deal. On Option B you pay R200 at the start of the month before using a single unit, and in return each kilowatt-hour costs only R1,70.

Both tariffs sit on one graph. Along the horizontal axis runs the usage, measured in kilowatt-hours from 0 up to 500. Up the vertical axis runs the monthly cost in rands. Before your eye touches either line, read the axes — the titles, the units, the scale. Without its axes a graph is only a drawing; with them it becomes a statement you can quote.

Next, name each line, and do it by asking what each option costs when nothing has been used. Option A: zero usage means zero rand, so its line must set off from the origin. Option B: zero usage still costs the R200 fixed fee, so its line begins two hundred rand up the vertical axis. That starting height is the vertical intercept, and on any cost graph the intercept always announces the fixed charge. One glance at where a line begins, and you know the entrance fee without touching a calculator.

From their different starting points both lines climb — using more always costs more — but they climb at different rates, and somewhere in the middle of the picture they cross. That crossing is where the whole decision will be settled.

The questions for this section are with you now. They ask about the two things a line reveals before it goes anywhere: the height at which it meets the vertical axis, and what that height means in rands for a home that used no electricity at all.

## Subtopic: Gradient — What "Steeper" Means in Rands

Watch the two lines rise. Option A's line is the steeper one. In ordinary talk, steeper means "climbs faster"; in this subject it has a precise cash meaning. The steepness of a line is its gradient, and on a cost graph the gradient is the price per unit — the number of rands the bill grows for each extra kilowatt-hour.

Option A climbs R2,50 for every unit you move along the horizontal axis: a gradient of 2,5 rands per kilowatt-hour. Option B climbs R1,70 per unit: gradient 1,7 rands per kilowatt-hour. A is steeper for one reason only — each unit costs more there. When a question asks you to explain the steeper line, that is the sentence to write: Option A's cost rises by R2,50 for every extra kilowatt-hour, against R1,70 on Option B, so A charges more per unit.

You can also measure a gradient straight off the picture, even with no tariff information at all. Choose two convenient points on the line, read how far the cost rises between them, and divide by the units that passed. On Option A's line, moving from 100 to 200 kilowatt-hours, the cost lifts from R250 to R500 — a rise of R250 across 100 units — which is R2,50 per unit. Rise divided by run, the plotting process run backwards.

So every tariff line publishes its own contract: the intercept declares the fixed charge, the gradient declares the price per unit. Option A: intercept 0, gradient 2,5. Option B: intercept 200, gradient 1,7. That is why banks, gyms and municipalities like to show their options as graphs — the whole deal is visible in two features.

Take this section's questions now. Each one wants a gradient stated with its unit — rands per kilowatt-hour, never a naked number — so keep the rise-over-run working from the 100-to-200 stretch within reach.

## Subtopic: The Break-Even Point

Now the crossing. The two lines meet at exactly one point. Drop from it to the horizontal axis and you land on 250 kilowatt-hours; slide across to the vertical axis and you read R625. That is the break-even point: the usage at which both tariffs charge identical money — 250 units for R625 either way.

Never leave a crossing unverified when the numbers allow a check. Option A at 250 units: 250 times R2,50 gives R625. Option B at 250 units: R200 plus 250 times R1,70 — that is R200 plus R425 — gives R625 as well. Two calculations, one answer, matching the picture. The break-even stands confirmed.

The break-even matters because it cuts the graph into two decision zones. To the left of 250 units — the low-usage side — Option A's line lies underneath Option B's, so A is cheaper there. Sensible: a light user gains little from discounted units and should not pay R200 for the privilege. To the right of 250 units, Option B's line slips below A's, and B becomes the cheaper choice — the saving of 80 cents on every unit gradually swallows the fixed fee and keeps going.

That zone-reading is the transferable skill. Below the crossing, the lower line names the winner; at the crossing the options are level; past it, the other line takes the lead. Every comparison of cellphone contracts, gym memberships or bank accounts you will meet in this subject is the same picture in different clothing.

This section's questions are in front of you. Establish the break-even both ways — read from the axes and proved by arithmetic — and be ready to say which option wins on each side of 250 kilowatt-hours, and why.

## Subtopic: Answering the 400 kWh Question — and the Exam Method

Here is the question that decides the household's bill: which option is cheaper at 400 kilowatt-hours a month?

Method one — read the picture. Find 400 on the horizontal axis and travel straight upward. The first line your finger meets is Option B's — the lower cost — with Option A's line above it. At 400 units, B sits clearly below A, so Option B is cheaper. Zone logic says the same thing before your finger even moves: 400 lies to the right of the 250-unit break-even, and past break-even the fixed-fee option always wins.

Method two — prove it with numbers, the way full answers are written. Option A: 400 times R2,50 equals R1 000. Option B: R200 plus 400 times R1,70 — R200 plus R680 — equals R880. B's R880 against A's R1 000 means Option B is cheaper by R120. Write the difference down; a comparison only earns its full credit when it carries both totals and the gap between them, not just the winner's name.

Now collect the whole method, because this family of questions returns in every finance context this subject touches. One: read the axes — variables, units, scale. Two: identify each line by its intercept, the fixed cost. Three: read each gradient as a price per unit — steeper means dearer per unit. Four: locate the crossing, the break-even, and verify it by calculation. Five: split the picture into zones and name the cheaper option on each side. Six: for any given usage, read vertically, compare the heights, and confirm with arithmetic that includes the rand difference.

One final habit marks out strong work: test every reading against real life. Should a heavy user prefer the fixed-fee tariff? Yes — cheap units reward bulk use. Should zero usage cost nothing on A and R200 on B? Yes — that is precisely what "no fixed charge" and "fixed charge" mean. When the graph, the arithmetic and common sense all agree, the answer is safe to write in ink.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. Every comparison in them wants three things in order: both rand totals, the name of the cheaper option, and the rand difference between them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same two tariffs, same R200, same R2,50 and R1,70 — but now told from the kitchen table, the way you would talk it through with someone who must load prepaid electricity tonight.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Pay — the Day Pass and the Membership

Picture the swimming pool down the road. It sells two deals. Deal one: no joining fee, but every swim costs you R2,50 at the gate. Deal two: pay R200 for a membership card before summer starts, and after that every swim costs only R1,70.

Electricity is sold in exactly those two shapes. Option A is the day-pass deal — nothing up front, R2,50 for every unit that goes through your meter. Option B is the membership deal — R200 leaves your pocket at the start of the month whether you cook or sit in the dark, and from then on each unit is R1,70.

Now imagine drawing the money against the units. Stand at the very start of the month, zero units used. On Option A you owe nothing, so its line begins right down on the floor at R0. On Option B you already owe R200 before the kettle boils, so its line begins up in the air at the R200 mark. That starting height is the joining fee, nothing fancier. Whenever a graph forgets its labels, ask each line what it charges at zero — the height where it starts is the answer.

From there the two lines climb, because both deals bill you for what you use, and neither line ever dips — no electricity bill shrinks because you used more. Everything else in this lesson is just watching how quickly each line climbs.

And that starting height carries a formal name you must use in written answers: the vertical intercept. On a money graph it always stands for one thing — the fixed charge.

The questions for this piece are with you now. They all live at the start of the month: what each deal costs before one unit is used, and which line begins at which height.

## Subtopic: How Fast Your Money Leaves

Now for the climbing. Think of two airtime bundles draining at different speeds — both count down, one just eats your money faster.

On Option A every unit adds R2,50 to the bill; on Option B every unit adds only R1,70. Drawn on the graph, A's line rises more sharply with every step to the right — A is the fast drain. The proper word for that sharpness is the gradient, but hold the plain meaning first: on a money graph, the steepness of a line is the price of one more unit. That is the whole idea.

You can read that price off the line even when nobody prints the tariff. Take two friendly spots on Option A's line: at 100 units the bill reads R250, and at 200 units it reads R500. Between those spots, 100 more units went by and the bill grew by R250. R250 spread over 100 units is R2,50 for one unit. You just lifted a price out of a picture — how much it rose, divided by how many units passed.

Try the same trick on Option B: at 100 units the bill is R370, at 200 units it is R540, so it grew R170 over 100 units — R1,70 a unit. Both lines are completely honest: where each starts is its joining fee, how steeply each climbs is its price per unit. Two numbers, and you know the whole deal.

So when a question asks what the steeper line means, never stop at "it goes up faster". Put rands on it: Option A charges R2,50 per kilowatt-hour against Option B's R1,70, so A's bill grows faster with every extra unit.

Your questions on this section are up now — take them slowly, because the credit sits in stating the steepness in rands per unit, not in calling one line steeper.

## Subtopic: Where the Lines Swap Places — and the 400-Unit Answer

Here is the part that makes the whole picture click. Option B takes R200 off you up front — but then it sells you every single unit 80 cents cheaper than Option A does. So that R200 is not money lost; it is money invested in a discount. The only question is how many units it takes for the 80-cent savings to pay the R200 back.

Work it out like change at a till: R200 to recover, 80 cents saved per unit, so R200 divided by 80 cents is 250 units. At 250 units you have clawed back exactly what you paid, and the two deals stand level. Prove it in rands. Option A: 250 units at R2,50 is R625. Option B: R200 plus 250 at R1,70, which is R200 plus R425 — also R625. Level at R625, exactly where the lines cross on the picture. The formal name for that spot is the break-even point.

That splits every month into two stories. Use fewer than 250 units and Option A wins — you would never earn the R200 back, so keep it. Use more than 250 units and Option B wins — every unit past 250 hands you a clean 80 cents. Light user, skip the membership. Heavy user, buy it.

Now the household's actual question: 400 units a month — which deal? You know before calculating, because 400 sits past 250, so B must win. But the credit lives in the rands, so say them. Option A: 400 times R2,50 is R1 000. Option B: R200 plus 400 times R1,70, which is R200 plus R680 — R880. Option B is cheaper by R120.

And watch how sweetly the story audits itself: at 400 units you saved 80 cents on each of 400 units, which is R320, and you paid R200 for the discount card. R320 minus R200 is R120 — the exact saving again. When the reasoning and the arithmetic land on the same number, write it down with confidence.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now. For every comparison, hand over all three pieces — what A costs, what B costs, and who wins by how many rands — and this whole family of questions, from data bundles to gym cards, is yours for good.
