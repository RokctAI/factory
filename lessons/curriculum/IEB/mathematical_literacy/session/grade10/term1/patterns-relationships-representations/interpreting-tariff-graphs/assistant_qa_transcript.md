**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first is the one everyone dreads: my test graph had two lines with no labels on them at all. How do I work out which line is Option A and which is Option B?

**Tutor:** Interrogate each line at zero usage — its starting height is its confession. Option A has no fixed charge, so a household that used nothing owes nothing; its line has to begin at the origin. Option B collects R200 before a single unit flows, so at zero usage the bill is already R200; its line has to begin two hundred rand up the vertical axis. That starting height is the vertical intercept, and on a money graph the intercept is always the fixed charge, so you match each option's fixed fee to the height where a line begins and the labels write themselves. If two options ever shared the same fixed charge, you would use the second identifier instead: the steeper line belongs to the option charging more per unit. Between where a line starts and how fast it climbs, every tariff line you will ever meet can be named without a printed label.

***

**Thandi:** Next question, and it is a fair one. In maths class the gradient was just a number we calculated. What is it actually doing on an electricity graph?

**Tutor:** Here the gradient earns a salary and wears a unit. On a cost line it is the price of one more unit — the rands your bill climbs when one extra kilowatt-hour passes through the meter. Option A's gradient is R2,50 per kilowatt-hour; Option B's is R1,70 per kilowatt-hour. That single fact explains the picture: A is steeper because each step to the right costs more money on A than on B. And you can harvest the gradient from the line itself with no tariff sheet in sight. On Option A, between 100 and 200 kilowatt-hours the bill grows from R250 to R500 — a rise of R250 while 100 units passed — and R250 over 100 units is R2,50 per unit. Rise divided by run. When a question asks what the steeper line means, remember the credit lives in the rands, not the adjective: say Option A charges R2,50 per unit against B's R1,70, so its cost climbs faster with usage. A number with its unit is an explanation; an adjective is only a description.

***

**Thandi:** This one arrives slightly annoyed. A learner wrote "Option B is cheaper" for the 400 kilowatt-hour question, lost credit, and wants to know what more there was to say.

**Tutor:** The evidence was missing. "Option B is cheaper" is a verdict without a trial. A comparison in Mathematical Literacy wants the working on display: Option A at 400 units is 400 times R2,50, which is R1 000. Option B is the fixed R200 plus 400 times R1,70 — R200 plus R680 — which is R880. Therefore Option B is cheaper by R120. Three moves every time: calculate both, compare them, state the difference. And that R120 is not decoration — it is the actual money the household keeps every month, the number that turns a correct answer into a useful one. Train yourself to end every comparison with the words "by R-something". If that blank is still empty, the question is still unfinished.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is a what-if. Suppose the question gives me the two tariffs in words and no graph at all. Can I still find the break-even?

**Tutor:** You can, and usually quicker than reading a crossing point. Ask what the fixed fee is purchasing. Option B takes R200 up front and in exchange sells each unit 80 cents cheaper — R1,70 instead of R2,50. So the break-even is simply: how many units before the 80-cent savings repay the R200? R200 divided by R0,80 per unit is 250 units — the very same 250 kilowatt-hours the crossing shows. Confirm it in rands: Option A gives 250 times R2,50, which is R625; Option B gives R200 plus 250 times R1,70, which is R200 plus R425, also R625. Equal, so the break-even stands. Carry that recipe everywhere: divide the fixed fee by the saving per use, and you have the usage at which any two deals — data bundles, gym cards, transport passes — swap places.
