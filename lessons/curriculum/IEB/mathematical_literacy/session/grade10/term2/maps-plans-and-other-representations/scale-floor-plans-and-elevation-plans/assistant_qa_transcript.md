**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: scale conversions keep flipping on me. How do I remember whether to multiply by 50 or divide by 50?

**Tutor:** Ask which world you are travelling towards, and let common sense be the umpire. The real house is huge and the page is small, so numbers must GROW when you move from the page into reality, and SHRINK when you move from reality onto the page. Page to real: multiply. Real to page: divide. Now stress-test it, because a sense check is what makes the rule permanent. The bedroom's long wall measures 9,0 centimetres on a 1 : 50 plan. Multiply: 9,0 times 50 is 450 centimetres, which is 4,50 metres. Is a bedroom wall about four and a half metres? Yes — that is a perfectly ordinary room. Divide instead and you get 0,18 centimetres — a wall thinner than a matchstick, which no house has ever had. Going the other way: the stoep is 4,8 metres long and the drawing is 1 : 100. Convert to centimetres first, 480, then divide by 100 to get 4,8 centimetres — a line that fits comfortably on the page. Two habits make this automatic: convert both quantities to the same unit before touching the ratio, and after every answer ask whether a real building could actually look like that.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the dimensions on our plan just say 4500 with no unit anywhere. Is that centimetres? Surely it cannot be metres.

**Tutor:** It is millimetres, and building plans use them almost universally for a very practical reason. Millimetres let the builder write every wall as a clean whole number with no decimal comma — 4500, 3200, 900 — and a decimal comma is exactly the kind of mark that vanishes on a dusty site or smudges in a photocopy. So the convention is to drop the unit and understand millimetres throughout. Your job is to convert before the number enters any area or cost calculation: divide by 1 000 to reach metres, so 4500 millimetres is 4,5 metres, 3200 is 3,2 metres, and 900 is 0,9 metres — that last one being the standard width of a doorway, which is worth recognising on sight. The danger is reading 4500 as centimetres, which would make the bedroom 45 metres long, or as metres, which would make it four and a half kilometres. Both are absurd, and the absurdity is your protection. Carry a few reference sizes in your head — a domestic room is roughly 3 to 5 metres across, a door is about 0,9 metres wide and 2,1 metres high, a ceiling sits around 2,4 to 2,7 metres — and any misread unit announces itself instantly.

**Thandi:** This one split the class down the middle. Why is the north elevation the side facing north? Half of us were sure it meant the drawing you make while standing in the north.

**Tutor:** Both halves of the class are describing the same thing, which is exactly why it feels slippery. Picture it carefully. Stand to the north of the house and look back at it: the wall filling your view is the wall that points north. So "north elevation" names the FACE, and standing to the north is simply where you must be to see that face — the two descriptions coincide. The reason it matters is not vocabulary but sunlight, which is why architects fuss over it. In South Africa the sun tracks through the northern half of the sky, so a north-facing wall collects sun for most of the day. Put the living-room glass on that side and the room is bright and warm through winter at no cost; put it on the south face and the room stays dim and cold all year. So when a question asks you to comment on a layout, treat the north arrow as evidence, not decoration. A strong answer reads like this: the bedroom's big window faces north, so the room gets sun through the middle of the day, which suits a town with cold winters, while the bathroom sits on the south side where the cool does no harm. Reasoning of that kind is precisely what the interpretation marks are waiting for.

**Thandi:** Last one: in the wall-area question I froze, because I did not know whether to measure the floor plan or the elevation. How do I choose?

**Tutor:** That question is a gentle trap, because the honest answer is that you need BOTH — and noticing that is what the marks reward. Sort every measurement by the kind of quantity it is. Anything horizontal lives on the floor plan: room lengths and breadths, floor area, the walk-around distance for skirting, where the door sits along a wall, whether the bathroom touches the bedroom. Anything vertical lives on the elevation: wall height, the pitch of the roof, how far above the floor the sill sits, the height of a step. Wall area is horizontal times vertical, so it needs one number from each sheet. In our room the floor plan supplies the perimeter, 15,4 metres, and the elevation supplies the height, 2,6 metres, so the gross wall surface is 15,4 times 2,6, which is 40,04 square metres. Then subtract the openings, because nobody plasters a doorway: the door is 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89, and the window is 1,5 by 1,0, which is 1,5 — leaving 36,65 square metres. Write that subtraction as its own visible line, because it is awarded separately, and close with a sentence stating the area and its unit.
