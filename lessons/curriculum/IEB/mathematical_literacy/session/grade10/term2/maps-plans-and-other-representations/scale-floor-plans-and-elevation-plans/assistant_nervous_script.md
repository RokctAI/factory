Hey, it's Thandi — the first time you see a building plan it looks like code: little arcs, shaded double lines, numbers floating around with no units. Breathe. There is far less here than it pretends.

The scale is one sentence long: 1 : 50 means the drawing is fifty times smaller than the real thing. That is the whole idea. Going from the page out into the world, you MULTIPLY. Going from the world onto the page, you DIVIDE. Say it once out loud — "page to real, times; real to page, divide" — and half the topic is already in your pocket.

The two drawings are just two ways of looking at one room. The floor plan is the bird's-eye view: roof lifted off, looking straight down, answering "where is everything?" The elevation is the standing-in-front view: you out on the grass, looking squarely at one wall, answering "how high?" Layout or floor space — go to the plan. Wall height or roof — go to the elevation. Wall AREA — you need both: the walk-around from the plan, the height from the elevation.

The small boring habit that quietly protects your marks: write the unit on every single line. Measure 9,0 cm, times 50, is 450 cm, is 4,50 m. Four lines, four units. Someone who writes 450 and stops has done the maths and dropped the mark.

And those bare numbers like 4500 on the plan? Millimetres, always. Divide by a thousand and you have metres: 4,5 m — a normal wall. If your conversion gives you a 45-metre bedroom, the units are shouting at you to look again.

You already read plans, you know. You have looked at a room and thought "that bed will never fit against that wall". Same skill — we are just adding a ruler. You've got this!
