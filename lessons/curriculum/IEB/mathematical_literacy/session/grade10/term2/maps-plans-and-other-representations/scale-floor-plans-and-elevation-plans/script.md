# Part 1 — Expert

A family in George is building a new bedroom onto the side of the house, and the builder leaves two sheets of paper on the table. One looks straight down on the room from above — walls, a door, a window, a small stoep. The other shows the same room from outside, as if you were standing on the grass in front of it. Between those two drawings and an ordinary ruler sits every number the family needs: how long the walls really are, how much carpet to order, how many paving blocks the stoep will swallow. This session builds scale, floor plans and elevation plans — and the arithmetic that turns a drawing into a priced quotation.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Scale and What They Promise

A SCALE states the relationship between a length on the drawing and the matching length in the real world. It comes in two forms, and both get examined.

A NUMBER SCALE is written as a ratio, such as 1 : 50. It reads: one unit on the plan stands for fifty of the same units in reality. The units must match on both sides — that matching is the whole discipline. On a 1 : 50 plan, 1 cm stands for 50 cm, which is 0,5 m. On a 1 : 100 plan, 1 cm stands for 100 cm, which is 1 m. On a touring map at 1 : 500 000, 1 cm stands for 500 000 cm, which is 5 000 m, which is 5 km.

A BAR SCALE is a small ruler printed on the drawing itself, cut into segments, each labelled with the real distance it represents. On our plan every segment measures 2 cm on the page and is labelled 1 m — the same relationship as 1 : 50, since 2 cm on the page stands for 100 cm of wall.

Now the question that keeps returning: why print a bar scale when the ratio is already on the page? Because the ratio is a promise about the page at its ORIGINAL size. Photocopy the plan at 80 percent, or pinch-zoom it on a phone, and the ratio quietly turns false — 1 cm no longer means 50 cm. The bar scale shrinks and stretches with the drawing, so it keeps telling the truth at any size. That is why builders measure against the bar and treat the printed ratio as a label.

Two more facts. The bigger the second number, the SMALLER the drawing is relative to reality: 1 : 100 fits more house onto the page but shows less detail than 1 : 50. And a ratio carries no units of its own — it works in millimetres, centimetres or metres, provided both sides use the same one.

This section's questions are with you now: turn a ratio into "1 cm represents…", and be ready to explain why a bar scale survives a photocopier when a number scale does not.

## Subtopic: From Ruler to Reality — and Back

Scale questions travel in two directions, and both appear wherever plans are tested.

Forwards, from plan to reality: measure, then multiply. On the 1 : 50 plan the new bedroom measures 9,0 cm along its long wall. Actual length = 9,0 × 50 = 450 cm = 4,50 m. Write the unit on every line, because 450 with no "cm" beside it earns nothing. The shorter wall measures 6,4 cm: 6,4 × 50 = 320 cm = 3,20 m.

Backwards, from reality to plan: divide. The stoep is to be 4,8 m long, and the draughtsman is working at 1 : 100. Convert first: 4,8 m = 480 cm. Then divide by the scale: 480 ÷ 100 = 4,8 cm on the page. The order is fixed — convert to a common unit, then apply the ratio.

Now turn the room into a quotation, because that is what this topic is for. Floor area = 4,50 × 3,20 = 14,4 m². Carpet costs R165,00 per square metre, so 14,4 × 165 = R2 376,00.

Skirting board runs around the edge of the floor, so it needs perimeter, not area: 2 × (4,50 + 3,20) = 15,4 m. The 0,9 m doorway carries no skirting, so 15,4 − 0,9 = 14,5 m at R38,00 a metre gives R551,00.

And the stoep outside: 4,8 m by 2,0 m is 9,6 m². Paving blocks measure 0,25 m by 0,20 m, so each covers 0,05 m². Blocks needed: 9,6 ÷ 0,05 = 192. Add 5 percent for breakage and cutting: 192 × 1,05 = 201,6, and materials round up — 202 blocks.

Note the two traps. Careless measuring: one millimetre of ruler error becomes 5 cm of real wall at 1 : 50. And switching units mid-stream: never multiply centimetres on the page by the scale and then label the answer metres.

Work through this section's questions now, showing the measured length, the multiplication by the scale, and the converted answer with its unit on every line.

## Subtopic: Reading a Floor Plan

A FLOOR PLAN is the view from directly above, as though the roof were lifted away, drawn to scale. It shows layout: where the walls run, where the openings sit, and how the rooms connect to one another.

Learn the standard symbols, because they are tested directly. Walls are drawn as two parallel lines with the gap between them shaded — that gap is the thickness of the wall itself. A DOOR is a break in the wall with a straight line for the leaf and a quarter-circle arc showing which way it swings; the arc is doing real work — it warns you if the door will open into the wardrobe. A WINDOW is a thinner break, usually shown as three parallel lines. Fixtures appear in outline from above: a rounded rectangle for a bath, a small circle for a basin, an oval for a toilet. Stairs are parallel lines with an arrow labelled UP.

Then the notation. Dimension lines run beside the walls with arrows at each end and the true measurement written on them — on a builder's plan, almost always in millimetres, so 4 500 on a plan means 4 500 mm, which is 4,5 m. Rooms carry short labels: BED, KIT for kitchen, BATH, WC for the toilet, sometimes SD for a sliding door. A NORTH ARROW sits in one corner, and it matters — it tells you which rooms catch the sun and which face the weather.

Analysing a layout is a skill in its own right. Ask what the family would ask. Does the door swing collide with the built-in cupboard? Can you reach the bathroom without crossing a bedroom? Is the kitchen against the dining space? Does the biggest window sit on the north wall, where the winter sun is? An answer that proposes a fix — "hinge the door on the other side so it opens flat against the wall instead of into the passage" — earns more than an answer that only describes what is there.

Pause for this section's questions now: name the symbols, read a millimetre dimension and convert it, and justify one improvement you would make to the layout.

## Subtopic: Elevation Plans and What They Add

An ELEVATION is a straight-on view of one side of a building, drawn to scale as though you stood squarely in front of that side. There is no perspective and no angle — vertical lines stay vertical, nothing shrinks into the distance, so every length on an elevation can be measured with a ruler and scaled up.

Elevations are named for the direction the wall FACES. The north elevation is the side that faces north — the view you get by standing to the north and looking back at the building. The naming trips people, so read it as "the face pointing that way".

What an elevation shows that a floor plan cannot: the HEIGHT of the walls, the shape and pitch of the roof, how high the windows and doors sit, steps, gutters, a chimney. What a floor plan shows that an elevation cannot: the arrangement of the rooms, floor areas, wall thicknesses, and how the spaces connect. Neither drawing is complete alone, and marks are earned by saying which one answers the question in front of you.

Read our elevation. The wall height scales to 2,6 m. The window sill sits 1,0 m above the floor and the window itself is 1,0 m tall, so the head of the window is at 2,0 m — leaving 0,6 m of wall above it before the roof line. The roof falls from the front wall towards the back, which tells you the rain runs to the back gutter.

Then combine the drawings, which is what a full question demands. The floor plan gives a perimeter of 15,4 m; the elevation gives a wall height of 2,6 m. Wall area = 15,4 × 2,6 = 40,04 m². Subtract the door, 0,9 × 2,1 = 1,89 m², and the window, 1,5 × 1,0 = 1,5 m²: 40,04 − 1,89 − 1,5 = 36,65 m² to be plastered or painted. Neither sheet of paper could have produced that number on its own.

Give the answer as a sentence, with units: "The new room has 36,65 m² of wall surface once the door and window are deducted."

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — match each described feature to the drawing that shows it, and combine a plan measurement with an elevation measurement to produce one area.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two drawings on the kitchen table, where nobody says "elevation" — they say "this one is looking down on it, and this one is what it looks like from the street".

## Subtopic: The Shrunk House

A plan is a house that has been shrunk until it fits on a page, and the scale is the honest label saying by how much.

1 : 50 means the drawing is fifty times smaller than the real thing. So every centimetre you measure on the paper is fifty centimetres of actual wall — half a metre. 1 : 100 means a hundred times smaller: one centimetre on the page is a full metre of wall. Read the ratio as a shrinking factor and the mystery evaporates.

To travel from page to reality, MULTIPLY. Measure the long wall of the new bedroom: 9,0 cm. Times 50 is 450 cm, and 450 cm is 4,50 m. Do the short wall: 6,4 cm times 50 is 320 cm, which is 3,20 m. You now know the room without ever standing in it.

To travel the other way — reality onto the page — DIVIDE. The stoep will be 4,8 m long and this drawing is 1 : 100. Turn the metres into centimetres first: 480. Divide by 100: it goes onto the page as a 4,8 cm line.

One last thing about scale, and it is the favourite question. Why do plans carry a little printed ruler as well as the ratio? Because a photocopier does not know about ratios. Shrink the page to 80 percent and "1 : 50" becomes a lie — but the little printed ruler shrank along with everything else, so it still tells the truth. That is why the builder trusts the little ruler.

Some questions on shrinking and growing are with you right now — page to reality you multiply, reality to page you divide, and the units travel with you the whole way.

## Subtopic: The Bird's Eye and the Standing-in-Front View

Two drawings, two entirely different jobs, and the confusion clears the moment you know which is which.

The floor plan is the bird's-eye view — roof lifted off, looking straight down. It answers "where is everything?" You can see the shape of the room, where the door and window sit along the wall, how much floor there is, and whether you must walk through the kitchen to reach the bathroom.

The symbols are only shorthand. Two lines with shading between them: a wall. A gap with a little quarter-circle sweeping across it: a door, and the sweep shows which way it swings — a door that opens into a cupboard is a real problem that costs real money to fix. A thin triple line: a window. A rounded rectangle: the bath. A small circle: the basin. The numbers along the walls are almost always millimetres, so 4 500 means 4,5 m.

The elevation is the standing-in-front view. Walk outside, stand square in front of one wall, and look straight at it — no angle. That is what an elevation draws. It answers "how high?" — how tall the walls are, how the roof slopes, how far off the floor the window starts.

So each drawing knows things the other cannot. Ask the floor plan about layout and floor space. Ask the elevation about heights and the roof. And watch the naming: the north elevation is the wall FACING north — the side that points that way.

Questions on the two views are here now. For each one, first decide which drawing could possibly answer it — bird's eye, or standing in front.

## Subtopic: Turning the Plan into a Shopping List

Here is why any of this matters: two sheets of paper plus a ruler equal a quotation, and you can check the builder's numbers before signing anything.

The floor first. The room is 4,50 m by 3,20 m, so the floor is 4,50 × 3,20 = 14,4 m². Carpet at R165 a square metre is 14,4 × 165 = R2 376,00.

Then the edge, which is a different measurement altogether. Skirting board runs along the bottom of the walls, so it is a walk-around, not a cover: 4,50 and 3,20 and 4,50 and 3,20 is 15,4 m. Take off the 0,9 m doorway, because no skirting crosses a door: 14,5 m at R38 a metre is R551,00.

Now the stoep. It is 4,8 m by 2,0 m, so 9,6 m² to pave. Each block is 25 cm by 20 cm — that is 0,25 m by 0,20 m, which covers 0,05 m². How many blocks fill 9,6 square metres? 9,6 ÷ 0,05 = 192. Add five percent because blocks chip and edges need cutting: 192 × 1,05 is 201,6, and materials always round up — 202 blocks.

Last, the walls, and this is where the two drawings shake hands. The floor plan gives the walk-around: 15,4 m. The elevation gives the height: 2,6 m. Multiply and you have the wall surface: 15,4 × 2,6 = 40,04 m². Remove what nobody plasters — the door, 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89 m², and the window, 1,5 by 1,0, which is 1,5 m² — and you are left with 36,65 m².

Neither piece of paper could have told you that by itself. That is the entire point of having two.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now. Measure carefully, write the unit on every line, keep the door and window subtractions visible as their own step, and finish with a sentence a builder could quote from.
