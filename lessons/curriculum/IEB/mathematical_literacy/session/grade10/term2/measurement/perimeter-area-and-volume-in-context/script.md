# Part 1 — Expert

A household in Mahikeng has three jobs on the go this month: fencing the vegetable garden before the neighbour's goats find it, tiling and painting a spare room, and working out how many days the new rainwater tank will carry them when the municipal supply is interrupted. Every one of those jobs is a measurement question with money attached — and every one of them is won or lost at the same two moments: the unit conversion at the start, and the rounding decision at the end. This session builds perimeter, area and volume in the contexts where they are actually used, and installs the habits that hold the marks together.

## Subtopic: Convert First — the Step That Decides the Mark

Measurement questions arrive with mixed units on purpose. The plan speaks millimetres, the price list speaks square metres, the tank is described in metres and the household thinks in litres. Convert everything to ONE unit before any formula is touched.

The length ladder you already know: 1 km is 1 000 m, 1 m is 100 cm, 1 cm is 10 mm. So 4,2 m is 420 cm, and 1 750 mm is 1,75 m. Multiply when stepping down to a smaller unit; divide when stepping up to a bigger one.

Area conversions are where candidates stumble, because the factor gets squared. A square metre is a square 100 cm by 100 cm, so 1 m² is 10 000 cm² — not 100. A hectare is 10 000 m². Volume factors get cubed: 1 m³ is 1 000 000 cm³.

Then the conversion this whole topic leans on: 1 m³ holds exactly 1 000 litres, and 1 cm³ holds exactly 1 millilitre. That single line converts every tank, drum and bucket question into an answer a household can actually use, because nobody thinks about water in cubic metres.

Two habits protect the marks. Write the unit on every line of working, not only at the end — a number without a unit is worth nothing. And never round mid-journey: carry the full value through and round once, at the end, to what the context demands — money to the cent, tins to whole tins, days to whole days.

Take on this section's questions now — convert before you calculate, and remember that the area factor is squared and the volume factor cubed.

## Subtopic: Fencing the Garden — Perimeter as a Shopping List

PERIMETER is the distance all the way around a shape, measured in units of length. For a rectangle, perimeter = 2 × (length + breadth).

The vegetable garden is 14 m long and 9 m wide. Substitute — and write the substitution line, because it carries its own mark: perimeter = 2 × (14 + 9) = 2 × 23 = 46 m. The most common error in the whole topic is stopping at 14 + 9 = 23 and buying half a fence.

Now turn 46 metres into a shopping list, because that is what the question really wants. A 2 m gate occupies part of the boundary, so the mesh needed is 46 − 2 = 44 m. Wire mesh is sold in 25 m rolls at R749,00 a roll. Divide: 44 ÷ 25 = 1,76 rolls. Nobody sells 0,76 of a roll, so round UP: 2 rolls, costing 2 × R749,00 = R1 498,00, with 6 m of mesh left over — real money, worth a mention in any answer about cost-effectiveness.

Poles stand every 2 m around the full 46 m boundary. Because the boundary closes on itself, the number of poles equals the number of gaps: 46 ÷ 2 = 23 poles at R72,00 each, which is R1 656,00. Total materials: R1 498,00 + R1 656,00 = R3 154,00.

Two examinable subtleties. Rounding goes UP because materials must cover the whole job — a metre short is a hole in the fence. And a closed boundary needs exactly as many poles as gaps, while a straight open run needs one more; check whether the shape closes before you count.

This section's questions are in front of you now: double the sum of length and breadth, subtract the openings, then round the material upward and cost it.

## Subtopic: Tiles and Paint — Area, Coverage and Waste

AREA is the surface a shape covers, measured in square units. For a rectangle, area = length × breadth. For a triangle, area = ½ × base × perpendicular height. For a circle, area = π × radius², with π taken as 3,142 unless told otherwise.

The spare room's floor is 4,0 m by 3,6 m. Area = 4,0 × 3,6 = 14,4 m². Tiles come in boxes covering 1,8 m² at R289,90 a box. Before dividing, add a WASTE ALLOWANCE, because tiles get cut at edges and some crack: the standard instruction is 10 percent. So 14,4 × 1,10 = 15,84 m². Boxes needed: 15,84 ÷ 1,8 = 8,8 → 9 boxes. Cost: 9 × R289,90 = R2 609,10.

Now the walls, which are a different area entirely. Wall area is the room's perimeter multiplied by the wall height: perimeter = 2 × (4,0 + 3,6) = 15,2 m, and the walls stand 2,6 m high, so 15,2 × 2,6 = 39,52 m².

Openings are not painted. The door is 0,9 m by 2,1 m, which is 1,89 m². The window is 1,4 m by 1,2 m, which is 1,68 m². Paintable area: 39,52 − 1,89 − 1,68 = 35,95 m².

The paint tin promises 8 m² per litre, and the job needs two coats, so the surface to cover is 35,95 × 2 = 71,90 m². Litres required: 71,90 ÷ 8 = 8,99 ℓ. Paint comes in 5 ℓ tins at R395,00, so 8,99 ℓ means two tins — 10 ℓ — costing R790,00, with about a litre spare for touch-ups.

Notice the discipline both jobs share: find the area, adjust it for waste or openings, divide by the coverage rate, then round up to whole units of whatever the shop actually sells. Coverage rates are given in the question; the marks are for using them correctly.

Work through this section's questions now, and show the subtraction of every door and window — that line is awarded on its own.

## Subtopic: The Water Tank — Volume, Litres and How Long It Lasts

VOLUME is the space inside a solid, measured in cubic units. For a rectangular prism, volume = length × breadth × height. For a cylinder, volume = π × radius² × height — the circle's area, pushed upward through the height.

The tank is a cylinder 1,2 m across and 1,8 m tall. Across means DIAMETER, and the formula wants the radius — half of it: 0,6 m. Halving the diameter is the single most forgotten step in the topic, and skipping it makes the answer four times too large.

Substitute in full: volume = 3,142 × 0,6² × 1,8. Work in order — square first: 0,6² = 0,36. Then 3,142 × 0,36 = 1,13112. Then × 1,8 = 2,036 m³.

Convert to litres, because households plan in litres: 2,036 m³ × 1 000 = 2 036 litres.

Now the decision the family actually faces. Four people use about 85 ℓ each per day for drinking, cooking, washing and the toilet: 4 × 85 = 340 ℓ a day. Days of supply: 2 036 ÷ 340 = 5,99 days.

Here the rounding reverses, and this is the judgement being examined. Materials round UP; supplies and whole units round DOWN. Even at 5,99, the family gets 5 full days — the sixth day runs dry a few litres before it ends. Answer in a sentence: "A full 2 036 ℓ tank supplies a household of four using 340 ℓ a day for 5 complete days."

One more conversion this context rewards. Rain harvested off a roof is a volume too. A roof of 75 m² catching 8 mm of rain collects 75 × 0,008 = 0,6 m³, because 8 mm is 0,008 m — that is 600 litres from a single decent shower, nearly a third of the tank. Convert the millimetres to metres before multiplying, or the answer arrives a thousand times too big.

The last questions of this part are with you now — halve the diameter, substitute the full formula, convert cubic metres to litres, and decide whether the context wants rounding up or down.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three jobs from out in the yard: a fence to walk around, a floor and walls to cover, and a tank to fill — with a tape measure, a price list and no formula sheet anywhere in sight.

## Subtopic: Walking Around It, Covering It, Filling It

Three questions, three completely different kinds of answer — and telling them apart is most of the topic.

"How far around?" is perimeter. Walk the garden boundary with the wire on your shoulder: 14 m up, 9 m across, 14 m back, 9 m across. That is 46 m of walking and 46 m of wire — every side walked once, long, short, long, short, which is why the quick method adds one long and one short and doubles it. The classic national mistake is answering 23, and buying exactly half a fence.

"How much to cover?" is area, and it counts squares. Picture the spare room's floor tiled in metre squares: 4 squares along one wall, 3,6 along the other, so 4,0 × 3,6 = 14,4 squares. Length times breadth, and the answer wears a small 2 up top because you are counting squares.

"How much to fill?" is volume — the squares stacked upward into a box, so the answer wears a small 3. And the line every South African should own: one cubic metre is exactly 1 000 litres. Learn that and every tank question becomes a water question.

One warning before we move. Because area counts squares, a square metre is not 100 square centimetres — it is a 100-by-100 block of them: 10 000. Volume doubles down on the same trap: a cubic metre is a MILLION cubic centimetres. When the shape gains a dimension, the conversion gains it too.

Some questions on telling the three apart are with you right now — for each one, decide first whether it is a walk-around, a cover, or a fill.

## Subtopic: Buying Enough Paint Without Buying Too Much

The fence first, because it is really a shopping list. You need 46 m of boundary. The 2 m gate takes its own space, so the wire must cover 44 m. The shop sells 25 m rolls at R749. One roll is short. So you buy two — R1 498 — and carry 6 m home unused. That leftover is real money, and a good answer says so out loud.

Poles every 2 m around a 46 m boundary: 46 ÷ 2 is 23. And because the fence runs all the way round and meets itself again, 23 gaps need exactly 23 poles. At R72 each that is R1 656, and the whole fencing job comes to R3 154.

Now the room. The floor is 14,4 m². Tiles come 1,8 m² to a box. But tiles get cut at the edges and one or two always crack, so add ten percent before dividing: 14,4 × 1,10 is 15,84 m². Divide by 1,8 and you get 8,8 boxes. Nine boxes, at R289,90 each — R2 609,10.

The walls are a different sum, and this is the classic slip. Do not reuse the floor area. Walk the room with your hand flat on the wall — that walk is the perimeter, 15,2 m — and the walls rise 2,6 m, so the wall surface is 15,2 × 2,6 = 39,52 m². Then remove what never gets painted: the door, 0,9 by 2,1, which is 1,89 m², and the window, 1,4 by 1,2, which is 1,68 m². That leaves 35,95 m² of actual wall.

Two coats means covering it twice: 71,90 m². The tin says 8 m² a litre, so 71,90 ÷ 8 is 8,99 litres. Five-litre tins at R395,00 — two tins, R790,00, with roughly a litre left for touch-ups. And the rule that never bends when buying materials: whatever the decimal, go UP. You cannot buy 0,99 of a tin, and arriving home short means a second taxi trip and a wall in two shades.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Remove the doors and windows before you divide, and round the shopping upward every time.

## Subtopic: How Many Days Does the Tank Give You

The tank is a drum: a circle pushed upward. So build the circle first, then push it up.

It measures 1,2 m across. That across-number is the diameter, and the formula wants the radius — half of it — 0,6 m. Halve it. Everyone forgets, and forgetting hands the family a tank four times bigger than the one in the yard, which is exactly the kind of wrong that looks fine on paper.

Circle area: 3,142 × 0,6 × 0,6. Square first, so 0,36, times 3,142 is about 1,13 m². Now push it up through the 1,8 m height: 1,13 × 1,8 gives 2,036 m³. One cubic metre is a thousand litres, so the tank holds about 2 036 litres.

Now the question the family actually asked. Four people, about 85 litres each a day — drinking, cooking, washing, flushing — is 340 litres a day. How many days? 2 036 ÷ 340 is 5,99.

And here is the twist to hold onto, because it is the exact opposite of the paint. You do NOT round 5,99 up to 6. Not even at ninety-nine hundredths. The sixth day runs dry a few litres before supper, so the honest answer is 5 full days. Materials round up, because you must have enough. Days and whole items round down, because a part of one does not count. Ask which kind of rounding the situation wants — that pause is precisely what the marker is watching for.

One more, because it is beautiful. A 75 m² roof in a shower that drops 8 mm of rain collects 75 × 0,008 — because 8 mm is 0,008 m — which is 0,6 m³: 600 litres. Nearly a third of the tank, from one honest North West thunderstorm.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now. Halve the diameter, keep the units on every line, turn cubic metres into litres, and end with a sentence a real family could act on — how many days, how many tins, how many rands.
