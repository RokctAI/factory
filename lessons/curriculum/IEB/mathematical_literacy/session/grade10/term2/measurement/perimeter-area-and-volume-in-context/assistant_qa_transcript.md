**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it fooled almost all of us: why is one square metre ten thousand square centimetres and not one hundred? A hundred centimetres make a metre, so a hundred feels obvious.

**Tutor:** Your instinct is perfect for lengths and wrong for areas, and the little raised two is the warning sign. Build the square in your mind. A square metre is a square with sides of one metre — one hundred centimetres each — so it is a hundred-by-hundred square. Count the one-centimetre squares that tile it: one hundred rows with one hundred squares in each, which is ten thousand. The conversion factor is applied twice because there are two dimensions, so the length factor of one hundred becomes one hundred squared. Volume takes the same idea one storey higher: a cubic metre is one hundred by one hundred by one hundred centimetre-cubes, a full million cubic centimetres. So the rule is compact and checkable: lengths use the factor once, areas square it, volumes cube it. And when a question hands you mixed units — a plan in millimetres, a tile price per square metre — convert the LENGTHS to metres first and only then compute the area, because converting lengths is the direction everybody gets right.

**Thandi:** Next: the floor area and the wall area came out as totally different numbers, and I used the floor number for both and lost the marks. How do I keep them apart?

**Tutor:** That mix-up is the most expensive one in the topic, and a physical picture will hold them apart for good. For the floor, lie flat and look down: one rectangle, length times breadth — 4,0 times 3,6, which is 14,4 square metres. For the walls, stand up, press your hand flat against the wall, and walk the whole way around the room without lifting it. The distance your feet cover is the PERIMETER: 2 times 4,0 plus 3,6, which is 15,2 metres. Your hand also has to sweep from floor to ceiling, 2,6 metres. Unroll all four walls into one long strip and it is 15,2 metres long and 2,6 metres high — 39,52 square metres. Perimeter times height: that is wall area, and the floor has nothing to do with it. Then take out what never gets painted: the door at 0,9 by 2,1 is 1,89 square metres, the window at 1,4 by 1,2 is 1,68, leaving 35,95 square metres. Write that subtraction as its own line — it is awarded separately — and only then divide by the paint's coverage.

**Thandi:** This one started a real debate. Twenty-three poles for a forty-six metre fence with a pole every two metres — but someone insisted it must be twenty-four, because of the fence-post thing. Who wins?

**Tutor:** You both do, in different geometries, and knowing which geometry you are standing in is the examinable part. Take a STRAIGHT run first: twenty metres of fence along one side of a driveway, a pole every two metres. Ten gaps — but the line has a beginning and an end, and each needs its own pole, so eleven poles: gaps plus one. That is the fence-post problem your classmate remembers, and it is real. Now take our garden. The fence travels the whole way around and arrives back where it started, so the boundary CLOSES — the final pole and the first pole are the same physical pole, and there is nothing extra to add. Forty-six metres at two-metre spacing gives twenty-three gaps and exactly twenty-three poles. The test is one question: does the last point meet the first? A rectangular garden, a circular kraal, a fenced netball court — closed, so poles equal gaps. One edge of a plot, a straight washing line, a driveway fence — open, so poles equal gaps plus one. Sketch the shape, ask whether it bites its own tail, and count accordingly.

**Thandi:** Last one: the rounding still makes me nervous. Our tank answer was 5,99 days and I so badly wanted to write 6. Is there a rule I can trust under pressure?

**Tutor:** There is, and your example is the perfect stress test, because 5,99 is as close to 6 as a decimal can decently get — and the answer is still 5. The rule has nothing to do with the size of the decimal; it is about what the number counts. Ask one question: must I have ENOUGH, or must it be COMPLETE? If the number is a material you buy so a job can be finished — paint, tiles, rolls of mesh — you must have enough, so you round UP however small the fraction: 8,99 litres means two five-litre tins, 8,8 boxes means nine, 1,76 rolls means two. If the number counts whole things that only exist entire — full days of water, complete taxi loads, whole loaves — then a part of one does not count and you round DOWN: 5,99 days is five days, because day six dies a few litres before it ends. And keep a third case filed separately: money rounds to the cent, two decimals, once, at the very end, never in the middle. So finish the arithmetic, stop, and ask what the number is counting before you write the final line. That deliberate pause is worth a mark in nearly every measurement question you will ever sit.
