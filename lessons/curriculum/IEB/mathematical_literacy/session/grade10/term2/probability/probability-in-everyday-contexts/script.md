# Part 1 — Expert

A school in Umlazi holds its winter fun day, and three questions land within the same hour. A learner wants her chance of pulling a blue sweet from the jar on the cake table. A stall running a two-coin game wants to know what prize it can offer without losing money. And a teacher, surveying how learners travel to school, wants to know whether age and transport are connected. All three are probability questions, and one small toolkit answers them all: a scale from 0 to 1, three ways of writing the same number, a table, and a tree. This session builds all four, and closes with the question that makes probability worth learning — is the game fair?

## Subtopic: The Scale from 0 to 1 and Three Ways to Say It

PROBABILITY is a measure of how likely an event is, and every probability that exists lies between 0 and 1 inclusive. Zero means IMPOSSIBLE — the event cannot happen. One means CERTAIN — it must. A half is an EVEN CHANCE. Below a half is UNLIKELY; above it, LIKELY. No probability is ever negative, and none exceeds 1 — an answer outside that range is not a discovery, it is an instruction to recheck your working.

The vocabulary must be precise. An OUTCOME is one possible result of a single action: rolling a 2, or drawing a white sweet. An EVENT is the outcome or collection of outcomes you care about: "rolling an even number" is an event holding three outcomes. The SAMPLE SPACE is the complete list of everything that could happen: for one die, the numbers 1 to 6.

One probability can wear three costumes, and questions ask for all of them. Take the jar on the cake table: 14 blue sweets, 10 white and 16 pink — 40 sweets in total. As a FRACTION, the probability of drawing blue is 14 out of 40, written 14/40. As a DECIMAL, divide: 14 ÷ 40 = 0,35. As a PERCENTAGE, multiply the decimal by 100: 35 percent. Same fact three ways, and the instruction word in the question tells you which to wear.

Then COMPLEMENTARY events, the time-saver of the whole topic. The probability of NOT blue is everything left over: 10 white plus 16 pink is 26 out of 40, which is 0,65, or 65 percent. Notice that 0,35 and 0,65 sum to exactly 1 — no accident, because an event and its complement between them cover the entire sample space. So the probability of NOT an event equals 1 minus the probability of the event. The moment "not" or "at least one" appears in a question, reach for the complement.

Take on this section's questions now: place each probability on the 0-to-1 scale, convert between fraction, decimal and percentage, and use the complement instead of counting the long way round.

## Subtopic: Theoretical Probability and Relative Frequency

Two different probabilities live in this subject, and blurring them costs marks.

THEORETICAL PROBABILITY is reasoned out before anything happens. It is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of possible outcomes, and it assumes every outcome is equally likely. For a fair die, the probability of an even number is 3 out of 6, which is 0,5 or 50 percent. For the sweet jar, the probability of pink is 16 out of 40, which is 0,4 or 40 percent.

RELATIVE FREQUENCY — also called experimental probability — is measured afterwards, from what actually happened. It is the number of times the event occurred divided by the number of trials. A learner stands at the rank near the school gate and counts 150 taxis, finding that 93 are headed for town: the relative frequency is 93 ÷ 150 = 0,62, or 62 percent. No theory was involved; the number was observed — and observation is the only route when outcomes are not equally likely, because nobody can reason out where taxis choose to go.

Now the relationship between the two, which gets asked directly. Toss a fair coin 60 times and you may get 27 heads — a relative frequency of 0,45, not the theoretical 0,5. That does not make the coin crooked. Toss it 6 000 times and the proportion will sit far closer to 0,5. The sentence to hold: as the number of trials increases, the relative frequency tends towards the theoretical probability. Small samples wander; large samples settle.

And the belief that refuses to die: after three heads in a row, tails is NOT "due". A coin has no memory. Every toss is an INDEPENDENT event, and the probability of a head on the next toss is 0,5, exactly as it was on the first. What a long run promises is a stable overall proportion — never a correction of the past.

This section's questions are in front of you now — say which of the two probabilities is being asked for, and be ready to explain how a small sample can disagree with theory while nothing at all is wrong.

## Subtopic: Two-Way Tables — Probability from Real Data

A TWO-WAY TABLE sorts one group by two characteristics at the same time, and it is the richest source of probability questions in the subject because the numbers come from real life.

The teacher surveyed 160 learners, recording age group and how they get to school. Of the learners under 16, 36 walk and 64 come by taxi, giving 100. Of those 16 and over, 28 walk and 32 come by taxi, giving 60. Column totals: 36 plus 28 is 64 walkers, and 64 plus 32 is 96 taxi users. Everything reconciles with the grand total of 160 — and checking those margins before using any table is a habit worth keeping, because a table that does not balance cannot produce answers that do.

Now read probabilities straight off it. The probability that a randomly chosen learner comes by taxi is 96 out of 160, which is 0,6 or 60 percent — a row or column total over the grand total. The probability that a learner is BOTH under 16 AND comes by taxi is the single cell where the two meet: 64 out of 160, which is 0,4 or 40 percent.

Then the reading that carries the heaviest marks, because it quietly changes the denominator. "Of the learners under 16, what proportion come by taxi?" The group has narrowed — we are no longer drawing from 160 learners but from the 100 who are under 16. The answer is 64 out of 100, which is 0,64, or 64 percent. Compare the older group: 32 out of 60, which is 0,533, about 53,3 percent. The younger learners are noticeably more likely to use a taxi — and stating the comparison with both figures is what a complete answer looks like.

The discipline is easy to say and easy to forget: identify the whole group before writing the denominator. "Out of all learners" means 160. "Out of the under-16s" means 100. The numerator is usually obvious; the denominator is where the marks live.

Work through this section's questions now, checking the table's totals first and naming the group each denominator is drawn from.

## Subtopic: Tree Diagrams and Fair Games

A TREE DIAGRAM lays out a two-stage or three-stage activity with a branch for every outcome at every stage. Its power is making the complete sample space visible — and the sample space is where most probability errors hide.

Take the stall's game: toss two coins. Stage one branches into heads and tails. From each of those, stage two branches into heads and tails again. Follow every path to its tip and there are four equally likely outcomes: heads-heads, heads-tails, tails-heads and tails-tails. Note with care that heads-tails and tails-heads are DIFFERENT paths, even though both feel like "one of each" — merging them is the classic error, and it turns the correct probability of 0,5 for "one of each" into a wrong 0,333.

So the probability of two tails is 1 out of 4, which is 0,25 or 25 percent. And the branch rule that makes trees scale: multiply ALONG the branches. The probability of tail then tail is 0,5 × 0,5 = 0,25 — the same answer with no listing, which is what you need when the stages stack up. Draw a sweet from the jar, replace it, and draw again: the probability of blue then blue is 0,35 × 0,35 = 0,1225, about 12 percent.

Now the fairness question, which is the point of it all. The stall charges R4,00 a turn and pays R12,00 for two tails. Reason over 100 turns. Money in: 100 × R4,00 = R400,00. Expected winners: 25 percent of 100 turns is 25, so money out is 25 × R12,00 = R300,00. The stall banks R100,00 for every 100 turns. The game is UNFAIR to the player — nothing is hidden and nothing is rigged; the prize is simply too small for the odds.

Repair it. A FAIR GAME is one in which, over the long run, neither side expects to gain: money in must equal money out. The 25 expected winners must share the R400,00, so the prize must be 400 ÷ 25 = R16,00. At R16,00 the game breaks even; below that the stall profits; above it, the stall bleeds. That one calculation decodes every raffle, lottery and casino floor a learner will ever meet, and it answers the question honestly: games of chance are priced so the house keeps its margin.

The last questions of this part are with you now — write out the full sample space before calculating, multiply along the branches, and judge fairness by weighing money in against money out over many turns.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas from the fun day itself, with the sweet jar on the cake table, two coins at the games stall, and the teacher's clipboard at the gate.

## Subtopic: From Never to Always — Where Your Words Sit

Every probability in the world lives on one short line, from 0 to 1. Nothing lives outside it.

At 0 sits IMPOSSIBLE — rolling a 7 on an ordinary die, frost in Durban in February. At 1 sits CERTAIN — the sun coming up, the bell ringing eventually. Halfway sits the EVEN CHANCE — a tossed coin. Below halfway is unlikely; above it, likely. If an answer of yours ever comes out negative, or bigger than 1, you have not found something remarkable — you have found a mistake, and found it free of charge, which is exactly what the scale is for.

Now the jar on the cake table: 14 blue sweets, 10 white, 16 pink. Forty sweets. Close your eyes and reach in. What is the chance of blue? Fourteen of the 40 are blue, so 14 out of 40. That is the fraction. Divide 14 by 40 and you get 0,35 — the decimal. Multiply by 100 and you get 35 percent. Three ways of saying one thing, like one person answering to their name, their nickname and their surname.

And the shortcut that keeps paying. What is the chance of NOT blue? You could add the whites and pinks — 26 out of 40 — or you could notice that blue and not-blue must cover the whole jar between them, so they add to 1. Not-blue is 1 minus 0,35, which is 0,65. Whenever a question says "not" or "at least one", ask what is LEFT OVER instead of counting the long way.

Some questions on the scale are with you right now — put each event in its place from never to always, and answer in the form the question asks for.

## Subtopic: Counting Instead of Guessing

There are two honest ways to answer "how likely?", and they come from opposite directions.

The first is thinking. Before touching anything, count what could happen and count what you want: 16 pink sweets out of 40 is a 40 percent chance, 0,4, two in five. No experiment required. That is theory, and it only works when every outcome is equally likely — every sweet the same size, the coin not bent.

The second is counting what actually happened. A learner stands at the rank and tallies 150 taxis; 93 head for town. So 93 out of 150 — 0,62, sixty-two percent. Nothing was assumed; everything was observed. And that is the only method on offer when outcomes are not equally likely, because no amount of reasoning tells you where taxis decide to go.

Now the two together, and here is the part that confuses everyone. Toss a coin 60 times and get 27 heads — 45 percent, not 50. Is the coin bent? No. Small runs wander. Toss it 6 000 times and you will land very close to half. The more you try, the closer the counting creeps to the thinking.

But — and burn this one in — a coin has no memory. Three heads in a row does not make tails "due". The coin does not know what it did last time. The next toss is 50-50, same as the very first. Real money is lost on this belief every single week.

Then the clipboard at the gate. Of 160 learners, 96 come by taxi, so the chance a random learner uses a taxi is 96 out of 160 — 60 percent. Now look closer: of the 100 learners under 16, 64 use a taxi — 64 out of 100, 64 percent — while of the 60 older learners, 32 do, which is 32 out of 60, about 53 percent. Spot what changed: when the question narrows the group, the bottom of the fraction narrows with it. Always ask "out of whom?" before writing anything.

Your questions for this section are ready now. For each one, say out loud which group the answer comes out of before you write the fraction.

## Subtopic: Is the Game Fair?

Two coins at the stall. R4 to play, R12 if both land tails. Sounds generous. Let us check.

First, list what can happen — slowly, because this is where everyone slips. Coin one lands H or T. For each of those, coin two lands H or T. So: HH, HT, TH, TT. Four outcomes, all equally likely. And yes — HT and TH are genuinely different, because the first coin and the second coin are different objects, even when the result looks identical in your palm. Sketch it as a small tree, two branches each splitting into two, and nothing gets lost.

Two tails is 1 outcome out of 4 — a 25 percent chance. There is also a quicker route that skips the drawing: tail AND then tail means multiplying along the path — a half times a half is a quarter. Multiply along the branches. That trick keeps working however many stages pile up.

Now the money, which is the real question. Let 100 people play. The stall collects 100 × R4, which is R400. About 25 of them win, and each collects R12, so the stall pays out 25 × R12 — R300. The stall pockets R100 of every R400 staked. Nothing is rigged, nothing is hidden — the prize is simply too small for the odds.

So what would a FAIR game look like? Fair means neither side expects to finish ahead in the long run: money in equals money out. R400 shared among the 25 expected winners is 400 ÷ 25 — R16. At a R16 prize the stall breaks even. A rand less and the stall profits; a rand more and it loses.

That one sum decodes every raffle, every lottery ticket and every game of chance you will ever be offered. Ask what it costs, ask what fraction win, ask what the prize is — then set money in against money out. Once you can do that, nobody can sell you a bad bet, and that is a life skill wearing an exam topic's clothes.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now. List the whole sample space before you calculate, multiply along the branches, and end with a sentence saying whether the game is fair and what prize would make it so.
