**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First, and it nearly caused a fight: why do heads-tails and tails-heads count as two different outcomes? In your hand they look identical — one head, one tail.

**Tutor:** Because the coins are two separate objects, and probability counts the ways the world can turn out, not what the result looks like once it has landed. Make the coins tellable-apart and the argument evaporates. Put a spot of red paint on one and leave the other plain. Toss them. There is one genuine way for reality to arrange itself with the red coin heads and the plain coin tails, and a second, different way with the red coin tails and the plain coin heads. Painting a coin cannot change any probability — it only reveals the ways that were always there. So the four outcomes are red-head-plain-head, red-head-plain-tail, red-tail-plain-head and red-tail-plain-tail, all equally likely. Now count. Two tails is one way out of four: 0,25. One of each is TWO ways out of four: 0,5 — twice as likely as a matched pair. Learners who merge the mixed results end up with three outcomes and claim a third each, which overstates two-tails and would have cost the stall real prize money. The safe habit is the tree: branch on the first coin, branch each of those on the second, and count the tips. A tree cannot merge outcomes by accident, which is exactly why it is insisted upon.

**Thandi:** Next: the coin at the stall came up heads four times running, and the whole queue was chanting that tails HAS to come next. Is there truly nothing in that?

**Tutor:** Nothing whatsoever for the next toss, and it is worth saying precisely why, because this belief empties wallets every weekend. A coin is a disc with two faces. It carries no record, no memory and no debt. The probability of tails on the next toss is 0,5, exactly as it was on the first toss and on every toss since. That property is called INDEPENDENCE: one trial has no influence on the next. Now, the subtle part — because there is a true idea buried underneath the false one. Over a very long run, the proportion of tails does settle near a half; that is the relative-frequency law you met today. But it settles by DILUTION, not correction. Suppose heads leads by four after four tosses. Toss ten thousand more and those four extra heads become a rounding error against ten thousand — the proportion drifts back toward 0,5 without a single toss ever being nudged toward tails. Nothing catches up; the past just gets outweighed. So the sentence that earns the mark is: each toss is independent, so the probability stays 0,5 regardless of previous results. Write "tails is due" and the mark is gone.

**Thandi:** This one gets me in every test. On the transport table I always pick the right top number and the wrong bottom number. How do I know what to divide by?

**Tutor:** You have diagnosed the exact disease, and the cure is a single question asked before you write anything: "out of whom?" The wording always answers it, and three patterns cover every case. Pattern one ranges over the whole survey — "what is the probability that a randomly chosen learner comes by taxi?" No narrowing, so the denominator is the grand total, 160, and the answer is 96 out of 160, which is 0,6 or 60 percent. Pattern two narrows the group first — "of the learners under 16" or "given that a learner is under 16". Those words shrink the world to one row, so the denominator becomes that row's total, 100, and the answer is 64 out of 100 — 64 percent. Pattern three takes one cell over everything — "the probability that a learner is both under 16 AND comes by taxi" — which is 64 out of 160, or 40 percent. Same cell on top in two of those, completely different meanings underneath. So underline the words that define the group before touching a number, and before that, check the margins: 36 plus 64 is 100, 28 plus 32 is 60, and the column totals of 64 and 96 reach 160. A table that does not balance can never give an answer that does.

**Thandi:** Last one: how can the coin game be unfair when nobody is cheating? The stall uses ordinary coins and the rules are written up on the board for everyone to read.

**Tutor:** That is precisely the insight this topic exists to hand you: in mathematics, unfairness lives in the PRICE, not in dishonesty. A game is FAIR when neither side expects to come out ahead over the long run — money in equals money out. Run the numbers on the stall's completely open game. Over 100 turns it collects 100 times R4,00, which is R400,00. The probability of two tails is 0,25, so about 25 of those turns win, each collecting R12,00 — R300,00 paid out. The stall keeps R100,00 of every R400,00 staked, a quarter of everything, without one crooked move. The prize is simply too small for the odds. Now solve for fairness: the whole R400,00 must flow to the 25 expected winners, so the prize would have to be 400 divided by 25, which is R16,00. At R16,00, nobody wins long-run. A rand below and the stall profits; a rand above and it bleeds. And this one method now travels with you everywhere — raffles, lotteries, competitions on a chip packet. Ask three things: what does it cost, what fraction win, and what is the prize. Then set money in against money out. Every commercial game of chance is priced on the house's side of that comparison — and from today you can measure exactly how far.
