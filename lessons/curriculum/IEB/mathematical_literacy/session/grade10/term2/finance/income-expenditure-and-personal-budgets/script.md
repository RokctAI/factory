# Part 1 — Expert

A family in Tembisa has one payslip, a back room rented out, a set of clippers and combs that earn on Saturdays, and a dozen accounts that all fall due before month-end. Underneath all of it sits a single number: what came in, minus what went out. This session builds that number carefully — reading gross and net income off a payslip, sorting expenditure into fixed, variable and occasional, laying a household budget out properly, and then applying exactly the same thinking to a small stall to find the point where it stops losing money.

## Subtopic: Income — Gross, Deductions and What Actually Arrives

INCOME is money received. A payslip shows it twice, and mixing up the two figures is the single most expensive mistake this topic offers.

GROSS income is the full amount earned before anything is removed. DEDUCTIONS are amounts the employer takes off and pays over on the worker's behalf. NET income — take-home pay — is gross minus total deductions, and it is the only figure a budget is allowed to use, because it is the only money that ever reaches the household.

Read this payslip. Gross salary: R10 500,00. Deductions: PAYE, which is income tax, R840,00; UIF — the Unemployment Insurance Fund, charged at one percent of earnings — R105,00; and a pension contribution of five percent, R525,00. Test that percentage before trusting it: five percent of R10 500 is 0,05 times 10 500, which is R525,00, so the slip is consistent with itself.

Total deductions: R840 plus R105 plus R525 gives R1 470,00. Net income: R10 500 minus R1 470 gives R9 030,00.

Most households have more than one stream of income, and every stream belongs in the budget. This family adds R1 100,00 rent from the back room and R1 070,00 earned braiding and cutting hair on Saturdays. Total household income: R9 030 plus R1 100 plus R1 070 gives R11 200,00 for the month.

Two principles carry the marks here. First, deductions are not expenditure. That money never entered the household, so writing it among the expenses subtracts it a second time and makes the family look poorer than it is. Second, treat variable income with suspicion: the R1 070 from hair was last month's figure, not a guarantee, and a careful budget either uses the lowest recent month or plans as though it might not arrive.

This section's questions are with you now — read gross and net off the payslip correctly, and be ready to explain why only one of those figures may ever enter a budget.

## Subtopic: Expenditure — Fixed, Variable and Occasional

EXPENDITURE is money paid out, and it is sorted into three kinds, because each kind behaves differently the moment money runs short.

FIXED expenses arrive at the same size every month, no matter how the month went: rent R3 400,00, the funeral policy R210,00, school fees R520,00, and the phone contract R229,00. Add them: R3 400 plus R210 plus R520 plus R229 gives R4 359,00. These are contracts and standing commitments — they cannot be shrunk this month by simply deciding to use less.

VARIABLE expenses move with how much is used or bought: groceries R2 750,00, prepaid electricity R820,00, taxi fare R940,00, airtime and data R260,00, and toiletries and cleaning materials R330,00. Add them: R2 750 plus R820 plus R940 plus R260 plus R330 gives R5 100,00. These are the household's only quick lever — the amounts that can genuinely be changed within a single month.

OCCASIONAL expenses come only a few times a year, but they arrive like a wave if nothing was saved for them: school uniforms and stationery, put aside at R350,00 a month, and December with its travel and family costs at R450,00 a month, together R800,00. Saving monthly for a known future cost is called PROVISIONING, and it is the difference between a December that was planned and a January spent repaying it.

Hold on to the distinction that gets tested hardest. Electricity is variable even though it is bought every month — the amount responds to use. Rent is fixed even though you could move next year — within this budget period, nothing you do changes it. The test is never "does it come every month?" It is "does the amount change with how much I use?"

Total expenditure: R4 359 plus R5 100 plus R800 gives R10 259,00.

Work through this section's questions now, classifying every item and giving the reason for each classification — the reason is where the marks sit.

## Subtopic: The Budget and Its Bottom Line

A BUDGET is a plan: expected income set against expected expenditure for a stated period, drawn up BEFORE the period starts. An INCOME AND EXPENDITURE STATEMENT is the record of what actually happened, written afterwards. Both get examined, and the difference in tense is the entire difference between them.

Lay the budget out. Income: R11 200,00. Expenditure: R10 259,00. Subtract: R11 200 minus R10 259 gives R941,00. Income exceeds expenditure, so the family has a SURPLUS of R941,00. Had the subtraction gone the other way, the shortfall would be a DEFICIT — and a deficit must always be funded from somewhere: savings, borrowing, or a bill left unpaid.

Now interpret, because a budget that has only been added up has not yet been used. Express the large items as percentages of income. Rent: 3 400 divided by 11 200 gives 0,3036, which is 30,4 percent. Groceries: 2 750 divided by 11 200 gives 0,2455, which is 24,6 percent. Transport: 940 divided by 11 200 gives 0,0839, which is 8,4 percent. The roof and the food together take fifty-five percent of everything this family earns — which is the honest shape of most South African household budgets, and the reason a rent increase is felt in the very same week it happens.

Then stress-test the plan. Suppose the Saturday hair work stops: income drops by R1 070 to R10 130,00, while expenditure stays at R10 259,00. The bottom line becomes R10 130 minus R10 259, which is negative R129,00 — a deficit of R129,00.

Close the gap in the correct order. Fixed items cannot move this month, so the variables must: airtime and data trimmed from R260 to R180 saves R80; groceries reduced by R100 saves R100; the December provision eased from R450 to R400 saves R50. Total recovered: R80 plus R100 plus R50 gives R230,00. New expenditure: R10 259 minus R230 gives R10 029,00, against income of R10 130,00 — a surplus of R101,00. Narrow, but positive, and achieved without breaking a single contract.

Take on this section's questions now: calculate a bottom line, name it surplus or deficit, and defend which items you would adjust first.

## Subtopic: Break-Even — When the Stall Starts Making Money

The same three ideas run a business. A koeksister stall outside the taxi rank pays R450,00 a month for its table and R270,00 for a gas refill — FIXED COSTS of R720,00, owed whether one koeksister is sold or none at all. Each koeksister takes R2,80 of flour, oil, syrup and spice — a VARIABLE COST, spent only when one is actually made. The selling price is R6,50.

INCOME is selling price times the number sold. TOTAL COST is fixed cost plus variable cost times the number sold. PROFIT is income minus total cost, and a negative profit is a LOSS.

The BREAK-EVEN POINT is the number sold at which income exactly meets total cost — the moment the stall stops going backwards without yet going forwards. Find it through the contribution each sale makes: R6,50 received minus R2,80 spent leaves R3,70 per koeksister towards the R720 of fixed costs. The number needed is 720 divided by 3,70, which is 194,59.

Now round with judgement, because that is the examinable step. Nobody sells 0,59 of a koeksister. Rounding DOWN to 194 leaves her behind: income is 194 times R6,50, which is R1 261,00, while total cost is R720 plus 194 times R2,80 — R720 plus R543,20, or R1 263,20 — a loss of R2,20. Rounding UP to 195: income R1 267,50 against total cost of R720 plus R546,00, which is R1 266,00 — a profit of R1,50. Break-even is therefore 195 koeksisters a month. Whenever a break-even calculation lands on a decimal, round up.

Then the actual month. She trades 24 days and sells about 15 a day: 24 times 15 gives 360 koeksisters. Income: 360 times R6,50 is R2 340,00. Total cost: R720 plus 360 times R2,80, which is R720 plus R1 008,00, or R1 728,00. Profit: R2 340 minus R1 728 gives R612,00 for the month.

Finish in a sentence, always: "The stall breaks even at 195 koeksisters a month and, selling about 360, makes a profit of R612,00." The number on its own is half an answer; the sentence is the whole one.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — separate fixed costs from variable costs before anything else, and round break-even upward with a stated reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same month at the kitchen table, with the payslip flattened out on one side, the accounts on the other, and the only question any household really asks: is there anything left?

## Subtopic: Money That Walks In, Money That Walks Out

Take two envelopes. Every rand that comes IN this month is written on the first envelope. Every rand that goes OUT is written on the second. That is a budget. The rest is labelling.

Now the payslip's first trick. It says R10 500 at the top, and you will never hold R10 500. Before the money reaches you, three pieces are carved off: tax, R840; a small slice for the unemployment fund, R105 — one rand out of every hundred earned; and R525 into a pension for the person you will be at sixty. Those three pieces come to R1 470, and what actually lands in the account is R10 500 take away R1 470 — R9 030.

Only that R9 030 may be written on the first envelope. Building a budget on the R10 500 is cooking supper with meat you have not bought yet. And the follow-on mistake matters just as much: never write "tax" on the second envelope. It never came in, so it cannot go out. Count it twice and the whole month quietly stops adding up.

Everything else that arrives belongs on the first envelope too. R1 100 from the back room. R1 070 from Saturday's clippers and braids. Add the three: R9 030 plus R1 100 plus R1 070 — R11 200 walked in this month.

One honest warning about that hair money: it is not a salary. It came last month; it might not come this month. A careful household budgets on the money it can rely on and treats the rest as a bonus when it lands.

Some questions on the in-envelope are with you right now — use what reaches the bank, never the number printed at the top of the slip.

## Subtopic: The Bills That Don't Care How Your Month Went

Now the second envelope, and notice that the bills are not all the same animal.

Some bills take no notice of you at all. Rent, R3 400. The funeral policy, R210. School fees, R520. The phone contract, R229. Careful month or careless month, they arrive at the same size — R4 359 of them altogether. You cannot rescue a bad month by using less rent.

Other bills listen to you. Groceries, R2 750. Prepaid electricity, R820. Taxi fare, R940. Airtime and data, R260. Toiletries and cleaning, R330 — R5 100 in total. Use less, pay less. This is the only part of the out-envelope that moves this week, which is why it is the first place to look when money runs short.

Then the ambush bills — the ones that come only now and then, but come heavy. Uniforms and stationery in January. December itself. A funeral, a wedding, a burst geyser. The trick every gogo already knows is to pay them in slices before they arrive: R350 a month towards uniforms, R450 a month towards December — R800 put aside so that those months arrive as a plan instead of a panic.

And here is the test that keeps you safe when a question asks you to classify. Do not ask "does it come every month?" Ask "does the amount change when I change what I do?" Electricity comes every month and still shrinks when you switch off the geyser — so it listens to you. Rent is identical every month whatever you do — so it does not.

Questions on sorting the bills are here now. For each one, name its kind and — more importantly — say WHY.

## Subtopic: The Koeksisters That Pay for the Table

Now the bottom line, which is one subtraction. R11 200 walked in; R10 259 walked out. R11 200 take away R10 259 leaves R941 standing at month-end. Money left over is a surplus; had the out-envelope been heavier, that gap would be a deficit — and a deficit is always paid by somebody: savings, a loan, or an account left unpaid.

Look at the shape of the month, not only the leftover. Rent takes R3 400 out of R11 200 — just over 30 out of every 100 rands. Groceries take R2 750 — nearly 25 in every 100. The roof and the food together swallow more than half of everything this family earns, which is exactly why a rent increase hurts the same week it is announced.

Now run the same thinking through a small business, because it is the identical sum. A woman sells koeksisters outside the taxi rank. Before she sells a single one she owes R720 for the month — R450 for the table, R270 for gas. Each koeksister costs her R2,80 to make and sells for R6,50, so every sale puts R3,70 in her hand towards that R720. How many must she sell before she stops going backwards? R720 shared into portions of R3,70 gives 194 and a bit. You cannot sell a bit of a koeksister, and 194 still leaves her R2,20 short — so she needs 195. When a break-even answer lands on a decimal, always go UP to the next whole one.

Then the real month: 24 trading days at about 15 koeksisters a day is 360 sold. R6,50 each brings in R2 340. Her costs are the R720 plus 360 portions of R2,80, which is R1 008 — R1 728 in total. R2 340 take away R1 728 leaves R612 profit. She is far past her 195, and now she knows exactly how far.

Your last questions are with you now. Do the subtraction, keep the rand sign and the cents, and end with one plain sentence saying what the household or the stall should actually do next — because a bottom line without a decision is only half the work.
