# Part 1 — Expert

A shopper in Polokwane sets a basket down at the till: rice, eggs, fresh vegetables, washing powder, a two-litre cooldrink and a packet of sweets. The slip that prints carries three numbers she needs to understand — the total, the VAT, and the part of the basket that was never taxed at all. Later that same week the household must decide whether to buy a washing machine at this week's sale price or leave the money in the bank earning interest. This session builds the three tools that settle both questions — VAT, discount and simple interest — and runs each one forwards and backwards, because questions come in both directions.

## Subtopic: VAT — the Tax Already Inside the Price

VALUE-ADDED TAX is a tax on goods and services, charged in South Africa at 15 percent and collected by the seller on behalf of SARS. Two terms decide every VAT question. A price EXCLUSIVE of VAT is the price before the tax goes on. A price INCLUSIVE of VAT is the price with the tax already inside it — and by law, the price on a South African shop shelf is the inclusive one.

Forwards, from exclusive to inclusive, is the friendly direction. A wholesaler quotes a camping gas stove at R840,00 excluding VAT. The VAT is 840 × 0,15, which is R126,00, so the inclusive price is R966,00. One step does the same work: 840 × 1,15 gives R966,00.

Backwards is where the marks fall. A till slip shows R1 069,50 including VAT — find the amount before VAT. Subtracting 15 percent is wrong, because the 15 percent was calculated on the smaller exclusive price, not on the R1 069,50. Undo the multiplication instead: 1 069,50 ÷ 1,15 gives R930,00. The VAT is then 1 069,50 − 930, which is R139,50, and the check closes the loop: 930 × 0,15 is R139,50.

Now the fact the basket forces into view: not everything is taxed. ZERO-RATED goods carry VAT at zero percent so that basic food stays affordable — brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, vegetable oil, tinned pilchards and illuminating paraffin all sit on that list.

Work the basket. Zero-rated: rice R145,00, eggs R55,00, fresh vegetables R48,00 — R248,00 carrying no VAT at all. Standard-rated: washing powder R62,50, the cooldrink R41,00, sweets R34,50 — R138,00, VAT already inside. The exclusive price of that portion is 138 ÷ 1,15, which is R120,00, so the VAT is 138 − 120, or R18,00. Basket total: R248,00 plus R138,00 gives R386,00, of which R18,00 is VAT.

The trap is now measurable: 15 percent of the R386,00 total would give R57,90 — more than three times the true VAT — because it taxes the rice and the eggs. Separate the zero-rated items before any VAT arithmetic begins.

The questions for this section are with you now: keep inclusive and exclusive apart, divide by 1,15 rather than subtracting, and check whether an item is zero-rated before you tax it.

## Subtopic: Discount — What Comes Off, and Off What

A DISCOUNT is a reduction on the MARKED PRICE, and every answer hangs on one question: what amount is the percentage being taken OF? A winter jacket is marked R780,00 and the sign promises 25 percent off. The discount amount is 780 × 0,25, which is R195,00; the SALE PRICE is 780 − 195, or R585,00. The single-multiplier route is quicker and safer: a 25 percent decrease leaves 75 percent, so 780 × 0,75 gives R585,00. Decrease means one minus the decimal; increase means one plus it.

Now the sign that catches nearly everyone. The rail says 15 percent off, and a poster at the till adds a further 10 percent. Instinct says 25 percent. The shop knows otherwise. Step one: 780 × 0,85 gives R663,00. Step two, taken on the NEW price: 663 × 0,90 gives R596,70. Combine the multipliers instead — 0,85 × 0,90 gives 0,765 — and the honest headline appears: the shopper pays 76,5 percent of the marked price, a discount of 23,5 percent, not 25. The saving is R183,30, not R195,00. Successive percentages never add, because each one bites only what the previous one left behind.

Two further facts worth banking. Because the marked price already contains VAT, a discount shrinks the VAT along with it: inside R596,70 sits VAT of R77,83, where inside the original R780,00 sat R101,74. And read promotional wording with a lawyer's eye: "buy two, get one free" is not half price — three items for the price of two is one item in three unpaid, a discount of a third, 33,3 percent.

Reverse discounts work exactly like reverse VAT. If R596,70 is the price after a combined 23,5 percent discount, the marked price is 596,70 ÷ 0,765, which is R780,00. A multiplier is always undone by dividing by it.

Take on this section's questions now, and be strict about the base: name the amount each percentage is coming off before your finger touches the calculator.

## Subtopic: Simple Interest — Growth in a Straight Line

INTEREST is the price of money — paid to you when the bank uses yours, paid by you when you use the bank's. The INTEREST RATE is a percentage; the INTEREST itself is a rand amount, and confusing the two is a steady source of lost marks. SIMPLE INTEREST is calculated on the ORIGINAL amount every period, never on interest already earned: interest equals principal times rate times time, with the rate written as a decimal and the time measured in YEARS.

A learner banks R6 000,00 from a holiday job in a fixed deposit paying 6,5 percent simple interest per year, for four years. Write the substitution line before anything else, because it carries its own mark: interest = 6 000 × 0,065 × 4. In order: 6 000 × 0,065 gives R390,00 — one year's interest — and R390,00 × 4 gives R1 560,00. The closing balance is R7 560,00.

Look at the shape of that growth. The interest is R390,00 in year one, in year two, in year three and in year four — identical every time, because each year's calculation goes back to the same R6 000. On a graph, simple interest is a straight line, and that line is the entire meaning of the word "simple".

Time must be in years, and this is the topic's most reliable trap. Nine months is 9 ÷ 12, which is 0,75 of a year; fifteen months is 1,25; six months is 0,5. Substitute 9 for nine months and the answer comes out twelve times too large.

Run one backwards, because questions do. An amount grows from R6 000,00 to R6 660,00 in two years — find the rate. The interest earned is R660,00. Substitute: 660 = 6 000 × rate × 2, so 660 = 12 000 × rate, and the rate is 660 ÷ 12 000, which is 0,055 — 5,5 percent per year. Peel off what you know, divide by what remains, and write "per year", because the unit is part of the answer.

Work through this section's questions now: substitute in full, convert months to years before anything else, and round once, at the end, to the cent.

## Subtopic: The Washing-Machine Decision — Three Tools, One Answer

This topic exists to serve decisions, so it ends with one. A washing machine is marked R6 800,00 and this week the store takes 20 percent off. The family has R5 900,00 in a savings account earning 5 percent simple interest per year, and had planned to buy in six months' time.

Price the sale first. The sale price is 6 800 × 0,80, which is R5 440,00 — a saving of R1 360,00. Should a question ask, the VAT inside that sale price is 5 440 − 4 730,43, which is R709,57.

Now price the waiting. Left untouched for six months, the savings earn 5 900 × 0,05 × 0,5 — six months being 0,5 of a year. That gives 5 900 × 0,025, which is R147,50. But in six months the sale is over and the machine is back at R6 800,00.

Set the options side by side. Buy now: R5 440,00 leaves the account, R460,00 stays behind, and the machine works for the family all winter. Wait: the savings grow to R6 047,50 while the machine returns to R6 800,00 — leaving the family R752,50 SHORT of buying it at all. Netting the two effects, waiting earns R147,50 of interest and surrenders R1 360,00 of discount: a net loss of R1 212,50.

Answer in a sentence, always: "The family should buy during the sale, because the R1 360 discount is roughly nine times the R147,50 of interest that waiting would earn — a net gain of R1 212,50 — and after six months their savings would still be R752,50 short of the full price."

Finally, the habits that turn working into marks across this whole topic: write the multiplier or the formula before substituting; keep the rand sign and the cents; divide rather than subtract when stripping VAT or undoing a discount; convert months to years; round once, at the end, to two decimals; and close with a sentence that answers the question actually asked.

The last questions of this part are in front of you now — compare the two options in full, show both totals and the difference, and end each answer with a recommendation in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three tools at the till of the corner shop, where nobody says "exclusive of value-added tax" and everybody still knows exactly how much money left their pocket.

## Subtopic: The Till Slip at the Corner Shop

Here is the thing about VAT that trips everyone up: it is already in the price. When the shelf says R62,50 for washing powder, R62,50 is what you hand over. The tax is not waiting to pounce at the till — it was folded into the dough before the bread was baked.

So the question "how much VAT did I pay?" really means "how much of this R62,50 was tax?" And you cannot answer that by taking 15 percent of R62,50, because the 15 percent was added onto a smaller number to build the R62,50 in the first place. Think of sugar stirred into tea: once it is in, you cannot measure the sugar by taking a percentage of the full cup.

The move that works is division. Price with tax equals price without tax times 1,15 — so travel backwards by dividing. Try it on the taxed part of the basket: R62,50 plus R41,00 plus R34,50 is R138,00. Divide by 1,15 and you get R120,00 — the shop's own price. Take that away from R138,00 and the tax stands alone: R18,00.

And now the kindness built into the system. The rice, the eggs and the vegetables pay no tax at all — zero-rated, along with brown bread, maize meal, samp, beans, milk, cooking oil, tinned pilchards and paraffin. That is R248,00 the taxman never touches. So the whole basket is R386,00 and only R18,00 of it is tax. Take 15 percent of R386,00 instead and you get R57,90 — and you have just taxed the rice, which nobody does.

Some questions on the slip are with you right now — first ask which items are taxed at all, then divide by 1,15 instead of subtracting.

## Subtopic: The Sale Sign That Says More Than It Gives

A winter jacket is marked R780,00 and the sign shouts 25 percent off. Ten percent of R780 is R78, so 20 percent is R156 and another 5 percent — half of R78 — is R39: R195 off, and you pay R585,00. The faster route, and the one that rescues you in every chained question: if 25 percent comes off, 75 percent stays on, so multiply by 0,75 in one press. R780 × 0,75 is R585,00.

Now the sneaky rail. One sign says 15 percent off; the poster at the till says a further 10 percent. Everyone's head says 25. It is not 25. The first sign takes 15 percent off R780, leaving R663,00. The second sign takes its 10 percent off the R663 — not off the R780 — leaving R596,70. Compare that with a true 25 percent off, which would be R585,00. You paid R11,70 more than the signs made you feel.

Why? Because the second discount arrives after the first has already shrunk the price, so it has less to work on: it removes R66,30 instead of R78,00. Percentages of different amounts are different amounts of money — the second percentage only eats what the first one left on the plate.

One more sign to translate: "buy two, get one free" sounds like half price and is not. Three items for the price of two saves you one in three — a third off, about 33 percent — and only if you actually wanted three.

Your questions for this section are here now. Before you calculate anything, say out loud which price the percentage is coming off — the marked price, or the already-reduced one.

## Subtopic: Money That Grows the Same Amount Every Year

Simple interest is the most honest growth there is, because it never changes its mind. Every year the bank pays the same amount, worked out on what you first put in.

A learner banks R6 000,00 from a holiday job at 6,5 percent a year — six rand fifty for every hundred. Each year the bank pays 6 000 × 0,065, which is R390,00. Not a cent more in year two, not a cent more in year four. Four identical payments come to R1 560,00, and the learner closes on R7 560,00.

That is the whole machine: same amount, every year, straight line. Interest equals the money you started with, times the rate as a decimal, times the number of years.

One warning that costs more marks than any other in this topic: the time goes in as YEARS. Six months is not 6 — it is 0,5. Nine months is 9 divided by 12, which is 0,75. Put 9 where 0,75 belongs and the answer balloons to twelve times its true size — and a savings account that somehow doubles your money in months should stop you cold.

Now the decision that gathers all three tools. A washing machine is R6 800,00, on sale at 20 percent off: 6 800 × 0,80 is R5 440,00 — R1 360 saved. The family has R5 900 in savings at 5 percent a year and meant to wait six months. That interest is 5 900 × 0,05 × 0,5, which is R147,50. So waiting earns R147,50 and throws away R1 360 — a net loss of R1 212,50. Worse still: in six months the savings stand at R6 047,50 while the machine is back at R6 800 — R752,50 out of reach. Buying now wins, and it is not even close.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now. Show the working line before the answer, keep the rand sign and the cents on it, and finish with one plain sentence saying what you would actually tell the family to do — because a number without a decision is only half of what the marker wants to see.
