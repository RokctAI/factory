**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that comes up every single time: why can't I just take 15 percent off a VAT-inclusive price to get back to the price before VAT? Adding 15 percent put the VAT there, so taking 15 percent off should remove it.

**Tutor:** Because adding VAT was never the addition of a fixed amount — it was a MULTIPLICATION by 1,15, and a multiplication is undone by dividing, not by subtracting. Watch the sizes and the error shows itself. Take the taxed part of our basket, R138,00 including VAT. The shop's own price was R120,00, and SARS took 15 percent of that R120,00, which is R18,00. Now run the wrong method: 15 percent of R138,00 is R20,70, and removing it leaves R117,30 — you have stripped out more tax than was ever charged, because you took the percentage of the bigger number. The correct method divides: R138,00 ÷ 1,15 gives exactly R120,00, and the VAT is the difference, R18,00. Prove it in reverse — R120,00 × 1,15 lands back on R138,00. So the rule to carry with you: multiply by 1,15 to put VAT on, divide by 1,15 to take it off. And here is a bonus shortcut for pulling the VAT straight out of an inclusive price: divide the inclusive amount by 23 and multiply by 3, because 15 over 115 simplifies to 3 over 23. R138,00 ÷ 23 is R6,00, times 3 is R18,00. Same answer, and a tidy check on your first method.

**Thandi:** Next: our whole basket came to R386,00 and the VAT on the slip was only R18,00. That is less than five percent. Did the shop get the tax wrong, or did we?

**Tutor:** Neither — you have just met zero-rating, and your slip measured it perfectly. South Africa charges VAT at 15 percent on most goods, but a defined list of basic foodstuffs and fuels is zero-rated, meaning the rate applied to them is zero percent. Brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans and lentils, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, vegetable oil, tinned pilchards and illuminating paraffin all sit on that list, and the reason is social policy: those items make up a far bigger share of a poor household's spending than of a wealthy one's, so taxing them would press hardest where there is least room. In your basket, R248,00 was zero-rated and only R138,00 was standard-rated. The R18,00 you paid is a full fifteen percent of the taxed portion — but under five percent of the whole basket, which is exactly the design working. The habit to take into any till-slip question follows directly: sort the items into taxed and zero-rated first, total the taxed ones separately, and only then divide by 1,15. Total everything first and then take 15 percent and you will overstate the VAT — on this slip, by more than three times.

**Thandi:** This one caused a proper argument. If a shop takes 15 percent off and then another 10 percent off, does the order matter? And why is it still not 25 percent off?

**Tutor:** Two sharp questions with opposite answers. The second one first. The discounts do not add because each is calculated on what the one before it left standing. Fifteen percent off R780,00 leaves R663,00. The ten percent then comes off R663,00, not off R780,00, so it removes R66,30 rather than R78,00 — and the shopper pays R596,70. Multiply the multipliers, 0,85 × 0,90, and you get 0,765: the shopper pays 76,5 percent of the marked price, a 23,5 percent discount, not 25. The missing R11,70 is precisely the gap between ten percent of R780 and ten percent of R663. Now for the order. Multiplication is indifferent to sequence: 0,90 × 0,85 is also 0,765, so ten percent first and fifteen percent second lands on the same R596,70 to the cent. That is worth knowing, because questions sometimes swap the order to test whether you follow a memorised recipe or actually command the multipliers. What DOES change with order is mixing a discount with a charge on a different base — a delivery fee added before a discount is itself discounted, added after it is not, and those totals genuinely differ. So read the wording: it tells you what each percentage is taken of, and that is the only thing that ever matters.

**Thandi:** Last one, about the washing machine. Waiting six months earns the family R147,50 of interest — money for doing nothing. How can you call that a loss?

**Tutor:** Because they end up with more money and a worse position, and prising those two ideas apart is the whole point of the exercise. Compare the complete pictures. Buying in the sale costs R5 440,00 — that is R6 800 × 0,80 — leaves R460,00 in the account, and puts a working machine in the house for the next six months. Waiting grows the savings by 5 900 × 0,05 × 0,5, which is R147,50, to R6 047,50 — but by then the sale is history and the machine is back at R6 800,00, which is R752,50 more than they have. The R147,50 is real; it is simply dwarfed by the R1 360,00 of discount they let expire. Net the two effects and waiting is R1 212,50 worse. The transferable lesson runs through all of financial mathematics: a 5 percent interest rate is a slow, steady trickle on the money you hold, while a 20 percent discount is an immediate lump on a much larger base. Never compare percentage against percentage — convert both into rand amounts on the actual sums involved, and compare the rands. Then write the recommendation as a sentence, because the reasoning mark cannot attach to a bare number sitting alone at the bottom of the page.
