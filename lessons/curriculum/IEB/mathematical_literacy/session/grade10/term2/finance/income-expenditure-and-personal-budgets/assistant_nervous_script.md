Hey, it's Thandi — a payslip, twelve accounts, three kinds of cost, and something called break-even. It can feel like somebody smuggled an accounting degree into your Mathematical Literacy lesson. They did not. This whole topic is one subtraction with tidy bookkeeping in front of it.

Start there. Money in, money out, subtract. That is the skeleton of every budget question you will ever meet. All the vocabulary — gross, net, fixed, variable, occasional, surplus, deficit — is just labels telling you which pile a number belongs to before you subtract.

The payslip is where the most marks leak away, so nail it down. Gross is what was earned. Deductions are what was removed before the money reached the bank. Net is what actually arrived, and NET is the only figure allowed into a budget. And the partner rule that saves you a second time: the deductions never arrived, so they never appear as expenses either. Count them once, on the way past.

Sorting the bills takes exactly one test, and it is not the obvious one. Never ask "does it come every month?" Ask "does the amount change with what I do?" Electricity arrives monthly and still shrinks when you use less, so it is variable. Rent arrives monthly and ignores you completely, so it is fixed. Say the test out loud for each item and you will not misclassify again.

For break-even, keep one sentence: every item sold contributes its selling price minus its own cost, and you keep selling until those contributions have paid off the fixed costs. R6,50 minus R2,80 is R3,70 a koeksister; R720 divided by R3,70 gives 194,59; and you round UP, because 194 still leaves her short. That is a rule with a reason, and writing the reason earns the mark.

One last habit, the one that turns a right answer into a full answer: end with a sentence. Not "R941" — "The family has a surplus of R941, which should go into the December provision." A number without a decision is half the work. You've got this!
