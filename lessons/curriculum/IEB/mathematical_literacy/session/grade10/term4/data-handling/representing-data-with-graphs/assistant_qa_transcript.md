**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up again and again with graphs! First: bar graph or histogram — I know one has gaps, but under pressure I freeze. Is there a five-second test?

**Tutor:** Ask one question of the horizontal axis: is it a list of names, or a number line? If the axis carries named groups — pie types, taxi routes, provinces, sports codes — nothing exists between the labels, so the bars stand apart with gaps: a bar graph. If the axis carries intervals of numbers — marks from nought to 49, ages, masses, waiting times — the intervals tile a continuous number line, 29 followed immediately by 30, so the bars must stand shoulder to shoulder: a histogram. The gaps and the touching are not style choices; each is a factual claim. A gap says nothing can live here — true between chicken and beef, false between 29 and 30 marks. That is also how you answer the two-mark explanation question — why do these bars touch — in one sentence: because the data is grouped along a continuous number line, and a gap would claim a stretch of values nobody could occupy. Five seconds: names apart, numbers together.

***

**Thandi:** Next: choosing a scale. I never know whether to count in fives, fifties or hundreds, and I am scared of losing easy marks there.

**Tutor:** Let the biggest value choose for you, under three constraints. Start at nought — a number axis starting anywhere else distorts every comparison. Use equal steps, because a graph's entire meaning is that equal distances stand for equal amounts. And pick the step so the biggest value fits in roughly ten to twelve jumps: pie counts peaking at 32 suit steps of 5 up to 35; takings peaking at R490 suit steps of 50 up to 500. Steps of 5 there would demand nearly a hundred gridlines, and steps of 500 would flatten the whole week into one stripe. Then write the numbers on the axis before drawing any bar, and plot by counting steps, not by eyeballing. Two further habits plug the common leaks: label the axis with its unit — takings in rand — because a bare axis costs a mark, and after drawing, read each bar back against the table. Most scale marks are lost not to bad choices but to unlabelled or uneven axes drawn in a rush.

***

**Thandi:** Third: the pie chart says chicken is 40 percent. One question asks how many pies that is, but another asked for the angle. Which calculation is which?

**Tutor:** Both grow from the same idea — the slice is a fraction of the whole — and they differ only in what the whole is. When the question wants an amount, the whole is the total number of items: 40 percent of 80 pies is 32 pies, and if a bigger week sells 150 pies in the same proportions, chicken is 0,40 times 150, which is 60 pies. When the question wants the angle, the whole is the circle itself: 360 degrees, so chicken's slice spans 0,40 times 360, which is 144 degrees. Same fraction, two different wholes — pies for amounts, degrees for angles. And both versions carry a built-in audit that markers love to test: the percentages of all slices must total 100, and the angles must total 360. Our four types: 144 plus 108 plus 72 plus 36 is exactly 360. If a printed pie's numbers fail that audit, the question is probably inviting you to find the error — and now you can, by adding the shares before doing anything else.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: interpretation questions say describe the trend and suggest a reason. My description was fine but my reason earned nothing. What counts as a good reason?

**Tutor:** A good reason is one the data itself makes plausible, tied to the exact feature you described. Structure the answer in three layers. Facts first, with numbers: takings fell to R120 on Wednesday, the week's lowest point, against a high of R490 on Friday. Trend second, with evidence: apart from Wednesday, the week rises steadily, Monday's 340 to Friday's 490. Reason third — and this is where marks leak away — the reason must connect the context to the dip: a power cut left the warmers cold, so no hot pies could be sold and buyers stayed away. That earns marks because it explains lower sales on that specific day. Reasons that fail are either fantasies the graph cannot support or vague mumbles like something went wrong. You are not being asked to know the true cause — the word suggest invites any sensible possibility — but sensible means: if your reason were true, the graph would look exactly as it does. Test your reason against that sentence before writing it, and the interpretation marks follow.
