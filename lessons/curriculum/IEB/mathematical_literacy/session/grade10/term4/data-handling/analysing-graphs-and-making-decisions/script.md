# Part 1 — Expert

Anyone can draw a graph; the marks that matter go to the person who can interrogate one, because interrogation is where data handling turns into decisions. Our material today is one Makhanda household's municipal year, told in three pictures. A line graph of monthly electricity: a summer floor of 320 units in January, a steady climb through 500 units in May, a winter peak of 575 in June, then a slide back down. A pair of bars showing water use before and after a leaking garden tap was replaced: 25 kilolitres down to 18. And a pie chart carving the household's R3 200 municipal bill into electricity, water, refuse and sewerage. From these three pictures we will lift values, compute ranges and percentage changes, convert shares into rand, and close with the analyst's last responsibilities: conclusions that stay inside the evidence, predictions that wear their conditions, and a trained eye for the axis trick that lets a perfectly honest graph mislead.

## Subtopic: Reading Values and Ranges from a Graph

Every piece of analysis rests on a reading, so the reading comes first — cold, careful extraction, with no opinions attached, because an error here contaminates every calculation built on top of it.

To lift a value off the electricity line: locate the month along the horizontal axis, travel straight up to the plotted point, then straight across to the vertical axis, and read the number against the scale. June: up to the summit of the line, across to 575 units. January: down at 320, the quietest month of the year. When a point sits between gridlines, read it proportionally — a point midway between 450 and 500 is 475 — and markers accept a small tolerance on between-line readings, but only a small one.

Maximum, minimum and range are still pure reading. The maximum is the highest plotted point: June's 575. The minimum is the lowest: January's 320. The range is the gap between them: 575 minus 320 gives 255 units. Range tells you how widely the household's behaviour swings over a year — a wide range like this one is the fingerprint of seasonal living, winter appliances stacked on top of the ordinary daily load.

Two habits guard everything that follows. First, interrogate the scale before touching any point: one gridline step might be worth 25 units or 50, and guessing plants the same error in every subsequent answer. Second, never let a number travel without its unit: 575 units, 25 kilolitres, R3 200. In data work the unit is part of the answer, and a bare 575 on its own will cost you the mark.

The questions for this section are with you now. Straight extraction: single values, the peak, the floor, and the range that separates them.

## Subtopic: Trends, Comparisons and Percentage Change

Now we let the graph speak. A trend is the overall direction of the data, stated with its evidence attached. The electricity line climbs from mid-summer towards winter — 320 units in January up to 575 in June — and then eases off towards spring. The sentence pattern that earns full trend marks: usage rises steadily from the January low of 320 units to a winter peak of 575 in June, then declines. Direction plus supporting numbers — that pairing is what a proper description looks like, and it will always outscore a single word like increasing.

Comparisons put numbers on differences. May against June: 575 minus 500 leaves 75 more units in June. The sharper instrument is percentage change, which measures the jump relative to its starting point: change divided by original, times 100. From May to June: 75 over 500 gives 0,15, times 100 — a 15 percent rise. The denominator is always the original, earlier value. Divide by the new 575 instead and you get about 13,0 percent — a genuine number, but the answer to a different question. Change is measured from where you started, never from where you arrived.

The water bars let the same tool shine. Before the repair: 25 kilolitres a month. After: 18. The saving is 7 kilolitres, and as a percentage of the before value: 7 over 25 gives 0,28 — a 28 percent drop. One replaced tap cut the household's water use by more than a quarter, and the bars prove it at a glance.

Why do percentages beat raw differences? Because 7 kilolitres means nothing until you know it came off a base of 25 — carved off a base of 200 it would be a rounding error. A percentage carries its own context inside it, which is precisely why assessment questions keep asking for one.

Work through this section's questions now: trends stated with evidence, straight differences, and percentage changes with the original held firmly in the denominator.

## Subtopic: From Graph to Rand — Calculating with What You Read

Municipal graphs earn their place in this course because they price real money, and questions always walk you across the bridge from picture to rand.

Electricity first. Prepaid units cost R2,40 each. June's 575 units cost 575 times 2,40 — split it as 575 times 2, which is 1 150, plus 575 times 0,4, which is 230 — R1 380 for the peak month. January's 320 units: 320 times 2,40 is 640 plus 128 — R768. The seasonal swing in rand: 1 380 minus 768 leaves R612 between the dearest and cheapest month, and the line graph has just become a budgeting document.

Now price the tap repair. Saving 7 kilolitres a month at R35 per kilolitre: 7 times 35 gives R245 saved every month. Over a year: 245 times 12 — R2 940. The plumber charged R580 for the job. Two months of savings give 490 — not there yet; three months give 735, comfortably past 580. The repair pays for itself during the third month, and every month after that is pure saving. This is the analysis assessments prize most: data, converted into money, converted into a decision the numbers can defend.

The pie chart finishes the tour. The whole bill is R3 200. Electricity takes 45 percent of the circle: 0,45 times 3 200 gives R1 440. Water takes 30 percent: 0,30 times 3 200 gives R960. Refuse 15 percent: R480. Sewerage 10 percent: R320. Audit the answer: 1 440 plus 960 plus 480 plus 320 lands exactly on 3 200, and the four shares total 100 percent. Every pie calculation is share times whole, and the audit line at the end catches your own slips as well as a question's deliberate inconsistencies.

Look at the common shape of all three calculations: read a number off the graph, multiply by a rate or a share, then make the answer mean something — a budget, a repair verdict, a bill breakdown. Reading, arithmetic, meaning: that is the three-beat rhythm of every analysis question.

This section's questions are in front of you — each one crosses that bridge from picture to rand.

## Subtopic: Conclusions, Predictions and the Axis That Lies

The closing skills are what separate analysis from arithmetic: claiming only what the data can carry, and catching graphs built to deceive.

A conclusion must stand on the numbers. Supported: winter months use the most electricity — June's 575 units against January's 320. Unsupported: the family is careless with electricity — nothing in the graph measures care. The discipline is simple: every conclusion sentence carries its evidence inside it, and asserts nothing the picture cannot show.

A prediction extends a pattern, and an honest prediction always wears its seatbelt. Next June will probably peak again somewhere near 575 units, IF the household's winter routine stays the same. The hedge words — probably, roughly, if the pattern holds — are rewarded, because data supports likelihood, never certainty. A prediction dressed up as fact is an overreach; refusing to predict at all wastes a perfectly good pattern. The middle road: a number, plus a condition.

Now for the lie. Imagine a flyer showing this household's January-versus-June usage as two bars — but with a vertical axis that starts at 300 instead of nought. January's bar shows 320 minus 300: a stump of 20. June's shows 575 minus 300: a tower of 275. The tower looks nearly fourteen times the stump, and the eye reports an explosion; the truth — 575 against 320 — is not even double. Nothing plotted is false; the axis simply amputated the region where both bars stood shoulder to shoulder, leaving only their difference on display. This device — the truncated axis — is the most common graphical deception in real media, and the favourite spot-the-problem question in assessments. The tell: a numerical axis that begins anywhere except nought. The remedy: read the actual values off the scale and rebuild the comparison yourself — which, from today, you do by reflex.

The analyst's checklist, four lines long. Read accurately, unit attached. Put the original under every percentage change. Conclude only what the numbers show, with the evidence in the sentence. And never trust a graph before glancing at where its axis starts.

The closing questions of this part are with you now — conclusions to sort into supported and unsupported, a prediction to hedge properly, and one dishonest axis to expose.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same three pictures, same household — but now we read them the way you actually would at home, with the municipal bill lying on the kitchen table.

## Subtopic: The Graph Is a Story About Your House

A municipal graph is not an exercise from a textbook — it is your own home describing itself. The electricity line idles around 320 units through the summer, wakes up in autumn, and shoots to 575 in June. You already know why without being told: winter. The heater running, the geyser fighting colder water, soup on the stove, lights on from five in the afternoon. That line is a diary of the family's cold months.

Reading it is a finger exercise. Want June? Finger on June along the bottom, slide up to the dot, slide left to the scale: 575. Want the year's swing? Highest dot minus lowest dot: 575 minus 320 leaves 255 units between the family's coldest and laziest-summer behaviour. That swing is the number worth remembering, because it is the only part of the bill you can actually attack — nobody cancels winter, but a blanket costs less than a heater.

The water bars tell a happier story. Before: 25 kilolitres a month, with a garden tap dripping around the clock. After the new washer: 18. One small repair, seven kilolitres a month, gone. The two bars stand together like a before-and-after photo, and the shorter one is the plumber's autograph.

So the opening move of every analysis question is a friendly one: ask what story this picture is telling about the house. Cold months. A cured leak. A bill cut four ways. Name the story first, and every calculation afterwards becomes a detail OF that story instead of a random sum.

The questions for this piece are with you now — read with your finger, and say out loud what each number is doing in the story.

## Subtopic: Numbers First, Opinions Second

Around the kitchen table somebody always arrives with an opinion ready: we are using FAR too much electricity. The analyst's habit — and the habit that earns marks — is to make the numbers speak before any feelings do.

Is June really a disaster? Read, then multiply: 575 units at R2,40 each is R1 380. January was 320 units — R768. So winter costs this family roughly R612 a month more than summer. THAT is a fact worth having an opinion about: either budget an extra R612 for each winter month, or pick one winter habit to trim.

Was the plumber worth the call-out? Numbers first: 7 kilolitres saved monthly, at R35 a kilolitre — R245 a month. The repair cost R580. By the third month the savings overtake the price — 735 against 580 — and from then on it is profit: R2 940 a year. Verdict, delivered entirely by arithmetic: the plumber was a bargain.

Where does the bill really go? The pie answers: electricity holds the biggest slice at 45 percent — R1 440 of the R3 200. Water takes 30 percent: R960. Refuse R480, sewerage R320. When the family argues about saving, the pie points at the widest slice — trimming electricity by 10 percent saves R144 a month, while trimming sewerage by 10 percent saves R32. Aim at big slices; that is the entire point of knowing the shares.

Notice the rhythm running through all three: read the number, do one multiplication, and only then judge. An opinion built on arithmetic collects full marks — and wins the kitchen-table argument too.

Your questions for this section are up. Read first, multiply second, judge last.

## Subtopic: When a Picture Exaggerates

One final skill, and it may be the most useful thing in the whole topic: recognising a graph that is technically accurate and thoroughly misleading at the same time.

The trick looks innocent. A chart compares January's 320 units with June's 575 — but its number axis begins at 300 instead of nought. What survives of January above the 300 line? A stub of 20. Of June? A column of 275. The column stands nearly fourteen times the stub, and your eye shouts that winter usage EXPLODED. The actual numbers say June is not even double January.

Where does the lie live? Not in the data — 320 and 575 are perfectly honest. The lie lives in the missing bottom of the picture: the region where both months stood level, from the floor up to 300, was chopped away, so only the difference got drawn. It is a photograph cropped down to the scowl — nothing false was added, but everything calm was removed.

Why would anyone draw a graph like this? Sometimes to sell a story: a pamphlet wants a crisis, an advert wants its product towering over the competition, a report wants a small improvement to look heroic. Sometimes it is plain carelessness. Your defence never changes and takes five seconds: check where the number axis starts. If it is not nought, stop believing your eyes, read the true values off the scale, and redo the comparison in your head — 575 against 320, under double, no explosion.

The assessment version asks two things: why does this graph create a misleading impression, and what does an accurate comparison show? You now own both sentences: the axis starts at 300, so the bars display only the portions above that line, exaggerating the gap; the true values, 320 and 575, differ by less than a factor of two.

Read the final questions of the lesson now. Check every axis's starting point before trusting any picture — the habit costs five seconds, and it is the difference between reading the news and being played by it.
