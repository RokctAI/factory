# Part 1 — Expert

This part of data handling puts the pencil in your hand: single bar graphs, histograms, line and broken-line graphs are yours to construct — while pie charts are yours to read and calculate from, never to draw, in Grade 10. All our data comes from one week at Lwazi's snack stand at school. The counts of the 80 pies sold, by type: chicken 32, beef 24, vegetable 16, cheese 8. The daily takings, Monday to Friday: R340, R430, R120, R450, R490 — with a mystery buried in Wednesday. And the class's 40 science marks for the term, grouped in tens. One dataset per graph type, because the deepest skill in this topic is matching the shape of the data to the right picture — and then drawing that picture so precisely that nobody can misread it.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Right Graph for the Data

Data arrives in three shapes, and each shape owns a graph.

Shape one: categories with counts. The pie types — chicken 32, beef 24, vegetable 16, cheese 8 — are separate named groups with nothing living between them: no pie sits halfway between chicken and beef. Categorical data takes a single bar graph: one bar per category, equal widths, gaps between the bars, heights carrying the counts.

Shape two: numerical data grouped into intervals. The 40 science marks, sorted into nought to 9, 10 to 19, 20 to 29, 30 to 39, 40 to 49 — intervals laid along a continuous number line, meeting edge to edge with no space between where one stops and the next starts. Grouped numerical data takes a histogram: bars that TOUCH, because the intervals themselves touch.

Shape three: one quantity followed through time. The daily takings — Monday's R340 through Friday's R490 — are a single thing measured again and again as the week passes. Time data takes a line graph: a point above each day, joined in calendar order. When the values leap about instead of flowing smoothly, the joined zigzag is called a broken-line graph.

And the pie chart? It displays shares of a whole — how the 80 pies divide among the four types — as slices of a circle. Grade 10 reads pie charts and calculates from them, but never constructs one: interpretation only.

The choosing rule in a single breath: named groups, bar graph. Grouped numbers, histogram. Change over time, line graph. Shares of a whole, pie chart — read, not drawn.

The questions for this section are with you now. Each hands you a dataset and asks which graph fits its shape — the decision that comes before any drawing does.

## Subtopic: Building a Bar Graph That Cannot Be Misread

A bar graph is a small machine with five parts, and markers inspect every one.

Part one: the title — what, where, when. Pie sales by type at Lwazi's snack stand, one week. A graph without a title is a picture of nothing in particular.

Part two: the category axis — the horizontal one — carrying the four pie types, evenly spaced, each name printed under its own bar.

Part three: the number axis — vertical — and here sits the first real decision: the scale. The largest count is 32, so run the axis from nought to 35 in steps of 5. Two laws govern the scale: it must start at nought, and its steps must be EQUAL — nought, 5, 10, 15 and onward, never nought, 5, 15, 32. Unequal steps stretch some values and shrink others, and the picture starts lying. Label the axis with what it counts: number of pies sold.

Part four: the bars. Equal widths, equal gaps, one bar per category. Chicken climbs to 32; beef to 24; vegetable to 16; cheese to 8. The gaps are doing real work: they declare that the categories are separate islands — which is precisely what makes this a bar graph and not a histogram.

Part five: the check. Read every bar back off your own axis and confirm it lands on the table's value. Drawing is transcription, and transcription gets audited.

Questions test this list from both directions: draw the graph from the table — marks for title, axes, labels, an even scale, correct heights — or find the fault in a graph already drawn: a missing label, a scale that jumps unevenly, a bar at the wrong height. Own the five parts and both question types belong to you.

Work through this section's questions before we reach the histogram, where one of these five parts flips in a way that catches half of every class.

## Subtopic: The Histogram — When the Bars Must Touch

The 40 science marks, grouped: nought to 9, three learners. Ten to 19, six. Twenty to 29, nine. Thirty to 39, fourteen. Forty to 49, eight. Total the frequencies: 3 plus 6 gives 9, plus 9 gives 18, plus 14 gives 32, plus 8 gives 40 — nobody missing, nobody counted twice. That totalling check earns a mark in assessments and is a habit worth keeping for life.

The histogram looks like a bar graph with one loud difference: NO gaps. The bars stand shoulder to shoulder, each covering its own interval on the horizontal axis, because that axis is a number line and the intervals tile it completely — 9 is followed by 10, 29 by 30; there is no empty ground between intervals for a gap to stand for. A gap in a histogram would claim there is some stretch of marks no learner could possibly score, which is false. Gaps in a bar graph tell the truth about separate categories; touching bars in a histogram tell the truth about a continuous number line. The classic two-mark explanation — why do these bars touch — is now one sentence you own.

Otherwise, build it like the bar graph: title, labelled axes, a vertical scale from nought to just beyond the biggest frequency — fourteen — in equal steps, and heights copied from the table: 3, 6, 9, 14, 8.

Then read the finished shape, because questions ask: where do the marks cluster? The tallest bar is 30 to 39, holding fourteen of the forty learners. The shape climbs to that peak and drops away — the bulk of the class sits in that interval, with tails of weaker and stronger marks either side. That interval, 30 to 39, is the modal class — the interval with the highest frequency.

This section's questions are in front of you: totals, touching bars, the modal class, and one deliberately faulty histogram to expose.

## Subtopic: Line Graphs, and Reading the Pie

The week's takings, plotted: Monday R340, Tuesday R430, Wednesday R120, Thursday R450, Friday R490. Days along the horizontal axis in calendar order; rand up the vertical, nought to 500 in steps of 50. Plot the five points, join them in order with straight segments — a broken line climbing, collapsing on Wednesday, then recovering through to Friday's peak.

Now read the story, because a line graph's shape IS a story. Across the week the general trend rises: Friday's 490 stands well above Monday's 340. But Wednesday breaks the pattern — takings crash to 120, then bounce back fully on Thursday. The graph cannot say why; it can only point. The context can: Wednesday morning the school lost power, so the warmers stood cold and no hot pies could be sold. Interpretation questions adore this pairing — describe the trend, then suggest a reason for the exception — and full marks needs both the numbers and a plausible, connected reason.

The pie chart completes the toolkit. Picture the pie-type data as a circle: the whole circle is all 80 pies; each slice is one type's share. Chicken's 32 out of 80 is 40 percent of the circle. Two calculations cover every pie question Grade 10 meets. From share to amount: vegetable holds 20 percent, and 20 percent of the 80 pies is 16 pies — and if next week sells 150 pies in the same proportions, chicken's share is 40 percent of 150, which is 60 pies. From share to angle, for auditing a drawn pie: the full circle is 360 degrees, so chicken's slice spans 40 percent of 360 — 0,40 times 360 gives 144 degrees. All four slices: 144 plus 108 plus 72 plus 36 — for beef 30 percent, vegetable 20, cheese 10 — landing on 360 exactly. Shares must total 100 percent and angles must total 360 degrees: the pie's built-in audit.

The closing questions of this part are with you now — plotting order, the Wednesday story, and pie shares converted into pies and degrees.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same snack-stand week — now with the graphs reduced to three simple pictures: piles, buckets and a journey.

## Subtopic: Piles, Buckets and Journeys

Every dataset you will meet in Grade 10 is one of three animals, and once you can name the animal, the graph chooses itself.

Animal one: piles. Sort the week's pies by type and you get four separate piles on the counter — 32 chicken, 24 beef, 16 vegetable, 8 cheese. Separate piles with nothing between them — no pie exists halfway between chicken and beef. Piles get a bar graph: one bar per pile, standing apart with gaps, because the piles themselves stand apart.

Animal two: buckets. The class's science marks run along a number line from nought to 49, and we scoop them into buckets: nought to 9, 10 to 19, and so on. The buckets stand rim against rim — a mark of 29 and a mark of 30 drop into neighbouring buckets with no space between them. Buckets get a histogram: bars that touch, because the buckets touch.

Animal three: journeys. The daily takings are one traveller — the cash box — checked every afternoon: 340, 430, 120, 450, 490. A journey gets a line graph: footsteps plotted in order and joined, so your eye can walk the same road.

And the pie chart? The pie is not a fourth animal — it is another way of looking at the piles: squash all four piles into one round tart and ask what fraction of the tart each type owns. You will always be handed the tart already sliced; your only job is to read it.

Piles, buckets, journeys. The questions for this piece are with you now — name the animal first, and the graph is the easy half.

## Subtopic: The Ruler Rules of a Good Graph

A graph is a promise: equal distances stand for equal amounts. Every drawing rule you are marked on exists to protect that single promise.

Rule one: the number axis starts at nought and climbs in equal steps. Nought, 50, 100, 150 — each step worth the same. The moment the steps go nought, 50, 300, the promise breaks: a small rise looks enormous or an enormous rise looks small. Choose the step size from your biggest value — Friday's 490 — and pick a step that clears it in a sensible number of jumps: steps of 50 up to 500.

Rule two: label everything. Title on top — takings per day at the snack stand for the week. Words on both axes — day of the week; takings in rand. A graph with naked axes is a sentence with no nouns; the marker cannot tell what was measured, and the marks drop off one by one.

Rule three: bars equal in width, and the gap rules obeyed — gaps between pile-bars, no gaps between bucket-bars. Width carries no meaning; only HEIGHT speaks. Fat and thin bars whisper a false order of importance.

Rule four: transcribe, then audit. Every bar height and every plotted point is copied from the table — so once you have drawn, read your own graph backwards and match each value to the table: does the chicken bar sit on 32? Does Wednesday's point sit on 120? One minute of checking has rescued more graph marks than any other habit in this subject.

These four rules are exactly the checklist a marker works down — title, labels, even scale, correct heights. Draw with the checklist beside you and the marks become mechanical.

Your questions for this section are up: each shows a graph breaking one ruler rule — name the rule that broke.

## Subtopic: The Wednesday Dip

Plot the week and watch what the line does: up from 340 to 430, cliff-dive to 120, leap back to 450, then on up to 490. That drop-and-recovery zigzag is the most question-rich shape in all of data handling, so read it slowly, in three layers.

Layer one: the facts. Lowest point Wednesday at R120; highest Friday at R490. The week's range: 490 minus 120 gives R370. Total for the week: 340 plus 430 gives 770, plus 120 gives 890, plus 450 gives 1 340, plus 490 — R1 830.

Layer two: the trend. Set Wednesday aside for a moment and the line climbs steadily all week — Monday's 340 up to Friday's 490. A trend is the line's overall direction stated with its evidence: rising, because Friday stands 150 above Monday.

Layer three: the exception, and its story. Wednesday refuses to fit the trend. The graph flags it; the context explains it — a power cut that morning left the warmers cold, so no hot pies could be sold. When a question says suggest a possible reason, it wants something the data makes plausible and that connects to the dip: no power, no hot food, few buyers. It does not want a fantasy the graph cannot support.

One warning while reading any line graph: the line between plotted points is a guide for the eye, not extra information. The segment joining Tuesday to Wednesday does not mean takings drifted smoothly through R275 at midnight — no pies were sold between days at all. The values live at the plotted points; the line only helps you walk from one to the next.

Facts, trend, exception-with-reason: answer in those three layers and the interpretation questions hand over their marks.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now — read the dip, name the range, defend the trend, and keep every reason anchored to the data.
