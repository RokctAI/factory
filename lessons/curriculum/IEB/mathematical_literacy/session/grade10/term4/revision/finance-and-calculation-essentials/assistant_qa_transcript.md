**Thandi:** Revision questions from the class! First: with so many finance topics packed into one assessment, how do I recognise which calculation a question actually wants?

**Tutor:** Read for the signal words, because each tool announces itself. The word of after a percentage means multiply: 30 percent of R650 is 0,30 times 650. The words increase, decrease or change mean a fraction: change over original, times 100 — and the word from names the original. Including VAT means divide by 1,15; excluding VAT with the till price wanted means multiply by 1,15. Per — as in per kilogram, per unit, per annum — announces a rate, so expect a division or a repeated multiplication. Best buy or better option demands two calculations plus a verdict sentence. And budget, profit or loss questions are always two lists and one subtraction. Before touching the calculator, underline the signal word and write the operation it orders. That one habit converts a wall of words into a one-line calculation, and it is exactly how the first method mark is earned — the marker looks for the correct operation written down, even before the arithmetic.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: the reverse VAT question. Why does subtracting 15 percent from R391 feel so right and score so wrong?

**Tutor:** Because it takes a correct percentage of an incorrect base. The R391 on the tag is the original price PLUS its VAT — 115 percent of the true price. Subtracting 15 percent of 391 removes 15 percent of the 115, which is more rand than the VAT actually was, landing on R332,35 instead of R340. The honest route runs through the structure: if 115 percent is 391, then 1 percent is 391 divided by 115, and 100 percent is that times 100 — which collapses into one move, divide by 1,15. The answer R340 then carries its own proof: multiply back by 1,15 and you land exactly on 391. Here is the reliable instinct: percentages only mean something relative to their base, so before calculating any percentage, ask what is 100 percent here. In an including-VAT question, the tag is never 100 percent — that is the whole trick, and the divide-by-1,15 move is the whole antidote.

**Thandi:** Third: budget advice questions. I calculated the deficit correctly and still lost marks on the advice part. What do markers want there?

**Tutor:** They want advice that stands on the classification you built earlier in the question. A deficit of R50 means expenses beat income, so something must change — but markers distinguish workable advice from wishful advice. Cut the rent is wishful: rent is a fixed expense, contractually bolted down. Reduce electricity usage or trim the food spending is workable: those are variable expenses, the ones a household can actually move this month. Full-credit answers name a specific variable item, propose a realistic cut, and show the arithmetic that closes the gap: reducing electricity by 10 percent saves R70, which turns the R50 deficit into a small surplus. Notice the three-part shape — item, action, numbers — mirroring the decision-because-numbers pattern from best-buy questions. One more expectation: if the question asks for TWO suggestions, give two different items, not two versions of the same cut. The advice mark is a mathematics mark in disguise; it pays for arithmetic aimed at the right target.

**Thandi:** Last one: tariff graphs. Two electricity options cross on the grid — what is the full sentence that gets all the marks?

**Tutor:** The crossing point is where the two options cost exactly the same, and the marks sit in reading it and then splitting the world in two. Suppose the lines cross at 180 units. Sentence one, the reading: at 180 units, both tariffs cost the same amount — read the rand value off the vertical axis and quote it. Sentence two, the advice, which is where most marks live: for usage below 180 units, the tariff whose line is lower on the left is cheaper, so light users should choose it; above 180 units, the other line is lower, so heavy users should switch. The structure to memorise is same-below-above: state where they are equal, then who wins below, then who wins above. And anchor each claim to the picture — the line starting higher usually carries the bigger fixed charge but the gentler slope, which is why it wins for heavy usage. If the question adds a specific household using, say, 300 units, finish by placing them: 300 sits above the crossing, so recommend that side's tariff, with its cost calculated. Reading, split, placement — the full-mark trio.
