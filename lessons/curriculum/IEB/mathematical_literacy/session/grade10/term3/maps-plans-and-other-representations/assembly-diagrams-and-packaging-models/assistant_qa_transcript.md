**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: assembly diagrams have no words — how can an exam even ask questions about a picture?

**Tutor:** The exam asks you to be the translator, and the questions follow four reliable patterns. Pattern one: what does this symbol mean — the numbered circle is a step, the arrow is a direction of movement, the curved arrow means turn, the magnified circle is a zoomed detail, the crossed-out image is the mistake to avoid. Pattern two: what does this step do in words — and you answer by pairing the step's letters with the parts list, so fix C to A becomes fix a bracket to a side panel. Pattern three: why is the order important — because a part attached too early can block a later one, like a back board trapping the final shelf. Pattern four: the quantity questions, which are really arithmetic wearing overalls — how many screws for all the brackets, are there enough parts in the box. If you can name every part, say every step aloud and multiply along the chain of quantities, there is nothing else the diagram can ask you. The candidates who struggle are the ones who try to answer from the picture alone without keeping a finger on the parts list.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: in the box question I divided the box's volume by the jar's volume and got a bigger number than the suggested answer. Where did the extra jars come from?

**Tutor:** From the corners — from space that exists but that no jar may occupy. Volume division treats the jars as liquid: it imagines melting them down and pouring atchar into every cubic centimetre, including the little pockets around each round jar and the leftover strip along a wall. Real jars are rigid cylinders standing in a rectangular box, so the honest count goes dimension by dimension: the 32-centimetre length takes 4 jars of diameter 8, the 24-centimetre width takes 3, so a layer holds 12, and the height decides the number of layers. Any remainder in those divisions is dead space — a box 35 centimetres long still takes only 4 jars along, because the spare 3 centimetres cannot hold a fifth. That is also why packing questions round DOWN while buying questions round UP: leftover space fits nothing, but a leftover need must still be covered. When your dimension-count and a volume-count disagree, the dimension-count is always the true capacity, and the difference between them is exactly the wasted air the question may ask you to comment on.

***

**Thandi:** Third: the big box is cheaper per jar, but the marking guidelines chose the small boxes. Is that inconsistent?

**Tutor:** No — two different questions were asked, and each answer is correct for its own question. Cost per jar shares the box's price across its full capacity: 6,00 over 12 gives 50 cents against 14,00 over 40 giving 35 cents, so on unit cost the big box genuinely wins. But a cost-for-the-job question locks you to the real quantity: for 15 jars, the big box costs R14,00 with 25 spots empty, while two small boxes cost R12,00 with only 9 spots empty — the small option wins by R2,00, because the big box's advantage lives in capacity that this job never uses. Bulk is only a bargain when the bulk is used. So before calculating, underline what the question is asking for: per jar, or for this order. If it asks which is more cost-effective with no quantity given, unit cost is the tool; the moment a real weekly or monthly quantity appears, cost the actual job with each option, rounding containers up to cover the need, and compare rand with rand. State the winner, the margin and the reason, and both styles of question will agree with you.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the question gave the jar width in millimetres and the box in centimetres, and half the class divided anyway. What is the safe habit?

**Tutor:** Convert everything onto one unit before any division, and write the conversion as its own line. A jar of 80 millimetres is 8 centimetres — divide 80 by 10, since ten millimetres make a centimetre. Then 32 centimetres over 8 centimetres gives the honest 4 jars. The students who divided 32 by 80 got a fraction of a jar and knew something was wrong; the more dangerous case is dividing 320 by 80 after converting the wrong quantity, getting a confident 4 by accident in one direction and nonsense in another. The protection is mechanical: before dividing, glance at the two units and say them aloud — centimetres over centimetres, or millimetres over millimetres, never mixed. The same discipline saves the height count, where a box quoted at 0,2 metres must become 20 centimetres before dividing by a 10-centimetre jar. Unit first, division second — in packing questions the conversion line is often a mark on its own, and it is the cheapest insurance in the section.
