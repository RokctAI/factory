# Part 1 — Expert

Two humble documents close the Maps and Plans topic: the assembly diagram that falls out of a flat-pack box, and the packaging problem of fitting round jars into rectangular boxes. Both are pure Mathematical Literacy — reading a technical picture, checking quantities by multiplication, and choosing the cheapest way to do a real job. Our storyline: Naledi buys a flat-pack storage cabinet for her family's spaza stall and must assemble it from the diagram, and then she must choose between two box sizes for transporting jars of atchar. The exam versions of these questions reward slow reading and small multiplications, and punish nothing so much as skipping the parts list.

## Subtopic: Reading an Assembly Diagram

An assembly diagram is a set of instructions that mostly refuses to use sentences. It communicates through numbered parts, exploded views and symbols, and the first skill is translating each of those into words.

The parts list comes first, and it is a table: each component has a letter or number, a name, a picture and a quantity. Naledi's cabinet lists: part A, side panel, quantity 2. Part B, shelf, quantity 3. Part C, bracket, quantity 6. Part D, screw, quantity 24. Part E, back board, quantity 1. Before any assembly, the instructions say: check the contents against this list. That instruction is arithmetic, and questions test it directly.

The exploded view is the diagram's main picture: the product drawn as if gently pulled apart, each part floating in the position it will occupy, with dotted lines showing what slides or screws into what. Read it as a frozen explosion: follow any dotted line and it tells you that this screw travels through this bracket into that panel.

The symbols carry the rest. A numbered circle marks a step: step 1, step 2, in order — and the order matters, because a back board attached too early can block the last shelf. An arrow shows direction of movement or turning; a curved arrow around a screw means turn to tighten. A magnified circle zooms into a detail — which way a bracket faces, which hole to use. A crossed-out picture shows the common MISTAKE — the panel upside down — and is often the most valuable image on the page.

Two habits make these questions easy: read the steps in number order, and keep one finger on the parts list so every letter in a step can be named aloud: step 3 says fix part C to part A — a bracket to a side panel.

The questions for this section are with you now. All of them ask you to translate a symbol, a dotted line or a step into plain words.

## Subtopic: Counting Parts and Catching Mistakes

The quantities on a parts list are not decoration — they interlock, and checking that they interlock is a classic question.

Do Naledi's numbers agree? Three shelves, and the design fixes each shelf with 2 brackets: 3 times 2 gives 6 brackets — matching part C's quantity of 6. Each bracket takes 4 screws: 6 times 4 gives 24 screws — matching part D. The list is internally consistent: every bracket has a shelf, every screw has a bracket.

Now the favourite twist: the packet actually contains 21 screws. How many complete brackets can be fixed? Each needs 4 screws: 21 divided by 4 gives 5 complete brackets with 1 screw left over — but 6 are needed. Short by one bracket means one shelf corner unfixed. The follow-up asks: how many screws are missing? Needed 24, have 21: 3 short. These are one-step calculations, but only for the candidate who first built the chain shelves-to-brackets-to-screws.

The same chain logic prices the job. If replacement screws sell in packets of 8 at R22 per packet, and Naledi is 3 short, she must still buy 1 full packet — the round-up rule of all purchasing — and pay R22 for three needed screws and five spares.

And a consistency check protects against misprints, which questions imitate: a list claiming 3 shelves, 6 brackets and 20 screws contradicts itself — 6 brackets at 4 screws each demands 24. The question asks: is the list correct, and if not, what should the screw quantity be? Answer with the chain: 3 times 2 is 6, 6 times 4 is 24, so 20 is a misprint for 24.

Work through this section's questions — every one is a small chain of multiplications hiding in a parts table.

## Subtopic: Packing Jars into Boxes

Now the packaging model. Naledi transports jars that are 8 centimetres in diameter and 10 centimetres tall, and the wholesaler offers a box whose inside measures 32 centimetres long, 24 centimetres wide and 10 centimetres high. How many jars fit?

Jars stand upright in rows, so work one direction at a time. Along the 32-centimetre length, each jar claims its 8-centimetre diameter: 32 divided by 8 gives 4 jars. Across the 24-centimetre width: 24 divided by 8 gives 3 jars. One layer therefore holds 4 times 3 — 12 jars. Height: the box is 10 centimetres high, the jar 10 tall, so exactly 1 layer. Box capacity: 12 jars.

The bigger box measures 40 by 32 by 20. Length: 40 over 8 gives 5. Width: 32 over 8 gives 4. Layer: 20 jars. Height: 20 over 10 gives 2 layers. Capacity: 40 jars.

Three rules keep this honest. First, divide dimension by dimension — length by diameter, width by diameter, height by jar height — and never volume by volume. Dividing box volume by jar volume pretends jars are liquid; round jars in square corners always waste space, so the true count is the dimension-by-dimension one. Second, when a division leaves a remainder, round DOWN: a box 35 centimetres long still fits only 4 jars of diameter 8, because 4 jars use 32 centimetres and the leftover 3 centimetres holds no fifth jar. Partial items do not exist in packing. Third, match units before dividing — a jar labelled 80 millimetres across is 8 centimetres; divide 32 by 8, not 32 by 80.

Notice the two rounding worlds in one topic: buying materials rounds UP; fitting objects into a space rounds DOWN. The question's verb — buy, or fit — chooses the direction.

This section's questions are in front of you. Take each box one direction at a time, and watch the millimetre labels.

## Subtopic: Choosing Cost-Effective Packaging

The final skill is judgement: which box should Naledi use? Cost-effective means the most service for the least money, and the standard tool is cost per item.

The small box costs R6,00 and holds 12 jars: 6,00 divided by 12 gives 50 cents per jar. The large box costs R14,00 and holds 40: 14,00 divided by 40 gives 35 cents per jar. Per jar, the large box is clearly cheaper — 15 cents saved on every jar, which across 400 jars a month is R60 of packaging saved. On pure unit cost, the big box wins.

But cost-effectiveness always faces a second question: how much do you actually need? Suppose Naledi moves only 15 jars a week. One large box holds all 15 with 25 empty spaces — R14,00 spent. Two small boxes hold 24 with 9 empty spaces — 2 times 6,00 gives R12,00. For THIS weekly job the small boxes are cheaper in actual rand, R2,00 saved, despite the worse cost per jar. Cheapest per unit and cheapest for the job are different questions, and exams ask both — read which one is in front of you.

The full method for any packaging decision, five steps. One: compute each container's true capacity, dimension by dimension, rounding down. Two: compute cost per item — container price over capacity. Three: read the actual requirement, and compute the real cost of meeting it with each option, rounding containers UP to cover the need. Four: compare like with like — rand with rand — and state the winner with its margin. Five: mention the practical factor if asked — wasted space invites broken stock, and a 40-jar box of full glass jars may simply be too heavy to lift safely.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They run capacity, unit cost and job cost against each other — exactly where marks are won and lost.

## Subtopic: The Instruction Sheet Is a Recipe

An assembly diagram is a recipe that swapped its words for pictures. A recipe starts with ingredients: the parts list is exactly that — 2 side panels, 3 shelves, 6 brackets, 24 screws, 1 back board, each with its own letter like ingredients with their own labels. And what does every good cook do first? Check the ingredients are actually in the kitchen. Same here: count the parts against the list before starting, because discovering a missing bracket at step 5 is how furniture ends up half-built in the passage for a month.

Then come the steps, numbered like a recipe's method — and, like baking, order is everything. You cannot ice a cake before it is baked; you cannot screw on the back board before the shelves are in, or it blocks the last one.

The pictures do the talking. The exploded view is the dish photographed mid-air — every part floating exactly where it belongs, dotted lines showing what slides into what. Arrows say which way something moves; a curly arrow around a screw says turn it. A little magnifying circle is the recipe saying pay attention here — the detail everyone gets wrong. And the crossed-out picture is the burnt version: this is what failure looks like, do not do this. Diagrams put the mistake on the page because it is cheaper to draw it than to answer ten thousand phone calls about it.

So when an exam hands you an assembly diagram, read it like a recipe: ingredients first, steps in order, zoom-boxes respected. Every question about it becomes show me where the sheet says so.

The questions for this piece are with you now — each one just asks what a picture is saying in words.

## Subtopic: Eggs in an Egg Box

Fitting jars into a box is the egg-box problem. Nobody pours eggs into a box and hopes; the box has spots — so many along, so many across. Work out the spots and you know the capacity.

Jars of diameter 8 in a box 32 long: how many 8-centimetre spots fit along 32? Four. Across the 24 width? Three. So the floor of the box is a grid of 4 by 3 — 12 spots. The box is 10 high and each jar is 10 tall: one storey only. Twelve jars, exactly like counting egg spots.

The big box is a double-storey egg box: 5 spots by 4 spots gives 20 per floor, and its 20 of height over the jar's 10 gives 2 floors: 40 jars.

Two things eggs teach us. First: leftover space is nothing. If the box were 35 long instead of 32, could a fifth jar squeeze in? A fifth jar needs 8 centimetres and only 3 remain — so no, and the answer stays 4. When fitting things in, always round DOWN. Contrast with buying paint or fencing from measurement: when BUYING, round up; when FITTING, round down. The verb in the question tells you the direction.

Second: never divide volumes. The clever-sounding shortcut — box volume over jar volume — overcounts, because jars are round and boxes are square: the corners around every jar are dead air that liquid maths would happily fill. Count spots along each direction instead — that is the count that matches reality.

Your questions for this section are up: one direction at a time, whole spots only.

## Subtopic: Big Box, Small Box, Best Box

The shop offers two boxes. Small: R6,00, holds 12. Large: R14,00, holds 40. Which is better? The trick is that better has two meanings, and questions swap them.

Meaning one: better value per jar. Share the box's price across its jars. Small: 6,00 over 12 — 50 cents per jar. Large: 14,00 over 40 — 35 cents per jar. The big box carries each jar more cheaply, the same way the big bag of maize meal costs less per kilogram. Bulk usually wins the per-unit contest.

Meaning two: better for the actual job. Naledi's job is 15 jars this week. The large box does it in one: R14,00, with 25 spots wasted. Two small boxes do it: R12,00, with 9 spots wasted. This week, the small option is cheaper in real rand — 12,00 against 14,00 — even though the big box is cheaper per jar. The bulk deal is only a deal if you use the bulk. It is the two-litre milk that goes sour before a small household finishes it: cheaper per litre, dearer per week.

So answer the question actually asked. Cost per jar: divide price by capacity, and the big box wins with 35 cents. Cost for 15 jars this week: count the boxes each option needs — rounding boxes UP, because a 15-jar job cannot travel in a fraction of a box — then price the count and compare: R12,00 beats R14,00. Two different winners from the same numbers, both correct, because two different questions were asked.

And when the question adds and give one practical reason — mention weight or wasted space: forty full glass jars is a heavy lift, and empty spots let jars rattle and crack.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Read which better is being asked for, divide the right things, and let the numbers pick the box.
