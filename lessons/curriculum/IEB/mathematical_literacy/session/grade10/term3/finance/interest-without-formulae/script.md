# Part 1 — Expert

Interest questions in Grade 10 come with a gift attached: no formulae are required or allowed. Everything is done with percentages you already own, one period at a time. What a question setter actually tests is whether you know the difference between two easily-confused things — the interest rate, which is a percentage, and the interest, which is an amount of rand — and whether you can grow or charge money step by step. Our story follows R4 000 down two different roads. Zanele puts R4 000 into a savings account earning 6 percent per year. Her brother Musa borrows R4 000, first from a bank at 15 percent per year, then — in the cautionary tale — from an informal moneylender at 25 percent per month. Same R4 000, very different endings.

## Subtopic: Interest and Interest Rate — a Percentage Is Not Money

Two definitions, and the favourite exam trick lives in the gap between them.

The interest rate is a percentage per time period — 6 percent per year, 25 percent per month. It is a speed, not an amount. On its own it buys nothing.

The interest is the actual rand amount that the rate produces when it acts on a sum of money. Zanele's 6 percent per year acting on R4 000 produces 0,06 times 4 000, which is R240 of interest in one year. The rate is 6 percent; the interest is R240. Write the units and you can never confuse them: percent for the rate, rand for the interest.

Direction matters just as much. When you save or invest, the bank is using your money, so the bank pays you interest — it arrives as a gain. When you borrow, you are using the bank's money, so you pay the interest — it lands as a cost. Same word, opposite pockets. A question that asks who receives the interest is testing exactly this.

One more term: the original amount that the rate acts on — the deposit or the loan — is called the principal. Rate acts on principal to produce interest. Say that sentence until it is boring.

The questions for this section are with you now. They check the rand-versus-percent distinction and which direction the money flows for a saver and for a borrower.

## Subtopic: Growing a Saving Year by Year

Zanele deposits R4 000 at 6 percent per year and leaves it alone. No formula — just take the percentage, one year at a time.

Year one: 6 percent of R4 000. Compute it: 10 percent of 4 000 is 400, so 1 percent is 40, and 6 percent is 6 times 40 — R240. The account grows to 4 000 plus 240, which is R4 240.

Year two is where thinking one year at a time pays off. If Zanele left the R240 in the account, the bank now pays 6 percent on the NEW balance of R4 240, not on the original R4 000. Compute: 1 percent of 4 240 is 42,40, times 6 gives R254,40. The balance becomes 4 240 plus 254,40, which is R4 494,40.

Look at what just happened: year two's interest, R254,40, is bigger than year one's R240, because last year's interest itself started earning. Interest on interest — money gathering speed. Grade 11 will give this a name and a formula; Grade 10 simply asks you to compute it step by step, and the step-by-step version is the one you will actually understand.

Compare the alternative: suppose Zanele withdrew the R240 each year and spent it. Then every year starts back at R4 000, and every year pays the same flat R240. After two years she has received 240 plus 240 — R480 — while the leave-it-alone version earned 240 plus 254,40, which is R494,40. The difference, R14,40, is exactly 6 percent of the first R240: the interest the withdrawn money never got to earn. Small after two years; after twenty, enormous.

Work through this section's questions now. They follow the two-year growth line by line and probe why the second year outgrows the first.

## Subtopic: The Cost of Borrowing — Bank versus Mashonisa

Now the other road. Musa borrows R4 000 from the bank at 15 percent per year, to be repaid after one year.

The interest: 15 percent of 4 000 is 0,15 times 4 000 — R600. So after a year Musa repays the principal plus the interest: 4 000 plus 600 gives R4 600. Borrowing R4 000 for a year cost him R600. That is the whole calculation — percentage of principal, added on.

Now the cautionary tale. The bank says no, and an informal moneylender — a mashonisa — offers the same R4 000 at 25 percent per month. The number 25 sits close to 15, and that closeness is the trap, because the periods are different. Per month, the interest is 25 percent of 4 000: R1 000. Every single month.

Put the two side by side over three months. The bank's 15 percent per year charges R600 for a whole year — three months would be a fraction of that. The mashonisa charges R1 000 in month one, R1 000 in month two, R1 000 in month three: R3 000 of interest in three months, and if Musa has only been paying the interest, the R4 000 principal is still fully owed. He has paid three-quarters of the loan again and owes as much as the day he started.

To compare rates honestly, put them on the same time period. Twenty-five percent per month, over twelve months of simple charging, is 25 times 12 — 300 percent per year. Against the bank's 15 percent per year, the mashonisa is twenty times more expensive. A rate means nothing until you read its time period; that one sentence is both an exam skill and a life skill.

This section's questions are in front of you. Watch the per-month and per-year labels in every option — the wrong answers all come from ignoring them.

## Subtopic: Finding the Rate, and the Full Method

Questions also run the calculation backwards: here is the interest, find the rate. Suppose a different lender charged R480 interest on a R4 000 loan over one year. What was the rate?

The rate is the interest as a percentage of the principal: 480 over 4 000, times 100. Compute: 480 divided by 4 000 is 0,12, and times 100 gives 12 percent per year. Always divide by the PRINCIPAL — the original amount — never by the total repaid. Dividing by the repayment of 4 480 gives around 10,7 percent, wrong, because the rate acted on what was borrowed, not on what was returned.

The complete method for any Grade 10 interest question, five steps. One: identify the principal, the rate and the time period, and underline whether the rate is per year or per month. Two: decide the direction — is this interest earned by a saver or paid by a borrower? Three: compute one period's interest as the percentage of the current balance. Four: for multiple periods, move one period at a time, and ask whether interest stays in the account and grows the balance, or is paid out or paid off, so the balance resets. Five: answer the actual question — sometimes the interest, sometimes the new balance, sometimes the total repaid, sometimes the rate found by dividing interest by principal and multiplying by 100.

The traps, in order of expense. Mixing periods — treating 25 percent per month as if it competed with 15 percent per year. Growing from the wrong balance — using R4 000 in year two when the account holds R4 240. Dividing by the repayment instead of the principal when finding a rate. And writing a rate as rand or interest as a percent — the units are the meaning.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They mix savers and borrowers, forwards and backwards, and every single one is solved by a percentage and a clear head.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same R4 000, same two roads — now told the way you would warn a friend.

## Subtopic: Renting Money

Interest stops being mysterious the moment you see it as rent. When you live in someone's room, you pay rent for using it. When you use someone's money, you pay rent for that too — and money-rent is called interest.

Borrow R4 000 from the bank for a year and the rent is 15 percent of what you borrowed: R600. You return the R4 000 — it was never yours — plus R600 for having used it. Total repaid: R4 600.

Savings are the same picture reversed. When Zanele deposits R4 000, the bank is renting HER money — using it while it sits there. So the bank pays her rent: 6 percent per year, which is R240. Saver receives rent; borrower pays rent. One idea, two directions, and now you always know who pays whom.

And the rate versus the interest? The rate is like the price per month on a to-let sign — a percentage, just information. The interest is the actual rent money that changes hands — rand. A sign saying 6 percent has paid nobody anything yet; R240 landing in the account is the event. In every answer you write, rate gets a percent symbol and interest gets a rand sign, and if your answer has the wrong one, you have answered a different question.

The questions for this piece are with you now. For each one just ask: who is renting whose money, and is this line a price tag or an actual payment?

## Subtopic: The Snowball in the Savings Account

Rolling a snowball along wet grass, it picks up more grass with every roll — because each layer it gains makes it bigger for the next roll. Zanele's savings account is that snowball.

Year one: R4 000 earns its 6 percent — R240 — and she leaves it in. The ball is now R4 240.

Year two: the bank pays 6 percent again, but on the bigger ball. Six percent of R4 240 is R254,40. More than last year — R14,40 more — and nobody worked harder for it. Last year's interest earned its own interest. The ball is now R4 494,40.

Now the friend who does it the other way: he withdraws his R240 every year to spend. Nothing wrong with that — but his ball never grows. Every January he is back at R4 000, and every December he gets the same flat R240. After two years: R480, against Zanele's R494,40. The gap looks tiny. But gaps like this are baby snowballs — give them twenty years and Zanele's account has pulled far ahead, all from interest earning interest.

One habit makes every exam version of this easy: move ONE year at a time, and before each year, write down the balance the year starts with. Year two starts at R4 240 — so year two's interest is 6 percent of R4 240, full stop. Students who lose marks here all made the same move: they took 6 percent of the original R4 000 twice. The snowball reminds you — measure the ball as it is NOW, not as it was when you started rolling.

Your questions for this section are up. Balance first, then percentage, one year at a time.

## Subtopic: Which Loan Hurts Less

Two people offer to lend you R4 000. The bank says 15 percent. The mashonisa down the road says 25. Twenty-five against fifteen — sounds like the same neighbourhood, right? Read the small print out loud: the bank's is 15 percent PER YEAR. The mashonisa's is 25 percent PER MONTH. Those are not neighbours; they are different planets.

The bank's rent for a whole year: R600. The mashonisa's rent for one month: R1 000. In his first MONTH you pay more than the bank asks for a whole YEAR.

Run three months, which is how these stories really go. Month one: R1 000. Month two: R1 000. Month three: R1 000. That is R3 000 paid in rent — and here is the part that traps real families: if you were only paying the interest each month, the R4 000 itself is still owed, in full. You have paid three-quarters of the loan's value and the loan is still there, staring at you.

Want the honest comparison? Stretch the mashonisa's rate to a year: 25 percent a month, twelve months, is 300 percent a year. The bank charges 15. Twenty times more expensive, hiding behind a number that looked only a little bigger.

So the rule, for exams and for life: never compare two rates until they wear the same time period. Per year against per year, per month against per month. Wrong options in questions are built out of exactly this confusion, and the mashonisa builds a business out of it.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Check every rate's time period before you touch the calculator — that one glance is where all the marks live.
