**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot here! First one: debit card, credit card, debit column, credit column — the same two words seem to point in opposite directions. How do I keep them straight?

**Tutor:** Separate the cards from the statement, because they answer different questions. The cards describe whose money is being spent: a debit card spends your own money straight from your account, while a credit card spends the bank's money, which you must repay later, with interest if you are slow. The statement columns describe direction of flow for your account: debit means money left the account, credit means money arrived. So when Lwazi's car-wash takings are deposited, the statement shows a credit; when a fee or a withdrawal happens, the statement shows a debit — even if he used a debit card to do it. Statement questions dominate this topic, so anchor this sentence: on a statement, debit is out, credit is in. If a statement line reads debit R60,00, that is R60 gone — probably that expensive branch-counter withdrawal — and the balance column immediately to the right should have dropped by exactly R60. Reading the balance column as a running check is also how you catch the statement errors that exam questions love to hide.

***

**Thandi:** Next: with the fee of R2,50 plus R1,20 per R100 or part thereof, why is the fee on R350 not R6,70? Three and a half hundreds seems obvious.

**Tutor:** Because the phrase part thereof forbids halves. The fee structure does not measure your withdrawal as a decimal number of hundreds; it counts how many R100 units the amount touches, and any partly-used hundred counts in full. R350 touches four hundreds: three complete ones and fifty rand into the fourth — so the count is four, and the fee is 2,50 plus 4 times 1,20, which is R7,30. Notice the direction of the rounding: always up, always in the bank's favour, never yours. It also means R350 and R400 carry the identical fee, which surprises people until they see the counting rule. This is the same logic as a taxi charging per kilometre started, or a parkade charging per hour or part of an hour — begin the unit, owe the unit. In calculations, do the count first and write it down: R350 means four hundreds; R701 means eight hundreds, because that single rand past seven hundred opens the eighth; R1 000 exactly means ten hundreds, no rounding needed since nothing is partial. The favourite wrong option in any question is always the decimal version — 6,70 in this case — sitting there looking reasonable.

**Thandi:** Third one: in the account comparison my totals were right but I still lost a mark. The marking guidelines wanted a sentence. What sentence?

**Tutor:** The comparison question is only finished when you announce the decision the numbers support. Computing R41,50 for PayPerUse and R48,00 for AllInOne earns the calculation marks, but the final mark sits in the conclusion: PayPerUse is cheaper for this month by R6,50, so Lwazi should choose PayPerUse. Three ingredients — the winner, the margin, and the recommendation tied to the given month of use. Leave out the margin and you have asserted rather than compared; leave out the winner and the marker must guess your conclusion. And be careful to answer for the month the question described, not the month you imagine: if the question then changes the usage to ten heavy withdrawals of R1 000, the totals become R150,00 against R48,00 and the recommendation legitimately flips to AllInOne, saving R102. Question setters deliberately run both scenarios in one question to test whether your advice follows the arithmetic rather than habit. Numbers, then sentence: that pairing is the full-mark shape of every comparison answer.

**Thandi:** Last one: the graph question says describe where the two fee lines cross and what it means. What are the marks actually looking for?

**Tutor:** Three specific observations, each in plain words. First, the shapes: the AllInOne account is a horizontal line at R48, because its cost ignores how many transactions happen, while PayPerUse is a rising line that starts at R5,00 — the admin fee charged even for an idle month — and climbs R7,30 with each R400 withdrawal. Second, the crossing: somewhere between five and six withdrawals the rising line passes the flat one; at five withdrawals PayPerUse totals R41,50, still below 48, and at six it totals R48,80, just above. Third, the meaning on each side: to the left of the crossing, the rising line is lower, so the pay-per-use account is cheaper for light users; to the right, the flat line is lower, so the bundle is cheaper for heavy users. The crossing point itself is the break-even, where the two accounts cost the same. Say those three things with the numbers attached and you have every mark — the graph question is the comparison question wearing a picture.
