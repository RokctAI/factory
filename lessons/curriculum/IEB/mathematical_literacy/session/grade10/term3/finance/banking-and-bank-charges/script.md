# Part 1 — Expert

Banks charge for almost everything they do, and exam questions love to ask exactly what a month of banking really costs. This lesson opens a first bank account alongside Lwazi, who runs a Saturday car wash at the taxi rank and is tired of carrying his takings home in a shoe box. He must choose between two accounts: PayPerUse, which charges a small fee for every transaction, and the AllInOne account, which charges one flat monthly amount for unlimited transactions. Between those two offers sits every skill this topic demands: banking vocabulary, reading a fee table, computing a transaction fee from a fixed-plus-rate structure, totalling a month of charges, and describing the graph where the two accounts cross.

## Subtopic: The Language of Banking

Banking questions begin as reading questions, so the vocabulary comes first.

Types of account. A savings account keeps money safe and pays a small amount of interest — the natural first account for Lwazi. A current account, also called a cheque account, is designed for money moving in and out constantly, usually with higher fees and almost no interest. A fixed deposit takes a lump sum and locks it away for an agreed period at a better interest rate — excellent for saving toward a goal, hopeless for daily spending. And a credit account or credit card lets you spend the bank's money first and pay it back later, with interest added if you take your time.

Movements of money. A deposit puts money into the account. A withdrawal takes money out. An EFT — electronic funds transfer — moves money from one account to another without any cash changing hands. A debit order is a standing instruction that allows a company, like a cellphone provider, to collect a regular payment from the account every month. On a bank statement, money flowing out is recorded in the debit column, money flowing in is recorded in the credit column, and the balance column shows what remains in the account after each line.

One warning about those two famous words: on the statement, debit means money leaving and credit means money arriving. Students confuse them because a debit CARD spends your own money while a credit CARD borrows the bank's money — but the statement columns only ever describe direction of flow. Nail down the statement meaning now, because statement-reading questions are guaranteed marks.

The questions for this section are with you now — pure vocabulary, the cheapest marks in the whole topic, lost only by guessing instead of learning.

## Subtopic: What a Withdrawal Really Costs

Now the fee table for Lwazi's PayPerUse account, read line by line. Monthly admin fee: R5,00. Cash withdrawal at the bank's own ATM: R2,50 plus R1,20 per R100 or part thereof. Cash withdrawal at a supermarket till — called cashback — R2,00 flat. Withdrawal inside a branch, over the counter: R60,00. Those four lines carry three different pricing shapes: flat fee, fixed plus rate, and painful flat fee.

Work the ATM line. Withdrawing R400: the fixed part is R2,50, and R400 contains four full R100s, so the rate part is 4 times R1,20, which is R4,80. Total: 2,50 plus 4,80 gives R7,30. Withdrawing R1 000: ten hundreds, so 2,50 plus 12,00 gives R14,50.

The phrase that every fee question tests: per R100 OR PART THEREOF. Withdraw R350. That is three full hundreds and fifty rand into a fourth — and a part counts as a whole. So the count is four, the rate part is 4 times R1,20, and the fee is 2,50 plus 4,80: R7,30. Notice the sting: R350 costs exactly the same fee as R400, because the bank rounds the hundreds UP, always in its own favour. The wrong answer, 2,50 plus 3,5 times 1,20 giving R6,70, is precisely the trap an exam option list will offer you.

Now compare channels for the same R400 in cash. Own ATM: R7,30. Cashback at the till while buying airtime: R2,00. Over the counter in the branch: R60,00 — about eight times the ATM, thirty times the till. Same rand notes in your hand, wildly different prices. Choosing the cheap channel is not stinginess; over a year of weekly withdrawals, the gap between the till and the counter runs into thousands of rand.

Work through this section's questions. Watch every option for the part-thereof trap and for fees quoted at the wrong channel.

## Subtopic: Two Accounts Head to Head

The AllInOne account makes the opposite offer: R48,00 flat per month, all transactions included, no admin fee on top. Which account should Lwazi choose? The only honest answer comes from his own month of banking.

His typical month: five ATM withdrawals of R400 each, and nothing else unusual. PayPerUse: each withdrawal costs R7,30, so 5 times 7,30 gives R36,50, plus the monthly admin fee of R5,00. Total: R41,50. The AllInOne at R48,00 would cost him R6,50 more. PayPerUse wins — for THIS pattern of use.

Now a heavier month, the kind a busy December brings: ten ATM withdrawals of R1 000. PayPerUse: each costs R14,50, so 10 times 14,50 gives R145,00, plus 5,00 — R150,00. The AllInOne still costs R48,00. AllInOne wins by R102,00.

So neither account is simply better; the break-even point decides. Ask: how many R400 withdrawals push PayPerUse past R48? Fees: 5,00 plus 7,30 per withdrawal. Five withdrawals: 5,00 plus 36,50 gives R41,50 — still cheaper than AllInOne. Six withdrawals: 5,00 plus 43,80 gives R48,80 — now just past it. So at five or fewer withdrawals a month, pay per transaction; at six or more, the flat fee is the bargain. This is exactly the reasoning an exam answer must show: compute both totals for the given month, compare, and state the winner with its margin.

The deeper lesson: a flat fee is insurance for heavy users and a waste for light users, and a per-use fee is the reverse. Banks design both and profit either way; the customer's job is arithmetic.

This section's questions are in front of you — every one is a small month of banking to cost out, so keep the fee table in your head as you go.

## Subtopic: The Fee Graph, and the Method for Any Charges Question

Picture the two accounts as a graph, money on the vertical axis, number of R400 withdrawals along the horizontal. The AllInOne draws a horizontal line at R48 — flat, unchanging, the same for zero withdrawals as for thirty. PayPerUse starts lower, at R5,00 — the admin fee is charged even in a month of no withdrawals — and climbs by R7,30 for every withdrawal: a staircase rising from left to right. On the left of the picture, the PayPerUse line runs below the AllInOne line: light users pay less per-use. Between five and six withdrawals the rising line climbs past the flat one — the crossing is the break-even — and to the right of it the flat AllInOne line is the cheaper of the two. Every tariff-comparison graph in this course tells this same story: a rising line and a flat line, and the crossing point where the advice changes.

The method for any bank-charges question, five steps. One: read the fee table and classify each fee's shape — flat, fixed plus rate, or percentage. Two: for fixed-plus-rate fees, count the R100s, rounding parts UP, then fee equals fixed part plus rate times the count. Three: total a month by multiplying each fee by how many times it happened, and never forget the monthly admin fee — it is charged for merely existing. Four: to compare accounts, compute each account's total for the same month of use, then state the cheaper one and by how much. Five: for a break-even question, step the number of transactions up until the totals cross, and report the crossing honestly: cheaper at five, dearer at six.

The traps: treating R350 as three-and-a-half hundreds instead of four; forgetting the admin fee; comparing accounts on different months of use; and reading the branch-counter fee as if it were the ATM fee. Every one of them will appear somewhere in your final exams, dressed as a plausible option.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. Take each month slowly: fee shape first, count second, total third.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same shoe box, same two accounts — now in the language of the car wash.

## Subtopic: The Bank Is a Shop That Sells Services

Lwazi's shoe box never charged him a cent, so the first shock of banking is that everything has a price. Think of the bank as a shop. Keeping your money safe: that is the monthly admin fee, R5,00 — like renting a locker. Handing you your own cash through a machine: R2,50 plus R1,20 for every R100 — a small service with a small price. Handing you cash through a human being behind a counter: R60,00 — the deluxe service you almost never need. The bank is not being sneaky when it charges — the prices are printed on the fee table, like a menu. The sneakiness only wins when nobody reads the menu.

And the words on the statement are simpler than they look. Deposit: money in. Withdrawal: money out. Debit column: out. Credit column: in. Debit order: you gave a company permission to help itself to a payment every month — handy for the cellphone contract, dangerous to forget about. Balance: what is standing in the account right now, after everything in and out.

Accounts are just different lockers. Savings: an ordinary locker that pays you a little rent. Fixed deposit: a locker with a time lock — better rent, but no access. Current account: a locker with a revolving door, built for business. Credit: not a locker at all — that is the shop lending you ITS money, and its rent runs against you.

The questions for this piece are with you now. Every term is on the menu you just read — no guessing needed.

## Subtopic: Small Fees Eat Big Money

Nobody loses their savings to one giant fee. They lose them R7,30 at a time, without watching.

Follow one R400 note out of Lwazi's account by three different doors. Door one, the supermarket till: he buys airtime, asks for R400 cashback, pays R2,00. Door two, the bank's ATM: R2,50 plus R4,80 — that is R7,30, more than three times the till. Door three, the branch counter: R60,00 — thirty times the till price for the identical notes. The cash is the same; only the door changed.

Now run the year. Weekly cash at the till: 52 times R2,00 — R104 for the year. Weekly cash at the counter: 52 times R60 — R3 120. The difference, over three thousand rand, could buy the car wash a new pressure washer. Same money, same year — the only variable was which door he made a habit of.

And the sneaky phrase "per R100 or part thereof" is the shop rounding in its own favour. Draw R350 and the machine does not see three and a half hundreds; it sees four, because a part counts as a whole. Fee: 2,50 plus 4,80 — R7,30, the same as drawing R400. The bank never rounds toward you. Expect it, count the hundreds up, and the fee table holds no surprises.

Your questions for this section are up. For each one, just ask: which door, and how many hundreds — counting the parts as wholes?

## Subtopic: Flat Rate or Pay-As-You-Go

You already understand the AllInOne account, because you buy data. Pay-as-you-go airtime is perfect when you barely use the phone — and ruinous the month you stream every night. A big monthly data bundle is wasteful for a light user and a bargain for a heavy one. Bank accounts copy this exactly. PayPerUse is pay-as-you-go: R5,00 to exist, then R7,30 per R400 withdrawal. AllInOne is the flat package: R48, all you can transact.

So the choice is not about which account is better — it is about which month is yours. Lwazi's quiet month has five withdrawals: pay-as-you-go costs 36,50 plus 5,00, R41,50, and the flat R48 would waste R6,50. His busy December has ten withdrawals of R1 000: pay-as-you-go balloons to R150,00, and the flat R48 saves R102.

Where does the advice flip? Step it up one withdrawal at a time: five withdrawals cost R41,50 — still under 48. Six cost R48,80 — just over. So five or fewer, stay pay-as-you-go; six or more, buy the bundle. That crossing point is the whole exam question, and you find it exactly the way you just did: count upward until the totals swap places.

Last habit, the one that saves real families real money: check the pattern once a year. A quiet car wash becomes a busy one, five withdrawals become fifteen, and the account that was right becomes the account that leaks. The bank will not phone to tell you — the fee table is public, but the arithmetic is yours.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Cost out both accounts for the month described, compare, and say the winner and the margin out loud — that full sentence is the full marks.
