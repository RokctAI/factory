# Part 1 — Expert

Profit and loss questions sit at the heart of the Finance work in this course, and they are built on one subtraction: money in, minus money out. The trading story that carries this lesson is Karabo's Saturday stand at the community sports ground over one ordinary month. She bakes muffins, sells bottles of juice, pays the club for her table, and at month end she wants one honest number: did the stand make money or lose it? Along the way we read the financial documents that feed that number — the supplier's till slip with its VAT line, the invoice for stock — and we sort every amount into fixed, variable or occasional, because that sorting is worth marks on its own. The month ends with a full income-and-expenditure statement, and then a second, harder month where the same statement turns negative.

## Subtopic: Income, Expenditure and the Profit Line

Three words carry the whole topic. Income is money flowing in. Expenditure is money flowing out. Profit is what remains when expenditure is subtracted from income — and when that subtraction goes negative, the negative answer has its own name: a loss.

Karabo's month, income first. She baked 180 muffins and sold 165 of them at R8 each: 165 times 8 is R1 320. She also sold 120 bottles of juice at R12 each: 120 times 12 is R1 440. Total income: 1 320 plus 1 440 gives R2 760.

Now expenditure. The muffin ingredients cost R3,00 per muffin, and she baked 180, so 180 times 3 is R540. The juice cost R7 per bottle from the wholesaler: 120 times 7 is R840. Stock therefore cost R1 380. Add the table rental of R300 she pays the club, the electricity coupon of R160 for the oven, and taxi fare of R150 to fetch stock. Total expenditure: 1 380 plus 300 plus 160 plus 150 gives R1 990.

The profit line: income minus expenditure, 2 760 minus 1 990, equals R770. Positive, so the stand made a profit of R770 this month.

One more view that questions love: profit per item. A muffin costs R3,00 to make and sells for R8, so each one sold carries a profit of R5,00. But notice the fifteen muffins she baked and did not sell — their ingredients cost 15 times 3, which is R45, and that money is spent whether or not anyone buys. Unsold stock quietly eats profit, and questions probe exactly that.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test the three definitions, the R770, and what those fifteen unsold muffins did to it.

## Subtopic: Reading the Financial Documents

Every number in a trading story arrives on a document, and Mathematical Literacy expects you to read those documents line by line. Take the till slip from the wholesaler where Karabo bought her stock. Near the bottom sit three lines that matter more than all the rest: a subtotal of R640,00, then a line reading VAT at 15 percent, R96,00, then a total due of R736,00.

Decode them. The subtotal is the price of the goods before tax. VAT — value-added tax — is the 15 percent the government adds to most goods: 15 percent of 640 is 0,15 times 640, which is 96, exactly the slip's VAT line. The total due is subtotal plus VAT: 640 plus 96 gives 736. Every till slip in the country follows this pattern, and a favourite question blanks out one of the three lines and asks you to rebuild it.

An invoice adds two ideas. First, quantity times unit price: the invoice for juice reads quantity 120, unit price R7,00, amount R840,00 — and you should always check that multiplication, because a mistyped invoice is a classic question scenario. Second, terms of payment: an invoice often says the amount is due within 30 days, which is a promise to pay, where a till slip records money already paid.

Vocabulary that earns direct marks: the balance is the amount currently owed on an account. Amount due is what must be paid now. A statement is the document that lists a month's transactions and ends with that balance. When the municipal account or the shop account asks for these words, they are asking for reading, not arithmetic — the easiest marks available, lost only through skimming.

Work through this section's questions. Each one hands you a document line and asks what it means or whether it is correct — exactly the habit real questions have.

## Subtopic: Fixed, Variable and Occasional Expenses

Questions constantly ask you to classify expenses, because planning depends on it. Three categories.

A fixed expense is the same amount every period, no matter how business goes. Karabo's table rental of R300 is fixed: brilliant month or terrible month, the club wants R300.

A variable expense changes with how much you do. Ingredients and stock are the purest examples: bake more muffins, buy more flour. The R1 380 of stock, the R160 of electricity — these rise and fall with trade. So does her taxi fare when extra stock runs mean extra trips.

An occasional expense arrives rarely and irregularly. When the pole of Karabo's gazebo snapped two months ago, the repair cost R280 — once. You cannot schedule it monthly, but a wise budget still sets something aside for it.

Why the sorting matters: fixed expenses set the floor. Before Karabo sells a single muffin, R300 of table rental is already owed. Variable expenses scale with income, so they behave; it is the fixed ones that sink a slow month, and the occasional ones that ambush a good one. A question will give a list — rent, airtime, stock, a burst geyser repair, a monthly insurance debit order — and ask you to place each in its category and say why. The reason is the mark: fixed because the amount never changes; variable because it depends on how much is sold; occasional because it is unpredictable and rare.

This section's questions are ready for you. For every expense you classify, rehearse the one-line reason — the category without the reason is half the marks.

## Subtopic: The Income-and-Expenditure Statement — Surplus, Deficit and the Method

Now assemble the month onto one page: the income-and-expenditure statement. Its shape never changes. Income items listed and totalled. Expenditure items listed and totalled. Then the bottom line: total income minus total expenditure.

Karabo's statement reads: income — muffin sales R1 320, juice sales R1 440, total income R2 760. Expenditure — stock R1 380, table rental R300, electricity R160, transport R150, total expenditure R1 990. Bottom line: 2 760 minus 1 990 gives R770. When income exceeds expenditure the result is a surplus — a business would call it profit. Statements for households and clubs prefer the word surplus; hold both words.

Now the harder month. Questions love the rainy-season scenario: fixtures are cancelled, so Karabo trades two Saturdays instead of four. Income falls to R1 700. But look at the expenditure side with your categories ready: stock shrinks because she buys less, yet the table rental does not shrink, and suppose the month's expenditure still totals R1 880. The bottom line becomes 1 700 minus 1 880, which is negative 180 — a deficit of R180. The stand lost money, and the statement shows exactly why: income collapsed while fixed costs stood still.

The method for any statement question, five steps. One: separate every amount into income or expenditure — read each item's direction, in or out. Two: total each side, showing the addition. Three: subtract expenditure from income, in that order. Four: name the result — profit or surplus if positive, loss or deficit if negative. Five: answer the why question with categories: fixed costs are the usual villain of a loss.

The traps. Counting the value of unsold stock as income — nothing sold, nothing in. Forgetting an expense the question tucked into the story a paragraph earlier. Subtracting in the wrong order and declaring a loss a profit. And rounding money anywhere except the final answer.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They walk the full statement, the deficit month, and the reasons behind both bottom lines.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same Saturday stand again, told the way you would explain it at the kitchen table — three piles of money and one page that settles every argument.

## Subtopic: Money In, Money Out, and What Is Left

Picture two shoeboxes under the table. Every rand a customer pays goes into the IN box. Every rand Karabo pays out — flour, juice, table rental, electricity, taxi — comes from the OUT box. At month end, one question: which box worked harder?

The IN box: 165 muffins at R8 is R1 320, and 120 bottles of juice at R12 is R1 440. Together, R2 760 came in.

The OUT box: R540 of muffin ingredients, R840 of juice from the wholesaler, R300 table rental to the club, R160 of electricity, R150 of taxi rides. Together, R1 990 went out.

Left over: 2 760 minus 1 990 is R770. That R770 is the profit — the money the month actually earned her. It is not the money in the cash tin; the cash tin held R2 760 at some point. Profit is what survives after every cost has taken its bite.

And here is the bite people forget. Fifteen muffins never sold. Nobody refunds the flour and eggs inside them — that R45 was spent the moment she baked them. Making things does not earn money; selling them does. That single sentence answers half the trick questions in this topic.

The questions for this piece are with you now. Keep the two boxes in your head and every one of them becomes a sorting job.

## Subtopic: The Bills That Never Budge and the Ones That Follow the Crowd

Sort Karabo's costs the way you sort washing — three piles.

Pile one: the bills that never budge. The R300 table rental is the same in a bumper month and a dead month. The club does not ask how sales went. That is a fixed expense, and it is the dangerous pile, because it must be paid out of the first money earned, before any profit exists.

Pile two: the costs that follow the crowd. Busy month, more flour, more juice, more electricity, more taxi trips to the wholesaler. Quiet month, less of all four. These are variable expenses, and they are the polite pile — they shrink when times are hard.

Pile three: the ambushes. The gazebo pole that snaps. The R280 that one repair cost arrives maybe once a year, never on schedule. Occasional expenses. You cannot predict them, but you can expect them — like knowing that somewhere this winter, somebody in the house will need new shoes.

Why bother sorting? Because it answers the question every failing business asks: where did the money go? A slow month does not lose money because of the variable pile — that pile shrank along with sales. It loses money because the fixed pile stood there, arms folded, demanding its R300. When a question asks why the rainy month's statement shows a loss, the sentence it wants is: income fell, but the fixed expenses stayed the same.

Your questions for this section are up. Every cost in them belongs to one pile — name the pile, then say the reason out loud.

## Subtopic: One Page That Settles the Argument

Suppose Karabo's mother says the stand is a waste of time, and her brother says it is a goldmine. The income-and-expenditure statement is the one page that ends the debate, because it holds nothing back.

Top half: everything that came in, item by item. Muffins R1 320. Juice R1 440. Total in: R2 760. Bottom half: everything that went out, item by item. Stock R1 380. Table rental R300. Electricity R160. Transport R150. Total out: R1 990. Last line: in minus out, R770 to the good. A surplus — the polite bookkeeping word for profit. Not a goldmine, not a waste of time: R770 of truth.

Then the rainy month tells the other story. Two trading Saturdays instead of four. Money in: R1 700. Money out: still R1 880, because the rental and the rest held firm. Last line: minus R180. A deficit — the month ran at a loss, and the page shows the exact reason sitting in the expenditure column.

When you build one of these in an exam, three habits protect your marks. Put every amount on the correct side — one misplaced item poisons both totals. Show both totals before you subtract, because each total usually carries its own mark. And name the bottom line in words: surplus or deficit, profit or loss. A bare number is an unfinished answer.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Build the page top to bottom, in before out, totals before the subtraction — and the bottom line will defend itself.
