**Thandi:** Time for the questions that come up a lot with this topic! First one: if Karabo still has fifteen muffins at closing time, can she count them as income since they are worth R8 each?

**Tutor:** No — and this is the single most common wrong instinct in the whole topic. Income is money that actually arrived, and those fifteen muffins brought in nothing. Worse, they already took money out: the flour, eggs and electricity inside them cost R3,00 each, so R45 of expenditure is sitting in that basket with no sale to answer for it. Value and income are different things. A shelf of unsold stock might have a selling value of R120, but value only becomes income at the moment a customer pays. That is why the statement records 165 muffins sold at R8 — R1 320 — and not 180. If she sells the fifteen on Monday at half price, Monday's income records R60. Questions test this by sneaking the words "baked 180" into a story where only 165 were sold, hoping you multiply the wrong number. Read for the word sold, every time.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: profit and surplus, loss and deficit — are those four words two pairs of twins, or do they mean different things?

**Tutor:** They are twins in the arithmetic and different in the setting. Both pairs come from the same subtraction: total income minus total expenditure. A business talks about profit and loss, because its purpose is to trade. A household, a school, a stokvel or a sports club talks about surplus and deficit, because their purpose is not trading — a family does not make a profit on its own groceries, but its month can still end with money left over, and that is a surplus. In an exam, use the vocabulary of the document in front of you. If the question shows a household income-and-expenditure statement and asks for the bottom line, write surplus or deficit; if it is Karabo's stand as a business, profit or loss is the natural pair. The number carries most of the marks, but the correct naming word usually carries one — and it is the cheapest mark available.

***

**Thandi:** This one arrives constantly: the till slip shows the total of R736 but the VAT line is smudged. How do I rebuild it?

**Tutor:** Use the relationship that never changes: subtotal plus VAT equals total. You have two of the three numbers, so subtraction rebuilds the third — 736 minus the subtotal of 640 gives the missing VAT of R96. Then verify it independently: VAT should be 15 percent of the subtotal, and 0,15 times 640 is indeed 96. That double route is the habit to build, because a question can smudge any of the three lines. Missing total: add 640 and 96. Missing VAT: subtract, then check with the percentage. Missing subtotal: subtract the VAT from the total, or, in the harder version where only the total is given, divide 736 by 1,15 to get 640 — because a VAT-inclusive total is always the subtotal multiplied by 1,15. One relationship, four question styles, same three numbers.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: my statement balances perfectly, but the question asks me to suggest how the stand could improve its profit. Is there a method for that?

**Tutor:** There is, and it comes straight off the statement's two halves: profit rises when income rises or when expenditure falls, so give one suggestion from each side and tie it to a real line item. On the income side: sell the fifteen muffins that currently go unsold, perhaps at a discount as the morning ends — even R4 each turns R45 of wasted ingredients into R60 of income. Or adjust the selling price, with the caution that a higher price can reduce the number of customers. On the expenditure side, aim at a line she can actually change: buy juice in bigger bulk for a lower unit price, or combine taxi trips to cut the R150 of transport. Do not suggest cutting the table rental — a fixed cost set by the club is not hers to change, and markers reward suggestions that respect which costs are controllable. Number plus reason, one from each side: that is a full-mark answer.
