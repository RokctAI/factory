# Part 1 — Expert

Perimeter and area questions always arrive wearing work clothes: a fence to price, a floor to pave, a round bed to plant. The formulae are printed on the formula sheet — your job is to choose the right one, feed it correctly, and turn the answer into money. Our project is a community play park: a rectangular plot 15 metres long and 10 metres wide, holding a triangular sandpit with base 5 metres and height 4 metres, and a semicircular flower bed of radius 3 metres against the south wall. We fence the plot, plant the lawn, and price everything — including the trap that catches more candidates than any other in this section: the flat edge of a fraction of a circle. Throughout, pi is taken as 3,142.

## Subtopic: Perimeter and Area — the Fence and the Floor

Two different measurements, two different units, two different jobs.

Perimeter is the distance around the outside — a length, measured in metres. It answers fencing, edging, skirting and framing questions. Area is the amount of flat surface inside — measured in square metres — and it answers paving, painting, planting and tiling questions. Before any formula, ask: is this question walking around the edge, or covering the inside? That single decision is the first mark.

The rectangular plot: length 15 metres, width 10 metres. Perimeter: twice the sum of length and width — 2 times 15 plus 10, which is 2 times 25: 50 metres of edge. Area: length times width — 15 times 10: 150 square metres of ground.

Context immediately complicates the clean numbers, and that is deliberate. The park needs a gate 2 metres wide, and nobody fences a gateway. Fencing needed: 50 minus 2 gives 48 metres. Questions almost always subtract an opening — a gate, a doorway, a driveway — and the students who lose marks are the ones who fenced straight across the gate.

Units guard the whole calculation. Perimeter answers in metres; area answers in square metres. If an answer to a fencing question says 150, the units have already caught the error: 150 what? Square metres cannot be bought as fencing. Say the unit with the number, every time, and wrong-formula errors announce themselves.

The questions for this section are with you now. Each one asks you first to decide: edge or inside — and then to respect the gate.

## Subtopic: Triangles and Circles with Given Formulae

The sandpit is a triangle with base 5 metres and height 4 metres. The given formula: area equals half times base times height. Substitute: half of 5 times 4 — half of 20 — 10 square metres. The height must be the perpendicular height — the straight-up distance from base to the top point, not the slanted side. Diagrams often print both lengths precisely to see whether you grab the slant by mistake; the formula wants the upright one.

Now the circle, with its two given formulae. Circumference — the circle's word for perimeter — equals 2 times pi times the radius. Area equals pi times the radius squared. One vocabulary pair first: the radius runs from the centre to the edge; the diameter runs edge to edge through the centre, so the diameter is twice the radius. Questions hand you the diameter hoping you will use it as the radius — halve it first, always.

Take a round splash pond of radius 4,5 metres. Circumference: 2 times 3,142 times 4,5. Compute: 2 times 4,5 is 9, and 9 times 3,142 gives 28,278 — about 28,28 metres of edging. Area: 3,142 times 4,5 squared. Square first: 4,5 times 4,5 is 20,25. Then 3,142 times 20,25 gives 63,6255 — about 63,63 square metres.

Watch the order in that area calculation: the radius is squared BEFORE multiplying by pi. Squaring 4,5 gives 20,25; multiplying 3,142 by 4,5 first and then squaring would give a wildly wrong answer. The formula reads pi times radius-squared: the little raised two belongs to the radius alone.

Work through this section's questions. They test the perpendicular height, the radius-diameter halving, and the square-before-multiply order.

## Subtopic: Fractions of Circles — the Trap in the Perimeter

Grade 10 measures quarter circles, semicircles and three-quarter circles, and this is where the section's biggest trap lives.

Area behaves politely. A semicircle is half a circle, so its area is half the full area. The flower bed: radius 3 metres. Full circle area: 3,142 times 3 squared — 3,142 times 9 — 28,278. Half of that: 14,139 — about 14,14 square metres of flowers. A quarter circle of the same radius: a quarter of 28,278 is about 7,07 square metres. Three-quarter: about 21,21 square metres. Take the fraction of the area, done.

Perimeter does NOT behave. Here is the trap: the perimeter of a semicircle is not half the circumference. Walk it and feel why. The full circumference of the radius-3 circle is 2 times 3,142 times 3 — 18,852 metres. Walking the curved edge of the semicircle covers half: 9,426 metres. But you are not back where you started — you are standing at one end of the flat side, and the walk around any shape ends where it began. The flat side is the diameter, 6 metres. True perimeter: 9,426 plus 6 gives 15,426 — about 15,43 metres. Forget the straight edge and the edging order comes up 6 metres short.

The quarter circle is worse, with TWO straight edges. Curved part: a quarter of 18,852 is 4,713. Plus two radii: 3 plus 3. Perimeter: 4,713 plus 6 gives 10,713 — about 10,71 metres. The three-quarter circle: curved part 14,139, plus the two radii where the missing quarter was cut out: 14,139 plus 6 gives 20,139 — about 20,14 metres.

The rule that holds all of this: area takes the fraction and stops; perimeter takes the fraction of the curve, then walks home along the straight edges. Every semicircular window frame, every curved flowerbed border in every practice set turns on that rule.

This section's questions are in front of you. For each shape, trace the walk in your mind and count every edge underfoot — curved and straight.

## Subtopic: From Measurements to Money

Now the part that makes this Mathematical Literacy: the shop. Materials are sold in fixed units — rolls, bags, boxes — and the shop does not sell fractions. The rounding rule of the entire Measurement section: material quantities always round UP.

Price the fence. We need 48 metres of fencing, sold in 15-metre rolls at R520 per roll. Divide: 48 over 15 gives 3,2 rolls. The shop sells 4 rolls or 3 — and 3 rolls is 45 metres, which leaves a 3-metre hole guarded by hope. Round up: 4 rolls. Cost: 4 times 520 gives R2 080. Rounding 3,2 down, or to the nearest whole, is the single most-penalised move in this question type; with materials, 3,2 becomes 4 even though 3,2 sits much nearer to 3 — and 3,9 would ALSO become 4.

Price the lawn. Grass seed goes on the plot area not occupied by the sandpit and the bed: 150 minus the triangle's 10 minus the semicircle's 14,14 gives 125,86 square metres. One bag covers 25 square metres and costs R150. Divide: 125,86 over 25 gives 5,0344 bags. Round up: 6 bags. Cost: 6 times 150 gives R900. Total so far: 2 080 plus 900 gives R2 980 — and a question will happily add cement, labour at a daily rate, or VAT on top.

The complete method, six steps. One: decide edge or inside — perimeter or area. Two: choose the printed formula and identify each letter's value, halving diameters as needed. Three: substitute and compute in order — squares before multiplication, fractions of circles handled curve-plus-straight-edges for perimeter. Four: subtract openings like gates, add straight edges of part-circles. Five: convert to purchase units and round UP. Six: multiply by price, state the answer in rand, and sense-check the size.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They run the full pipeline — shape to formula to metres to rolls to rand.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same play park, same numbers — now with mud on our boots.

## Subtopic: Walking the Edge, Painting the Floor

Stand at the park gate. There are only two questions any measurement problem can ask about this plot, and your feet can tell them apart.

Question one: how far do you WALK to go all the way around? That is perimeter. It is a walk, so it is measured in metres, and it prices anything that follows the edge — fencing, plastic edging, rope.

Question two: how much ground would you PAINT if you painted everything inside the fence? That is area. It is a surface, measured in square metres — imagine metre-by-metre carpet tiles laid out — and it prices anything that covers — seed, paving, fertiliser.

The walk around our 15 by 10 plot: down a long side, 15. Across the short side, 10. Back along the other long side, 15. Home across the last side, 10. Total: 50 metres. The paint job: 15 rows of 10 carpet tiles each — 150 tiles — 150 square metres.

Then reality edits the sum: the gate. It is 2 metres of the walk where no fence goes — so the fence shopping list reads 48 metres, not 50. Almost every plot in a question has a gate, a doorway or a gap, and it is always mentioned in the words, not always shown clearly. Read for the opening before you price the edge.

Feet for the edge, paint for the inside — the questions for this piece are with you now. Ask which one before touching any formula.

## Subtopic: Half a Pizza Still Has a Straight Side

The semicircle trap makes sense the moment you think about pizza. A full pizza has a crust all the way around — that crust is the circumference. Now take half the pizza. How much crust? Half, obviously — the curve. But run your finger around the edge of that half-pizza: curve... and then the straight cut edge. The half-pizza's full edge is half the crust PLUS the straight cut. That straight edge is exactly what students forget when a question asks for the edging around a semicircular flowerbed.

Numbers for the flower bed, radius 3 metres. Whole crust: 2 times 3,142 times 3 — 18,852 metres. Half the crust: 9,426. The straight cut is edge to edge through the centre — the diameter — 6 metres. Full edge of the bed: 9,426 plus 6, which is 15,43 metres of edging, said to two decimals. Order 9,43 and the bed lies open along its whole straight side.

Cut the pizza again into a quarter and now there are TWO straight sides, each one a radius. Quarter crust: 4,713. Two straight sides: 3 and 3. Edge: 10,71 metres. And the three-quarter pizza — the one with a slice missing — keeps three-quarters of the crust, 14,139, plus the two straight sides of the missing slice's cut: total 20,14 metres.

The filling, though, never tricks anyone: half a pizza has half the topping, a quarter has a quarter. Area just takes the fraction. It is only the EDGE — the walk, the crust — that picks up straight sides. Curve plus cuts: say it every time you meet a part-circle.

Your questions for this section are up. Trace the crust with your finger, then count the cuts.

## Subtopic: The Shop Sells Rolls, Not Metres

The park maths says 48 metres of fencing. Now stand at the hardware counter and hear the assistant say: it comes in 15-metre rolls, R520 each. The shop does not cut rolls. So how many rolls?

Divide first: 48 over 15 is 3,2 rolls. And here is the one place in all of mathematics where your rounding teacher's rule gets overruled: 3,2 rounds UP to 4 — even though 3,2 sits snugly next to 3. Because 3 rolls is 45 metres, and a 45-metre fence around a 48-metre job is a fence with a hole in it. When buying materials, the question is never which whole number is nearest; it is what is the smallest amount that finishes the job. That is always the round UP. Four rolls: R2 080.

Same story at the seed shelf. The lawn is the plot minus the sandpit and the bed: 150 minus 10 minus 14,14 leaves 125,86 square metres. A bag covers 25 square metres: 125,86 over 25 is 5,0344 bags. Round up: 6 bags at R150 — R900. Yes, there will be seed left over. Leftovers are the price of a finished lawn; a shortfall is a bald patch in the middle of the park.

So the money pipeline is always: measurement, divide by pack size, round UP, multiply by price. Fence: 48 becomes 4 rolls becomes R2 080. Seed: 125,86 becomes 6 bags becomes R900. Project total: R2 980 — and if the question adds a labourer at R300 a day for two days, that is R600 more, giving R3 580. Every step shown, every unit named, every rounding upward.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Measure, divide, round up, price — and read the packaging line in the question as carefully as the shape itself.
