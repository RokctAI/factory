# Part 1 — Expert

Substitution into formulae quietly carries marks all across Mathematical Literacy — in Measurement, where perimeter, area and volume formulae are printed for you, and in Finance, where tariff formulae do the same work. A formula is a machine: values go in, an answer comes out. This session runs one machine in both directions. The machine is a bakkie-hire tariff — cost equals R380 plus R9,50 times the number of kilometres driven. Forwards, it prices a 260 kilometre trip. Backwards, it answers the harder question of how far a budget of R1 900 will take you. Between the two directions sit the errors that cost the most marks: breaking the order of operations, and dividing a budget before removing the fixed charge.

## Subtopic: Reading a Formula Like a Sentence

Cost = R380 + R9,50 × km. Before any calculating, translate the symbols into the story they tell, because a formula you can narrate is a formula you cannot misuse.

The R380 stands alone — no kilometres multiply it. It is the fixed charge: hand over the keys and R380 is owed before the wheels turn. Perhaps it covers admin and insurance; whatever the reason, it does not care how far you drive.

The R9,50 is glued to the kilometres by multiplication: every kilometre adds R9,50 to the bill. It is the rate — the variable part, the part that grows with usage. Drive nothing, pay nothing here; drive far, and this term does the damage.

So the formula says, in words: the total cost is the R380 entrance fee, plus R9,50 for every kilometre driven. Fixed plus rate-times-usage — the same skeleton as electricity tariffs, cellphone contracts, plumber call-out fees, delivery charges. Learn this skeleton once and you will recognise it in half the Finance and Measurement questions you ever meet.

Two vocabulary notes questions rely on. The quantities that can change — cost and kilometres — are variables. The kilometres, which we choose, is the independent variable; the cost, which responds, is the dependent variable — the cost depends on the distance. Say it in the direction the dependence flows and you will never mix up which value to substitute where.

The questions for this section are with you now. They test whether you can name each part of the formula in words: which number is the fixed charge, which is the rate, and which of the two variables depends on the other.

## Subtopic: Forward Substitution — Pricing the 260 km Trip

The first question: what does a 260 kilometre trip cost? The kilometres are known; the cost is wanted. When the known value slots straight into the right-hand side, that is forward substitution — the easy direction, if you respect the order of operations.

Write the formula first, always: cost = 380 + 9,50 × km. In an exam, that written line is usually a mark all by itself.

Substitute, replacing the variable with its number: cost = 380 + 9,50 × 260. Write this substitution line too — another mark, and your safety net: a marker can follow your thinking even if a calculator slip follows.

Now compute — and here stands the great trap of forward substitution: the order of operations. Multiplication BEFORE addition, always. The wrong road adds first: 380 plus 9,50 gives 389,50, times 260 gives a monstrous R101 270 — a bakkie-hire bill that buys the bakkie. The right road multiplies first.

So: 9,50 times 260. Break it apart: 9 times 260 is 2 340; 0,50 times 260 is 130; together 2 470. The distance charge is R2 470.

Then add the fixed charge: 380 plus 2 470 equals 2 850.

The 260 kilometre trip costs R2 850. Sense-check it against the story: the trip charge should dwarf the entrance fee on a long drive — R2 470 of driving against R380 of admin. Reasonable. And units: rands out of a rand formula. Done.

Work through this section's questions before we turn the machine around. Between them they cover the order of operations, the value of the substitution line, and the sense-check that would have caught the R101 270 disaster before it reached the marker.

## Subtopic: Reverse Substitution — How Far Does R1 900 Go?

Now the direction that separates candidates: I have R1 900 — how many kilometres does it buy? This time the COST is known and the kilometres are wanted. The known value belongs to the left-hand side, and the unknown hides inside the right. Substitute what you know, then dig the unknown out.

Substitute: 1 900 = 380 + 9,50 × km.

Read what the equation says before touching it: the R1 900 covers the R380 fixed fee plus some driving. So the first move almost states itself: remove the fixed fee to see what money remains for actual kilometres. Subtract 380 from both sides: 1 900 minus 380 is 1 520. So 1 520 = 9,50 × km. In money language: after paying the entrance fee, R1 520 remains for driving.

Second move: each kilometre swallows R9,50, so ask how many R9,50s fit into R1 520. Divide both sides by 9,50: km = 1 520 over 9,50. Compute: 9,50 times 160 is 1 520 — exactly. So km = 160.

R1 900 buys a 160 kilometre trip.

Notice what we did in algebra terms: the formula was built by multiplying then adding; we unwound it by subtracting then dividing — inverse operations, applied in reverse order, like taking off shoes before socks. That unwinding IS the method for every reverse question: peel away the fixed part first, then divide by the rate.

Verify — and reverse questions practically beg for verification, because you can run the machine forwards with your answer. Substitute 160 back: cost = 380 + 9,50 times 160 = 380 + 1 520 = 1 900. Exactly the budget. The answer is proven, not just hoped.

This section's questions are in front of you. Watch the option offering 200 kilometres — it is the entire trap compressed into one line — and be ready to state how a reverse answer proves itself.

## Subtopic: The General Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Skill Lives

The complete method, six steps, for any substitution question in any context. One: write the formula down unchanged. Two: identify which variable is known and which is wanted. Three: substitute the known value, writing the full line. Four: forwards — compute respecting order of operations; backwards — unwind with inverse operations, fixed part first, rate last. Five: state the answer WITH its unit — R2 850; 160 kilometres. Six: verify — sense-check the size, and for reverse answers, substitute back.

Now the traps, ranked by the damage they do. Trap one: order of operations — adding the fixed charge to the rate before multiplying. The R101 270 disaster. Your defence: multiplication first, always, and a sense check that would have screamed at a six-figure bakkie hire. Trap two, in reverse questions: dividing the whole budget by the rate without removing the fixed charge — 1 900 over 9,50 gives 200 kilometres, wrong, because R380 of that budget never bought a single kilometre. Peel the fixed fee first. Trap three: dropping units or mixing them — if a formula wants kilometres and the question supplies metres, convert before substituting. Trap four: rounding midway — carry full values through and round only the final answer, and with money, sensibly: to the cent, or as the context demands.

Where does this skill live in your exams? Everywhere. Measurement: perimeter, area and volume formulae — substitution. Finance: tariff formulae exactly like today's — substitution, both directions. Conversion formulae between units, body mass index calculations, distance-speed-time — substitution again. The formula changes costume; the six steps never change.

One last encouragement: substitution is the rare skill where the exam GIVES you the mathematics — the formula is printed on the paper. Your job is only to feed it carefully and unwind it patiently. Write every line, respect the order, check the answer. Do that, and these are marks you collect every single time.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They run across the unwinding order, unit conversion before substitution, and when rounding is permitted — three habits that protect marks in every formula question you will ever be set.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same bakkie from the counter at the rental yard — same R380, same R9,50 a kilometre, same two answers, told the way you would work it out for a family trip.

## Subtopic: The Gate Fee and the Meter

Think about going to a soccer match. There are two completely different kinds of money in your pocket that day. There is the gate fee — you pay it once, at the entrance, and you pay it whether you watch the whole match or leave at half-time. Then there is the money you spend inside, which depends entirely on how many pies you buy. One is fixed. The other grows.

Hiring the bakkie is exactly that. The R380 is the gate fee: it is charged the moment they hand you the keys, and it is the same R380 whether you drive to the corner shop or to Polokwane. The R9,50 is the pie money: every single kilometre you drive costs you another R9,50. So the bill is always the gate fee plus the driving money.

Notice how the formula says the same sentence. Cost equals R380 plus R9,50 times the kilometres. The R380 sits by itself with nothing attached to it — that is your clue that it is the once-off charge. The R9,50 is stuck to the kilometres by a multiplication sign — that is your clue that it repeats, once for every kilometre.

Once you can hear that sentence in a formula, you can hear it everywhere. The plumber who charges to come out and then charges by the hour. The prepaid electricity meter with a service fee. The cellphone contract with a monthly subscription and then a rate per gigabyte. Same skeleton: one amount that never moves, and one amount that grows with how much you use.

And one last piece of vocabulary, which is easier than it sounds. The cost depends on how far you drive — you choose the kilometres, and the bill answers you. So the kilometres are called the independent variable, because they go first and choose freely, and the cost is called the dependent variable, because it waits to see what you did.

Good — the questions for this piece are with you now. All they want is that you can point at each number in the formula and say, in ordinary words, which one is the gate fee and which one is the meter running.

## Subtopic: Running the Machine Forwards — and the R101 270 Disaster

You are driving 260 kilometres to a family wedding. What does the bakkie cost?

Take it in the order the day happens. First the driving: 260 kilometres at R9,50 each. Do it in two friendly pieces — 260 nines is 2 340, and 260 half-rands is another R130 — so the driving comes to R2 470. Then, at the very end, the gate fee: R380. Add them: R2 470 plus R380 is R2 850. The trip costs R2 850.

Now the famous disaster, because a question will offer it to you as a tempting option. Somebody reads left to right, adds the R380 to the R9,50 first to get R389,50, and multiplies that by 260. Out comes R101 270. That is not a bakkie hire, that is buying the bakkie.

Here is what actually went wrong, in plain language. Adding first is like paying the stadium gate fee once for every pie you buy. You bought 260 kilometres, so you paid the R380 gate fee 260 times over — and 260 times R380 really is R98 800. Add the honest R2 470 of driving and there is your R101 270, phantom gate fees and all. Nobody is charged to walk in 260 times. The gate fee happens once, at the end of the sum, and never gets multiplied.

So the rule everybody teaches as "multiply before you add" is not an arbitrary maths law here. It is just the story: work out the part that repeats, then add on the part that happens once.

Finish with the check that costs you nothing. Does R2 850 sound like a bakkie hire for a long trip? Yes — most of it is driving, R2 470, with a small admin charge on top. Does R101 270 sound like a bakkie hire? Absolutely not, and that alarm should ring before you circle anything.

Your questions on this section are up now. Do the multiplying part on its own first, then add the once-off, and let your common sense look at every answer before you commit to it.

## Subtopic: Running It Backwards — R1 900 in Your Pocket

Now flip the machine around, because this is the version real life asks. You have R1 900. How far can you actually go?

Do not touch the R9,50 yet. First ask what you have to pay before you drive anywhere: R380, at the counter, gone. So from your R1 900, only R1 900 minus R380 — that is R1 520 — is money that can actually turn into kilometres. It is like walking into a shop with R1 900 in your hand after paying R380 for the taxi to get there: the shelves can only see R1 520.

Now the easy part. Each kilometre costs R9,50, so how many R9,50s fit into R1 520? Nine fifty times one hundred and sixty is exactly one thousand five hundred and twenty, so one hundred and sixty kilometres. R1 900 buys you a 160 kilometre trip.

Here is where people lose it. If you skip the peeling step and just divide the whole R1 900 by R9,50, you get 200 kilometres and you feel confident. But look at the extra 40 kilometres you invented: that is exactly what R380 would buy if the counter had let you spend it on driving — R380 divided by R9,50 is 40. Those kilometres were never on offer. Your error explains itself right down to the number.

The order matters and it is worth saying out loud, because it is backwards from how the bill was built. The bill was built by multiplying and then adding. So you undo it by subtracting and then dividing — like getting undressed at night: shoes come off before socks, in the opposite order from how they went on.

And now the best habit in the whole lesson: prove your own answer. Take the 160 kilometres and run the machine forwards on it. One hundred and sixty times R9,50 is R1 520, plus the R380 gate fee is R1 900 — precisely your budget. Nothing left over, nothing missing. When your answer walks back through the machine and lands exactly on the number the question gave you, you are not hoping any more; you know.

Last questions of the lesson are with you now. Peel the once-off charge first, divide second, then send your answer back through the formula to check it — and formula questions will start feeling like free marks, because that is exactly what they are.
