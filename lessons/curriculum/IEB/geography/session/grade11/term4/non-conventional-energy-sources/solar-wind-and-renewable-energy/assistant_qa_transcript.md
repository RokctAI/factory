**Bianca:** First up, the one that confuses half my class: if the Northern Cape has the best sun on the planet, why do we not just cover it in panels and shut everything else down? What actually stops us?

**Tutor:** The stop sign is not in the sky — it is in the wires, and understanding that turns a vague answer into a precise one. Electricity is the one product that must be consumed the instant it is made, and it can only travel where a transmission line exists with spare room on it. The Northern Cape's corridors were built decades ago for a province of sheep farms and a few mines; they were never sized for gigawatts of new generation streaming toward Gauteng. Today those lines are effectively full: developers with financed, approved projects queue for grid connections the way cars queue at a single toll gate, and some auction rounds have seen the sunniest bids fail purely for lack of a socket. Building new lines is slower than building the farms themselves — a big transmission corridor needs servitudes negotiated across hundreds of farms, environmental approvals, steel, and years of construction, which is why the lesson calls the grid the binding constraint. There is a second, subtler stop sign: even with infinite wire, a solar-only system would still face the evening problem, because the panels resign at dusk exactly as the country sits down to cook. That is why the plan is a mix spread across provinces — Eastern Cape wind that often blows at night, storage in salt and batteries, pumped storage in the mountains — rather than one gigantic solar carpet. So the two-sentence answer: the resource is world-class but the delivery system is not yet, and the bottleneck is measured in kilometres of wire, not hours of sunshine.

**Bianca:** Next: everyone throws around the claim that wind and solar are now cheaper than coal. How can that be true when a solar farm costs billions to build, and how would I defend the claim in an essay?

**Tutor:** Defend it the way analysts actually compute it: never compare price tags, compare the cost of each unit of electricity over a plant's whole life. Take everything a plant will ever cost — construction, land, maintenance, fuel, financing — and divide by every unit it will ever generate. The moment you do that division, the fossil plant's weakness appears: it must buy fuel every single day of a forty-year life, and that fuel bill dwarfs the construction cost by the end. The solar farm's fuel bill is zero from the first morning to the last. Its costs are almost entirely upfront, and those upfront costs collapsed — panel prices fell by around ninety per cent in the decade after 2010 as global factories scaled up. Now add the evidence you can cite from home: the REIPPPP auctions are sealed competitive bids, and across successive rounds the winning wind and solar prices fell below what new coal capacity could be built for per unit — the market said so with money, not with opinions. Then, because evaluation answers must be honest, concede the asterisk: that cheap unit arrives when the weather chooses, so a full system also pays for storage and backup, which narrows the gap without closing it. And note the direction of travel — every year the auctions run, the renewable price falls further while coal's fuel, water and carbon costs rise. The essay sentence: on a lifetime per-unit basis, proven by our own auction results, new sun and wind now undercut new coal — with an honest asterisk for the cost of covering the night.

**Bianca:** This one comes straight from the farm kids in my class: do wind turbines really kill birds, and does that not undo the whole environmental argument for them?

**Tutor:** It is a real cost, and the mature answer neither denies it nor lets it swallow the comparison. Yes — turbine blades kill birds and bats, and in South Africa the concern is sharpest for large, slow-breeding raptors, because a species that raises one chick every year or two cannot absorb many collisions. That is why our wind farms operate under bird and bat monitoring programmes, why sensitive routes and ridgelines are mapped before licensing, and why mitigation exists — siting turbines away from flight corridors, and even curtailing blades during peak migration or bat activity hours. Now put the cost in its column and weigh it, which is what the question is really testing. The alternative to a wind farm is not zero harm; it is more coal-fired generation, and coal kills birds too — through habitat loss to opencast mines, poisoned wetlands from acid drainage, the Highveld's polluted air, and above all through climate change, which threatens entire ranges and food webs rather than individual animals. Measured per unit of electricity, careful studies consistently find fossil generation costlier to birdlife than wind. So the two-column verdict: bird and bat mortality is wind's genuine environmental debit, it is manageable with careful siting and monitoring and never zero — but set against the coal baseline it is the smaller harm by a wide margin. Write it exactly that way: cost named, mitigation named, comparison made. That is what weighing means.

**Bianca:** Last one: my cousin works at a colliery in Mpumalanga, and in our house the just transition sounds like a polite word for retrenchment. What is the strongest honest version of it for an exam answer — and for him?

**Tutor:** The strongest honest version starts by granting the fear, because it is rational. If the transition is done carelessly, your cousin's town loses the mine wages, the municipal rates, the shop turnover and the school fees all at once — and no wind farm eight hundred kilometres away in the Eastern Cape repairs that. A just transition is the set of commitments designed to prevent exactly that outcome, and it has concrete parts you can list. Timing: coal stations retire on a published schedule over decades, not overnight, so the workforce shrinks mainly through retirement and planned exits rather than mass retrenchment. Money: international partners have pledged transition finance measured in tens of billions of dollars, aimed at grid construction, retraining programmes and new industry in the coal belt specifically — the point is that the region that carried the country's energy burden gets the first claim on the replacement investment. Work: decommissioning and rehabilitating mines and stations is itself years of skilled employment; the transmission build-out needs artisans; and policies that anchor manufacturing — turbine towers, components, batteries — near existing industrial towns aim to put new factories where the old jobs were. Voice: unions and municipalities sit in the planning structures, which is what separates a transition done WITH a region from one done TO it. And then the honest caveat that earns trust in any essay: none of this is automatic. Promised funds must actually arrive, retraining must match real vacancies, and the history of mining towns abandoned after gold and asbestos shows what failure looks like. So the answer for the final exams — and for your cousin — is the same sentence: the transition to cheaper, cleaner power is coming regardless, because the economics have already turned; justice is the difference between his town being part of the plan or part of the wreckage, and that difference is built from schedules, money, factories and a seat at the table.
