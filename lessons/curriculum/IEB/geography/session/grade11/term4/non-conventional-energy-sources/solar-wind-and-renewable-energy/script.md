# Part 1 — Expert

The energy story so far ended at a fork: coal on a countdown, nuclear clean but slow and costly. This session takes the other lane — NON-CONVENTIONAL energy: which sources qualify and why South Africa's endowment is extraordinary; solar energy with named South African and world examples; wind energy treated the same way; and the future of non-conventional energy here, evaluated for its effects on both economy and environment.

## Subtopic: What Counts as Non-conventional, and Why South Africa Is Blessed

NON-CONVENTIONAL energy sources are the challengers to the established order of fossil fuels and nuclear power — solar and wind above all, with a supporting cast of biomass and biogas, geothermal heat, wave and tidal power, and small-scale hydro. Most of the family is RENEWABLE, meaning the flow replenishes itself naturally, which no coal seam ever does. Keep the two terms as cousins rather than twins: a feedlot digester making biogas from manure is non-conventional and renewable but tiny, while a source can sit in the non-conventional column without being a large-scale renewable at all.

Two properties rule the whole family. Property one: the FUEL COSTS NOTHING. Sunshine and moving air are delivered daily without a mine, a railway line or an import contract — nearly all the money goes into the machine that does the catching. Property two: the supply is INTERMITTENT. The sun keeps office hours and the wind takes unscheduled leave, so unlike coal's constant baseload, non-conventional power must be married to storage, backup or a carefully designed mix. Every advantage and every complaint in this session traces back to these two properties.

Now measure the endowment. Most of the South African interior records more than two thousand five hundred hours of sunshine a year, and the solar radiation falling daily on the Northern Cape ranks with the strongest reliably measured on the planet — by physics alone, it is premium solar territory. Along the coasts, two oceans drive strong, dependable winds onto the Eastern Cape, the southern Western Cape and the west coast belt running north of the Cape. For perspective, Germany — famously grey — built a world-leading solar industry on roughly half the sunshine our sunniest province receives. Whatever slowed South Africa's build-out, the resource was never the problem.

Name the delivery mechanism: the RENEWABLE ENERGY INDEPENDENT POWER PRODUCER PROCUREMENT PROGRAMME, REIPPPP, under which private developers compete at auction to build wind and solar plants and sell their output to the grid on long-term contracts. Since 2011 the programme has mobilised hundreds of billions of rand of private money and delivered more than a hundred plants — and the bidding wars drove prices down so far that new wind and solar now cost less per unit than new coal. That quiet reversal of the price table is the engine under this entire topic.

The questions for this section are ready: the definition of the family, its two ruling properties, the evidence of the endowment, and what REIPPPP does.

## Subtopic: Solar Energy: South African and World Examples

Solar power works through two distinct technologies, and questions expect you to keep them apart.

PHOTOVOLTAIC panels — PV — turn light straight into electricity inside semiconductor cells. Nothing moves, nothing burns, no water is needed; the same physics runs a pocket calculator and a farm of a million panels; output tracks the daylight moment by moment, peaking at midday and vanishing at dusk. CONCENTRATED SOLAR POWER — CSP — takes the thermal route instead: a field of sun-tracking mirrors, called heliostats, aims reflected sunlight at a single receiver until the working fluid — usually molten salt — reaches several hundred degrees; the heat raises steam, and beyond the turbine the plant behaves like any thermal station. CSP's trump card is STORAGE: molten salt held in insulated tanks stays fiercely hot for hours, so a CSP plant can go on generating deep into the evening — sunshine delivered after sunset, which attacks intermittency at its root.

The South African examples cluster where the resource is richest: the Northern Cape. Around Kimberley and De Aar stretch some of the country's biggest PV farms, Jasper and Droogfontein among them; the CSP fleet stands nearby — Kathu Solar Park's parabolic troughs beside the iron-ore town of Kathu, with about four and a half hours of molten-salt storage; Bokpoort near Groblershoop, whose tanks hold roughly nine hours; and Ilanga outside Upington. Between them they turn Northern Cape lunchtime sun into supper-time electricity. At household scale, rooftop PV multiplied through the load-shedding years, and the humble SOLAR WATER HEATER — a panel that warms water directly, no electronics at all — remains the cheapest solar technology in existence, biting straight into the geyser's share of the bill.

World examples for scale: China manufactures most of the world's panels and hosts the largest installed fleet, with desert arrays visible from space; Germany demonstrated that policy can outweigh cloud; and Spain's pioneering storage plants on the Andalusian plateau proved decades ago that molten salt could carry solar power through the night.

Work through this section's questions now: PV against CSP, why storage separates them, and one South African and one world example of each.

## Subtopic: Wind Energy: South African and World Examples

A wind turbine is one short sentence built at enormous scale: moving air turns shaped blades, the blades turn a shaft, the shaft turns a generator — kinetic energy converted to electricity with no furnace, no steam and no water anywhere in the chain. The size is not vanity but physics: a turbine's power grows with the SWEPT AREA of its blades and with the CUBE of the wind speed — double the wind and the power multiplies by eight — so towers climb beyond a hundred metres to reach faster, smoother air, and a single blade can outmeasure a rugby field. Location decides everything: coastal ridges, open plateaus and gaps that funnel the wind.

South Africa's wind province is the coastal belt, and the EASTERN CAPE wears the crown: Gibson Bay and the Kouga farm near Oyster Bay turn Indian Ocean winds into power, and Amakhala Emoyeni spins on the high ground near Bedford, with further clusters feeding the Coega industrial zone. The WESTERN CAPE follows with farms like Gouda in the wheatlands, and on the west coast north of Vredendal stands Sere, the national utility's own hundred-megawatt farm. The NORTHERN CAPE interior is filling too — Loeriesfontein's turbines rise from Bushmanland scrub. Under REIPPPP auctions, wind became one of the cheapest new sources in the country, and every farm carries construction work, twenty-year land rentals to farmers — sheep graze on undisturbed veld between the towers — and a contracted share of revenue for surrounding communities.

World examples: DENMARK pioneered the industry and now draws roughly half its electricity from wind while exporting turbines to the planet — a small country owning a global technology; CHINA operates the largest fleet in absolute terms; and the NORTH SEA nations plant farms offshore, where wind blows harder and steadier than over any land — a frontier South Africa's deep, rough coastline has yet to open.

Wind's honest two-column check: clean, fuel-free, fast to construct and cheap at auction — set against intermittency, objections from neighbours over sight and sound, bird and bat collisions that demand careful siting, and the standing need for backup when the air goes still.

Attend to this section's questions: the cube law, two named South African farms with their provinces, Denmark's share, and wind's cost column.

## Subtopic: The Future: Economy, Environment and the Honest Obstacles

The syllabus closes the topic with an evaluation: the FUTURE of non-conventional energy in South Africa, weighed for its effects on ECONOMY and ENVIRONMENT.

Economy, positive column: new wind and solar are now the cheapest new electricity available in the country, so every renewable megawatt added holds future tariffs down; REIPPPP has pulled in private investment by the hundreds of billions of rand; construction and maintenance work lands in rural districts the industrial economy always bypassed — the Northern Cape now hosts plant worth billions that nothing else would have brought there; community trusts convert plant revenue into local bursaries, clinics and enterprise funding; and a green manufacturing ladder — assembling panels, fabricating towers — waits if localisation rules hold. Negative column: coal supports on the order of ninety thousand mining jobs concentrated in Mpumalanga towns with few substitutes, while a finished wind or solar farm employs only a small permanent crew, usually in a different province. The transition therefore shifts work between regions and skill sets — the reason the phrase JUST TRANSITION exists: scheduled retraining, pensions and replacement industry for coal country, financed partly by international climate packages.

Environment: measured against the coal baseline the gains are overwhelming — no carbon dioxide, no sulphur dioxide, no Highveld smog, and close to zero water consumption, which in a dry country is a decisive virtue on its own. Honest accounting still lists the debits: big land footprints, though grazing continues beneath turbines; bird and bat mortality where siting is careless; and the mining, manufacture and eventual disposal of panels, blades and batteries, which carry real material demands of their own.

Three obstacles will set the pace. The GRID: the best sun and wind sit in the Northern and Eastern Cape while the demand sits in Gauteng, and the transmission corridors between them are saturated — thousands of kilometres of new line are now the binding constraint. INTERMITTENCY: answered by the mix — coastal wind often blows at night while solar sleeps, CSP salt and batteries store, pumped storage shifts energy across the hours, and gas or hydro can cover the peaks. POLICY CERTAINTY: investment arrives when auction rounds run on schedule and freezes when they stall.

The defensible conclusion: South Africa's non-conventional future is not limited by resource — the resource is world-class — but by delivery: wires, storage, policy rhythm and justice for coal country. Sun and wind will take a growing share of the mix; the only genuine argument is how fast.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: both economic columns, the environmental balance, and the three obstacles.

## Subtopic: Fuel That Falls From the Sky

Picture a filling station that never sends a bill for the petrol. You pay once — for the pumps, the pipes, the forecourt — and from then on the fuel simply arrives, every morning, free of charge, forever. That is the entire business case for non-conventional energy in one image: sunshine and wind cost nothing; only the catching machinery costs money. Coal must be dug, railed and burned afresh every day of its working life; a solar panel is bought once and then gathers free fuel for a quarter of a century.

Name the catch immediately, because honesty scores. The free fuel keeps its own diary: the sun signs off at dusk, and the wind disappears without notice. Coal's one great talent was burning steadily through the darkest, stillest night — so the free filling station needs either a storage tank or a reliable partner. Compress the trade-off into five words and keep them: free but moody, costly but steady. The whole modern energy argument lives inside that line.

Now the nearly unfair part: South Africa's sky is one of the most generous on the planet. The Northern Cape banks over two thousand five hundred sunshine hours a year — premium-grade sun by world measure — while two oceans push dependable wind onto our coasts. Germany, under permanent grey cloud, became a solar superpower with about half our sunshine. The fuel was never our problem; the race is entirely about pumps.

And the pumps are going in: through the REIPPPP auctions, private companies bid to build wind and solar farms, more than a hundred plants already stand, and the bidding drove prices below new coal per unit. The free fuel turned out to be the cheap fuel as well.

Good going — the questions for this piece are with you now: the free filling station, the moody diary, and the endowment.

## Subtopic: Sun Traps and Wind Catchers

Three machines do the harvesting, and each hides a one-line secret.

The PV panel: a slab that converts light straight into current with NO moving parts — no steam, no spinning, silent as glass. Sun in, electricity out; strongest at noon, asleep by nightfall. This is the machine that spread across South African rooftops through the load-shedding years and carpets the mega-farms around De Aar and Kimberley.

The CSP plant: remember focusing the sun with a magnifying glass until a leaf smoked? Scale that trick to a field of thousands of steerable mirrors, all bending sunlight onto one receiver until molten salt inside glows at several hundred degrees. The salt boils water, and from the steam onward it is the familiar giant kettle. But CSP owns a superpower: hot salt in an insulated tank is a thermos the size of a building, staying hot for hours — Bokpoort near Groblershoop banks roughly nine hours of heat, Kathu about four and a half — so the plant serves supper-time electricity brewed from lunchtime sun. Solar after sunset: the moody-fuel problem answered head-on.

The wind turbine: a pinwheel taller than a city block, and its secret is the cube law — power rises with the CUBE of wind speed, so doubling the wind multiplies the power by EIGHT. That is why towers stretch past a hundred metres into faster air, why one blade outmeasures a rugby field, and why the machines stand where they stand: Gibson Bay and Kouga on the Eastern Cape coast, Gouda among the Western Cape wheatfields, Sere on the west coast, Loeriesfontein out in Bushmanland. Sheep graze between the towers, farmers bank the rent, and the neighbouring community holds a slice of the revenue.

World name-drops for the essay: China builds and hosts the most of nearly everything; Denmark runs about half its country on wind; Spain proved molten salt could carry the night decades ago.

## Subtopic: The Relay Race and the Baton

See South Africa's energy future as a relay. Coal has run a long, powerful, filthy leg — and the runner is spent: an old fleet, banks refusing new finance, carbon tolls rising at the borders. Sun and wind are the fresh legs warming up on the infield: cheaper per unit than new coal, clean, and fed by a world-class sky. Races, though, are lost at the BATON PASS, and three fumbles threaten this one.

Fumble one: the extension cord is too short. The finest sun and wind live in the Northern and Eastern Cape; the hungriest sockets live in Gauteng; and the transmission lines between them are already full. Thousands of kilometres of new wire are now the true bottleneck — a megawatt with no line to ride is a megawatt wasted.

Fumble two: the night shift. The fresh runners keep odd hours, so the grid needs storage and teamwork — CSP's tank of hot salt, batteries, pumped storage lifting water uphill for the evening — plus the convenient habit of coastal wind to blow at night while solar sleeps. A well-built MIX covers all twenty-four hours; no single source ever has to.

Fumble three: the coal towns. On the order of ninety thousand mining jobs and whole municipal budgets — Emalahleni, Secunda — stand on the tired runner's leg. Drop the baton carelessly and those towns break; pass it justly — retraining, pensions, new factories, international transition finance — and the country gains a province instead of losing one. That is the meaning that must always travel with the phrase JUST TRANSITION.

The finish-line verdict, both columns open: environment — sun and wind win going away: no carbon, no smog, almost no water, against real but manageable costs in land, birds and disposal. Economy — cheaper power, vast investment and rural jobs, against genuine redistribution pain in coal country. The direction of the race is settled; only the speed is still being argued — and the speed hangs on wires, storage and justice.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the three fumbles, the mix that covers the clock, and the verdict argued in both columns. Land these and the energy unit closes in your favour.
