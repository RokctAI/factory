# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session gathers the physical geography of the Grade 11 year into one structure: the atmosphere from the first term — the planet's energy budget, the global circulation it drives, Africa's climates, and the droughts and desertification that follow failed rain — and the geomorphology of the second — landscapes carved from horizontal, inclined and massive rock, the slope as the basic unit of landscape, and mass movement as the slope in motion — ending with the mapwork that ties everything to the 1 in 50 000 sheet, because every landform revised here is also a pattern of contour lines. All descriptions are built in words, so that any diagram you meet in your final exams is one you have already constructed mentally.

## Subtopic: The Energy Engine and Global Circulation

Start where the physics starts: unequal heating of a sphere. At the tropics the sun's rays strike almost vertically — concentrated on a small patch of surface after a short passage through the atmosphere. Near the poles the same rays arrive at a shallow angle, spread thin across a wide area after a long slanting journey through the air. The consequence is an ENERGY SURPLUS from the tropics to roughly 40 degrees of latitude and an ENERGY DEFICIT beyond it. The axis tilts at 23 and a half degrees, and as the earth completes its yearly revolution that tilt walks the overhead sun from the Tropic of Cancer in June to the Tropic of Capricorn in December, dragging the entire heating pattern with it. Left uncorrected, the tropics would cook while the poles froze harder every year; the correction is CIRCULATION, the atmosphere and oceans exporting surplus heat poleward.

The atmosphere corrects through one repeating chain: temperature sets pressure, pressure sets wind. Warm air expands, rises, and leaves LOW pressure behind it at the surface; cold air contracts, sinks, and stacks up HIGH pressure; and wind is simply air draining from high toward low. Run the chain around a rotating globe and the pressure belts appear in order: the EQUATORIAL LOW under the climbing air of the overhead sun; the SUBTROPICAL HIGHS near 30 degrees, where the air that rose at the equator, now cooled, presses back down; the SUBPOLAR LOWS around 60 degrees; and the POLAR HIGHS of dense, frigid air over the ice. None of these belts is nailed down — the whole set migrates a few degrees with the overhead sun, southward in our summer and northward in our winter, and that small migration writes entire climates, handing the south-western Cape and the Mediterranean their opposite rainy seasons.

Between the belts turn the cells. In the HADLEY CELL, air rises at the equator, streams poleward high above the surface, subsides near 30 degrees, and flows back equatorward along the surface as the TRADE WINDS. The FERREL CELL between 30 and 60 degrees powers the WESTERLIES, the storm-steering winds of the mid-latitudes that push winter cold fronts onto the Cape. The POLAR CELL finishes the set with cold easterlies spilling off the poles. And every one of these winds bends, because on a rotating planet nothing travels straight: the CORIOLIS FORCE deflects moving air to the LEFT in the southern hemisphere and to the right in the northern. That single rule turns an honest south-to-north surface flow into the south-east trades that ride onto the KwaZulu-Natal coast, and the poleward mid-latitude flow into the south-westerly gales of a Cape winter. Recite the model in sequence — heating, pressure, cells, deflection — and any circulation question unpacks itself.

Take this section's questions now: the geometry of surplus and deficit, the belts and their migration, the three cells, and the southern-hemisphere bend.

## Subtopic: Africa's Climate, Droughts and Desertification

Aim the machine at our continent. Africa straddles the equator, so it holds the equatorial low and BOTH subtropical high belts simultaneously, and a single master switch governs its rainfall: CONVERGENCE versus SUBSIDENCE. Air that converges and rises builds cloud and rain; air that subsides forbids both. The rising seam is the INTER-TROPICAL CONVERGENCE ZONE, the ITCZ — the belt of towering thunderstorms where the trade winds of the two hemispheres collide beneath the overhead sun. It migrates with the sun, and its migration is the continent's calendar: sweeping south across southern Africa in our summer to deliver the wet season, retreating north in winter and taking the rain with it — one band of storms wiping across Africa like a slow windscreen wiper, granting the equator two wet seasons a year and the tropical margins just one. The sinking branches are the subtropical highs, and southern Africa is boxed in by three: the SOUTH ATLANTIC HIGH off the west coast, the SOUTH INDIAN HIGH off the east coast, and, in winter, the KALAHARI HIGH parked over the interior plateau, whose subsiding air seals the Highveld under months of cloudless, windless, frost-at-dawn weather. The coasts differ because the oceans differ: the South Indian High pushes warm, moist air across the warm Agulhas current onto the east coast, feeding its rains, while the west coast suffers twice — subsiding dry air overhead and the cold BENGUELA current below, chilling the onshore air until it can manage fog but hardly ever rain. That double lock is the making of the Namib.

When rain fails, use the vocabulary precisely. DROUGHT is not the same as aridity: a desert is permanently dry by climate, while drought means rainfall significantly BELOW WHAT A REGION EXPECTS — which is why even a wet region can be droughted. The syllabus ranks drought in four stages: METEOROLOGICAL, the rainfall shortfall itself; HYDROLOGICAL, when rivers, dams and aquifers draw down; AGRICULTURAL, when soil moisture can no longer carry the crops; and SOCIO-ECONOMIC, when the failure reaches jobs, livelihoods and food prices. Southern African droughts have a familiar driver: EL NIÑO, the periodic warming of the eastern Pacific that tilts our summer rains toward failure, with LA NIÑA as its wetter opposite. The effects cascade in order — harvests fail, herds die, dams empty, rural incomes crumble, and people move to the towns.

DESERTIFICATION is a different concept, and the difference is a favourite test: drought is a temporary shortfall; desertification is long-term DEGRADATION of dryland until it behaves like desert — and its causes are mostly human. Overgrazing flays the veld past recovery; overcultivation exhausts the soil; fuelwood cutting bares the ground; badly managed irrigation waterlogs and salts it. Drought then unmasks what misuse prepared: bared, compacted soil sheds the rare rain instead of drinking it, erosion removes it, and the productive rim of the drylands retreats — the Sahel along the Sahara's southern edge at continental scale, stretches of the Karoo and Northern Cape at home. Management answers each cause in turn: stock at the veld's carrying capacity, rotational grazing, drought-hardy planting — the Sahel's Great Green Wall is the continental attempt — contour cultivation, and support for the rural poor whose survival pressures drive the misuse.

Pause for this section's questions: convergence against subsidence, the wandering ITCZ, the three highs boxing southern Africa, the four-stage drought ladder, and drought set against desertification.

## Subtopic: Reading Rock into Landscape — Horizontal, Inclined and Massive

The whole of the geomorphology term divides along one question: how does the rock lie? Where sedimentary strata lie FLAT — the great layered basin of the Karoo — erosion produces the tabletop family. A resistant CAP ROCK, in the Karoo usually dolerite that squeezed in as horizontal sills, shields the softer shale beneath it. Rivers incise, slopes eat backward, and the survivors line up in a shrinking sequence: the PLATEAU, a broad upland still fully capped; the MESA, a flat-topped hill wider than it is high; the BUTTE, the next stage, now higher than its narrow top is wide; and last the CONICAL HILL, its armour completely stripped, its profile relaxed into a cone. The process behind the sequence is DIFFERENTIAL EROSION — weak rock retreating faster than its hard protector — with the cap's edge maintained as a small vertical cliff while broken debris aprons the slope beneath. Any Karoo horizon shows the whole family at once.

Tilt those same strata and a new family forms: asymmetrical ridges, each with two unequal sides — a gentle DIP SLOPE running down the top of the tilted resistant layer, and a steep SCARP SLOPE cutting across the layers' broken ends. The dip angle names the ridge: a gentle dip, up to about 25 degrees, builds a CUESTA, long soft back and short steep face; a moderate dip builds a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE, its two slopes converging in steepness; and near-vertical strata build a HOGSBACK, both faces steep and nearly matched, the crest as narrow as a rooftop ridge. The Cape's folded mountain belt supplies the showpieces, cuestas on its flanks and hogsbacks where the folding stands the beds on end. And these ridges earn their keep: dip slopes gather and guide groundwater, scarp faces yield building stone, and the gaps cut through the ridges — the poorts — decide where the roads and railways go.

Where rock is MASSIVE — igneous, unlayered, structureless — the landscape turns rounded. A BATHOLITH is an enormous body of granite that cooled and crystallised deep in the crust; when erosion finally unroofs it, it emerges as sweeping bare domes. Granite peels: freed from the confining pressure of the rock that once lay above it, the surface expands minutely and sheds curved shells — EXFOLIATION, the onion-skin weathering that keeps granite hills smooth and stepless. Paarl Mountain's grey domes above the Berg River valley are the national exhibit. Where joints slice the granite into buried blocks, water rots each block's corners chemically underground; strip away the rotted grit and the surviving rounded cores stand piled on one another as TORS — the balanced castle-rock stacks of the Magaliesberg foothills and the Matobo hills over the border. Keep the master sentence: flat strata make steps and tables, tilted strata make asymmetric ridges, massive granite makes domes and tors — one sentence that files every landform of the term.

This section's questions are ready: the plateau-to-cone sequence with its process, the three tilted ridges ranked by dip, and the granite line from batholith to tor.

## Subtopic: Slopes, Mass Movement and the Map

Every landscape above is assembled from slopes, and the syllabus dissects the slope into four ELEMENTS, always recited downhill: the CREST, the rounded convex summit surface, smoothed by long weathering; the CLIFF, or free face, the wall of bare bedrock that supplies every falling fragment; the TALUS slope, the straight apron of angular broken rock resting below the cliff at the steepest angle loose debris can hold; and the PEDIMENT, the long, gently concave foot slope easing out to the plain. Not every slope owns all four — a young mountain front does, an ancient smoothed surface may keep only crest and pediment — and slopes evolve: the classic dryland story is PARALLEL RETREAT, the free face gnawing backward at a constant angle while the pediment spreads at its foot, which is precisely the mechanism that shrinks mesa to butte to conical hill.

Slopes also fail, and MASS MOVEMENT is the downslope transfer of rock and soil under GRAVITY alone — no river, wind or ice required. Classify by speed and by water. SOIL CREEP: dry and imperceptibly slow, betrayed only by fence posts leaning downhill, tilted telephone poles and tree trunks curved at the base. The FLOWS: soil soaked toward fluid — an EARTHFLOW oozing downhill, or after a cloudburst a MUDFLOW, fastest and deadliest of the wet family, racing down existing channels; South Africa's grimmest example was engineered rather than natural, when a saturated mine tailings dam at Merriespruit in the Free State failed one evening in 1994 and a mudflow of slimes buried part of the town. The SLIDES: a LANDSLIDE shears suddenly along a straight plane; a SLUMP rotates backward along a curved surface as it drops, leaving a crescent-shaped scar at its head. ROCKFALL: simplest and fastest of all, blocks parting from the cliff and dropping free to feed the talus. The analytical core is the priming list: steepness; WATER, the great trigger, both adding weight and greasing the failure plane; the removal of support at the slope's foot by rivers or road cuttings; stripped vegetation whose roots once stitched the soil; and shaking from blasting or tremors. The human costs are current: shack settlements on steep, saturated slopes carried downhill in the KwaZulu-Natal flood disasters, and the steel rockfall canopies and catch nets bolted along mountain passes like Chapman's Peak Drive after fatal falls.

Now close the year on the map sheet, because every form above has a contour signature. A mesa is a tight ring of crowded contours — the cliff — around an almost empty summit, skirted by lines that space out steadily across the pediment; a butte is the identical signature drawn small. A cuesta declares itself by asymmetry: contours packed hard along the scarp, relaxed and widely spaced down the dip slope. A granite dome draws smooth, concentric, evenly curved rings. Slope elements read straight off spacing — crowding at the free face, even moderate spacing on the talus, progressive widening over the pediment; a convex crest bunches its contours on the downhill side, a concave pediment bunches them uphill. Then the standard routines: GRADIENT is vertical interval over horizontal equivalent, stated as one in so many — a rise of one hundred metres across two kilometres is one hundred over two thousand, which simplifies to a gradient of one in twenty; cross-sections are traced contour crossing by contour crossing; INTERVISIBILITY asks whether a convex bulge humps up between two stations and blocks the sight line. The physical year ends where the assessment begins: on the map.

The last expert questions are with you now: the four elements in order, the movements ranked by speed and water with their triggers, and the contour signatures of mesa, cuesta and dome.

## Subtopic: Three Conveyor Belts and a Merry-Go-Round

The circulation topic is a courier problem: the equator was over-delivered heat and the poles were shorted, so the atmosphere runs courier routes to balance the books. Route one: air above the equator heats like the air over a fire, rises, drifts poleward at altitude, cools, and comes back down around 30 degrees — a closed loop, the first conveyor belt. Now look hard at the descending end, because it hides the trick that unlocks half the map: sinking air is dry air pressing downward — it dissolves cloud instead of building it — and 30 degrees is precisely the latitude where the planet's great deserts lie. Sahara, Kalahari margin, the Australian interior: not coincidences, but the dry exhaust of the equator's rising rain. One loop manufactures both the rainforest and the desert.

Two further belts finish the machine — the middle one driving the westerlies that steer the Cape's winter fronts, the polar one flinging cold easterlies off the ice — but the favourite test is the bend. On a spinning planet, nothing travels straight. Stand on a turning merry-go-round and roll a ball to a friend across it: the ball runs true through space, yet from your rotating seat its path curves away. Winds ride the same illusion over the turning earth, curving LEFT in the southern hemisphere and right in the northern. That is Coriolis — and it is the whole reason the trades reach our east coast from the SOUTH-EAST and the winter storms strike the Cape from the SOUTH-WEST: straightforward north-south deliveries, bent sideways by the spin.

Finally, slide the machine with the seasons and South Africa's split personality appears. Summer: the overhead sun stands south, and the great storm seam — the ITCZ, where the two hemispheres' trades crash together and boil upward — dips far enough south to drench the interior: thunderstorm season on the Highveld. Winter: the seam retreats north, the Kalahari High settles its lid of sinking air over the plateau — dry, still, frostbitten mornings — while the displaced westerlies finally drag their fronts across the Cape. Johannesburg dries out in the very months Cape Town gets soaked: one migrating machine, two opposite rain calendars, one country.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: the loop that builds deserts at 30 degrees, the merry-go-round bend, and the migrating seam behind our two rain seasons.

## Subtopic: The Layer Cake, the Tilted Books and the Rising Loaf

The entire geomorphology term comes out of a bakery. First, the layer cake: soft sponge layers under a single hard chocolate lid. Leave it in the rain. Exposed sponge washes away fast, but wherever the chocolate lid holds, the sponge below is safe. The rain gnaws in from the edges, the lid's rim snapping off crumb by crumb, and the cake shrinks by stages — a wide table, then a small table, then a stump taller than it is wide, and finally, the day the last chocolate is gone, a soft cone of unprotected sponge. Wide table, small table, tall stump, cone: plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill. The Karoo skyline is that cake, and its chocolate is the dolerite cap.

Second, tilt the bake. Lean a shelf of hardcover books at a shallow angle and study one book: a long gentle climb up the front cover, a short steep drop off the spine. That is a CUESTA — gentle dip slope on the tilted hard layer, steep scarp slope across the snapped edges. Prop the books steeper and the two faces grow more alike: a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE. Stand them almost upright and both faces fall away steep and matched, the crest narrow as a rooftop: a HOGSBACK. One hook holds all three — the steeper the tilt, the more equal the slopes.

Third, the loaf with no layers at all. Granite bakes underground as one huge unlayered batch — a batholith — and when erosion pulls off the crust above it, it behaves like a hot loaf turned out of its tin: the released surface expands and flakes away in curved crusts. That peeling is EXFOLIATION, onion-skin weathering, and it is why granite country rolls in smooth domes and never in steps — no layers, no staircases. Paarl's rounded grey summits are the loaf at landscape scale. And where cracks carve the buried granite into blocks, groundwater rots the corners off each block the way tea rounds the edges of a dunked rusk; sweep away the rotted crumbs and the hard rounded cores remain stacked in improbable piles — TORS, the balancing boulder castles that look built and are only chemistry plus time.

This section's questions are up now: the shrinking cake in order, the three book angles, and the peeling loaf with its rounded-block towers.

## Subtopic: One Walk Down the Hillside — Slopes, Slides and the Exam

Walk down one Karoo koppie slowly and the last topics of the year walk beside you. You set off on the CREST — a rounded shoulder underfoot, smoothed by ages of weathering. A few paces on, the ground falls out of sight: the CLIFF, the free face of naked bedrock, the birthplace of every loose block below. Under it your boots crunch onto sharp, loose rubble resting as steeply as loose rock can rest — the TALUS — and you can test its honesty with a kick: the stone clatters, slides, and stops, because the whole apron already lies at its angle of rest. The rubble thins, the gradient softens, and the long gentle run-out to the flats carries you home — the PEDIMENT. Crest, cliff, talus, pediment: four elements, one walk; the marks come from naming each, saying what builds it, and why it holds its angle.

Now watch the hill move. On most days it creeps too slowly to see — SOIL CREEP — confessed only by fence posts tilting downhill like tired sentries and tree trunks curving to stay vertical. Add water and the pace lifts: soak the soil to porridge and it flows as an EARTHFLOW, or after a cloudburst as a MUDFLOW sprinting down the channels — the Merriespruit tailings disaster showed how lethal fluid mud can be when a saturated dam wall gave way over a town. Undercut the slope — a river chewing its foot, a road blasted through its base — and whole slabs release at once: a LANDSLIDE along a straight plane, or a SLUMP rotating backward on a curved one and leaving a crescent bite at its head. And straight off the cliff, the simplest event of all: ROCKFALL, blocks dropping free to restock the talus. Whatever the movement, the question underneath never changes: what primed the slope — steepness, water, lost vegetation, a cut-away foot — and the strongest answers name the trigger, which is nearly always water or us.

End the walk holding the map, because that is where the year is assessed. Your koppie prints itself as rings: crowded rings where the cliff stood, evenly spaced rings down the talus, rings relaxing wider and wider across the pediment. A cuesta signs its name in asymmetry — lines jammed together on the scarp flank, strolling far apart down the dip. A granite dome draws pond-ripple circles, smooth and concentric. Then run the routines rehearsed since Grade 10: GRADIENT as rise over run — one hundred metres of climb across two thousand metres of ground is one in twenty; the cross-section spoken crossing by crossing; intervisibility settled by asking whether a convex bulge lifts between the two points. Walk the hill, read its rings, run the routines — and nothing in the physical questions will be ground you have not already stood on.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the four-element walk, the movements from creep to rockfall with their triggers, and the koppie read from its rings.
