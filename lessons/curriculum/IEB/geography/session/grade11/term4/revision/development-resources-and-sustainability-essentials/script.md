# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session gathers the human geography of the Grade 11 year into one argument: the development topic — what development actually means, the indicators that measure it, the models that explain its geography, and the roles of trade and aid — joined to the resources topic — the conventional and non-conventional energy powering South Africa, the management of energy toward a greener economy, and the soil beneath it all. The thread stitching everything together is SUSTAINABILITY: meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own — a definition worth carrying word for word, applied in this session to money, power stations and topsoil alike.

## Subtopic: What Development Means and How It Is Measured

DEVELOPMENT means improvement in the quality of human lives — never merely an economy getting bigger. That distinction governs the whole topic: ECONOMIC GROWTH is more output, countable in currency; development asks whether the output turns into health, schooling, choice and dignity. Use the standard vocabulary precisely: DEVELOPED and DEVELOPING countries; the NORTH-SOUTH DIVIDE, the old line across the world map placing the wealthy industrial North — which swallows Australia and New Zealand despite their latitude — above the poorer South; and the caution that the categories blur, straddled by fast-industrialising economies like Brazil, India and China.

Measurement is where the marks concentrate. Economic indicators first: GROSS DOMESTIC PRODUCT counts the value of everything produced INSIDE a country in a year; GROSS NATIONAL INCOME counts the income of a country's people and firms wherever on earth they earn it; and both are normally quoted PER CAPITA — divided by population — so that countries of different sizes can be compared. Their shared weakness is the weakness of all averages: a per-person figure is silent about who actually pockets the money. The GINI COEFFICIENT speaks to exactly that silence, scoring inequality from zero, perfect equality, toward one, total concentration — and South Africa's score, among the very highest recorded anywhere, is the statistic standing behind every photograph of a golf estate fenced beside a shack settlement. Social indicators reach where money cannot: LIFE EXPECTANCY; INFANT MORTALITY, the single most sensitive test of real living conditions; LITERACY; access to clean water and sanitation. The HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX folds three dimensions — a long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living — into one score between zero and one, and its deepest lesson is comparative: two countries with identical incomes can hold very different HDI scores, because policy decides whether money becomes clinics and classrooms or stays as figures in accounts.

Development is also uneven WITHIN countries, and the frame is CORE AND PERIPHERY: the core — Gauteng, the industrial heartland — attracts and accumulates investment, skills and services; the periphery — the former homeland regions above all — exports labour and raw materials and lives partly on remittances sent home. The model scales beautifully: the world has a global core and periphery, each country repeats the pattern internally, and so does each city. Beside it stands ROSTOW'S MODEL, five stages from traditional society through the preconditions for take-off, take-off itself and the drive to maturity, to high mass consumption. Use it as a vocabulary of stages, then criticise it, because the criticism carries marks of its own: it assumes every economy climbs the same staircase the West climbed, ignores colonial histories and the handicaps of late starters, and treats arrival at wealth as guaranteed.

Take this section's questions now: growth against development, the indicator toolkit and its flaws, Gini and HDI, and core-periphery set against Rostow.

## Subtopic: Trade, Aid and the Rules of the Game

No country develops in a vacuum; all of them trade inside a system whose rules were written by the incumbents. The founding pattern is colonial and has never fully dissolved: the periphery exports PRIMARY products — ores, crops, crude oil — and buys back MANUFACTURED goods. The trap hides in the prices: primary prices lurch and drift downward relative to the price of manufactures, so the TERMS OF TRADE — the ratio of what your exports earn to what your imports cost — slide against raw exporters year after year: the same trainload of ore purchases fewer machines than it did last season. The recognised escape is BENEFICIATION and industrialisation — processing your own materials at home and keeping the value added — which is why development plans everywhere, South Africa's included, preach climbing the value chain: sell the jewellery, not just the gold; the stainless steel, not just the chrome ore.

Keep the trade vocabulary exact. The BALANCE OF TRADE is goods exported minus goods imported — surplus earns, deficit drains. FREE TRADE dismantles barriers; PROTECTIONISM erects them through tariffs, quotas and subsidies — and the developing world's oldest grievance is that wealthy countries preach open markets while subsidising their own farmers so heavily that African producers are undercut in their own home towns. GLOBALISATION is the tightening integration of markets, transport and communication: it has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty along the East Asian route — begin with cheap manufactured exports, climb the skill ladder — yet it also concentrates power in multinational corporations and leaves small economies exposed to shocks decided on other continents. South Africa trades inside this system through BRICS, through the continental promise of the African Continental Free Trade Area, and still partly through the old dependency it is working to shed: minerals outbound, machinery inbound.

Then AID, the part of the topic where judgement is tested. The types first: HUMANITARIAN or emergency aid, the immediate response to disaster; DEVELOPMENT AID, longer-horizon support for projects and capacity; TECHNICAL ASSISTANCE, the transfer of skills; MULTILATERAL aid routed through institutions such as the World Bank, against BILATERAL aid passed state to state; and TIED or CONDITIONAL aid, wrapped in strings — buy the donor's goods, adopt the donor's policies. Argue the debate in both directions, always. In favour: aid saves lives in emergencies; it builds the clinics, schools and dams a strained budget never could; it moves skills across borders; and at its best — mass vaccination, the campaign against HIV — it has changed whole societies. Against: aid can breed DEPENDENCY, ruining local producers when donated food undercuts the farmer at the market; it leaks into corruption; tied aid serves the giver before the receiver; and loans dressed as help became the debt trap that strangled African budgets through the 1980s and 1990s. The balanced conclusion: aid succeeds when it builds capacity and answers priorities set locally — and the deeper cure for underdevelopment is fair trade and investment, not charity.

Pause for this section's questions: the primary-product trap and the terms of trade, protectionism against free trade, globalisation weighed honestly, and the aid debate argued both ways.

## Subtopic: Powering the Country — Conventional and Non-Conventional Energy

Energy is where development promises collide with physics. South Africa's electricity remains overwhelmingly CONVENTIONAL and overwhelmingly COAL: more than four-fifths of generation burns coal mined cheaply from the shallow seams of the Mpumalanga Highveld, where the giant thermal stations sit directly on the coalfields and the transmission network fans outward to the country. Be able to narrate the process cleanly: coal is milled to powder and burned; the heat drives water into superheated steam; the steam spins a turbine; the turbine spins a generator; and the cooling towers exhale the waste heat as the white plumes on the Highveld skyline. Coal's defence: cheap, local, proven technology, tens of thousands of jobs, and dependable BASELOAD — the constant floor of supply every grid must stand on. Coal's charge sheet: carbon dioxide that ranks South Africans among the heavier per-person emitters on earth; sulphur dioxide and particulates trapped in the Highveld airshed; scarce water consumed by the gigalitre; mining-scarred land; and ACID MINE DRAINAGE, the acidic, metal-laden water seeping out of old workings toward Witwatersrand rivers. Complete the conventional family: NUCLEAR at Koeberg outside Cape Town, low-carbon and steady but expensive, slow to build and trailed by a waste problem measured in centuries; and HYDRO-ELECTRICITY, clean and instantly dispatchable but starved here by the oldest fact in the water topic — too few strong rivers — leaving our hydro small and partly imported from Cahora Bassa in Mozambique, while PUMPED-STORAGE schemes like Drakensberg and Ingula serve not as sources but as giant water batteries, pumping uphill in the quiet hours and generating at the evening peak.

NON-CONVENTIONAL energy is the second lane, and South Africa's endowment is exceptional. SOLAR: the interior's dry, clear skies deliver sunshine ranked with the world's best, and the Northern Cape hosts both photovoltaic farms and concentrated solar plants whose mirror fields bank heat in molten salt and keep generating after dark. WIND: the Eastern and Western Cape coastal belts spin turbine farms from the Kouga coast to the West Coast north of the Cape. Know the mechanism that built them: the RENEWABLE ENERGY INDEPENDENT POWER PRODUCER PROCUREMENT PROGRAMME, the competitive bidding rounds that drew private billions into wind and solar and drove their price below new coal. Then weigh honestly: renewables are clean, fuel-free, fast to build and now the cheapest new electricity available — but the sun sets and the wind lulls, INTERMITTENCY, so the system needs storage, flexible backup and a stronger grid; and the coalfield towns whose livelihoods hang on the old system need a JUST TRANSITION — the policy phrase for changing the energy base without discarding the people who built the last one.

This section's questions are ready: the coal chain from seam to socket with its costs, nuclear and hydro's trade-offs, the water battery, and the solar-wind case with intermittency and the just transition.

## Subtopic: Soil, Sustainability and the Greener Economy

The year's final resource is its most fundamental: SOIL. It forms at the pace of centuries — several hundred years for a few centimetres of topsoil under our conditions — and is lost at the pace of single storms, which makes it effectively NON-RENEWABLE within any human lifetime. Only about 12 per cent of South Africa is arable and barely 3 per cent high-potential, yet national losses run to several hundred million tonnes of soil a year. Narrate the erosion ladder without hesitation: land is laid bare by overgrazing, overcultivation or clearing; raindrops splash the naked soil loose; SHEET EROSION shaves the surface thinly and almost invisibly; runoff gathers into RILLS, channels a plough can still cross; and rills deepen into GULLIES — the DONGAS that scar the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal — after which the land is finished for farming. The remedies work the ladder backward: keep ground covered with residue and cover crops; plough along the CONTOURS so each furrow becomes a small dam; terrace the steep land; rotate crops and rest the veld; hold herds to the veld's carrying capacity; and treat existing dongas with GABIONS, wire baskets of rock that trip each flood into dropping its sediment until the channel silts closed.

SUSTAINABILITY is the concept that binds soil to energy to development: meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own — living off the interest of natural systems and never their capital. Applied to energy it becomes the GREENER ECONOMY, with responsibilities assigned by name. GOVERNMENT: chart the path away from coal, run the renewable procurement rounds, enforce air and water standards, tax carbon, and manage a just transition for the coal regions. BUSINESS: generate and purchase clean power, audit and cut energy waste, design efficient buildings and processes, and account for emissions in audited numbers rather than advertising copy. INDIVIDUALS: the accumulated megawatts of small decisions — efficient lights and appliances, solar water heaters, insulated ceilings, shared and public transport, and the plain discipline of switching off — remembering always that the cheapest, cleanest unit of electricity is the one never used.

Close the year by walking the circle, because the richest essay questions live in the joins. Development needs energy; coal energy spends water, air and climate; a warming climate loads the dice for the droughts and hammering storms of the climate topic; those storms strip bare soil into dongas; and lost soil starves the farming that development promised to feed. The way out is the sustainability toolkit applied at every join: renewable power under some of the best sun on the planet, soil anchored by cover and contour, trade that captures value at home instead of exporting it raw, and aid and investment that build capacity rather than dependency. Walking that circle — resource to economy to atmosphere and back — is the difference between reciting topics and actually doing geography.

The last expert questions are with you now: the erosion ladder with its remedies, sustainability defined word for word, the three responsibility lists, and the circle that joins the year.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same year again — through a school report, a rigged shop, a braai fire and a bank account.

## Subtopic: The Scoreboard and the Rigged Shop

Judging a country by total GDP is like ranking schools by total marks: the biggest school tops the table every time, even if half its learners are failing. Fix one: divide by the number of learners — per capita. Fix two: look past the average at the SPREAD, because a class average of 60 per cent can hide a row of distinctions seated beside a row of zeros. The spread has a name and a number — the Gini coefficient — and South Africa's, among the highest measured on earth, is the statistical shadow of the security estate sharing a fence line with the informal settlement. Fix three: stop counting only money. The Human Development Index reads three lines of the report card together — how long people live, how much learning they get, what they can afford — and scores each country between zero and one. Two countries can earn the same income and score differently, because the index measures what the money was turned INTO. The whole measurement section in one sentence: count per person, check the spread, and follow the money into lives.

Now, why some countries stay poor: imagine a shop whose rules were written before you walked in. You are permitted to sell only raw things — ore, beans, crude — and required to buy back finished things — phones, machines, medicine. Raw prices wobble and sag; finished prices climb steadily. Each year your trolley of ore buys a little less than the year before — that sliding ratio is the TERMS OF TRADE, and it is the trap built into the old colonial pattern: the wealth pools wherever the MAKING happens. The escape is to start making — beneficiation, turning your own gold into jewellery and your own chrome into stainless steel, climbing the value chain exactly as East Asia climbed from cheap factories to rich economies. And the same shop operates inside the country: Gauteng is South Africa's own core, making and banking, while the former homeland regions supply labour and receive remittances — one rigged shop, running at two scales.

Aid is the shop's charity counter, and it must be argued both ways in a single breath. When the flood strikes, emergency aid is simply right. But permanent free bread bankrupts the local baker — that is dependency. Aid tied with strings — spend it on the donor's trucks — serves the giver first. The conclusion that always earns its keep: aid that builds bakeries beats aid that delivers bread, and fairer shop rules would beat both.

## Subtopic: The Braai Fire and the Two Free Fuels

Think of South Africa's electricity as one enormous braai fire that has burned without a break since your grandparents were young. The fuel is coal, dug on the Mpumalanga Highveld where the power stations squat directly on the seams; the fire boils water, the steam spins turbines, and the smoke — carbon dioxide and sulphur — drifts across the Highveld while the cooling towers breathe their white plumes. The fire's virtues are genuine: cheap fuel, local jobs, and above all steadiness — a good fire holds its heat all night, and a grid needs that constant floor, the baseload. But the account is filling with bills: one of the heavier per-person carbon footprints on the planet, dirty air over the coal towns, scarce water spent by the gigalitre, and old mines seeping acid water toward the rivers.

Now the two free fuels. The Northern Cape's sun is among the best measured anywhere — mirror farms near Upington focus its heat into molten salt and keep serving power after dark — and the Cape coasts run steady wind through forests of white turbines from the Kouga coast to the West Coast. Their fuel costs nothing, they build in a couple of years, and through the bidding rounds that set private builders competing, their electricity became cheaper than new coal. So why not switch tomorrow? Because the sun knocks off at dusk and the wind takes unscheduled leave — intermittency — and a country cannot take leave from electricity. The braai answer: keep something steady glowing under the grill while the free fuels carry the daytime; build batteries, including the water kind — pumped-storage schemes like Ingula that push water uphill at midnight and let it fall through turbines at supper time, a dam doubling as a battery; and thicken the wires that must carry Karoo sunshine to Gauteng stoves. Koeberg's reactors already supply one steady low-carbon ember, and hydro stays small because our rivers are small, topped up through the line from Mozambique.

The final piece is the people of the fire. Whole municipalities on the coalfields eat, school and pay rates from coal wages, and a switch that abandons them would swap one injustice for another. The policy name for doing it properly is the JUST TRANSITION: retrain the workers, raise the new industries where the old ones stand, and give coal communities a stake in the sun and wind that replace the seams. An answer that counts kilowatts but forgets the miners is half an answer; the complete answer powers the country AND carries its people across.

This section's questions are up now: the fire's virtues and bills, the two free fuels and their days off, the dam that doubles as a battery, and the transition done justly.

## Subtopic: Living Off the Interest — Soil and the Sustainable Year

Finish with the bank account underneath everything: topsoil. Nature deposits roughly a teaspoon a year — centuries of patience for the few dark centimetres that feed the nation — while careless farming withdraws by the bucket: one thunderstorm on a bare, overgrazed slope can empty decades of deposits before supper. Watch the theft unfold in stages: veld grazed naked; raindrops hammering the exposed ground loose; a thin sheet of runoff shaving the surface so quietly no one sees it leave; the sheet gathering into finger channels; the channels joining and cutting, storm upon storm, until a donga gapes where a field used to be — cliff-walled, spreading, finished. The repair list runs the stages in reverse: cover the ground so rain lands on mulch; plough along the contour so every furrow is a miniature dam; terrace what is steep; rest and rotate the veld; match the herd to what the grass can carry; and where a donga already yawns, stack gabions across it — wire baskets of rock that trip each flood into surrendering its stolen soil until the wound silts closed.

Now say the principle the whole year has been circling: SUSTAINABILITY — meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own. Translate it into a banking rule: live off the interest, never touch the capital. Soil is capital and the harvest its interest — erosion is spending capital and booking it as income. A river's flow is interest; draining the wetland that feeds it is burning capital. Coal is pure capital, a once-off inheritance going up in smoke; sun and wind are pure interest, deposited fresh every morning forever. Even development itself obeys the rule: an economy that grows by hollowing out its people's health and education is liquidating capital and calling it growth.

The closing move is the one that wins essays: walk the circle of the year and point at the joins. Development needs energy — the human topics lean on the resource topics. Coal-fired energy spends water and warms the climate — the resource topics reach back into the atmosphere machine, loading the dice for El Niño droughts and harder storms. Those storms strip bare soil — the climate falls upon the dongas. And the lost soil starves the farms that development was meant to feed — the circle closes where it began. Sustainability is the habit of inspecting every link of that circle before acting: greener power under world-class sun, soil pinned down by cover and contour, trade that ships the jewellery rather than the ore, aid that builds the bakery. Hold the circle, keep the definition word-perfect, assign the three responsibilities — government, business, you — and the human half of the year holds no ambush your final exams can spring.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the teaspoon and the bucket, capital against interest across soil, water and coal, and the circle of the year walked join by join.
