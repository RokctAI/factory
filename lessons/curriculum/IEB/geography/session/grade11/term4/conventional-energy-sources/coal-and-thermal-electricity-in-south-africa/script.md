# Part 1 — Expert

Every factory, geyser and phone charger in South Africa depends on a single dominant habit: turning coal into electricity. This session surveys the country's conventional energy sources — thermal generation and the modest hydro-electric contribution a dry land allows; follows the full journey of energy through a coal-fired power station; audits coal honestly, from cheap and reliable baseload to poisoned rivers and smoky Highveld skies; and finishes with the syllabus question of whether conventional sources alone can supply the decades ahead, using nuclear power as the case study.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Energy Mix: Thermal and Hydro

Geology dealt South Africa an unusual hand: thick coal seams lying close to the surface across the Mpumalanga Highveld, stretching into the Waterberg in Limpopo and the northern Free State. Shallow coal is cheap coal, and cheap coal decided where the power stations went. Study any energy map of the country and the cluster is unmistakable — Tutuka near Standerton, Duvha and Kendal on the Highveld, Matimba and the enormous Medupi in the Waterberg — all planted directly on top of the seams they burn. The logic is transport economics: moving electricity along a high-voltage line costs far less than hauling millions of tonnes of coal by rail, so the coal stays put and the power travels, most of it flowing north to the mines, factories and cities of Gauteng.

The result is a lopsided energy mix. Around eight in every ten units of South African electricity come from coal-fired thermal stations. The remaining slice is shared by Koeberg, the nuclear station north of Cape Town; a handful of hydro-electric and pumped storage schemes; and the fast-growing renewable share that the next session explores.

Hydro-electricity — the second conventional source — converts the energy of falling water into rotation. South Africa uses little of it for one stubborn reason: rainfall is low and rivers are seasonal, so there is rarely enough reliable falling water. The Gariep and Vanderkloof stations on the Orange River generate at times of peak demand, and the country buys additional hydro-power from the giant Cahora Bassa scheme on the Zambezi in Mozambique — evidence that the continent's true hydro wealth lies far to our north. Then come the pumped storage schemes — Palmiet in the mountains near Grabouw, Steenbras above Gordon's Bay, Ingula on the Drakensberg escarpment. Picture two dams, one perched high, one lying low. In the small hours, when demand slumps, surplus grid electricity pumps water to the top dam; when the evening rush hits, the water plunges back through the turbines. A pumped storage scheme uses more energy than it returns, so it generates nothing new — it is a water battery, banking cheap midnight energy and paying it out at the expensive evening peak.

The questions on this section are open for you now: why the stations crowd the coalfields, the shape of the mix, and what separates pumped storage from true hydro.

## Subtopic: Inside a Coal-Fired Power Station

Assessment questions on this topic reward a process told in the right order, so trace the energy through its four costumes: chemical, heat, movement, electricity.

Coal reaches the station on conveyor belts, often straight from a mine at the fence line, and is first crushed in mills until it is finer than cake flour. Fine particles expose enormous surface area, so the dust ignites instantly and burns out completely when it is blasted into the furnace — a chamber the height of an office block running at roughly one thousand four hundred degrees Celsius. The furnace walls are not brick alone: they are woven with kilometres of steel tubing carrying purified water. This is the boiler, and inside it water flashes into superheated steam, driven past five hundred degrees at crushing pressure.

The steam is fired at the angled blades of a turbine, spinning its shaft three thousand times every minute — heat has become movement. Bolted to the same shaft is the generator: a massive electromagnet whirling inside coils of copper, inducing current in those coils — movement has become electricity. Finally the transformers raise the voltage, up to four hundred kilovolts, before the power enters the transmission network. High voltage is chosen because it slashes the energy lost as heat along hundreds of kilometres of cable; substations near the customers step it down again to usable levels.

Two housekeeping systems keep the cycle turning. Cooling: the exhausted steam must be condensed back into water for another lap of the boiler. The classic solution is the curved concrete cooling tower, whose billowing white plume is nothing but water vapour. In the bone-dry Waterberg, Matimba and Medupi cool with moving air instead of evaporating water — dry cooling, a field where South African engineers lead the world. Waste: combustion leaves mountains of ash, some of which is absorbed by the cement industry, while the flue gases climbing the smokestacks carry carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen — the debts weighed in the next section.

One number to carry into any evaluation: only about a third of the chemical energy in the coal ever leaves the station as electricity. The remaining two thirds are surrendered as waste heat, mostly at the cooling stage.

## Subtopic: Weighing Coal: Advantages and Disadvantages

A strong answer argues both columns with specifics, so assemble each in turn.

The advantages. Security: South Africa's coal reserves will outlast every learner in this class at current burn rates, and no tanker or pipeline politics can interrupt them. Cost: seams a few metres down, beside the stations that burn them, once gave this country some of the cheapest electricity on the planet — the magnet that drew smelters, mines and heavy industry here. Dependability: coal supplies baseload, the constant floor of power that flows at midnight and midday alike, keeping ventilators, cold chains and railway signals alive. Livelihoods: direct coal mining employment sits near ninety thousand people, and towns such as Emalahleni, Middelburg and Secunda are economically built on the seam. Add export income: the terminal at Richards Bay ships South African coal to the world and earns foreign exchange doing it.

The disadvantages carry real weight. Climate: coal releases more carbon dioxide per unit of energy than any other major fuel, making South Africa the continent's heaviest emitter — increasingly a trade problem as major markets prepare to tax the carbon embedded in imports. Air: the ring of stations on the Mpumalanga Highveld concentrates sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides into one of the world's most polluted airsheds, and clinics in Emalahleni treat the asthma and bronchitis that result. Water: wet-cooled stations evaporate vast volumes in a country short of every drop, while acid mine drainage — rainwater turned acidic and metal-laden inside abandoned workings — trickles out of old mines toward the Olifants River system for generations after the last shift ends. Land: opencast pits strip some of the country's best maize soils, and rehabilitation is routinely evaded when mines change hands or are abandoned. Safety: underground work still injures and kills. And dependability in practice: years of skipped maintenance across an ageing fleet delivered load-shedding — a country rich in coal, sitting in the dark.

The sentence that balances the ledger: coal financed South Africa's industrial rise and still powers it today, but the climate, air, water, land and health accounts are all presenting their invoices at once.

Attend to this section's questions: two advantages and two disadvantages argued with detail, and why Emalahleni belongs in both columns.

## Subtopic: Can Conventional Sources Carry the Future? Nuclear as Case Study

Now the forward-looking question the syllabus poses directly: can conventional sources meet South Africa's long-term energy needs?

The case for staying the course: decades of reserves remain underground; the mines, rail links, stations and engineering skills already exist and are paid for; Medupi and Kusile, two of the largest coal stations ever built, are engineered to operate towards mid-century; and coal wages hold up entire municipal economies. The case against is stacking higher every year: most of the fleet is old and breaks down often — load-shedding was its public report card; global lenders now refuse to finance new coal projects; every new wet-cooled station deepens the water deficit; carbon border taxes threaten the competitiveness of everything manufactured with coal power; and South Africa's own international climate commitments require emissions to bend downward. A conventional-only strategy is therefore a clock running down, not a plan.

The prescribed case study is nuclear power at Koeberg, on the Atlantic coast about thirty kilometres north of Cape Town: two reactors, roughly one thousand nine hundred megawatts, and the anchor of the Western Cape's electricity supply since 1984. It was deliberately built at the opposite end of the country from the coalfields, strengthening the far corner of the grid. The physics: uranium fission. Splitting heavy uranium atoms releases ferocious heat, the heat raises steam, and from the turbine hall onward Koeberg is simply another thermal station. What sets nuclear apart is energy density — a single fuel pellet smaller than your thumbnail carries about as much energy as a full tonne of coal.

Nuclear's advantages: no carbon dioxide while generating; genuine round-the-clock baseload; fuel deliveries measured in truckloads rather than trainloads, and no ash dumps; and South Africa mines its own uranium. Its disadvantages: the highest capital costs and the longest construction times of any generation technology — the proposal to build a new reactor fleet collapsed precisely because the country could not afford it; high-level radioactive waste that must be kept isolated for thousands of years, held today at Koeberg itself and at the Vaalputs disposal site in the Northern Cape; accident risk that is extremely rare yet catastrophic in scale when it materialises; and the reality that reactors age too, as Koeberg's extensive life-extension refits prove.

The conclusion that earns full credit: conventional sources on their own cannot carry South Africa's long-term needs. Coal's limits are structural, nuclear is clean but slow and ruinously expensive to expand, and so the defensible strategy is a diversified mix — the story the next session completes.

## Subtopic: The Kettle the Size of a Suburb

Forget the pylons and the concrete for a moment. A coal power station is the kettle in your kitchen, enlarged until it can boil for sixty million people.

Your kettle: an element heats water, steam pushes at the lid. Tutuka or Kendal: coal milled finer than flour burns at one thousand four hundred degrees, and the water sits inside kilometres of steel tubes built into the furnace walls. This steam has no lid to rattle — it slams into the blades of a turbine and spins them three thousand times a minute. On the far end of that spinning shaft sits a huge magnet turning inside coils of copper wire, and that is the entire secret of a generator: spin a magnet in a coil and electricity flows. Transformers then squeeze the voltage up to four hundred thousand volts, because electricity leaks far less energy on a long journey when it travels at high pressure — and Gauteng is a long journey from almost everywhere.

Three details to keep. First, the giant curved towers are coolers, not chimneys: the white clouds rolling off them are water vapour, while the true smoke exits the tall thin stacks alongside. Second, the stations sit on the coalfields and not near the cities because a cable is cheaper than a coal train — let the electricity do the travelling. Third, the humbling arithmetic: of every three bags of coal that go into the furnace, roughly one bag's worth of energy comes out as electricity; the other two escape as heat. Chant the chain until it is automatic: chemical, heat, spin, spark.

Well done so far — the questions for this piece are with you now: the geyser-to-generator chain, what the towers really do, and the one-bag-in-three arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The Drawer of Unpaid Bills

Give coal a fair hearing with two drawers: one for everything it has paid us, one for every bill still outstanding.

The paid-us drawer. Electricity cheap enough to build a mining and manufacturing economy on — that was coal. A steady current at three in the morning and six at night, in any weather — baseload, and coal delivers it. Around ninety thousand mining pay cheques, feeding whole towns whose very names, like Emalahleni, the place of coal, tell you what they stand on. Ships loading at Richards Bay, carrying coal out and bringing foreign earnings in. Genuine payments — never wave them away in an answer.

Now the bills drawer. The climate bill: the most carbon-heavy fuel there is, making us Africa's largest emitter just as trading partners start charging for carbon at their borders. The air bill: a circle of smokestacks has given Highveld towns some of the dirtiest air measured anywhere, and the children of Emalahleni pay it in inhalers. The water bill: thirsty stations in a dry country, plus the cruellest item — acid mine drainage, flooded old workings quietly bleeding acidic, metal-rich water toward the Olifants River for a century after closure. The land bill: opencast pits eating the maize fields of Mpumalanga. The reliability bill: a worn-out fleet that turned load-shedding into a household word.

And notice the town that files papers in both drawers — Emalahleni, where the wages and the clinic queues share an address. That is the honest verdict: coal raised the house and is now scorching its walls. Any answer worth full credit opens both drawers.

Your questions for this section are ready: three payments, three bills, and the town that appears twice.

## Subtopic: The Fork in the Road

Can the country simply keep driving the coal road? Read the signboards before answering.

Signboard one: fuel remaining — plenty; the seams hold decades more. Signboard two: vehicle condition — poor; apart from the two new giants, Medupi and Kusile, the fleet is elderly, and load-shedding was its breakdown log. Signboard three: financing — closed; the world's banks have stopped writing cheques for new coal. Signboard four: tolls ahead — carbon charges at foreign borders that will tax everything made with coal power. Fold in the water shortage and the country's climate promises, and the coal-only road ends at a wall; the only question is the date.

The signposted detour is nuclear, and the case study is Koeberg, just up the coast from Cape Town: two reactors that have anchored the Western Cape's supply since 1984. Its party trick is concentration — split uranium atoms and a pellet smaller than your thumbnail releases the energy of a tonne of coal; after that it is the giant kettle all over again, steam, spin, spark. The attractions: zero carbon while running, power all day and all night, no ash heaps, and uranium mined at home. The warnings: the most expensive, slowest construction on earth — South Africa's plan for a new reactor fleet died of its own price tag; waste that stays lethal for millennia, parked at Koeberg and at Vaalputs in the Northern Cape; and accidents that almost never happen but change history when they do.

So the verdict to write at the fork: no single road works — coal is a countdown, nuclear is clean but slow and costly — so the country must drive several roads at once, a mix, and the solar-and-wind lane of that mix is precisely where the next session begins.
