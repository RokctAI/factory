Hey, it's Bianca — turbines, kilovolts, reactors: this session sounds like it belongs in an engineering degree, but honestly it is three pictures. A kettle, two drawers and a fork in the road. Hold those and every mark follows.

The kettle: coal milled finer than flour burns at one thousand four hundred degrees, boiling water inside kilometres of tubes in the furnace walls. The steam spins a turbine three thousand times a minute, the turbine spins a magnet inside copper coils — and that is the whole secret of a generator. Transformers push the voltage up to four hundred thousand volts because power leaks less on a long journey at high pressure. Say the chain out loud: chemical, heat, spin, spark. Two favourites for the final exams: the curved towers make clouds of water vapour, not smoke, and only about ONE THIRD of the coal's energy becomes electricity.

The two drawers: what coal paid us — cheap power that built industry, day-and-night baseload, about ninety thousand pay cheques, Richards Bay exports — and what it still owes: Africa's heaviest carbon emissions, the Highveld's dirty air, acid mine drainage bleeding for a century, maize land lost to pits, load-shedding. Emalahleni sits in both drawers. Always open both.

The fork: coal-only is a countdown — old fleet, no finance, carbon tolls. The detour is Koeberg: two reactors near Cape Town since 1984, a thumbnail pellet worth a tonne of coal, clean but the slowest, priciest build there is, with waste parked at Vaalputs. Verdict every time: the mix.

Kettle, drawers, fork — now go prove it on the practice questions. You are more ready than you feel!
