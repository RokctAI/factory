**Bianca:** Let me start with the one my class always argues about. A pumped storage scheme like Palmiet or Ingula gives back less electricity than it swallows — so why would anyone spend billions building a machine that loses energy on purpose?

**Tutor:** Because the price of electricity is not one number — it is a tide that rises and falls twice a day, and pumped storage is built to surf it. At two in the morning the country is asleep, but the coal fleet cannot simply be switched off like a bedside lamp; boilers that take hours to heat and cool are left running, producing power almost nobody is buying. That midnight electricity is nearly worthless. At six in the evening, every kettle, geyser and stove in the country switches on within the same hour, and electricity becomes precious exactly when the grid is most stressed. Pumped storage buys low and sells high: worthless midnight power lifts the water; priceless evening power comes back down the penstock. Yes, roughly a fifth to a quarter of the energy is lost in the round trip — but compare the alternative, which is building whole extra power stations that would sit idle for twenty-two hours a day just to cover the evening rush. There is a second job too, and it may matter even more: a pumped storage turbine can go from standing still to full output in a few minutes, while a coal boiler needs most of a day. When a big unit trips somewhere on the grid, Ingula is the paramedic that arrives first. So write it this way: pumped storage is not a source, it is a battery and a shock absorber — the service it sells is timing, and timing is worth more than the energy it costs.

**Bianca:** Next one: the lesson keeps repeating that only about a third of the coal's energy becomes electricity. That sounds like terrible engineering. Where do the missing two thirds actually go, and why has nobody fixed it in a hundred years?

**Tutor:** Trace the energy and you find the loss is a law of nature wearing overalls. Small leaks first: hot flue gases carry some heat up the stack, and a little leaves in the ash — a few percent between them. The great loss happens at the cold end. For the turbine to keep spinning, the steam leaving it must be condensed back into water, and condensing steam means deliberately dumping its leftover heat into the air — that is the entire job of those huge curved towers. Here is the deep reason you cannot skip that step: a steam cycle only produces work from the difference in temperature between its hot side and its cold side. Think of heat as water and the turbine as a waterwheel — a waterwheel needs the water to fall somewhere lower, and if you dam up the bottom, the wheel stops. Physics puts a hard ceiling on how much of the heat flowing between two temperatures can ever become work, and a real coal station, at around thirty-five percent or a bit more, already sits close beneath that ceiling. Engineers do claw at it — superheating steam beyond five hundred degrees, the supercritical boilers at Medupi and Kusile — and each trick buys a few points. In cold countries the reject heat is piped into homes as district heating, but our cities were never plumbed for it. So the mark-earning sentence is this: the cooling towers are not a design mistake to be fixed; they are the rent the steam cycle pays for running at all.

**Bianca:** Here is the uncomfortable one. Given everything in the bills drawer — the air, the acid water, the carbon — why does South Africa not simply shut the coal stations down now? What is the honest essay answer?

**Tutor:** The honest answer refuses both easy slogans and holds four truths at once. Truth one: dependence. Eight in every ten units of our electricity are coal; there is no spare fleet waiting in a warehouse, so an immediate shutdown means a dark country — and the first casualties would be hospitals, water pumps and the poorest households, not the polluters. Truth two: livelihoods. Around ninety thousand mining jobs, plus the station crews, the rail workers and the municipal budgets of towns like Emalahleni that are funded by coal rates and wages — switch it off overnight and you decree a regional depression. Truth three: the bills are just as real. The Highveld's lungs, the acid seeping toward the Olifants, the carbon charges gathering at foreign borders — delay is not free either; it simply mails the invoice to the people living nearest the stacks. Truth four: sequencing exists. Old stations can retire on a published schedule while renewables are built at speed, workers are retrained and coal towns are helped to diversify — that is what a just transition means, and international funding packages have been negotiated to pay for exactly this. So structure the essay as a rejection of both extremes: shutting down tomorrow confuses the destination with the first step, and running forever pretends the bills drawer is empty. The defensible thesis is a managed countdown — deliberate, dated and funded — fast enough for the climate and trade deadlines, slow enough to keep the lights on and the towns alive. An answer built that way earns its marks on both sides of the argument.

**Bianca:** Last question, on the case study. If Koeberg has run clean, steady baseload for forty years, why did the plan to build a whole new fleet of reactors fall apart?

**Tutor:** Because when the two columns of the case study were finally weighed against a national budget, the disadvantage column won — and narrating that weighing is the best answer you can give. The attraction column is genuine: four decades of carbon-free baseload anchoring the Western Cape, fuel so dense that a thumbnail pellet equals a tonne of coal, uranium mined at home. On those facts alone, more reactors look obvious. Now the other column. Nuclear stations are the most expensive machines a country can commission — the proposed fleet was priced around a trillion rand at a moment when the treasury was already stretched, and the courts separately found the procurement process unlawful, a governance failure worth naming in any answer. Time deepens the cost: a reactor takes a decade or more to build, so the billions are spent years before a single unit of electricity flows — while a wind or solar farm signed on the same day is generating within about two years, at prices that fell throughout the debate. Add the waste that must sit at Vaalputs for thousands of years and an accident risk that is tiny in probability but continental in consequence, and the arithmetic closed the case. Notice what the syllabus is really teaching here: a technology can be genuinely clean and genuinely unaffordable at the same time — advantage lists are not decoration, they are weighed, and the weighing produces a decision. Koeberg itself gets refits to extend its life, because keeping a reactor you own is far cheaper than building one you do not. And the destination is the one this whole session keeps arriving at: no single source wins — the mix does.
