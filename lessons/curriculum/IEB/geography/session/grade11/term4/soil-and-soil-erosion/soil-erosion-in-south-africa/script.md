# Part 1 — Expert

The resources theme opens with the resource beneath every other one: soil. This session measures how slowly soil is built and how quickly it is stripped; sorts the causes of erosion into human, animal and physical, reaching into both past and present; reads the evidence and effects across South Africa; sets out the management strategies that prevent and control the damage; and closes with the GIS toolkit the syllabus attaches to this topic.

## Subtopic: Soil: The Living Skin That Feeds the Country

SOIL is far more than powdered rock. It is a living blend of mineral grains weathered from the parent rock below, decayed organic matter called HUMUS, water, air and an unseen workforce of billions of organisms, stacked in layers called HORIZONS: the dark, humus-charged TOPSOIL at the surface, the paler and poorer SUBSOIL under it, then broken weathered rock fading into solid parent material. Almost everything a plant needs lives in that thin dark top layer — the humus banks nutrients, holds moisture, and glues loose particles into stable crumbs that resist washing away.

One number powers the entire topic: the rate of formation. Under our conditions, building a few centimetres of topsoil can demand several hundred years of patient weathering, root growth, decay and burrowing. Yet one violent thunderstorm over a bare, sloping field can remove more than that before sunset. On any human timescale, therefore, soil behaves as a NON-RENEWABLE resource — and every argument in this session grows out of that brutal imbalance.

South Africa's stakes are national. Only around 12 per cent of the country's surface is arable — capable of supporting crops — and scarcely 3 per cent counts as genuinely high-potential; the remainder is too dry, too steep or too shallow. A nation feeding sixty million people from so narrow a fertile strip cannot afford to lose it, yet the estimated national loss runs to several hundred million tonnes of topsoil every year, much of it riding the rivers to the ocean.

Fix one process image before moving on: vegetation is the soil's bodyguard. Leaves absorb the punch of falling raindrops, roots lace the crumbs together, and dead plant litter feeds the humus that binds the whole layer. Nearly every cause of erosion you will ever name does its work by dismissing that bodyguard.

The questions for this section are ready: the horizons, the formation-against-loss imbalance, and the arable percentages.

## Subtopic: Causes of Soil Erosion: Human, Animal and Physical

The syllabus arranges the causes into three families and asks you to reach into both past and present.

PHYSICAL causes are the erosive agents themselves. WATER works through an escalating sequence: SPLASH erosion, where raindrops strike bare ground and knock particles loose; SHEET erosion, a thin even film of runoff skimming topsoil off the whole surface; RILL erosion, where the sheet gathers into small cuttable channels; and finally GULLY erosion, when unchecked rills deepen into the steep-sided dongas that scar so many South African hillsides. WIND erosion lifts fine, dry soil off bare fields — the lesson the ploughed-out American plains taught the world in the Dust Bowl of the 1930s. South Africa is physically primed for erosion before anyone touches it: steep escarpment slopes, violent summer thunderstorms over the interior, recurring drought, and naturally thin cover. But carry the crucial nuance: physical agents usually complete what land use begins — bare ground is the precondition, and bare ground is almost always manufactured.

HUMAN causes are the manufacturers. OVERCULTIVATION mines the humus out of fields planted season after season without rest or replenishment. Ploughing straight up and down a slope converts every furrow into a stormwater drain. DEFORESTATION — for timber, firewood and land clearing — removes the bodyguard wholesale, and dependence on household firewood keeps rural woodlands under steady pressure. Mining strips cover and leaves dumps that wash and blow. Construction and the growth of informal settlements skin the ground raw on the edges of towns. History is also a cause: the homeland system of the apartheid era packed too many people and livestock onto the smallest, poorest land, engineering the overuse whose scars still run through the former Transkei and Ciskei districts today. And the present keeps pressing: poverty, insecure land tenure and population growth continue to push marginal land past its limits.

ANIMAL causes revolve around OVERGRAZING — more mouths than the grass can regrow for, until the surface lies exposed — and TRAMPLING, which compacts soil along stock paths so rain can no longer soak in and must run off instead. The daily cattle route down to the drinking point, beaten hard as concrete, is often the very first channel a future gully will follow.

Work through this section's questions now: the four-step water sequence, one cause from each family, and the past-and-present framing.

## Subtopic: Evidence and Effects Across South Africa

The evidence is carved into the landscape. DONGAS — raw, steep-walled gullies, some deep enough to hide a truck — slice through parts of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal's Thukela basin and Limpopo's Sekhukhune district, most densely in the former homeland areas where overuse was imposed by the map. Rivers turn chocolate-brown after storms — that colour is topsoil in transit. Dams fill with mud: reservoirs around the country have surrendered serious storage capacity to sediment, and the Hazelmere Dam on the Mdloti River in KwaZulu-Natal is a striking case, its basin so invaded by silt that engineers had to raise the dam wall to win back the lost storage. Add the smaller signatures: dust plumes streaming off dry, bare fields; plant roots left standing naked above the surface; fence posts perched on little pedestals of soil after the wind has stripped the ground around them.

Now the effects, which the syllabus wants sorted onto PEOPLE and the ENVIRONMENT.

On people: yields fall as the fertile layer thins, shrinking farm incomes and nudging food prices upward; degraded grazing and cropland dissolve rural livelihoods and push families toward the towns; silt shortens the working life of the dams that store water for cities and irrigation, a water-security bill every user helps pay; and the fertility that washed away for free must be bought back, expensively, in fertiliser bags.

On the environment: sediment chokes rivers and wetlands, smothering the life on their beds; land that once soaked up rain and released it slowly loses that sponge function, so floods spike higher and dry-season flows fall lower; degraded veld supports thinner biodiversity; and at the far end of the process, erosion crosses into DESERTIFICATION — the concept from earlier in the year returning — where once-productive land becomes effectively barren.

The sentence worth memorising: erosion is a disaster in slow motion — no single storm makes the news, yet the accumulating loss undermines food security, water security and rural livelihoods all at once.

Attend to this section's questions: three pieces of evidence with named places, and paired effects on people and environment.

## Subtopic: Management, and the GIS Toolkit for Soil

Management divides into PREVENTION — keeping soil covered and anchored so damage never starts — and CONTROL, repairing the wounds already open.

Prevention on croplands: CONTOUR PLOUGHING lays the furrows along the slope's contour lines so each ridge acts as a miniature dam instead of a drain; STRIP CROPPING alternates bands of dense cover crops with row crops so any soil that moves is caught within metres; CROP ROTATION and rest seasons rebuild the humus; MULCHING and retained stubble keep a blanket on the field through the dry months so the ground is never naked; and planted windbreak lines take the sting out of erosive wind. On grazing land: hold stock numbers at or below the veld's CARRYING CAPACITY, rotate herds between camps so each pasture recovers, and spread out the watering routes so trampling never concentrates into one hardened path.

Control where the gullies already run: low check dams and GABIONS — wire cages packed with rock — placed across the channel to trip the floodwater, slow it and force it to drop its sediment load, gradually raising the gully floor; grasses and tough shrubs planted on the raw banks to knit them closed; and fences to keep livestock off land that is healing. National programmes scale the work up: LandCare organises farm-level conservation, Working for Water clears invasive alien plants whose thirst and shade crowd out the binding groundcover, and Working on Fire defends the vegetation shield itself.

The syllabus ends the topic with GIS, because the menders need maps. SPATIALLY REFERENCED DATA is any information anchored to coordinates on the earth's surface — the position of a gully head, a farm boundary, a rain gauge reading tagged with its station's location. Much of it arrives by remote sensing, and two RESOLUTIONS describe any sensor: SPATIAL resolution is the ground size of a single pixel — a ten-metre pixel can see a field but not a furrow — while SPECTRAL resolution lists the wavelength bands the sensor records; healthy vegetation reflects infrared light strongly, so the infrared bands expose bare or stressed ground long before an ordinary photograph would. Inside the GIS, features are stored three ways: POINT data for a borehole or a gully head; LINE data for a river, a contour or a fence; and AREA, or polygon, data for a field, a catchment or an erosion-risk zone. Stack the slope, rainfall, soil and vegetation layers on top of one another and the GIS draws the map of concentrated risk — telling the teams exactly where the first gabions belong.

## Subtopic: The Bank Account That Fills by Teaspoons

Imagine topsoil as a savings account with a strange rule: deposits arrive at one teaspoon a year. Rock crumbling, leaves rotting, earthworms turning the dark layer — centuries of patient saving to build a few centimetres of fertile ground. Then picture a single thunderstorm over a bare, ploughed slope making a withdrawal: decades of savings gone brown down the river in one afternoon. Teaspoon deposits, bucket withdrawals — that is the whole soil crisis in six words, and it is why geographers file soil under non-renewable: what you lose today will not return in your lifetime, or your grandchildren's.

Now weigh the stakes for this country. Draw South Africa as ten plates on a table: only about one plate stands on land good enough for crops, and just a corner of that plate is truly first-class. Sixty million people eat from that single plate — while the country sheds hundreds of millions of tonnes of topsoil a year. The plate itself is washing toward the sea.

The last piece of the picture: the bodyguard. Every tuft of grass is security hired to protect the account — leaves break the raindrops' punch, roots grip the crumbs, litter feeds the glue. Hold that image and every cause of erosion collapses into one sentence: somebody, somehow, fired the bodyguard.

Good going — the questions for this piece are with you now: teaspoons and buckets, one plate in ten, and the bodyguard.

## Subtopic: How a Footpath Becomes a Canyon

Erosion follows a script, and any overstocked camp will perform it for you.

Act one: too many cattle, too little grass — overgrazing — and a bare patch appears. Act two: rain arrives on the naked ground like a hail of tiny hammers, each drop splashing particles loose. Act three: the trampled, compacted surface refuses to drink, so the water travels — first as a thin silent sheet that shaves the whole surface a millimetre at a time. Act four: the sheet organises itself into finger-width channels — rills — shallow enough to step over and easy to ignore. Act five: the rills merge and bite deeper with every storm, until one season the farmer finds a DONGA across the camp — a raw canyon with vertical walls, eating the field that fed a household. And the path the herd walked to water every day, packed hard as a stoep floor? It was the original drain. A great many dongas were born as footpaths.

Wind performs a parallel script on dry, bare fields: it lifts the finest, most fertile grains first and abandons the coarse sand — the plot of America's Dust Bowl in the 1930s, staged wherever soil is left naked and dry.

One further act, because history acts in South Africa's version: the apartheid homeland map squeezed people and cattle onto the smallest, weakest land. Too many hooves on too few hectares was written into law — which is why the deepest dongas today run through the former Transkei and Ciskei and the crowded districts of the old homelands. A past cause wearing a present scar.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the five acts in order, the footpath clue, and history's part in the script.

## Subtopic: Stitching the Land Back Together

A torn shirt never mends itself; a needle has to pass through it. Damaged land obeys the same rule, and each stitch has a name.

To prevent new tears, keep the cloth covered. Plough ALONG the slope — contour ploughing — so every furrow works as a little dam instead of a downhill drain. Never leave a field undressed between seasons: mulch, stubble and cover crops are its winter blanket. Rotate the crops so the humus glue can re-set. With livestock, carry only what the veld can feed — its carrying capacity — and walk the herd from camp to camp so every pasture takes its turn to rest.

Where a donga already yawns, the stitching means slowing the water. Gabions — wire cages packed with rock — go across the channel like speed bumps: each flood trips over them, drops its cargo of soil, and season by season the canyon floor climbs until grass can anchor it again. Plant the raw walls, fence out the animals, and give the wound years. National teams work this needle at scale — LandCare sewing the farm-level stitches, Working for Water uprooting the thirsty alien trees that shove aside the binding grass, Working on Fire guarding the green cover itself.

And how do the menders choose where to stitch first? They look down from orbit. Satellite readings arrive pinned to coordinates — spatially referenced data. Every sensor has a pixel size — spatial resolution, the smallest thing it can see — and a set of light bands — spectral resolution; ailing or absent vegetation betrays itself in infrared, so bare ground glows guilty from space. Inside the digital map, a gully head is a point, a river is a line, a risk zone is an area — and stacking the slope, rainfall and soil layers reveals precisely which valley needs its gabions before the next storm arrives.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the stitches for fields and herds, the speed bumps in the donga, and the view from orbit matched to its terms. Hold these and you carry both the wound and its cure.
