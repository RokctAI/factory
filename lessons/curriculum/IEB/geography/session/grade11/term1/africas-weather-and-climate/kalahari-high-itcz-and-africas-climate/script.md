# Part 1 — Expert

The global machine is built; now aim it at Africa. Because the continent straddles the equator, it hosts the equatorial low, both subtropical high belts and two contrasting ocean systems at the same time. Four instalments explain its weather: convergence and subsidence as the master switch between rain and drought; the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone that tows the rainy season up and down the continent; the Kalahari High and the two currents that decide which South African coast turns green and which turns to gravel; and the synoptic chart and satellite image on which the whole story is read at a glance.

## Subtopic: Subsidence, Convergence and the Rain Switch

Whether any African location is wet or dry reduces to one question: is the air over it going up or coming down?

CONVERGENCE happens where surface winds flow toward one another or into a low-pressure cell. Air piling in from the sides has a single exit — UPWARD. As it rises it expands into thinner surroundings and cools adiabatically, its vapour condenses into deep cumulus and cumulonimbus, and convectional rain follows. Where convergence rules, the land is green: the Congo Basin, the Guinea coast of West Africa, the summer interior of southern Africa.

SUBSIDENCE is the mirror image: air descending from aloft and fanning outward at the surface as DIVERGENCE. Descent compresses and warms the air adiabatically, relative humidity plunges, cloud dissolves and rain becomes nearly impossible. Subsidence also manufactures a TEMPERATURE INVERSION — a layer in which temperature climbs with height — and that warm layer behaves as a ceiling, stopping surface thermals long before they can build cloud. Where subsidence rules, the land is brown: the Sahara beneath the northern subtropical high, the Kalahari and the Namib beneath the southern one.

So the climate map of Africa is one switch in two positions. The equatorial belt sits under permanent convergence and stays wet through the year. The belts near 30 degrees north and south sit under permanent subsidence and hold the deserts. Everything in between is wet only when the rising side of the machine pays a visit — and the visitor has a name, which is the next section.

The questions for this section are ready: convergence and subsidence each linked to their weather, the inversion explained as a ceiling, and the continent's wet and dry belts placed by latitude.

## Subtopic: The ITCZ — the Rain Belt That Migrates

The INTER-TROPICAL CONVERGENCE ZONE — the ITCZ — is the trough of low pressure where the south-east trade winds of the southern hemisphere collide with the north-east trades of the northern hemisphere. The colliding trades drive air upward on a continental scale, so on satellite imagery the ITCZ shows as a ragged girdle of enormous thunderstorm cloud wound around the tropics.

The girdle does not stay put. It tracks the overhead sun with a lag of about a month, because the trades converge wherever surface heating is fiercest. In January, with the overhead sun south of the equator, the ITCZ lies across southern Africa — draped over Angola, Zambia and Zimbabwe and brushing South Africa's far northern districts — and this is the engine room of the southern summer rains. By July the belt has crossed the equator and settled near 15 to 20 degrees north over the Sahel, delivering the wet season to Senegal, Mali, Niger and northern Nigeria while the southern half of the continent stands in dry winter sunshine.

That single annual journey writes the rainfall calendar for the entire continent. Places close to the equator — the Congo Basin, southern Uganda — are crossed TWICE a year, once on the belt's way north and once on its way south, and so record a DOUBLE RAINFALL MAXIMUM. Places out near the tropics — Zimbabwe on one side, the Sahel on the other — are reached only once, at the belt's furthest stretch, and record a SINGLE summer maximum followed by a long dry season. And precisely because these outer places rely on the belt's maximum reach, a season in which it stalls or arrives feebly is the margin between a full granary and a failed one, in Harare and in Timbuktu alike.

Take this section's questions now: the ITCZ defined by its converging trades, its January and July positions located, and double versus single rainfall maxima explained by counting crossings.

## Subtopic: The Kalahari High and the Oceans on Either Side

Now the home chapter. Three semi-permanent anticyclones surround South Africa — the South Atlantic High off the west coast, the South Indian High off the east coast, and the KALAHARI HIGH parked over the interior plateau. The Kalahari High is the continental limb of the subtropical high-pressure belt, and its seasonal rhythm conducts the country's rainfall.

In WINTER the high is at full strength and clamps onto the plateau. Its subsidence drives a hard inversion that lies BELOW the crest of the escarpment, so moist maritime air battering at the edges cannot climb onto the plateau at all. The result is the trademark plateau winter: cloudless days, icy nights, not a drop of rain from Polokwane to Bloemfontein. In SUMMER furious surface heating undermines the anticyclone, the inversion floats up ABOVE the escarpment crest, and Indian Ocean moisture pours over the eastern rim onto the plateau — the feedstock of the afternoon thunderstorms that hammer Mpumalanga, Gauteng and the Free State from November to March. One anticyclone, breathing seasonally, explains dry winters and wet summers across the whole interior.

The oceans complete the mechanism. Down the west coast creeps the cold BENGUELA CURRENT, its surface water dragged up from depth by upwelling. Air lying on cold water is cooled from below, gathers almost no vapour and becomes stubbornly stable; the most it can offer the shore is fog. Hence the Namib and the parched diamond coast — Alexander Bay measures its rain in spoonfuls. Down the east coast runs the warm MOZAMBIQUE-AGULHAS system. Air lying on warm water is heated from below, loads up with evaporated moisture and turns unstable — spring-loaded to rise and rain. Hence the cane fields and humidity of the KwaZulu-Natal coast: Richards Bay, at almost exactly Alexander Bay's latitude, is soaked, green and sticky. Nearly the same latitude, opposite currents, opposite worlds.

Work through the questions for this section: the Kalahari High's winter and summer behaviour with the inversion against the escarpment, and each coastal current tied to the climate it manufactures.

## Subtopic: Synoptic Charts, Satellites and El Niño

A SYNOPTIC WEATHER MAP freezes the atmosphere over a region at a single moment, and learning to read one is a core skill for your final exams. Follow a fixed routine. Start with the ISOBARS, drawn every four hectopascals: a closed cell marked H is an anticyclone — subsiding air, settled weather; a cell marked L is a low — rising air, cloud and rain; crowded isobars mean strong wind. Locate the three regulars — South Atlantic High, South Indian High, Kalahari High — then hunt for a COLD FRONT, the line of triangles pointing the way the front is travelling, curling up toward the country from the south-west with rain, a wind swing and a sharp temperature drop on its heels.

Next the STATION MODEL, the tight cluster of symbols plotted at each reporting station: air temperature at the upper left, dew-point temperature at the lower left, a circle shaded according to cloud cover, and a wind shaft flying FROM the direction the wind comes from, its feathers counting speed at ten knots per full feather. Drill it until a station reads like a sentence — Upington, 34 degrees, dew point 6, skies clear, light north-easterly.

Satellites extend the chart from above. Visible and infrared images show the ITCZ as its band of storm cloud, a cold front as a long curved bar of cloud, and the cloudless void of subsidence sitting over the interior in winter. Layering these views in a GIS with rainfall figures, dam levels and vegetation indices lets forecasters and farm advisers watch a wet or dry season develop district by district and act early.

One global driver rounds out the picture as enrichment: EL NIÑO and LA NIÑA. When the eastern Pacific runs unusually warm — El Niño — the global circulation reorganises and southern Africa's summer rains usually shrink; the El Niño of 2023-24 seared the region's midsummer, cutting harvests across Zambia, Zimbabwe and South Africa. When the same ocean runs cool — La Niña — our summers usually turn generous, as the drenched seasons at the start of the 2020s showed.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: highs, lows, fronts and isobars read off the chart, the station model decoded in order, and satellite cloud patterns matched to rising and sinking air.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole continent again — this time with a duvet, a hot-air balloon, a municipal water truck and two pots on the stove. Same weather, same answers.

## Subtopic: The Duvet and the Hot-Air Balloon

Hold the whole topic in two bedroom-and-birthday pictures. A hot-air balloon rises because the air inside it is warmer and lighter than the air around it — that is convergence country: heat the ground, let air flood in from the sides, and it has nowhere to go but up. Up-going air makes cloud and rain. Now picture a thick duvet being pressed down slowly over the bed. That is a subsiding high: a mass of air sinking from above and squashing everything under it. Nothing rises through a descending duvet — no lift, no cloud, no rain.

Stretch those pictures across Africa. Over the Congo Basin the burner is on full: air converges and balloons upward, and it rains nearly every afternoon of the year. Near 30 degrees on either side, the air that went up comes back down — the duvet — and beneath the two duvets lie the Sahara on one side, the Kalahari and Namib on the other. A balloon rising in the middle, duvets pressed down on both flanks: that is the continent's climate map from memory.

The duvet has a technical name worth using: a temperature inversion. Air normally cools as you climb, but under a sinking high there is a layer where it WARMS with height instead. Warm sitting on top of cool is upside-down and perfectly stable — a bubble of warm ground air rises, meets that warmer layer, finds itself the cooler and heavier one, and simply stops. In winter you can watch the proof over any plateau town: smoke climbs, hits the invisible ceiling and flattens out sideways like a table top. No bubble gets through, so no cloud forms, and the sky stays blue for weeks.

Good going — this section's questions are with you: the balloon, the duvet, and the flattened smoke that betrays the inversion.

## Subtopic: The Water Truck on Its Route

Now for the rain belt, and a municipal picture. Imagine one enormous water truck that drives a single north-south road across Africa, spraying rain as it goes. The truck is the ITCZ — the band where the two hemispheres' trade winds crash together and hurl air upward into thunderstorms. Wherever the truck is parked, it is pouring.

The truck keeps a strict timetable: it chases the overhead sun, running about a month behind it. In our summer, January, it parks at the southern end of its route, hosing Angola, Zambia and Zimbabwe and splashing the far north of South Africa. In July it has driven to the far northern end, watering the Sahel on the desert's doorstep, while everything down here waits, dry, in the winter sun.

Your rainfall calendar is simply how often the truck passes your gate. Live at the middle of the route — the Congo Basin, southern Uganda — and it passes twice every year, once northbound and once southbound: two rainy seasons. Live near either end of the route — Zimbabwe, or Mali — and the truck barely reaches you once before turning back: one rainy season and a long dry wait. Now feel the danger in that arrangement: if the truck runs late one year, or turns around early, the end-of-route customers get nothing at all. That is what a tropical drought usually is — not a broken sky, just a truck that never quite arrived. Whole harvests in the Sahel and in southern Africa hang on the last fifty kilometres of that route.

Nearly there — your questions for this part are ready: the truck's January and July stops, the twice-a-year middle, and the once-a-year ends of the route.

## Subtopic: Two Pots and the Weather Map

Why is Richards Bay green and muggy while Alexander Bay, at nearly the same latitude, is bare desert? Stand at a stove with two pots. One simmers gently; the other is full of iced water. Hold your hand over each: the simmering pot loads the air above it with vapour that climbs at your palm; the iced pot leaves the air above it cool, dry and dead still. Now stretch the pots into ocean currents. The east coast runs on the simmering pot — the warm Mozambique-Agulhas water — so the air above it is damp and eager to rise, and it rains on the cane fields. The west coast runs on the iced pot — the cold Benguela — so the air above it is chilled, heavy and dry, managing nothing better than morning fog on the diamond coast. Same sun overhead; the pot below decides everything.

The interior answers to the Kalahari High, and its inversion works like a security gate at the top of the escarpment. In winter the high is strong and the gate is bolted BELOW the rim: ocean air cannot get up onto the plateau, and the Highveld runs cloudless, freezing and dry. In summer the baking plateau weakens the high and the gate lifts ABOVE the rim: moist Indian Ocean air streams over the edge, and by mid-afternoon the anvil clouds are queuing over Gauteng. The interior's whole year is that gate going up and down.

Last skill, and it is pure translation: the weather map says all of this in symbols. H is a duvet — sunshine, no rain. L is a balloon — cloud and rain on the way. Isobars packed tight mean wind. A line of little triangles is a cold front, and the triangles point where it is heading — usually at the south coast, out of the south-west. At each town, the number top-left is the temperature, the number below it the dew point, the shaded circle the cloud, and the feathered tail flies FROM the wind's direction. And when the news says El Niño, hear "warm Pacific, thin summer rains"; when it says La Niña, hear "cool Pacific, fat wet summers".

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the two pots, the gate on the escarpment, and a chart read symbol by symbol. Master these and every satellite loop and weather map you ever meet is already half-read.
