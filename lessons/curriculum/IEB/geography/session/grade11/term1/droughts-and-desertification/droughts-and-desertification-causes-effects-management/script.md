# Part 1 — Expert

The circulation lessons showed why the rains sometimes stay away; this session follows what happens when they stay away for seasons on end, and what people do to the land in the meantime. Four instalments: drought defined with precision and traced to its natural and human causes; desertification, the human-driven degradation that drought unmasks; the costs that both impose on environments and communities from the Sahel to the Karoo; and the management toolkit — forecasting, water discipline and land repair — that turns a recurring hazard into something a country can survive.

## Subtopic: Drought — Definitions and Causes

A DROUGHT is an extended stretch of rainfall far below the average that a particular place normally receives and has built its life around. Every word of that matters. Springbok functions perfectly well on roughly 200 millimetres a year; hand that total to Mbombela, which expects nearer 750, and you have a disaster. Drought is therefore always measured against the LOCAL normal — a definition that says below-normal rainfall, never merely low rainfall.

Geographers track drought through four deepening stages. METEOROLOGICAL drought is the shortfall itself: a season, or several, delivering well under the expected rain. Let it continue and AGRICULTURAL drought sets in — soil moisture drops beneath what crops and veld need, plantings wilt and grazing thins. Persist further and HYDROLOGICAL drought appears, the shortage reaching the storage system: rivers dwindle, dam levels slide, boreholes chase a sinking water table. The terminal stage is SOCIO-ECONOMIC drought, when supply can no longer meet human demand and the shortage starts writing prices, unemployment figures and migration statistics. One poor season is meteorological; a third poor season in a row is usually all four at once.

The causes begin with the circulation already in your toolkit. Southern Africa lives on the subsiding flank of the subtropical high-pressure belt, so any season in which the sinking strengthens — an ITCZ that halts short of its southern limit, a continental high that will not lift — arrives dry. EL NIÑO stacks the odds: when the eastern Pacific runs warm, the global circulation rearranges and our summers usually shrink, as the 2023-24 event proved by scorching midsummer harvests across Zambia, Zimbabwe and South Africa. CLIMATE CHANGE loads the dice further, raising temperatures, accelerating evaporation and making rainfall swing harder between extremes. Human pressure then converts shortfall into crisis: growing cities and thirsty crops push demand upward, leaking mains squander supply, and stripped catchments shed water in floods instead of banking it.

Take this section's questions now: drought anchored to the local normal, the four stages in their deepening order, and natural causes distinguished from human amplifiers.

## Subtopic: Desertification — the Land Degradation Disease

DESERTIFICATION is the degradation of land in arid and semi-arid regions until it behaves like desert — soil stripped of vegetation, structure and nutrients until it can neither hold water nor grow cover. It is not a desert marching outward like a wall of dunes; it is productive land dying in patches, almost always where human pressure bears down hardest. Drought reveals desertification, but people manufacture most of it.

Recite the causal chain. OVERGRAZING: herds too large for the veld bite the grass down faster than it regrows, while hooves compact the soil so rain sheets off instead of soaking in. OVER-CULTIVATION: the same lands ploughed and cropped without rest or replenishment until the soil's nutrients and crumb structure are spent. DEFORESTATION: trees and shrubs taken for fuel and building until the ground stands naked before wind and rain. POOR IRRIGATION: water splashed liberally in hot climates evaporates and leaves its salts behind — SALINISATION — until the ground whitens and nothing germinates. Behind all four sits POPULATION PRESSURE, more people needing food, fuel and grazing from the same fragile margin, usually because poverty offers no alternative, with climate change turning up the heat on everything.

Once the cover is gone, the physics runs by itself. Bare soil crusts and bakes; rain that should infiltrate races off in flash floods, slicing open gullies — DONGAS — and freighting the topsoil away; wind lifts the loosened fines into dust plumes. Thinner soil regrows less cover, which bares more soil, which erodes faster: a positive feedback spiralling downhill, moving at the pace of seasons rather than centuries. The world's cautionary landscape is the SAHEL, the semi-arid ribbon along the Sahara's southern rim, degraded across vast areas by grazing pressure, fuelwood cutting and repeated drought. South Africa runs its own versions: national land-degradation surveys flag much of the country as vulnerable, and the symptoms — dongas, dust, shaved veld — are plain on the Karoo margins, along the Kalahari fringe and in the crowded communal lands of the old Eastern Cape reserves, where history concentrated too many people and animals on too little ground.

This section's questions are ready: desertification defined as land degradation, the five-link causal chain, and the feedback spiral explained in full.

## Subtopic: Effects on People and the Environment

Start with the environment. Losing VEGETATION triggers the cascade: grazing vanishes, habitats unravel, and biodiversity drains away as animals die or move on. SOILS follow — structure collapses, and wind and water carry off in one season what took a thousand years to form; no human timescale rebuilds it. WATER systems fail from the sky downward: rivers turn to sand, wetlands crack, dams sink toward their dead storage and boreholes are drilled ever deeper after a retreating water table. Even the atmosphere joins in, as dust storms carry the lost land across provinces.

The human costs follow the water down. FOOD SECURITY breaks first: harvests fail, herds die, and staple prices climb steepest for the households least able to pay — the 2023-24 El Niño season forced disaster declarations across several neighbouring countries as the region's maize withered. LIVELIHOODS go next: seasonal farm work evaporates, debts compound, and rural families dispatch members to the cities in the quiet, steady flow of DROUGHT MIGRATION that swells informal settlements. HEALTH deteriorates where clean water runs short and diets thin out. And scarcity sharpens CONFLICT, from quarrels over boreholes and grazing camps to friction between users of shared rivers — the herder-farmer clashes of the Sahel are the continental-scale warning.

South Africa's own recent emergency is the case study to carry: the NELSON MANDELA BAY water crisis. From about 2015 the rains over the Eastern Cape's supply catchments failed season after season, and by the winter of 2022 the metro's supply dams had sunk together toward roughly one-tenth of capacity, with the largest, the Kouga Dam, among the worst. Gqeberha and its neighbours stared at the real prospect of dry taps: officials mapped suburbs for collection points, throttled the mains pressure, hunted the enormous share of water lost to leaks, drilled emergency boreholes and hurried extra capacity from the Nooitgedagt scheme, which treats water transferred from the Orange River. Households were pressed toward fifty litres per person per day. The full failure never quite arrived — repairs, restrictions and, at last, the rains of 2023 pulled the metro back — but the crisis showed a modern city living one season from the bottom of its dams.

Work through the questions for this section: the environmental cascade from vegetation loss, the human costs from food security to migration, and the Nelson Mandela Bay story with its numbers.

## Subtopic: Management — Predicting, Saving and Restoring

Management sorts into three families: see it coming, spend less, and heal the land.

Seeing it coming is EARLY WARNING. Forecasters track rainfall, Pacific sea temperatures and satellite indices of veld condition; a GIS drapes those layers over the map of drought-prone districts so that fodder, tankers and relief budgets move BEFORE the peak instead of after it. Farmers convert warnings into action by DESTOCKING early — selling animals while they still fetch a price — planting drought-tolerant crops and keeping hardy indigenous breeds suited to dry country, and banking fodder in the good seasons.

Spending less is WATER DEMAND MANAGEMENT. Households harvest roof water, reroute greywater to gardens and live within restrictions, while steep block tariffs make the heaviest users carry the cost. Agriculture, the country's largest water consumer, swaps flood furrows for DRIP IRRIGATION that feeds the roots drop by drop, waters at night to beat evaporation and blankets the soil with mulch to hold moisture down where it works. Municipalities chase their leaks — a large slice of urban supply never reaches a tap — and spread their risk across groundwater, re-use and, at the coast, desalination.

Healing the land unpicks the desertification chain link by link. ROTATIONAL GRAZING rests each camp until the grass recovers, with stocking rates pegged to what the veld can actually carry. Croplands rest under ROTATION and cover crops; contour ploughing and terraces slow the water on slopes; check dams in the dongas trap soil and let the gullies heal. Windbreaks and woodlots return trees to the landscape and lift the fuelwood pressure off the veld. South Africa's WORKING FOR WATER programme pays crews to clear water-hungry alien invaders — wattle, pine, eucalyptus — out of catchments, recovering streamflow while creating rural jobs, and LandCare projects rehabilitate eroded communal land district by district. At continental scale the GREAT GREEN WALL carries the same logic across the Sahel from Senegal to Djibouti — not one literal wall of trees, but thousands of village-led restoration projects stitched under a single banner.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: the three families of strategy, concrete measures filed under each, and Working for Water and the Great Green Wall quoted as named examples.

## Subtopic: The Prepaid Data Bundle

Think of a place's rain as a prepaid data bundle that loads once a month. Springbok runs happily on a tiny bundle — every farm, tank and habit there is set to low-data mode. Mbombela runs on a huge one, and its orchards, rivers and towns are built to spend it. So a drought is not a small bundle. A drought is THIS month's bundle loading at a fraction of what THIS account was built around. That is why the definition must say below NORMAL rainfall for that place — the word normal carries the meaning.

Now watch an account fail, in four stages, in order. Stage one: the bundle simply loads short — rain below normal, and that is meteorological drought. Stage two: the everyday apps start failing — the soil dries past what crops and grazing can stand, and that is agricultural drought. Stage three: your saved, offline reserves run out — rivers shrink, dams drop, boreholes dig deeper, and that is hydrological drought. Stage four: the failure reaches your whole life — work, prices, food, moving house — and that is socio-economic drought. Bundle, apps, reserves, life. Four stages, always in that order, because the shortage physically works its way down from the sky to the street.

What shorts the bundle? Mostly the machinery from earlier lessons: we live on the sinking-air side of the planet's circulation, so any year the rain belt stalls short, or the high over the interior refuses to lift, the bundle loads light. An El Niño year loads it lighter still — 2023-24 burnt harvests across the whole region — and climate change makes every month's delivery jumpier. Then people finish the job by spending harder exactly when the bundle shrinks: more taps, thirstier crops, leaking pipes.

Good going — this section's questions are with you: the bundle anchored to its own account, the four stages in order, and what makes a light month load lighter.

## Subtopic: How to Make a Desert

Here is the recipe, cooked by accident all over the world. Take dry, fragile land. Graze too many animals on it, so the grass is bitten and trampled faster than it can regrow. Plough the same lands every year until the soil turns to grey powder. Cut the trees for firewood. If you irrigate, slosh the water around in the heat until the ground crusts white with salt. The land is now bare — and bare is the point of no return.

Every school has the proof in miniature: the goalmouth on the sports field. Where boots pound the same patch every day, the grass gives up; the mud bakes to a tile in the sun; the first storm turns it to a slippery sheet that pours off sideways instead of sinking in; by August the wind is lifting dust off it. Now blow that goalmouth up to the size of a district. Rain bounces off the crusted ground and tears open gullies — dongas — that bleed the topsoil away toward the sea. Wind takes the rest as dust. Less soil grows less grass, which bares more ground, which erodes quicker: the process feeds on its own damage, a spiral with no brakes of its own. That spiral is desertification — not the Sahara on the move, but ordinary land dying patch by patch where it is leaned on hardest.

You need not travel far to see it. The Sahel is the world's warning strip, but the Karoo margins, the Kalahari's edges and the crowded old communal lands of the Eastern Cape show the same worn hills and open gullies, because history parked too many people and animals on too little land and gave them no alternatives.

Nearly there — your questions for this part are ready: the recipe, the goalmouth, and the spiral that keeps feeding itself.

## Subtopic: Gqeberha and the Empty Dams

When drought corners a modern city, it looks like Nelson Mandela Bay. Season after season from about 2015, the rains missed the Eastern Cape catchments that fill the metro's dams. By the winter of 2022 the combined supply had slumped toward one-tenth, the big Kouga Dam was nearly out of the game, and Gqeberha was drawing up maps of water collection points for the day the taps failed. The city fought on every front at once: mains pressure turned down, leak crews chasing the huge share of water that never reached a tap, emergency boreholes, extra Orange River water hurried in through the Nooitgedagt scheme, and residents pushed toward fifty litres per person per day — one filled bath is more than that. The taps, just barely, stayed open until the 2023 rains lifted the siege. Hold both halves of the story: the danger was completely real, and determined management genuinely held it off.

The bigger fight has three moves, and they organise any answer you will ever write. Move one, SEE IT COMING: satellites watch the veld browning, forecasters watch the Pacific, and a GIS shows exactly which districts are sliding — so farmers sell stock early at decent prices, plant drought-hardy crops and bank fodder before the worst. Move two, SPEND LESS: drip lines that feed roots instead of the sky, night watering, mulch over the soil, fixed leaks, harvested rainwater, tariffs that bite the biggest users — and since farming drinks most of the country's water, a small saving in the lands outweighs a big one in the bathroom. Move three, HEAL THE LAND: rest the veld camp by camp, match herds to what the grass can carry, plug the dongas with check dams, plant windbreaks, and clear the alien trees that drink the rivers — that is Working for Water, which turns catchment repair into thousands of jobs. And across the Sahel the same playbook runs continent-sized as the Great Green Wall: thousands of villages re-greening their own ground from Senegal to Djibouti.
