**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one split the class down the middle: is a desert just a drought that never ended? Half of us said the Namib must be the world's longest drought.

**Tutor:** It is a tempting sentence, and it is exactly wrong — separating the two is one of the cleanest tests of understanding in this topic. A desert is a CLIMATE: a place whose NORMAL rainfall is tiny, and whose entire system — vegetation, animals, farming, settlement — is built in equilibrium with that tiny normal. The Namib receiving almost no rain is the Namib functioning exactly as expected; nothing has deviated, so nothing is in drought. A drought is a DEPARTURE: a temporary fall well below whatever a place's own normal happens to be, and it ends when the rains return to that normal. The consequence is that drought can strike anywhere on earth, including the wettest places — if the Congo Basin's rainfall halved for two years, that would be a ferocious drought even though the totals would still dwarf what Springbok sees in a decade. And a desert can, strange as it sounds, have a drought of its own: a run of years when even its meagre expected showers fail, and the desert-adapted plants and animals that budgeted for them begin to die. So anchor both definitions to the word normal: a desert is a low normal; a drought is a broken normal. Write those two sentences side by side and the distinction is permanent.

***

**Bianca:** Next: drought is supposed to be a natural hazard, but the lesson kept saying people deepen it. Can human beings actually cause a drought, or do we only make a natural one worse?

**Tutor:** Keep the two layers separate and the answer organises itself. The rainfall shortfall itself — meteorological drought — is set by the circulation: the subsiding flank of the subtropical belt, a stalling ITCZ, an El Niño year. No amount of human behaviour switches the rain off, and in that strict sense people do not cause drought. But remember that the stages which actually hurt — agricultural, hydrological, socio-economic — are all about whether the shortfall becomes a shortage, and that conversion is substantially human. Consider two towns receiving the identical poor season. The first has matched its demand to its supply, maintains its pipes, protects its catchment vegetation and keeps its dams' capacity in hand; it rides out the season with restrictions and bruises. The second has let demand balloon past what its storage can carry, loses a large share of its water to leaks before it reaches a tap, and has bared its catchments so the little rain that falls races off instead of recharging soil and groundwater; the same meteorological event pushes it into full socio-economic crisis. Same sky, different outcomes — the difference is entirely human. Climate change complicates the neat division further, because emissions are shifting the odds of the shortfalls themselves. So the polished answer runs: nature writes the rainfall, people decide how much disaster each millimetre of shortfall produces, and increasingly people are leaning on the rainfall side of the scale too.

**Bianca:** Third one: why did Nelson Mandela Bay's crisis drag on for seven years when other cities seem to bounce back after one or two bad seasons? What made that drought so stubborn?

**Tutor:** Three ingredients stacked on top of each other, and naming them turns the case study from a story into an analysis. First, the rainfall geography: the metro's catchments sit in a transition zone that depends on a mixture of frontal and cut-off systems, and for season after season the big rain-bearing events simply slid past — a multi-year meteorological failure, not one bad summer. When successive seasons each deliver, say, two-thirds of normal, the deficit compounds, because dams that never refill start each new year lower than the last. Second, the storage arithmetic: reservoirs are a buffer, and a buffer buys you exactly one bad stretch. The first failed seasons were absorbed by dam levels; by the time the public felt urgency, the buffer was spent, and the crisis phase began with the dams already low — that is why hydrological drought always arrives late and quietly. Third, the demand side: a large share of the metro's treated water was escaping through leaks before reaching a tap, which meant the city was effectively fighting the drought with a holed bucket, and fixing mains under crisis conditions is slow, expensive work. Put those together — compounding shortfall, exhausted buffer, leaking system — and you see why recovery needed both the 2023 rains AND the repairs. The transferable lesson for any water question is that a city's real protection is measured in how many failed seasons its storage and its discipline can absorb, and most cities discover that number only by testing it.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the management section, is there a way to stop the three families becoming a list we just recite? We keep writing see it coming, spend less, heal the land and then blanking on the details.

**Tutor:** Attach each family to a person and a decision, and the details regenerate themselves. For seeing it coming, put yourself behind a farmer's kitchen table in September with a warning of a poor season on the radio. What must be decided NOW? Sell a third of the herd while the auction price is still decent — destocking. Choose the drought-tolerant cultivar and the hardy indigenous breed. Fill the fodder shed while feed is cheap. Every early-warning detail is just that table, that month, that choice — and the satellite and GIS exist to make the warning arrive in time for it. For spending less, stand in two queues: the household queue, where the details are the rain tank, the greywater bucket, the tariff that stings the heavy user; and the farm queue, where they are drip lines to the roots, watering after dark, mulch on the soil, and the repaired leak — remembering that because agriculture drinks most of the nation's water, the farm queue is where the big volumes live. For healing the land, walk one degraded valley and fix it feature by feature: rest this camp until the grass returns, set the stocking rate to what the veld can carry, wedge check dams into the donga, plant the windbreak, clear the wattle from the stream — and there is Working for Water, and scaled to a continent, the Great Green Wall. Three people — the forecaster-farmer, the spender, the healer — each making concrete decisions: recall the person and the list writes itself.
