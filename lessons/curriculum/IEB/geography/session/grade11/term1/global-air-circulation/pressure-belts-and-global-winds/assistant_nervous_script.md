Hey, it's Bianca — I know global circulation looks like a wall of vocabulary. Hadley, Ferrel, geostrophic, anabatic, Buys Ballot. Take a breath, because the entire wall stands on two bricks you already own.

Brick one: warm air rises and leaves LOW pressure behind; cold air sinks and stacks up HIGH pressure. Brick two: rising air rains, sinking air shines. Now walk the ladder from the equator to the pole saying only those bricks. Equator — rising, wet. Thirty degrees — sinking, desert. Sixty degrees — rising, cold fronts. Pole — sinking, frozen. Say it twice out loud and the pressure belts are yours for good.

The direction rules are where marks leak, so pin these three to your wall. One: a wind is named for where it comes FROM. Two: in the Southern Hemisphere, moving air bends LEFT. Three: back to the wind, low on your RIGHT. Every direction answer you ever write gets checked against those three lines in ten seconds.

And the calculation habit: the moment a question mentions air going up or down a slope, write the rate before anything else — dry air, about 1 degree per 100 metres; saturated air, about 0,5. Then height divided by 100, times the rate, unit on every line. That is how 1 500 metres of descent becomes 15 degrees of warming and a 29-degree berg-wind day, laid out for full marks.

Two bricks, three direction rules, one lapse-rate habit — that really is the whole topic. Now go and take on the practice questions while it is fresh. You've got this!
