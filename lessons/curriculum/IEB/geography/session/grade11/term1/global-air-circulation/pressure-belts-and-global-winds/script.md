# Part 1 — Expert

The energy budget left the tropics overloaded and the poles starved, and the atmosphere's response is a planet-wide delivery system. This session assembles it piece by piece: the causal chain running from temperature through pressure to wind; the belts of high and low pressure that circle the globe and drift with the overhead sun; the three great cells and the forces that curve every wind travelling inside them; and the air masses, coastal winds and monsoons that bring the whole machine home to South Africa.

## Subtopic: Temperature, Pressure and Wind — the Chain

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the entire column of air standing above a point. It is measured with a barometer in hectopascals, and the mean value at sea level is 1 013 hectopascals.

Temperature starts the chain. Warm air expands, thins out and RISES; with part of the column lifting away, fewer molecules bear down on the surface, so the reading FALLS and a LOW-pressure cell is born. Chilled air does the opposite — it contracts, densifies and SINKS, stacking molecules onto the surface so the reading RISES: a HIGH. At the surface, warm belongs with low and cold belongs with high.

Wind follows: the horizontal flow of air out of high pressure toward low. On a weather chart the pressure pattern is drawn with ISOBARS, lines connecting equal pressure, and they read like contour lines on a relief map. Isobars crowded together signal a steep PRESSURE GRADIENT and a hard blow; isobars spaced far apart signal calm, gentle air.

The weather itself is decided by the vertical motion rather than the horizontal. Inside a low, air is RISING; rising air expands into thinner surroundings and cools adiabatically, its vapour condenses, cloud builds and rain arrives — lows mean unsettled, wet weather. Inside a high, air SUBSIDES; sinking air is squeezed and warms adiabatically, its relative humidity collapses and cloud dissolves — highs mean settled, clear, dry weather. Those two sentences settle more questions in this topic than anything else you will learn.

End with the naming rules, because careless direction costs easy marks. A wind is always named for where it comes FROM: air streaming out of the north-west is a north-westerly. The compass rose is divided into 16 points, and since 360 divided by 16 is 22,5, each point is 22,5 degrees wide. North-east sits at a bearing of 045 degrees, south-east at 135, south-west at 225 and north-west at 315. The blustery wind that follows a Cape cold front, arriving out of the south-west, therefore blows from 225 degrees.

Take this section's questions now: the chain from temperature to pressure to wind, isobar spacing translated into wind strength, and a wind named and converted to its bearing.

## Subtopic: The World's Pressure Belts

Heating varies in orderly fashion with latitude, so pressure does too — arranged in belts that wrap the whole planet.

Over the equator, relentless heating drives air upward day after day: the EQUATORIAL LOW, also known as the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone. Surface winds drift in from both sides and rise, which leaves the horizontal winds weak and fitful — sailors named these calms the doldrums — while towering convectional storms deliver rain almost daily.

Around 30 degrees north and south, the air that climbed at the equator arrives aloft, cooled and heavy, and comes back down: the SUBTROPICAL HIGH-pressure belts. Descending air warms and dries, which is why this latitude hosts the planet's great arid lands — the Sahara in the north, the Namib and Kalahari in the south, the dead heart of Australia. Our own region answers to three semi-permanent anticyclones: the South Atlantic High off the western seaboard, the South Indian High off the eastern seaboard, and the continental high resting on the interior plateau, most muscular in winter.

Around 60 degrees lie the SUB-POLAR LOWS, where mild westerly air runs into frigid polar air along the POLAR FRONT and is forced to climb — the nursery of the travelling depressions whose cold fronts batter the southern coast in winter. Over the poles themselves, intensely cold, dense air settles to form the POLAR HIGHS.

Now the behaviour that explains South Africa. The whole set of belts drifts with the overhead sun, roughly five degrees of latitude each way — northward in our winter, southward in our summer. Come June, the system shifts north: the westerlies and their trains of cold fronts ride up over the south-western corner of the country, soaking the winter-rainfall region, while the continental high squats on the plateau and hands the interior its famous dry, frosty, blue-sky winter. Come December, everything slides south again: the fronts pass mostly out to sea, and moist tropical air pushes deep over the summer-rainfall interior. One slow migration, and one country ends up with two opposite rainfall calendars.

This section's questions are with you now: each belt named with its latitude and vertical motion, and the five-degree migration used to explain winter rain in the south-west against summer rain inland.

## Subtopic: The Tri-cellular Circulation and the Forces That Bend It

Between the equator and each pole, the circulation settles into three linked vertical loops — three conveyor circuits stacked from the tropics to the ice.

The HADLEY CELL runs from the equator to about 30 degrees and is the powerhouse, driven directly by heating. Air rises in the equatorial low, sheds rain and latent heat, glides poleward ten to fifteen kilometres up, cools, and subsides at 30 degrees to feed the subtropical highs, returning along the surface as the steady TRADE winds.

The FERREL CELL occupies 30 to 60 degrees and is the borrowed-motion cell — spun by the two cells flanking it rather than by its own heating. Its surface stream leaves the subtropical high heading poleward as the WESTERLIES, rises at the polar front, and drifts back aloft.

The POLAR CELL completes the set: bitterly cold air subsides over the pole, creeps equatorward along the surface as the POLAR EASTERLIES, and rises where it meets the westerlies at the polar front. Along the boundaries between cells, near the top of the troposphere, race the JET STREAMS — narrow cores of west-to-east wind that regularly pass 200 kilometres per hour.

Now the forces that steer everything. The PRESSURE GRADIENT FORCE pushes air from high toward low, at right angles to the isobars, and grows as the isobars crowd. The CORIOLIS FORCE, born of the earth's rotation, bends every moving airstream — LEFT in the Southern Hemisphere, right in the Northern. It vanishes at the equator, peaks at the poles, and alters direction only, never speed. That bending is the reason our trade winds arrive from the south-east and our frontal westerlies from the north-west.

Above roughly one kilometre, surface friction falls away and the two forces settle into an exact standoff called GEOSTROPHIC FLOW: the wind runs PARALLEL to straight isobars rather than across them. Down near the ground, friction drags the air slower, Coriolis weakens with it, and the wind angles across the isobars into the low — about ten to twenty degrees over open water, twenty-five to forty-five over rough country. The field shortcut is Buys Ballot's Law, southern version: stand with your back to the wind and low pressure lies on your RIGHT. From that one law follow both rotations: Southern Hemisphere lows turn clockwise and spiral inward, highs turn anticlockwise and spiral outward.

Work through the questions for this section: the three cells with their surface winds, the Coriolis rule stated for our hemisphere, and geostrophic flow separated from friction-layer flow.

## Subtopic: Air Masses, Berg Winds and Monsoons

An AIR MASS is a broad body of air with roughly uniform temperature and moisture, stamped with the character of the SOURCE REGION where it lingered and slowly modified as it moves. Maritime tropical air ripened over the warm Indian Ocean arrives warm and damp, feeding rain to the eastern seaboard and the Lowveld; continental tropical air from the Kalahari arrives hot and parched, the raw material of Northern Cape heatwaves; maritime polar air off the Southern Ocean arrives cold and moist, riding in behind every winter cold front that reaches Gqeberha.

Local winds run on local pressure contrasts. SEA and LAND BREEZES exploit the different heating rates of land and water: through the day the land warms faster, its pressure sags, and a cool onshore sea breeze arrives — the late-afternoon relief that Gqeberha counts on in summer; after dark the land cools faster and the flow reverses into an offshore land breeze. VALLEY and MOUNTAIN WINDS repeat the trick on slopes: sun-heated valley sides drive air upslope by day as an anabatic wind, while at night cold dense air slithers downslope as a katabatic wind and ponds on the valley floor — the source of the frost pockets and stubborn night inversions that trouble fruit farmers in the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands.

The BERG WIND is the country's own Föhn-type wind, and it carries a calculation you must be able to produce on demand. Interior air is drawn off the plateau, spills over the escarpment and descends to the coast, warming by compression at the DRY ADIABATIC LAPSE RATE of about 1 degree Celsius per 100 metres. Run the numbers: air leaves the plateau rim at 1 500 metres at 14 degrees Celsius and sinks to sea level. Warming equals 1 500 divided by 100, multiplied by 1 degree, which is 15 degrees. Arrival temperature equals 14 plus 15, which is 29 degrees Celsius. The descending air gains no moisture while its capacity to hold moisture soars, so relative humidity crashes: berg-wind days are hot, dusty, bone-dry, carry serious fire danger, and commonly arrive in the day or two ahead of a cold front. The Föhn of the Alps and the Chinook of the Rockies share the mechanism, adding a first act — moist air climbing the windward slope at the saturated rate of about 0,5 degrees per 100 metres and raining as it goes, before descending the lee side at the full dry rate to arrive warmer than it started.

A MONSOON blows the sea-breeze idea up to continental and seasonal scale. In summer the Asian interior bakes, a vast thermal low forms over the land, and saturated ocean air is hauled in as the SOUTH-WEST monsoon that drenches India. In winter the interior chills, a great high builds over Siberia, and dry continental air drains seaward as the NORTH-EAST monsoon.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: air masses tied to their source regions, the berg-wind warming worked in full with units, and the monsoon explained as a seasonal reversal of a land-sea pressure contrast.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same circulation again — this time with campfire smoke, a shop freezer, a mall full of escalators and a bicycle pump. Same winds, same answers.

## Subtopic: Smoke, Freezers and the Bicycle Pump

Watch the smoke above a campfire. It never falls; it always climbs. Warm air is lighter than the air around it, so up it goes — and as it leaves, it stops pressing on the ground beneath, so the pressure there drops. You have made a LOW.

Now stand at the open chest freezer in the corner shop and feel the cold pour over your feet. Cold air is heavy. It sinks, piles up and squashes down on the floor. That is a HIGH.

Wind is simply the move from crowded to empty. Think of a packed school hall with the doors thrown open onto an empty corridor: people stream from the crush toward the space, and they stream faster when the crush is worse and the doorway close. Air does exactly this, flowing out of the high into the low, and the isobars on the map tell you about the crush — lines squeezed tightly together mean a big pressure difference over a short distance, so expect a strong wind.

The weather trick lives in the up-and-down, not the sideways. Rising air cools, and cooling air must drop its moisture — the same reason your breath turns to a small cloud on a cold morning. So a low, where air rises, delivers cloud and rain. Sinking air gets squashed, and squashing warms it — pump a bicycle tyre hard and feel the pump barrel heat up in your hand. Warm, squashed air is dry air. So a high, where air sinks, delivers sunshine and blue sky. Low, wet; high, dry. Two words each, half the topic done.

One habit before you move on: a wind is named for where it comes FROM, and the compass is sliced into 16 points of 22,5 degrees each, because 360 divided by 16 is 22,5. North-east is 045 degrees; south-west, the after-the-front wind, is 225.

Strong work — the questions for this section are with you now: rising smoke and sinking freezer air, the crowded hall, and the pump barrel that explains a cloudless day.

## Subtopic: Three Loops Around the Planet

Picture the atmosphere between the equator and the South Pole as three escalator loops in a giant shopping mall, each one endlessly cycling air up, across, down and back.

Loop one serves the tropics. Over the equator the ground bakes all year, air rides up, wrings out its rain and drifts poleward near the ceiling. By about 30 degrees south — the shelf of latitude holding the Kalahari and the Namib — it has chilled, grown heavy and steps off downward. Coming-down air is drying air, which is exactly why a ring of the world's harshest deserts circles the globe at 30 degrees. At the floor, the air walks back to the equator as the trade winds. This is the strongest loop, because the sun powers it directly.

Loop two serves the middle floors, 30 to 60 degrees, and it freeloads — it has no engine of its own and is dragged around by the loops on either side. Its floor-level wind is the westerlies, the belt that flings winter cold fronts at the southern Cape.

Loop three serves the pole: air so cold and dense it slides downward and creeps outward along the floor as the polar easterlies, until it meets the westerlies near 60 degrees and the collision forces both upward. Where the loops meet, up near the roof, run the jet streams — skinny rivers of wind that outrun highway traffic several times over, passing 200 kilometres per hour.

Here is the sentence that unlocks our country: the whole escalator hall is on rollers. It trundles about five degrees north in our winter and five degrees south in our summer, following the overhead sun. Slide north in June and the westerlies with their cold fronts reach the south-western Cape — winter rain for that corner, while the interior sits under a parked high, dry and frosty. Slide south in December and the fronts miss the country, while moist tropical air floods the interior — summer thunderstorms for the plateau. Two rainfall seasons, one moving machine.

Good going — your questions for this part are ready: the three loops, the desert ring at 30 degrees, and the rolling hall that gives one country two rainy calendars.

## Subtopic: Why Wind Never Walks Straight

Put a lazy Susan on the table — one of those spinning trays from a restaurant — set it turning, and try to draw a straight chalk line across it from edge to edge. Look at the tray afterwards: your line is a curve. Your hand moved dead straight; the tray rotated underneath. Nothing pushed the chalk sideways, yet the path bent. That is the CORIOLIS force, and the spinning earth is the tray.

The rule for our side of the world is short: in the Southern Hemisphere, moving air bends LEFT. It bends hardest near the poles, not at all on the equator, and it changes only the direction of the wind, never its speed. The bend is why air heading for the equator turns up as a south-easterly trade wind instead of a straight southerly.

And there is a trick that turns your own body into a weather instrument. Put your back to the wind, here in the south, and low pressure sits on your RIGHT — Buys Ballot's Law. Reason around a low with that rule and the rotations fall out by themselves: our lows spin clockwise, our highs anticlockwise.

Now the descending monster. A berg wind begins as cool, dry air on the high interior plateau. Pulled toward the coast, it pours over the escarpment and falls — and falling air gets squashed by the heavier air beneath, and squashed air heats up, about 1 degree Celsius for every 100 metres of descent. Start it at the plateau rim, 1 500 metres up, on a gentle 14-degree day, and let it fall to the beach: 1 500 divided by 100 is 15, so it warms 15 degrees and lands at 14 plus 15, which is 29 degrees Celsius. Heat gained, not a drop of moisture gained — so the air arrives scorching and desert-dry, perfect for runaway fires, and it loves to show up a day before a cold front does.

Last piece: a monsoon is a sea breeze that swapped its wristwatch for a calendar. In summer India's landmass heats far faster than the ocean beside it, pressure over the land collapses, and soaked ocean air floods in for months. In winter the land turns cold, pressure rebuilds, and dry air drains back out to sea. Gqeberha's four o'clock sea breeze, stretched over half a year and a whole subcontinent.

Here come the final questions of the lesson — the lazy Susan and the left-hand bend, back to the wind with the low on your right, and the berg wind worked to 29 degrees. Take these and the planet's whole delivery network is in your hands.
