**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the trade winds blow toward the equator and the westerlies blow away from it. If all wind just runs from high to low pressure, why do these two belts end up travelling in opposite directions from the same highs?

**Tutor:** Lovely question, because it forces the two halves of the theory to work together. Both wind belts are indeed born at the same place — the subtropical high near 30 degrees — and both begin as air draining out of that high down the pressure gradient. The air leaving on the equatorward side heads for the equatorial low; the air leaving on the poleward side heads for the sub-polar low. So far, pure pressure gradient. Then the Coriolis force takes hold of each stream and bends it LEFT, because we are in the Southern Hemisphere. The stream travelling toward the equator is moving roughly northward; bend a northward flow to its left and it acquires a westward lean, arriving as the SOUTH-EASTERLY trades. The stream travelling poleward is moving roughly southward; bend a southward flow to its left and it acquires an eastward lean, arriving as winds out of the north-west — the westerlies. Same parent high, same rule, opposite headings, so the deflection produces winds that look like different species. Write the two-step logic — gradient sets the initial direction, Coriolis bends it left — and both belts fall out of one mechanism.

**Bianca:** Next: the doldrums confused half the class. The equatorial low has the strongest heating and the strongest rising air on the planet, so why are the surface winds there famously weak instead of the strongest on earth?

**Tutor:** Because strength in the vertical does not translate into strength in the horizontal, and the equatorial low is nearly all vertical. Two things quieten the surface there. First, the geometry of convergence: the trade winds arrive from the north-east and the south-east and meet nearly head-on, and air that converges must escape upward — the motion turns into a massive slow updraught rather than a fast horizontal stream. Standing under it, you feel stagnant, humid calm, with the action happening kilometres above your head in the thunderstorm towers. Second, the Coriolis force is ZERO at the equator, and Coriolis is what organises pressure differences into brisk, sustained, rotating wind systems elsewhere on the planet. Without it, the equatorial belt cannot spin up the tight circulations that give the mid-latitudes their gales; pressure differences there just fill slowly and gently. That is why the age-of-sail crews dreaded the region — ships could drift for weeks in glassy calm under daily downpours. So remember the pairing: the doldrums are calm horizontally precisely because the air is so busy vertically, and because the organising force that makes wind elsewhere switches off at the equator.

**Bianca:** Third one: in the friction layer the wind crosses the isobars at ten to twenty degrees over the sea but twenty-five to forty-five degrees over land. Why does rougher ground make the wind cross MORE steeply? I would have guessed friction just slows it down.

**Tutor:** Friction does slow it down — and the slowing is exactly what steepens the crossing angle, so your instinct is one step from the full answer. Recall the balance that holds above the friction layer: the pressure gradient force pulls air toward the low while the Coriolis force, which grows with wind SPEED, pulls it aside, and at full speed the two balance so perfectly that the wind runs parallel to the isobars. Now drag that flow across a rough surface. Friction cuts the wind's speed, and because the Coriolis force depends on speed, the deflection weakens in proportion. The pressure gradient force, though, does not care about speed at all — it depends only on the spacing of the isobars, so it keeps pulling at full strength. The tug-of-war is now lopsided: the gradient wins by a wider margin, and the wind swings further off the isobars, angling more directly toward the low. Over a smooth sea the speed loss is modest, so the imbalance is small — a crossing of ten to twenty degrees. Over hills, forests and buildings the speed loss is severe, the Coriolis force fades badly, and the wind cuts across at twenty-five to forty-five degrees. One sentence carries it: friction weakens the wind, the weakened wind feels less Coriolis, and the undiminished pressure gradient claims the difference.

**Bianca:** Last one: everyone can recite that the berg wind warms one degree per hundred metres, but nobody in our group can explain WHERE the heat comes from. The air starts cold on the plateau and nothing burns it — so what actually heats it on the way down?

**Tutor:** The heat comes from the air's own squeezing, and the beauty is that no outside heat source is needed at all. Air pressure increases toward sea level, because there is ever more atmosphere stacked overhead. So as a parcel of plateau air descends the escarpment, the surrounding air presses in on it harder and harder, compressing it into a smaller volume. Compression is work being done ON the parcel — countless collisions driving its molecules closer and faster — and faster molecules ARE higher temperature. That is the same physics you feel when a bicycle pump barrel grows hot in your hand: nothing is burning inside the pump; your compression strokes are doing work on the trapped air and the work appears as heat. Run the pump in reverse and you get the rising branch: air ascending in a thunderstorm expands into thinner surroundings, does work pushing outward, and cools at the same rate. Because no heat is exchanged with the environment either way, we call the process ADIABATIC, and for dry air the exchange rate between height and temperature settles at about one degree Celsius per hundred metres. So in the calculation — 1 500 metres of descent, 15 degrees of warming, 14 becoming 29 — the fifteen degrees were not collected from anywhere along the route. They were manufactured inside the parcel by compression, which is why the air arrives hot yet bone-dry, without a single extra gram of moisture to show for it.
