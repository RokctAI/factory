# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 settled one strange fact: the air is warmed from underneath, by the ground it rests on. This session asks the next question — why that warming is so uneven — and follows the consequences: the geometry that concentrates sunlight in the tropics and dilutes it toward the poles, the fixed lean of the axis that swings the advantage back and forth through the year, the five processes that shift heat once it has landed, and the planetary bookkeeping that forces energy to flow poleward without rest.

## Subtopic: Insolation and the Latitude Effect

Start with the delivery rate. INSOLATION — incoming solar radiation — arrives at the top of the atmosphere at close to 1 367 watts per square metre, measured face-on to the beam. Above the Richtersveld and above the South Pole that number is the same. The inequality is created lower down, by geometry, and its first name is the ANGLE OF INCIDENCE.

Where the midday sun stands nearly overhead, as it does in the tropics, the beam meets the ground almost at right angles, so a parcel of radiation of fixed width lands on a small footprint and each square metre receives a generous share. Toward the poles the curved earth tilts the ground away from the beam, and the identical parcel is dragged out across a far wider, slanted footprint. The energy has not changed; the area sharing it has.

The slant exacts a second toll, called PATH LENGTH. A near-vertical ray punches through the atmosphere by the shortest possible route. A low-angle polar ray must slide through several times that thickness of air, and all the extra kilometres scatter, reflect and absorb a portion of the beam before any of it touches the surface. High latitudes therefore receive light that is both spread out and pre-weakened.

The third factor is ALBEDO — the percentage of arriving insolation a surface bounces straight back unused. Fresh snow and ice return roughly 80 to 90 per cent; dark open ocean returns less than 10. So the coldest surfaces reject most of the little they are offered, while warm tropical seas swallow nearly all of theirs. Albedo does not create the gap between equator and pole, but it widens whatever gap geometry has opened — a self-reinforcing effect worth naming as positive feedback.

Close the section with the standard accounting. Of all insolation reaching the top of the atmosphere, roughly 30 per cent is reflected back to space, roughly 20 per cent is absorbed within the atmosphere itself, and about half is absorbed by the surface. The heated surface then warms the air above it, which is why temperature falls with altitude at an average of about 6,5 degrees Celsius per kilometre — and why the top of Sani Pass can be near freezing on a day when Durban, at sea level, is sweltering.

Work through this section's questions now: the angle of incidence, the path-length penalty, albedo as a feedback, and the thirty-twenty-fifty budget.

## Subtopic: The Axis, the Revolution and the Seasons

The earth's axis leans 23,5 degrees away from the vertical — the same thing as 66,5 degrees from the orbital plane — and it keeps that lean aimed at the same point in space all year long. That fixed aim is called PARALLELISM, and it is the ingredient people forget: a tilt that swivelled to face the sun permanently would give no seasons at all.

Add REVOLUTION — one full orbit every 365 and a quarter days — and the point where the sun stands directly overhead glides between 23,5 degrees north and 23,5 degrees south. Around 22 December it reaches the Tropic of Capricorn, giving the southern hemisphere its summer solstice, the year's highest sun and longest day. Around 21 June it reaches the Tropic of Cancer, our winter solstice, lowest sun and shortest day. Around 21 March and 23 September it stands over the equator — the equinoxes, when every latitude on earth receives twelve hours of daylight.

Now make it numerical, because this is where the calculation lives. Midday solar altitude equals 90 degrees minus the angular distance between your latitude and the latitude of the overhead sun. Take Johannesburg at 26 degrees south. On 22 December the overhead sun sits at 23,5 degrees south, so the angular distance is 26 minus 23,5, which is 2,5 degrees, and the midday sun stands at 87,5 degrees — practically overhead, which is why a December lunchtime shadow in Johannesburg almost vanishes. On 21 June the overhead sun sits at 23,5 degrees north, so the distance is 26 plus 23,5, which is 49,5 degrees, and the altitude drops to 40,5 degrees. A swing of 47 degrees, reinforced by day length shrinking from close to fourteen hours to about ten and a half. Weaker sunlight, delivered for fewer hours — that is winter, twice derived.

Carry one South African detail: the Tropic of Capricorn runs through Limpopo, passing north of Polokwane, so the far north of the country lies inside the tropics and watches the sun cross directly overhead twice each year, something no town south of that line ever sees.

Finally, disarm the classic error before it costs you. The orbit is slightly elliptical and the earth is actually NEAREST the sun in early January — in the middle of the southern summer and of Europe's coldest weeks. If distance controlled the seasons, both hemispheres would warm and cool together, and they plainly do not. The tilt, not the distance, is the cause.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: parallelism, the four key dates, and the Johannesburg altitude calculation for both solstices.

## Subtopic: How Energy Moves Through the Atmosphere

Delivered energy must now travel, and each transport process has a precise name the syllabus expects.

RADIATION carries energy as electromagnetic waves and needs no material to cross. Solar radiation is SHORTWAVE, because an extremely hot body radiates at short wavelengths; the earth, being far cooler, re-radiates LONGWAVE energy. The atmosphere is nearly transparent to the incoming shortwave but not to the outgoing longwave: water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane absorb it and return part of it downward. That selective trapping is the greenhouse effect, and it lifts the planet's average surface temperature from about minus 18 degrees Celsius to about plus 15 — the difference between a frozen world and a habitable one.

CONDUCTION passes heat by direct molecular contact. Because air conducts very poorly, conduction matters only in the few centimetres of air actually touching a hot surface — midday sand in the Richtersveld scorching the thin layer resting on it. It explains nothing higher up, and offering it for anything higher is a standard error.

CONVECTION moves heat VERTICALLY inside a fluid. Surface-warmed air expands, loses density and rises, while cooler, denser air slides underneath. On a summer afternoon over the Free State that ordinary rising motion, repeated on a giant scale, hoists moist air until it condenses into a flat-based cumulonimbus and the evening thunderstorm follows.

ADVECTION is the HORIZONTAL twin: heat carried sideways by wind or by flowing water. The cool sea breeze that arrives in East London on a hot afternoon is advection; so is the warm Agulhas Current ferrying tropical water down the east coast, and a hot berg wind spilling interior air onto the coastal belt.

LATENT HEAT is the invisible carrier. Evaporating one kilogram of water locks away roughly 2,5 megajoules without any rise in temperature; the energy travels silently inside the vapour and is released only where condensation happens — often far inland and kilometres above the ground. Measured over the whole planet, more heat rides poleward hidden in water vapour than by any other atmospheric process.

The questions for this section are with you now: each process matched to a fresh example, and a justification of why conduction stays in the bottom centimetres while latent heat crosses oceans.

## Subtopic: Surplus, Deficit and the Global Heat Engine

Now audit each latitude. Compare the shortwave energy it ABSORBS against the longwave energy it LOSES to space. Between about 40 degrees north and 40 degrees south, absorption wins: a permanent energy SURPLUS. Poleward of about 40 degrees in each hemisphere, loss wins: a permanent DEFICIT. South Africa sits astride the interesting edge — the Limpopo valley lies comfortably in surplus country, while the stormy seas south of Cape Agulhas belong to the deficit side.

Here is the observation that turns a diagram into a machine. Left uncorrected, a permanent surplus would heat the tropics without limit and a permanent deficit would freeze the poles without limit. The records show neither: the temperature gap between equator and pole holds broadly steady, decade after decade. The only possible conclusion is that energy is being exported poleward, continuously and on an enormous scale. That export is the engine driving everything else in this term.

The freight is shared by two carriers. The ATMOSPHERE handles roughly three-quarters: the great vertical circulation cells, the mid-latitude westerlies with their travelling depressions, the jet streams aloft, and latent heat riding inside water vapour. The OCEANS handle the remaining quarter. South Africa displays both limbs of the oceanic correction at once: the warm Agulhas Current sweeps tropical water southward past East London, while the cold Benguela Current draws sub-Antarctic water northward past Lambert's Bay. One country, two coasts, two halves of the same repair job.

Finish with the planetary rule. Averaged over the whole globe and over several years, energy in equals energy out. Disturb either side and the surface temperature must shift until the books balance again: thicken the greenhouse layer, and outgoing longwave is obstructed, so the surface warms until the outflow recovers. Treat the energy budget as an accounting identity the planet is forced to satisfy, not as a picture to memorise.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: the surplus and deficit zones by latitude, the argument that proves poleward transfer must exist, and the three-quarters to one-quarter split between air and ocean.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same planet, same physics — this time with a hosepipe, an orange and your own kitchen. Nothing is left out; it only gets friendlier.

## Subtopic: A Hosepipe on the Lawn

Point a hosepipe straight down at the lawn and you soak one small circle of grass until it floods. Now swing the nozzle so the water flies out at a low angle: the same flow spreads into a long, thin, barely damp stripe. Not a drop was lost — it was simply shared across more lawn.

That is the equator and the poles in one picture. The sun delivers the same beam everywhere, but near the equator it arrives almost straight down and drenches a small patch, while at the poles the earth's curve lays the ground back so the same beam smears into a weak stripe. Small soaked circle, long damp stripe — that is why Giyani bakes in January while the South Pole never truly thaws.

The poles get cheated a second time. Think of crossing a packed school corridor: straight across between classrooms is quick, but walking the corridor's full length means squeezing past everyone, and you arrive flattened. Slanted polar sunlight travels the long way through the atmosphere, and the air pinches off a share of it the whole way — scattered, bounced, absorbed. What finally lands is spread thin AND already taxed.

Then there is colour. On sports day, the learners in white shirts stay bearable while the one in the black tracksuit is cooking — dark surfaces swallow light, pale surfaces fling it back. Snow and ice are the palest surfaces on the planet and throw back as much as nine-tenths of their sunlight; the dark ocean keeps almost all of it. Cold places stay cold partly because they are white, and geographers call that reflected share the albedo.

Strong start — the questions for this piece are with you now: the hosepipe angle, the long corridor, and the white shirt against the black tracksuit.

## Subtopic: The Orange on a Leaning Needle

Push a knitting needle through an orange, tilt the needle by about 23 and a half degrees, and carry the orange in a slow circle around a candle on the kitchen table. One rule the whole way: the needle keeps its lean and never changes the direction it points. That stubborn lean is the entire secret of the seasons.

Walk to the side of the table where the orange's bottom half leans TOWARD the flame. The light now falls steeply on the southern part of the orange — that is December: high sun, long days, school holidays at the beach in Ballito. Carry on around to the opposite side and the bottom half leans AWAY: light arrives at a slant, feeble and brief — that is June, with frost on the grass in Bloemfontein.

Numbers pin it down for Johannesburg, which sits at 26 degrees south. In December the midday sun climbs to about 87 and a half degrees — so close to overhead that your shadow at lunchtime is a puddle at your feet on the netball court. In June it manages only about 40 and a half degrees, and that same lunchtime shadow stretches metres across the tar. A drop of 47 degrees, and on top of it the day shrinks from nearly fourteen hours of light to about ten and a half. Weaker rays for fewer hours — winter explained twice over.

Now bury the myth. People insist winter comes because the earth drifts further from the sun. In fact we are CLOSEST to the sun in early January — during our summer, while Europe shivers through snow. If distance were in charge, the whole planet would warm and cool together, and it does not. It is the lean. And the two in-between stops complete the tour: around 21 March and 23 September the candle shines square on the orange's middle, and every latitude gets twelve hours of light and twelve of dark.

Your questions for this section are ready now — the needle that never straightens, December against June in Johannesburg, and why the distance story collapses.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen and the Power Grid

Your own kitchen demonstrates every way heat can travel, all before supper.

Open the oven door to check the roast and warmth washes over your face across the gap — nothing touching you, no wind. That is RADIATION, energy crossing space in straight lines, exactly how the sun reaches the earth and how the earth leaks heat to the stars at night.

Leave a teaspoon standing in a mug of hot tea and two minutes later the handle burns your fingers. The heat crept up the metal by touch, molecule by molecule: CONDUCTION. Air is hopeless at it, which is why only the paper-thin layer of air lying directly on hot ground is warmed this way.

Watch a pot of porridge on the stove: the hot porridge in the middle swells up while cooler porridge slides down the sides to replace it, turning the pot over and over. That rolling is CONVECTION — heat travelling UPWARDS in a fluid — and blown up to province size it is the Free State afternoon in December, where rising warm air builds a thunderhead by four o'clock.

Now open the kitchen window and the breeze carries the warm cooking smell down the passage. Heat moving SIDEWAYS on moving air is ADVECTION — the same thing as the cool sea breeze reaching East London on a summer afternoon, or warm Agulhas water gliding down the east coast.

Last, hang wet washing on the line. It dries because liquid water is being turned into vapour, and that conversion STEALS heat — which is also why a damp shirt feels cold in the wind. The stolen heat hides inside the vapour and is paid out again wherever the vapour condenses into cloud, sometimes a thousand kilometres away. That hidden cargo is LATENT HEAT, and the oceans ship colossal amounts of it.

Finish with the budget, and think of the national power grid. The generating stations cluster in one part of the country and make more power than they can use — a surplus. Distant towns produce none and need plenty — a deficit. The lights stay on everywhere only because transmission lines carry the difference, day and night, without a pause. The planet runs the same grid for heat: the tropics generate the surplus, the poles run the deficit, the winds are the main transmission lines carrying about three units in every four, and the ocean currents carry the rest — warm water south past East London, cold water north past Lambert's Bay.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — kitchen heat matched to its proper name, and the surplus, the deficit and the grid that connects them told in your own words. Get these and the engine behind the whole term belongs to you.
