# Part 1 — Expert

The previous session taught you to measure development; this one supplies the FRAMEWORKS — the aspects a full development plan must cover, and the two great models geographers use to explain WHERE and HOW development happens. The core-periphery model explains the map; Rostow's stages claim to explain the timeline. Each model pays twice: once when you describe it accurately, and again when you criticise it intelligently.

## Subtopic: The Five Aspects of Development

Five aspects, and an answer that touches all five reads as complete.

ECONOMIC development is the engine room — rising production, employment, income and investment. Nothing else gets funded without it, though the last session proved it insufficient on its own.

SOCIAL development is the conversion step, turning money into lives: schools, clinics, housing, safe water — and the standing of women, because no country develops while half its people are shut out of classrooms and workplaces.

SUSTAINABLE development carries the famous Brundtland definition, worth learning word for word: development that meets the needs of the present WITHOUT compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. An economy that fells every forest and drains every aquifer to inflate this decade's output is not developing — it is spending its children's inheritance.

APPROPRIATE-SCALE development sizes the solution to the community. A coastal metro justifies a desalination plant and a national grid; a remote Wild Coast village is often better served by rooftop solar, rainwater tanks and a hand-repairable pump. Bigger is not automatically better — the technology must match local skills, budgets and needs, which is why the phrase appropriate technology always travels alongside.

SPATIAL development asks the geographer's question: WHERE? Development never spreads itself evenly, and honest planning must confront the widening gap between booming cores and neglected peripheries — precisely the problem the next subtopic turns into a model.

The questions for this section are ready: name the five aspects, deliver Brundtland precisely, and fit appropriate technology to its setting.

## Subtopic: The Core-Periphery Model

The CORE-PERIPHERY model holds that development organises space into a powerful centre and a dependent edge — and that the pattern repeats at every scale you zoom to.

The CORE is where economic activity, investment, skills and political power pile up: the factories, head offices, universities and best infrastructure. The PERIPHERY feeds the core raw materials and labour and receives little back. Many versions add a SEMI-PERIPHERY between them — regions in transit from one status toward the other.

The engine of the model is CUMULATIVE CAUSATION: success recruits success. A plant opens in the core; suppliers, financiers and job-seekers follow; the tax base funds infrastructure; better infrastructure lands the next plant. The periphery meanwhile suffers the BACKWASH EFFECT — its young, its skilled and its savings drain toward the core, leaving behind an older, poorer region ever less able to close the gap. Flowing the opposite way, when conditions allow, are SPREAD EFFECTS — investment, remittances and demand trickling outward from core to periphery. Most regional development policy is a single project: strengthen the spread, throttle the backwash.

Run it at three scales, because that is how the model is examined. Globally: the industrialised North as core, with much of Africa, Asia and Latin America as periphery supplying minerals and crops. Nationally: Gauteng — generating roughly a third of South Africa's GDP on the smallest provincial area — as core, while the former homeland districts of Limpopo and the Eastern Cape serve as periphery, exporting their workers to the cities. Locally: a metro's office towers and industrial parks as core, ringed by townships and informal settlements on the far edge, joined to it by long, costly daily commutes.

Work through this section's questions now: define core, periphery and backwash, and apply the model at global, national and local scale.

## Subtopic: Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth

Walt Rostow's model of 1960 claims that every economy climbs the same staircase of five steps.

Step one, TRADITIONAL SOCIETY: subsistence farming dominates, technology is simple, surplus barely exists. Step two, PRECONDITIONS FOR TAKE-OFF: commercial agriculture spreads, the hard skeleton of infrastructure — railways, harbours, banks — is assembled, often with outside finance, and an entrepreneurial class appears. Step three, TAKE-OFF: the decisive burst — manufacturing surges, investment climbs past roughly ten per cent of national income, and growth begins to feed itself. Step four, DRIVE TO MATURITY: industry diversifies well beyond the take-off leaders, technology penetrates the whole economy, cities swell. Step five, the AGE OF HIGH MASS CONSUMPTION: household incomes stretch to cars and appliances, and services overtake industry as the main employer.

Rostow read the staircase off the history of Britain and the United States — which is at once the model's strength and its flaw. The strength: the pattern is real. Industrialisation did transform Britain, then Germany, then Japan, then South Korea, and take-off usefully names the moment growth becomes self-sustaining.

The weaknesses are what a strong answer argues. The model assumes one path — the Western one — as though culture, resources and political choice counted for nothing. It is silent on colonialism: Britain's take-off drew fuel from colonies whose own development was deliberately suppressed, so the ladder was pulled up behind the first climbers. It ignores the core-periphery trap — a periphery locked into raw exports may never accumulate the investment take-off demands. And it crowns mass consumption as the summit, with no step for sustainability: a five-stage race to the shopping mall, designed before anyone asked whether the planet could carry eight billion mass consumers.

Attend to this section's questions: sequence the five stages, state the take-off conditions, and prepare two criticisms you can defend.

## Subtopic: Reading Real Places With Both Models

Models earn their keep only against real ground, so test both on South Africa.

Rostow first. The preconditions arrived with the mineral revolution — railways and harbours flung inland after 1870 to serve diamonds and gold. Manufacturing take-off followed roughly between the 1930s and 1960s behind tariff walls. Today the economy wears drive-to-maturity clothing — diversified industry, service-dominated employment — while high mass consumption describes the insured suburbs and not the periphery. One country refuses to stand on one step, and that refusal is itself a criticism: apartheid deliberately barred most of the population from the staircase, and no stage model has a stage for that.

Core-periphery explains what Rostow cannot — the SPATIAL pattern. Apartheid was core-periphery engineering written into law: the homelands were designed as labour reservoirs, peripheries whose backwash of migrant workers fed the mining core. That is why the model fits South Africa so uncomfortably well, and why post-1994 policy — spatial development initiatives, industrial development zones at Saldanha Bay and Richards Bay, corridor projects such as Lubombo linking north-eastern KwaZulu-Natal toward Maputo — is an explicit attempt to bolt new cores onto the periphery and pump spread effects outward.

The synthesis worth writing: Rostow draws a TIMELINE and assumes every region rides it together; core-periphery draws a MAP and insists they do not. Use Rostow when a question asks about a country's history, core-periphery when it asks about its geography — and criticise both: the first for ignoring space and historical injustice, the second for offering no automatic way out.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: South Africa on Rostow's staircase, apartheid read through core-periphery, and the two models set side by side.

## Subtopic: Five Boxes Every Plan Must Tick

Picture a development plan passing across a desk, and five stamps it must collect before anyone signs.

Stamp one asks about the WALLET: will the economy produce more — jobs, factories, income? No engine, no journey. Stamp two asks about the PEOPLE: is the money turning into clinics, classrooms, taps and safety? A warehouse of bricks is not a home. Stamp three asks about TOMORROW: are we building with what we can replace, or torching next year to warm this one? That is sustainability, and the sentence to memorise word for word comes from the Brundtland report — meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Stamp four asks about the FIT: is the solution the right SIZE for the place? Hand a remote village a gleaming high-tech treatment works nobody local can repair, and you have donated a monument, not development. Rooftop solar, rainwater tanks and a pump the community fixes itself — that is appropriate scale and appropriate technology. Stamp five asks about the MAP: is the building happening everywhere, or only in the polished suburb while the rest of the district waits its turn? Development that heaps up in one corner is geography's oldest complaint — and it has its own model, coming next.

The questions for this piece are with you now: five stamps, five aspects, one Brundtland sentence.

## Subtopic: The Magnet and the Empty Edges

Scatter iron filings across a table with a magnet at the centre and watch them crawl inward. That is the whole core-periphery model in a single image.

The magnet is the CORE — the region where the factories, banks, universities and ministries cluster. The table's edges are the PERIPHERY — the districts that send the core their minerals, their harvests and, most painfully, their children. Every new year, taxis and buses leave the villages of Limpopo and the old Transkei for Johannesburg, loaded with the young and the capable. That drain has a name: BACKWASH — the periphery's best, flowing coreward. And every arrival strengthens the magnet: more workers bring more shops, more taxes, better roads, and the better roads land the next factory. Success recruiting success — cumulative causation.

Sometimes current flows outward instead: a mineworker's remittances raise a homestead roof; a firm opens a branch plant in a small town. Those outward trickles are SPREAD EFFECTS, and regional planning is honestly just plumbing — widen the spread pipes, narrow the backwash drain. The industrial development zone at Richards Bay, and Saldanha's steel-and-gas zone on the West Coast, are attempts to bolt brand-new magnets onto the periphery.

The move that scores: the picture repeats at every zoom. Zoom out — industrial North, supplying South. Zoom national — Gauteng magnet, homeland-era edges. Zoom to one metro — office-tower core, township rim, and the long taxi ride stitched between them. One model, three scales, full marks.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the magnet, the backwash, the spread, and the three zoom levels.

## Subtopic: The Aeroplane on the Runway

Rostow's claim is that every economy is an aeroplane and history is one long flight plan.

The plane begins parked at the terminal: TRADITIONAL SOCIETY — everyone farming to eat, nothing to spare. The ground crew arrives: PRECONDITIONS — rails laid, harbours dredged, banks opened, farms selling surplus to town. Then the moment the model is famous for, TAKE-OFF: factories multiply, investment pushes past about ten per cent of national income, and speed starts generating lift — growth feeding growth without further shoving. The climb is the DRIVE TO MATURITY, industry spreading through every sector; cruising altitude is HIGH MASS CONSUMPTION, where ordinary families buy cars and fridges and most people earn their living in services.

A tidy story — and for Britain, Japan and South Korea the flight genuinely happened. Now the criticisms, because evaluation is where the marks concentrate. One: the model issues every country the same flight plan, the Western one, as if there were a single route to a good life. Two: it forgets that some planes fuelled up by draining other planes' tanks — colonial powers climbed partly on resources and markets taken from colonies whose own engines were deliberately switched off. Three: some planes are held at the gate by the magnet problem — peripheries stuck exporting raw materials never bank the fuel that take-off burns. Four: the destination is a shopping mall — the model crowns mass consumption and never asks what eight billion mass consumers would do to the atmosphere.

And South Africa? Sections of the economy are cruising while millions of passengers were barred from boarding for decades by law. A one-staircase, one-aeroplane model has no stage for apartheid — write that sentence in an essay and you are doing real geography.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the five flight phases, the take-off conditions, and the criticisms that turn description into evaluation. Land these and both great models travel with you.
