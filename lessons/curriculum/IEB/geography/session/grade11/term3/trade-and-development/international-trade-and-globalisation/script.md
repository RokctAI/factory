# Part 1 — Expert

No country develops inside a sealed container. Nations buy, sell, borrow and compete, and the rules of that exchange quietly decide who accumulates wealth and who stays a supplier. This session works through international trade and world markets, the terms of trade that tilt the playing field, the recognisable types of trading relationship, globalisation with its double-edged effects, and the export-led strategy that carried a handful of countries from poverty to wealth — examined critically, because uncritical praise is not geography.

## Subtopic: International Trade and World Markets

INTERNATIONAL TRADE is the exchange of goods and services across borders. EXPORTS are what a country sells to the world; IMPORTS are what it buys in; the difference between them is the BALANCE OF TRADE — a surplus when exports exceed imports, a deficit when imports win.

For development, the decisive question is WHAT crosses the border. PRIMARY PRODUCTS are raw materials — ores, crops, timber, unprocessed fish. MANUFACTURED GOODS carry skill and technology folded into them: machinery, vehicles, electronics, medicines. The bulk of the value is added during processing, so a country shipping out raw ore while shipping in finished machinery is selling cheap and buying expensive — the textbook profile of a peripheral economy.

COMMODITIES — standardised raw materials such as platinum, coal, maize, crude oil and cocoa — are priced on WORLD MARKETS, where global supply and demand set one ruling price that no individual producer can move. Those prices also lurch violently. A frost in Brazil sends coffee soaring; a construction slump in China drags iron ore and platinum down together. A country hitched to one or two commodities therefore rides a rollercoaster with no steering wheel — Angola swings with oil, Botswana holds its breath with every diamond downturn.

South Africa's own trade profile makes this concrete. Major exports: platinum-group metals, gold, coal, iron ore and manganese, alongside citrus, wine and motor vehicles. Major imports: machinery, electronics, refined petroleum and pharmaceuticals. The vehicles — assembled in Kariega, East London, Durban and Rosslyn — are the hopeful exception: manufactured, value-added exports, precisely the basket development policy works to enlarge.

The questions for this section are ready: exports, imports and the balance, primary against manufactured, and the risk in commodity dependence.

## Subtopic: Terms of Trade and Trading Relationships

TERMS OF TRADE measure what a country's exports can actually BUY: the ratio of average export prices to average import prices. When export prices climb faster than import prices, terms of trade improve — the same tonnage buys more machines. When import prices pull ahead, terms of trade DETERIORATE — you must load ever more ore onto the ship to bring the same machine home.

Name the structural problem behind this. Over the long run, prices of primary products have tended to slide relative to prices of manufactured goods. Raw materials are easily substituted and endlessly competed over, while manufactured goods shelter behind technology, patents and brands that defend their prices. Countries locked into primary exports therefore face slowly deteriorating terms of trade — running up an escalator that is moving down. That is the machinery inside the phrase TRADE IMBALANCE between developing and developed economies, and it is why simply exporting more raw material almost never closes the gap.

Trading relationships come in types you must be able to match to examples. FREE TRADE strips away tariffs and quotas so goods move unhindered — efficient on paper, but it sets unequal players against each other in the same ring. PROTECTIONISM shields home industries behind tariffs, quotas and subsidies; the sore point is rich-world farm subsidy, which lets European and American produce undercut African farmers on their own continent. TRADE BLOCS free trade among members: the European Union abroad, and closer to home SADC and the African Continental Free Trade Area, which sets out to stitch Africa's many small markets into one continental market. TRADE AGREEMENTS such as AGOA grant South African vehicles and citrus preferential entry into the United States. And FAIR TRADE certification pays registered producers — of wine, rooibos, cocoa — a guaranteed floor price plus a social premium, exchanging a sliver of efficiency for a great deal of stability.

Work through this section's questions now: define terms of trade, explain the long slide against primary exporters, and pair each trading relationship with its example.

## Subtopic: Globalisation and Its Impact on Development

GLOBALISATION is the deepening interconnection of the world's economies, cultures and people. Its drivers: transport made radically cheaper — above all by the shipping container — communication made instant, and trade barriers lowered round after round. Its signature institution is the TRANSNATIONAL CORPORATION, or TNC: headquarters in one country, factories in others, customers everywhere — Samsung, Nestlé, Sasol, Shoprite.

The development impacts run in both directions, and a complete answer keeps both columns.

The gains column. Globalisation opens the whole world as a market rather than just the neighbours; it carries investment, factories and technology into developing economies; it created hundreds of millions of manufacturing jobs and lifted East Asia out of poverty at a speed history had never seen; and it hands consumers cheaper goods. South African entries: vehicle plants wired into global supply chains, citrus and wine on northern-hemisphere shelves, Shoprite and MTN trading across the continent.

The costs column. The benefits cluster — the map of globalisation reproduces the core-periphery pattern, with investment favouring a shortlist of locations. TNCs out-bargain small governments: they extract tax holidays and light regulation, send profits home to headquarters, and pack up for cheaper labour elsewhere at short notice — the RACE TO THE BOTTOM. Infant local industries can be flattened by imports before they ever mature; South Africa's clothing and textile factories, Newcastle's among them, shed tens of thousands of jobs to cheaper Asian competition. And interconnection is a transmission line for shocks — a banking crisis that began on Wall Street in 2008 was cutting South African exports and jobs within months.

The balanced verdict to carry: globalisation is a powerful engine that pulls unevenly. Those holding skills, infrastructure and bargaining power ride it; those without can be run over by it.

Attend to this section's questions: the drivers, the TNC, and two impacts from each column.

## Subtopic: Export-led Development, Critically Examined

EXPORT-LED DEVELOPMENT grows an economy by selling manufactured goods to the world market rather than only replacing imports at home. Its showcase is the ASIAN TIGERS — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong — with China later running the same play at continental scale.

The recipe as South Korea performed it: begin with cheap-labour manufactures such as textiles; bank the foreign exchange; reinvest it deliberately in schooling and infrastructure; then climb the value ladder rung by rung — textiles to steel to ships to cars to semiconductors. In 1960 South Korea's income per person matched some of the poorest countries on earth; within two generations it sat among the developed economies. Nor did the state watch from the stands: it sheltered infant industries, steered bank credit, and disciplined firms by making support conditional on export success.

Now the critical examination the syllabus explicitly requires. One: the strategy presumes open, growing world markets — it worked while wealthy countries absorbed the exports, and if everyone climbs at once the markets flood. Two: conditions were unrepeatable — the Tigers enjoyed market access and geopolitical backing that later climbers struggle to find. Three: the bottom rungs are cruel — low wages, long hours and suppressed unions carried the first decades, which forces the question of who pays for take-off. Four: export economies import shocks — 2008 struck them first and hardest. Five: the environmental bill was deferred, and the smog over industrial East Asia is that bill arriving.

And South Africa? We run export-led elements — vehicles under successive motor industry programmes, citrus and wine, AGOA access — while carrying burdens the Tigers never did: distance from the big markets, unreliable electricity, skills shortages and rigid input costs. The honest conclusion: export-led growth is a proven route but not a guaranteed one; it demands state capacity, education and dependable infrastructure — which are themselves developmental achievements that must come first.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: the recipe, the Tiger evidence, and three critical limits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same trade story now at street level — a spaza, a swap, and a ladder. Every fact kept.

## Subtopic: The Spaza and the Supermarket

Put one street in your head with a spaza shop at one end and a giant supermarket at the other. The spaza sells what it can gather cheaply — loose oranges, bundles of firewood, amagwinya. The supermarket sells processed goods — bread, data, medicine — and the profit hides in the processing. Watch the money: the spaza owner sells cheap raw things and buys expensive processed things, day after day, and the money drifts up the street in one direction only.

That street is the world economy in miniature. Countries selling raw materials — ore, raw cocoa, unprocessed fruit — stand at the spaza end; countries selling machines, medicines and electronics own the supermarket. A cocoa farmer earns a few rand on a kilogram of beans; the same kilogram, processed into slabs and branded, sells for hundreds in a mall. The difference — the value added — settles where the processing happens, never where the pod was picked.

South Africa works both ends of the street. We sell platinum, coal and iron ore spaza-style — but the vehicle plants of Kariega, East London and Durban sell finished cars to the world, supermarket-style. The whole project of trade policy fits in one line: stop selling only the beans; start selling the chocolate.

The questions for this piece are with you now: the spaza, the supermarket, and where the value hides.

## Subtopic: Swapping Bags of Oranges for Phones

Terms of trade sounds like an accountant's phrase; it is really a swap rate. Suppose this year one bag of oranges swaps for one phone. Next season, phone prices climb while orange prices sag — now the same phone costs FOUR bags. You planted more, picked more, shipped more, and can afford less. Your terms of trade deteriorated, and you did nothing to deserve it.

That drift is the quiet unfairness wired into world markets. Across the decades, raw products — the oranges, the ore, the beans — have tended to lose ground against manufactured goods, because almost anyone can grow oranges while only a few firms can build phones, and the phone-builders own the patents and the brands. Raw-material countries end up running up an escalator that is moving down.

What pushes back? Team rules, signed between countries. Trade blocs such as SADC and the African Continental Free Trade Area cancel the fees between neighbours, so that Africa can trade with Africa instead of only shipping raw cargo north. Agreements like AGOA wave South African cars and citrus through the American gate at a discount. Fair trade labels promise the farmer a floor price, so the swap rate cannot collapse underneath her mid-season. And the sharpest rule to argue in any essay is the rich world's own trick — subsidising its farmers until their produce undercuts African harvests in African markets.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the swap rate, why it drifts against raw sellers, and the team rules that push back.

## Subtopic: The Ladder the Tigers Climbed

Sixty years ago South Korea was as poor as almost anywhere on earth. Today it manufactures the phone in your pocket. The route was a ladder climbed one rung at a time — and it is the most argued-over ladder in all of economics.

Rung one: sew T-shirts — cheap labour, simple factories, sell everything abroad, bank every dollar. Rung two: pour those dollars into schools and steel mills. Rung three: ships. Rung four: cars. Rung five: semiconductors. On every rung the country sold outward to the world market — export-led — and reinvested the earnings into the rung above. China ran the identical ladder at many times the size.

Before calling that a recipe, read the label's warnings. The ladder needs buyers waiting at the top — it worked because rich markets stayed open, and they cannot absorb every country climbing at once. The bottom rungs bruise — the first generation works brutal hours at low pay so the next generation can study engineering. A tremor at the top, like 2008, shakes every climber below. And the smoke — East Asia postponed its environmental bill, and it was delivered as smog.

South Africa has hands on the ladder — the vehicle exports prove it — but we climb wearing a heavy backpack: power cuts, distant markets, a skills gap. So the safe verdict for your essays: export-led growth is a real ladder, proven by the Tigers, but it is not an escalator — it lifts only the climbers who grip it with education, infrastructure and a capable state.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the rungs in order, the warnings on the label, and South Africa's backpack. Hold these and the trade story is yours to argue from either side.
