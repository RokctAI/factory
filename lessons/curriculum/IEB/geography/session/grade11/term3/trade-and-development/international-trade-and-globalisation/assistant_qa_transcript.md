**Bianca:** Here's the one my study group fought about: if trade makes both sides better off, why does the lesson keep saying the game is rigged?

**Tutor:** Both statements are true, and holding them together is the whole skill. Trade genuinely benefits both sides at the moment of exchange — the ore exporter wanted the machine more than the ore, and the machine-maker wanted the ore. The rigging is not in any single deal; it is in the STRUCTURE of who sells what, repeated over decades. Value settles where processing happens, so the country selling raw materials banks a thin margin while the country selling manufactures banks a thick one — every transaction, every year. Then the terms of trade drift makes it worse: raw products face endless competitors and substitutes, while manufactures shelter behind patents and brands, so the swap rate slides against the raw seller even when nobody cheats. Add commodity volatility — Angola's budget lurching with the oil price — and you see a game where the rules are neutral on paper but the starting positions decide the outcome. So in an essay, never write that trade is simply good or simply exploitative. Write that trade rewards whoever adds the value and holds the patents, that the current structure assigns those roles unevenly, and that development strategy is precisely the effort to change your role in the game — from selling the beans to selling the chocolate. That is a balanced answer with a mechanism inside it, which is what earns the top band.

**Bianca:** I mix up all the trade arrangements — blocs, agreements, fair trade, protectionism. Is there a quick way to keep them apart?

**Tutor:** Sort them by one question: WHO is inside the fence, and which way does the fence face? Protectionism is a fence facing outward around one country — tariffs, quotas and subsidies shielding home industries from everyone else; its notorious case is rich-world farm subsidy, which lets subsidised European and American produce undercut African farmers on African soil. A trade bloc is a fence around a CLUB — members drop their fences toward each other but keep a shared one toward outsiders, or at least coordinate; that is the European Union, SADC in our region, and the African Continental Free Trade Area, whose whole purpose is to let African countries trade with each other instead of only shipping raw cargo to the north. A trade agreement is narrower: a negotiated gate between specific partners — AGOA is the gate through which South African vehicles and citrus enter the United States on preferential terms. Fair trade is different in kind: it is not a fence or a gate but a promise attached to a product — certified producers of wine, rooibos or cocoa receive a guaranteed floor price plus a social premium, trading a little market efficiency for stability the farmer can plan a life around. And free trade is the ideal of no fences at all — efficient in theory, but it puts unequal players in the same ring. Fence, club, gate, promise, no fences. Five images, five terms, permanently sorted.

**Bianca:** Globalisation questions ask for balance, but my balanced answers feel like fence-sitting. How do I get balance WITH a spine?

**Tutor:** Balance without a spine is a list; balance with a spine is a verdict that explains WHO wins and who loses, and why. Build it in three moves. First give the gains column honestly: access to world markets, inflows of investment and technology, hundreds of millions of manufacturing jobs — East Asia's escape from poverty is the strongest development story of the modern era, and pretending otherwise wrecks your credibility. Then give the costs column just as honestly: benefits clustering in a few favoured locations, TNCs out-bargaining small states — tax holidays, repatriated profits, the race to the bottom — infant industries flattened, like our clothing factories in Newcastle losing out to cheaper imports, and shocks arriving down the same wires, as 2008 proved within months. Now the spine, which is the third move most learners skip: notice that the two columns are not random. The winners are those holding skills, infrastructure and bargaining power; the losers are those without them. That single observation converts your two lists into one argument — globalisation is an engine that pulls unevenly, and the variable deciding your fate is your own preparedness. Land there and you can even attach the policy conclusion: the task is not to wall globalisation out but to build the skills and infrastructure that let a country ride it. That is a verdict, not a fence-sit, and it is built entirely from your two columns.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the export-led essay, what's the cleanest way to use South Korea without just re-telling its story?

**Tutor:** Use it as evidence inside an argument, not as a biography. The question behind every export-led essay is really: is this a recipe others can cook? So structure around that. State the claim the Tigers prove: a poor country CAN reach the developed world in two generations by selling manufactures outward and reinvesting the earnings — textiles to steel to ships to cars to semiconductors, with a state that sheltered infants, steered credit and demanded export success in return. Two sentences, story done. Now interrogate the recipe's ingredients, because that is where the marks sit. Ingredient one: open, hungry markets at the top — present then, crowded now, and they flood if everyone climbs at once. Ingredient two: unrepeatable conditions — the Tigers had access and geopolitical backing later climbers rarely get. Ingredient three: a price paid below — the first generation's low wages, long hours and suppressed unions, which any honest essay must weigh. Ingredient four: deferred costs — the smog was part of the bill. Then bring it home: South Africa runs the strategy's elements — vehicle programmes, citrus, AGOA — while carrying a backpack Korea never wore: power cuts, distance, a skills gap. Your conclusion writes itself: the ladder is real but not an escalator; it lifts countries that first build education, infrastructure and state capacity — which means the prerequisites for export-led growth are development achievements in their own right. One story, four tests, one verdict. That is analysis, and it reads nothing like a re-telling.
