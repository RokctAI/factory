**Bianca:** Everyone wants to know this one: if donors have selfish motives — influence, trade, security — does that make the aid itself bad?

**Tutor:** No, and separating motive from effect is one of the most grown-up analytical moves this topic lets you make. A vaccine does not check the giver's intentions before it works; the smallpox campaign eradicated the disease regardless of the geopolitics that funded it. So judge aid by its EFFECTS — did the clinic function, did the road carry traffic, did life expectancy climb — and treat motives as a separate, equally legitimate line of analysis. Where motives matter is in predicting the SHAPE aid takes. A donor pursuing influence tends to fund visible prestige projects over invisible maintenance; a donor pursuing trade ties tends toward procurement tying, so the money cycles home through its own firms; a donor pursuing security funds stability in strategically placed states rather than the poorest ones. Mixed motives, in other words, explain patterns in the aid map that pure charity cannot — why some countries are showered and others ignored, why some aid arrives with strings. In an essay, do both moves in sequence. First evaluate outcomes on the evidence, both pans of the scale. Then note that donor motives shape where and how aid flows, and that recipients negotiate best when they see the motives clearly. That reads as analysis. What loses marks is the shortcut in either direction — aid is generous therefore good, or aid is self-interested therefore bad. Both skip the evidence, and the evidence is where geography lives.

**Bianca:** The structural adjustment story confuses me. Cutting overspending sounds responsible — my parents budget like that. Why did it go so wrong?

**Tutor:** Because a household budget and a national economy respond differently to cuts, and the difference is the whole tragedy. When your parents cut spending, their income stays put — the salary still arrives, so the maths works. When a government cuts spending in a recession, the cut itself shrinks the economy: retrenched nurses stop buying from the spaza, the spaza owner's takings fall, tax revenue falls with them, and the budget hole the cut was meant to close partially reopens. Now add WHERE the cuts landed. Spending on clinics and classrooms is not consumption; it is investment with a twenty-year payback — the child kept healthy and schooled in 1990 is the productive worker of 2010. Cut there and the books look better this year while the country's future capacity quietly bleeds out. That is why the era's harshest legacy took a generation to surface: fewer trained nurses and teachers, weakened health systems, and in some countries a lost decade of human development. The fair balance for an essay: the economies in question genuinely had unsustainable debts and deficits — doing nothing was not an option — and some discipline was needed. The failure was in the design: one prescription for every patient, imposed from outside rather than negotiated, with the burden aimed at the social spending whose loss costs most and shows last. That is also exactly why the co-operation model insists recipients set their own priorities now.

**Bianca:** Gift of the Givers seems to work brilliantly. Why can't all aid just be run like an NGO?

**Tutor:** Because what makes a relief NGO brilliant is specialisation, and specialisation cuts both ways. Gift of the Givers excels at exactly the job it built itself for: speed. It holds pre-positioned supplies, trained volunteers and thin decision chains, so while formal bureaucracies are still convening meetings, its trucks are already moving — that is why it reached flooded KwaZulu-Natal streets in days and Turkish earthquake rubble within the week. Judge it as a fish-deliverer, and it is world class. But now scale the question up. A national antiretroviral programme needs decades of guaranteed drug procurement, thousands of salaried nurses, laboratory networks and a supply chain into every district — no NGO balance sheet survives that; it took Global Fund and PEPFAR money running THROUGH the state system to carry the world's largest treatment programme. Roads, power lines and water schemes need engineering budgets and maintenance commitments only concessional finance and governments sustain. And a thousand small NGOs, each excellent, create the fragmentation problem — a small ministry buried under a hundred projects, each with its own reports and logos. So the honest architecture assigns each actor its strength: NGOs for speed and last-mile reach, states and multilateral finance for scale and permanence, co-operation agreements to keep them pointed at one plan. In essay terms: name what NGOs do best, name what they cannot do, and resist the temptation to crown any single channel. The channels are tools, and tools are judged against jobs.

**Bianca:** For the big essay — does aid help or harm — how do I structure it so it doesn't collapse into 'a bit of both'?

**Tutor:** 'A bit of both' is a shrug; what you want is a verdict with conditions, and there is a clean four-part build. Part one: two pans, evidence in each, mechanisms attached. Triumphs — smallpox eradicated, ARVs restoring South African life expectancy from the low fifties to above sixty, malaria deaths cut, relief reaching flood victims in days. Failures — free food that beached local farmers, loans that became budget-eating debt, tied aid cycling money home, governments answering to donors instead of voters. Never list a failure without its mechanism; the mechanism is what proves you understand rather than remember. Part two: notice aloud that both pans are real, so the question cannot be about aid in general — it must be about WHICH aid, under WHICH conditions. That sentence is your pivot, and it lifts the essay above the shrug. Part three: extract the conditions from your own evidence. The successes share a profile — specific, measurable, finishable problems, aligned with the recipient's own plan, sustained to completion. The failures share the opposite — open-ended, supply-driven, donor-designed, permanent. Part four: land on the scaffolding sentence. Aid is scaffolding: essential while the building rises, never mistaken for the house, designed from day one to come down. That closing image is a verdict — it says yes, aid works, WHEN it is built to make itself unnecessary — and it leaves the reader with your argument compressed into something they can see. Evidence, pivot, conditions, image. Four parts, no shrug.
