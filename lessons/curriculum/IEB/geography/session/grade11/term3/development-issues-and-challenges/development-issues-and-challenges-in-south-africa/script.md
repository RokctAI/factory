# Part 1 — Expert

Why does development remain so difficult to achieve? This session takes on the web of factors that hold countries back — and insists from the first minute that they are COMPLEX and INTERRELATED, never one convenient villain. From there it narrows to South Africa's own particular burdens, looks at community-based development in rural and urban settings, and closes with the uncomfortable relationship between development and the environment it depends on.

## Subtopic: The Tangle of Factors That Hold Development Back

Learn the factors as a chain in which every link pulls on the others.

ACCESS TO RESOURCES: some countries sit on fertile soil, minerals and reliable rivers; others are landlocked, drought-prone or resource-poor. Yet resources decide nothing by themselves. Angola pumps oil and most Angolans stay poor; Botswana turned its diamonds into schools, clinics and one of Africa's steadiest economies. When mineral wealth is captured by a narrow elite and fuels conflict instead of classrooms, geographers call it the resource curse — it is never the rocks, it is what a country does with them.

ENERGY: without dependable power there are no factory shifts, no vaccine fridges, no irrigation pumps, no studying after sunset. Hundreds of millions of Africans remain unconnected, and even wired economies seize up when supply fails — South Africa's rolling blackouts have drained billions of rand and untold jobs from the economy.

HISTORY: colonial powers drew borders that split peoples and forced rivals together, laid railways from mine to harbour rather than from town to town, and schooled only a tiny elite; apartheid then manufactured underdevelopment on purpose. Today's poverty maps still shadow those old lines.

TRADE IMBALANCES: exporting cheap raw materials while importing expensive manufactured goods bleeds away exactly the capital development requires — a mechanism this term returns to.

POPULATION GROWTH: when numbers rise quickly, every year's gains are split among more people, and schools and clinics chase a target that keeps moving.

EDUCATION AND TRAINING: the master key. Without skills, investors find no workforce and new technology finds no users; every development success of the past century put education first.

NATURAL-RESOURCE LIMITS and ENVIRONMENTAL DEGRADATION: exhausted soils, stripped forests and emptied fishing grounds subtract from next year's production, so poverty forces short-term choices that dig poverty deeper.

The word that unlocks every question here is INTERRELATED. A drought cuts the harvest, so a teenager leaves school to earn, so the district's skills shrink, so investment goes elsewhere, so incomes fall, so the land is overworked, so the next drought cuts deeper still. Treat any single factor in isolation and you have missed the whole design of the topic.

The questions for this section are ready: name the factors, show how they interlock, and apply the resource curse accurately.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Particular Challenges

South Africa carries the universal burdens plus a set stamped by its own past.

The APARTHEID SPATIAL LEGACY: forced removals and the homeland system deliberately settled millions far from work, on the poorest land, with the thinnest services. The laws are gone; the map they drew remains. Township workers still spend hours and heavy shares of their wages commuting, and the former homeland districts are still the poorest places in the country.

INEQUALITY AND UNEMPLOYMENT: a Gini coefficient near 0,63 alongside unemployment above thirty per cent — and above forty per cent among young people — sits at the centre of every other problem. An economy that expands without absorbing workers stores up pressure rather than releasing it.

EDUCATION AND SKILLS: enrolment is high but outcomes are weak, so the pipeline leaks — firms advertise for artisans and engineers they cannot find while millions search for work. The bottleneck is the mismatch, not a shortage of ambition.

SERVICE DELIVERY: since 1994 millions of households have gained housing, electricity and piped water — a real achievement by any standard — yet backlogs, collapsing municipal maintenance and protests over dry taps and dark streets show the delivery machinery under severe strain.

HEALTH: the HIV and Aids epidemic slashed life expectancy before the world's largest antiretroviral programme bent the curve back upward; tuberculosis and the quiet rise of lifestyle diseases continue to load the clinics.

ENERGY INSECURITY: rolling blackouts are a development problem, not a mere irritation — they switch off the spaza shop's fridge, the clinic's vaccine cold chain and the factory's production line, and they frighten off the investment that would have created jobs.

The assessment skill is balance. Enormous post-1994 gains in access to services, and social grants that hold millions above destitution, stand beside stubborn unemployment, extreme inequality and institutional strain. Both halves are true at once, and an essay that argues only one half earns only half.

Work through this section's questions now: the spatial legacy, the inequality and unemployment figures, and the balanced judgement.

## Subtopic: Community-Based Development: Rural and Urban Approaches

Grand top-down projects often sail straight past the poorest, so COMMUNITY-BASED DEVELOPMENT starts at the other end: the community names its own needs, contributes its own labour and knowledge, and owns whatever gets built. The principles to memorise: PARTICIPATION — residents plan, they do not merely receive; respect for local knowledge; technology appropriate to the place; skills transferred so the project outlives its funders; and ownership, because people maintain what belongs to them.

Rural cases worth naming. The WORKING FOR WETLANDS programme employs local teams to rebuild eroded wetlands — plugging gullies, replanting reeds — so that catchments hold and filter water again, while workers earn wages and portable skills. In the Cederberg, small rooibos farmers around Wupperthal organised into cooperatives that process and market their tea jointly, keeping profits in the valley that a lone grower could never negotiate. Community food gardens and stokvel savings circles build food security and working capital in places commercial banks never visit.

Urban cases. At Warwick Junction in Durban, planners worked WITH the thousands of street traders who pass through the precinct daily — designing shelters, storage and cooking facilities around their actual trade — and turned a dangerous interchange into one of the city's most productive informal economies. In Stellenbosch's Enkanini settlement, a community-run solar project brought pay-as-you-go electricity to shacks years before the grid could arrive. And upgrading informal settlements IN PLACE — delivering tenure, water, electricity and walkways where people already live — has largely replaced demolition and distant relocation, because relocation was severing the job links and social networks that keep poor households alive.

Now the critical sentence that earns evaluation marks: community projects are small against national need, can be captured by local elites, and lean on patient funding. They complement state-scale delivery; they cannot substitute for it. The strongest answers name a case, its principle, its gains, and this limit.

Attend to this section's questions: the principles, one rural and one urban case in detail, and the honest limitation.

## Subtopic: Development and the Environment

Development remakes the environment that carries it, and this topic demands you face that trade-off squarely.

The damage is concrete and South African. Mining: seepage from old workings turns groundwater acidic and metal-laden, and the Olifants River, threading through coalfields, ranks among the country's most polluted. Energy: coal-fired stations around eMalahleni make that airshed one of the dirtiest anywhere, settling on the same Mpumalanga soils that grow the nation's maize. Agriculture: overgrazing on communal rangelands strips topsoil that washes downstream as silt — dams in the Eastern Cape lose storage year by year to it — while careless irrigation slowly salts the soil. Urbanisation: settlements spread across wetlands and aquifer recharge zones, and failing sewage works pour into rivers such as the Hennops and the Vaal. Industry: effluent, waste dumps and greenhouse gases — coal dependence places South Africa among the world's heaviest per-person carbon emitters.

Two truths must be held simultaneously. First, environmental damage is anti-development in the medium run: eroded soil grows less food, polluted water sickens workers, a degraded coastline loses its tourists. Destruction is not the price of development; it is a loan against it, repaid with interest — and repaid first by the poor, who live nearest the damage and can least afford to move away from it. Second, poverty itself degrades environments: households without electricity strip the last woodland for fuel, and hungry herders overstock fragile veld. So environment and development are not opponents; they are partners in one solution — SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT, meeting present needs without compromising future generations. Its tools have names: environmental impact assessments required before major projects, legally binding rehabilitation duties on mines, protected areas that convert wild land into tourism income, and the growing shift from coal to sun and wind.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: two damage cases anchored to places, the loan-against-development argument, and the poverty-degradation circle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same session again in three ordinary pictures: a knot, a stoep meeting, and a borrowed roof. Every fact kept.

## Subtopic: The Knot in the Earphones

Why is poverty so stubborn? Because its causes arrive pre-tangled, like earphones pulled from a school bag. Tug one wire and the knot tightens somewhere else.

Watch the knot close around one West Coast fishing family. The fish stocks thin out — that is the resource wire. The catch shrinks, so the eldest daughter leaves school to gut and pack at the harbour — the education wire pulls tight. Without matric there is no better job later; the family sells whole fish cheaply to a buyer who trucks it away, then buys tinned fish back at four times the price — the trade wire. The settlement has no reliable electricity, so no ice machine, no freezer, no selling on the family's own terms — the energy wire. And beneath everything, the family lives on this windblown edge because their grandparents were moved off the front-line fishing grounds by decree — the history wire, knotted through every other one.

Here is the skill: never explain one wire on its own. The marks live in the tangling — thin catches pull a child from school, no school pulls down wages, low wages pull the sea harder, and the stocks thin further. Write the chain and use the exact word interrelated.

One wire has a strange name: the resource curse. Treasure under the floor should make everyone rich — but when a few hands grab it, the treasure pays for their quarrels and the rest stay poor. Angola has the oil; Botswana took its diamonds and built schools. Never the rocks — always what a country does with them.

The questions for this piece are with you now: the wires, the tangling, and the curse.

## Subtopic: The Meeting on the Stoep

Two ways to mend a struggling neighbourhood. Way one: distant officials design something big, trucks arrive, the thing is built, the officials leave — and too often the pump rusts because nobody nearby can repair it or feels it is theirs. Way two begins with chairs dragged onto a stoep: residents saying what they actually need, planning it, building it with their own hands, and owning it afterwards. That is community-based development, and its golden rule fits on one line — people maintain what is THEIRS.

Real stoep-projects to name. Working for Wetlands pays local crews to heal torn wetlands — plugging the gullies, replanting the reeds — so the catchment holds water again while the crew banks wages and skills. In the Cederberg, the rooibos farmers of Wupperthal pooled their harvests into a cooperative, processed the tea themselves, and finally saw prices no single farmer at a farm gate ever got. At Warwick Junction in Durban, the city planned WITH its street traders — shelter, storage, cooking bays along the routes they already worked — and a feared interchange became a thriving market. In Enkanini outside Stellenbosch, residents run their own solar service, lighting shacks the grid had not reached. And the modern rule for informal settlements is upgrade IN PLACE — bring taps, tenure and power to where lives are already built, instead of trucking families away from their jobs and their people.

Keep one honest sentence ready for the evaluation mark: stoep projects are powerful but small — they will never build a national grid or a highway. Community hands AND state muscle, each doing what only it can.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the golden rule, two named projects, and the honest limit.

## Subtopic: Borrowing From Tomorrow's Roof

Picture a family burning its own roof beams to stay warm through winter. Warm tonight; drenched when the rains come. That is development that eats its environment — not progress, but a loan from tomorrow, repaid with interest.

South Africa signs such loans on every side. Coal country around eMalahleni keeps the lights burning AND breathes some of the world's dirtiest air, over the very soils that grow the nation's maize. The Olifants River carries the acids and metals of a century of mining past farms that must irrigate from it — a bill still arriving long after the profits were spent. Overgrazed communal slopes wash into Eastern Cape dams as silt, so the land loses its skin and the towns lose their water storage in a single motion. And notice where the bill is delivered: to the shack settlement beside the dump, drinking from the brown river — never to the estate upwind.

Then flip the coin, because both directions score. Poverty also strips the environment: no electricity means felling the last stand of trees for firewood; no income means one cow too many on exhausted veld. Poverty damages environment damages poverty — the knot from earlier, tied around the land itself.

So the answer is not to stop building; it is to build without burning the beams. The tools have names: impact studies before any big project, mines legally bound to rehabilitate what they dig, parks that turn wild coastline and bushveld into tourism wages, and the steady swing from coal toward sun and wind. One sentence gathers the whole term: meet today's needs without stealing from your grandchildren's — development built to be standing when they arrive.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the roof-beam loan, the bill that lands on the poor, and the tools that build differently. Hold these and the whole development story stands together.
