# Part 1 — Expert

The third term moves from the physical earth to its people, and to the largest question human geography asks: why does a child born in one country face a long, schooled, comfortable life while a child born in another faces the opposite — and what, honestly, can be done about it? This session assembles the toolkit for the whole term: the precise meaning of development, the vocabulary that keeps your answers sharp, the indicators that turn opinion into measurement, and a fair way of comparing countries that refuses to sort the world into two crude piles.

## Subtopic: What Development Means — and What It Does Not

DEVELOPMENT is the process by which people's quality of life improves — their incomes, certainly, but just as much their health, their schooling, their safety, their freedom to choose and their dignity. Keep that double-barrelled definition close, because the oldest mistake in this topic is to treat development as a synonym for getting richer.

Set it against ECONOMIC GROWTH. Growth simply means the economy produced more this year than last — a larger pile of goods and services. Growth is real and it matters, but it is silent on the question of who benefits. An economy can expand impressively while most citizens feel nothing, if the new wealth gathers in a handful of accounts. Development only happens when growth is converted into better lives — water in the taps, medicine on the clinic shelves, learners finishing school. The sentence to carry into any essay is this one: growth is necessary for development, but it is not sufficient.

Two more terms travel as a pair. STANDARD OF LIVING is the material half of life — what your income can buy in housing, goods and services. QUALITY OF LIFE is the whole of it — the material half plus health, education, security, a livable environment and a political voice. Think of a shift-worker at a smelter earning a solid wage while the plant's fumes settle over his street: a decent standard of living, a compromised quality of life. Being able to prise those two apart is a skill that earns marks all term.

Finally the labels. MEDCs — more economically developed countries — include Canada, the Netherlands and New Zealand. LEDCs — less economically developed countries — include Niger, Mozambique and Nepal. Between the two sit the NICs, the newly industrialised countries whose factories rose within a generation: India, Malaysia, Turkey, and on most lists South Africa. The old First World and Third World labels have aged badly; prefer MEDC, LEDC and NIC, with developed and developing as the acceptable plain-language pair.

The questions for this section are with you now: development against growth, standard of living against quality of life, and the labels placed correctly.

## Subtopic: Measuring Development in Money: GDP, GNP and the Gini Coefficient

Comparison needs measurement, and the money measures come first.

GDP — gross domestic product — totals the value of everything produced INSIDE a country's borders in a year, whoever owns the firms doing the producing. GNP — gross national product — totals everything produced by a country's OWN citizens and companies, wherever on the planet they happen to operate. Take the BMW plant at Rosslyn outside Pretoria: it stands on South African soil, so its cars enter South Africa's GDP — but the company is German, so the same production belongs to Germany's GNP. Flip it around: when MTN earns revenue from its networks in Nigeria, that activity sits in Nigeria's GDP and in South Africa's GNP. Domestic follows the soil; national follows the passport.

Raw totals deceive, because populous countries produce large totals almost automatically. The correction is GDP PER CAPITA — divide the total by the population to get output per person — so that a small wealthy country is no longer buried under a big poor one's sheer size. South Africa's GDP per capita of roughly six thousand US dollars stands well above Mozambique's few hundred and far below Switzerland's ninety thousand: the classic signature of a middle-income country.

Yet per capita is an average, and an average says nothing about spread. That is the job of the GINI COEFFICIENT, which scores income inequality from 0 — every person earning exactly the same — to 1, where a single person earns everything. The Nordic countries sit near 0,27. South Africa's figure of about 0,63 ranks among the very highest recorded anywhere, which is how a middle-income average can share a country with mass poverty. Hold the pairing in one line: per capita measures how big each person's share of the cake would be; the Gini measures how the cake is actually cut.

Work through this section's questions now: soil versus passport, the per capita correction, and what 0,63 reveals that the average conceals.

## Subtopic: Beyond Money: HDI and the Social Indicators

Money is a means; the social indicators measure the ends.

LIFE EXPECTANCY is the average number of years a newborn can expect to live, and it condenses an entire health system into a single figure — nutrition, safe water, clinics, disease control, road safety. Japan passes 84 years; several LEDCs remain in the fifties; South Africa, recovering strongly since antiretroviral treatment became universal, now stands in the mid-sixties.

INFANT MORTALITY RATE counts deaths of babies before their first birthday per one thousand live births, and it is the single most sensitive indicator geographers own. A baby survives only when everything works simultaneously — the mother's antenatal visits, the vaccines, the sanitation, the household income for food. Finland records about 2; the hardest-pressed states exceed 60; South Africa has come down to the mid-twenties. When that number falls year after year, development is genuinely happening.

Alongside these sit literacy and years of schooling, which measure whether people can seize opportunity, and access to safe water and electricity, which measure whether the state delivers.

The HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX — HDI — was built to force money and wellbeing into one score. It averages three dimensions: health, through life expectancy; education, through years of schooling; and income, through GNI per capita. The result lies between 0 and 1. Switzerland and Norway exceed 0,95; Niger and Chad fall below 0,45; South Africa scores about 0,71 — carried by income and schooling, held back by health. That resistance to being bought is the index's entire point: no country can purchase a top score while its people die young, so the HDI exposes precisely the gap between wealth and wellbeing that GDP cannot see.

Attend to this section's questions: why infant mortality is uniquely revealing, the three HDI dimensions, and why South Africa's HDI sits below its income rank.

## Subtopic: A Continuum, Not Two Boxes

The last tool is judgement about the categories themselves.

In 1980 the BRANDT LINE drew a boundary between a wealthy global North and a poorer global South, curving under Europe and North America and dropping south to gather in Australia and New Zealand. As a first sketch of global inequality it still earns its place on the page. But it is a photograph of one moment, and the scene has moved: Singapore and South Korea, both south of the line, now out-earn much of Europe, while several economies north of the line have stagnated.

The more truthful image is a CONTINUUM — every country occupying its own position on an unbroken line from least to most developed, with no empty stretch and no clean break where a boundary could honestly be drawn. And the continuum runs inside countries too. In eThekwini, the glass offices of Umhlanga and the informal settlements of Inanda belong to one metro; the Western Cape's economy operates in a different register from the former Transkei districts of the Eastern Cape. Differences WITHIN a country can be as steep as differences between countries — which is exactly why the Gini coefficient belongs in this topic.

Placing South Africa is the classic essay task, so gather the evidence on both sides. For development: an upper-middle-income economy, a diversified industrial base, deep financial markets, HDI around 0,71. Against: unemployment above thirty per cent, a Gini near 0,63, life expectancy in the sixties. The defensible verdict is that South Africa is a developing country — a newly industrialised one — holding pockets of MEDC prosperity and pockets of LEDC deprivation, and any answer that grabs one box without weighing the evidence surrenders the evaluation marks.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: the Brandt line and its limits, the continuum, and the evidence-led placement of South Africa.

## Subtopic: Two Streets in One Town

Take a Karoo town and stand on two of its streets, five minutes apart. On the first, the road is tarred, the taps run all day, the clinic keeps its fridge stocked with vaccines, and the school's matric class has a study centre with wifi. On the second, the road is gravel, water arrives by municipal tanker twice a week, and the nearest nurse visits on Thursdays. The first street shows you what development looks like when it lands; the second shows you its absence. Development is the long work of bringing the second street up to the first — and read the list again: tar, taps, vaccines, teachers. Most of it is not simply money.

That gap is also the gap between two terms you must keep separate. STANDARD OF LIVING is what your pay packet buys — the stove, the phone, the roof over you. QUALITY OF LIFE is the whole picture — add health, schooling, safety, clean air, a say in decisions. A farm foreman earning well while drinking from a contaminated borehole has the first without the second.

And growth versus development? Suppose a solar farm rises outside town and the district's output jumps — but the dividends flow to investors overseas and the town sees three permanent jobs. The district GREW; the town did not DEVELOP. Growth is rain falling on the catchment; development is water actually reaching the taps.

The questions for this piece are with you now: the two streets, the two terms, and the rain that never reached the taps.

## Subtopic: The Scoreboard Problem

Comparing countries is like comparing cricket clubs, and the trap is reading one number off the scoreboard.

Total runs scored in a season — that is GDP, the whole economy's output. But a club fielding forty players will always out-total a club fielding eight, so divide: runs per player, which is GDP per capita, output per person. Better already. Now the subtler wrinkle — WHERE and by WHOM. Every run scored at your home ground, whoever bats, is GDP: production inside the borders. Every run scored by your own contracted players, at whatever ground in the world they tour, is GNP. The BMW plant at Rosslyn bats at South Africa's ground — but it scores for Germany's club.

Last, the cruel trick averages play. A team totals one hundred and ten, and the scoreboard average says ten runs a player — healthy enough. Then you read the batting card: one player made ninety-one, and the other ten scraped nineteen between them. Same average, hollow team. That is South Africa's income story: a respectable average beside a Gini coefficient of about 0,63 — among the most unequal score-sheets on earth. The Gini runs from 0, all equal, to 1, one player scores the lot. So carry both numbers everywhere: the average sizes each person's slice of the cake; the Gini tells you how the knife actually cut.

Your questions for this section are ready now: whose ground, runs per player, and the batting card behind the average.

## Subtopic: The Report Card With Three Subjects

Money is one subject on a country's report card. The HDI insists on marking three.

Subject one, health — graded by life expectancy, the years a newborn can expect. Subject two, education — graded by years of schooling. Subject three, income — earnings per person. Average the three marks and the country scores between 0 and 1. Switzerland sits above 0,95 — distinctions across the card. Niger sits below 0,45. South Africa earns about 0,71: strong in income, solid in schooling, pulled down hard by health. And that is deliberate design — no country can cram the money subject and top the class, which is exactly how the index catches states that are rich on paper and unwell in person.

The sharpest single test on the card is also the smallest: INFANT MORTALITY, babies lost before age one per thousand born. Keeping an infant alive needs everything working at once — the clinic visits, the cold-chain for vaccines, clean water, food in the house. South Africa's figure has fallen to the mid-twenties, and a falling line there is development you can trust.

Close by discarding the two-box map of the world. The Brandt line of 1980 split rich North from poor South, but Singapore long since crossed it, and our own metros hold Umhlanga and Inanda inside one municipal boundary. Picture instead a long staircase with every step occupied. South Africa stands mid-staircase — a developing country with one foot on a high step and one on a low one. Argue it that way, with the numbers in hand, and the evaluation marks follow.

Here come the final questions of the lesson — the three subjects, the infant test, and South Africa's honest place on the staircase. Secure these and every development topic this term stands on solid ground.
