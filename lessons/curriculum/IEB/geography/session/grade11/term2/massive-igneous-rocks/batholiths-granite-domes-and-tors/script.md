# Part 1 — Expert

Not all of South Africa stands on layered rock. Beneath and between the strata lie enormous bodies of MASSIVE igneous rock — magma that rose into the crust, stalled, and cooled underground into structureless masses with no layering at all. Erosion has since uncovered many of them, and they build some of the most recognisable scenery in the country. Four movements this session: the family of intrusions from batholith to sill, classified by shape and by their relationship to the surrounding layers; the landform each one becomes when erosion finally exposes it; the granite dome and the peeling process that keeps it round; and the tor, a stack of boulders assembled underground long before anyone could see it.

## Subtopic: The Intrusion Family — Batholith to Sill

Magma that never reaches the surface cools slowly at depth into INTRUSIVE igneous rock — granite and dolerite are the local staples — and slow cooling grows the large crystals that make these rocks coarse-grained and tough. Intrusions are classified by SIZE, by SHAPE, and above all by how they sit against the layering of the surrounding COUNTRY ROCK. An intrusion that slips in BETWEEN the strata, lying parallel with them, is CONCORDANT; one that smashes ACROSS the layering is DISCORDANT. That single distinction unlocks every identification question in the topic.

The head of the family is the BATHOLITH: a colossal, deep-seated mass of granite, discordant, tens to hundreds of kilometres across, its top gently domed and its floor never found. The Cape Granite Suite — the rock beneath the winelands, shouldering out of the sea in the giant rounded boulders of the Peninsula's coves — crystallised in batholiths beneath the Cape well over five hundred million years ago. The LACCOLITH is the pocket-sized concordant cousin: magma injected between strata with enough push to arch the overlying layers upward, setting into a mushroom shape — flat floor, domed roof.

The LOPOLITH inverts the laccolith: a vast concordant intrusion so heavy that it sagged the strata BENEATH it into a saucer. South Africa holds the largest on the planet — the BUSHVELD IGNEOUS COMPLEX north of Pretoria, whose layered saucer supplies most of the world's platinum along with chrome and vanadium. And then the thin sheets. A DYKE is discordant: magma frozen in a near-vertical fracture, a buried wall cutting across the strata. A SILL is concordant: a near-horizontal sheet driven between layers — and the Karoo dolerite sills capping the flat-topped koppies of the earlier sessions are the everyday South African example.

Take this section's questions now: concordant against discordant, the five intrusions identified by shape, and the Bushveld Complex and Karoo sills placed on the map of the country.

## Subtopic: From Buried Intrusion to Standing Landform

An intrusion begins its life invisible, sealed under kilometres of country rock. Two steps turn it into scenery: erosion strips the cover, and then differential erosion takes over — because granite and dolerite usually out-resist the sedimentary rock around them, the exposed intrusion stands while its softer surroundings waste away.

Each family member surfaces in its own costume. A BATHOLITH, huge and structureless, weathers into ranges of massive rounded granite mountains and bald dome country — the granite hills of the Lowveld around White River, with the great dome of Legogote rising over the plantations, show a batholith's roof emerging from its cover. A LACCOLITH betrays itself first as a rounded hill where the arched strata above it thin out, and finally as a granite-cored dome mountain once the roof is stripped entirely. The LOPOLITH, being a downward saucer, seldom makes a single peak: the Bushveld Complex floors a broad basin rimmed by higher country, and reveals itself less through skyline than through economy — the arc of platinum and chrome mines tracing its layered edge past Rustenburg and Mokopane.

The sheets carve the sharpest lines in the landscape. A resistant DYKE emerges as a long, narrow, dead-straight WALL of rock striding across country regardless of hills and rivers — Karoo dolerite dykes often show from the air as dark ruler-lines of denser bush across the veld; where a dyke is softer than its surroundings it leaves a straight trench instead. A resistant SILL emerges as a horizontal cliff band running at one level along a valley side, as the protective cap of a mesa, or as a waterfall's lip — at Howick, the Umgeni River pours ninety-five metres over the edge of a dolerite sill in a single leap. Their map signatures are opposites: the dyke a straight ridge indifferent to drainage and contours, the sill a cliff line that clings faithfully to one contour level.

This section's questions are ready: the two-step exposure story, each intrusion matched to its landform, and dyke against sill on the map.

## Subtopic: Granite Domes and Exfoliation

Why does exposed granite so often stand as smooth, bare, rounded DOMES — the shining head of Legogote, the whaleback summits of the Lowveld, the great bornhardts of Spitzkoppe across the Namibian border? Because granite carries inside itself the mechanism of its own rounding.

Granite that crystallised kilometres underground did so squeezed by the entire weight of the rock above it. Erosion then spends millions of years removing that load. As the weight comes off, the granite expands — minutely, but irresistibly — the way a diver's chest eases as the pressure falls on the way up. The expansion fractures the rock in curved sheets lying parallel to the ground surface: SHEET JOINTS, also called unloading joints, nested one beneath another around the mass. The process is PRESSURE RELEASE or UNLOADING — physical weathering caused not by temperature but by the removal of weight.

Once the sheet joints exist, ordinary weathering exploits them. Water works into the curved cracks, day-night temperature swings flex the outer shell, and sheet after sheet loosens and slides off the dome's shoulders in great curved slabs. This shedding is EXFOLIATION — the word dermatologists use for skin, applied to mountains — and its geometric consequence is the key sentence of the subtopic: because the sheets are curved, every loss leaves a SMOOTHER, MORE ROUNDED surface underneath. The dome does not erode into ruin; it erodes into better roundness, renewing its own curves with every slab it drops. The shed slabs lie broken around the base like pieces of a giant's crockery.

The finished landform — a bald, smooth granite swell rising abruptly from the plain — is an EXFOLIATION DOME, and a large isolated one standing over an erosion plain earns the name BORNHARDT, a term born in Africa. The Lowveld's granite heads gleaming after summer rain, and Spitzkoppe's towers over the Namib gravel plains, tell the identical story in two countries.

Work through the questions for this section: unloading explained, sheet joints linked to exfoliation, and the self-rounding dome placed with named examples.

## Subtopic: Tors — Boulder Stacks Born Underground

A TOR is a small castle of rounded granite boulders balanced on a hillcrest or slope — three, four, five great stones stacked as though placed by hand. No hand placed them. The stacking was done underground, by chemistry, long before the boulders ever met the sky.

Granite masses are broken by JOINTS — natural cracks in roughly vertical and horizontal sets that divide the rock into rectangular blocks, like invisible brickwork. While the granite still lies buried beneath its blanket of soil and rotten rock, water seeps along every joint, and CHEMICAL WEATHERING attacks each block from its cracks inward. The attack is unfair by geometry: a block's CORNERS are assaulted from three directions at once, its edges from two, its faces from only one — so corners decay first and every block rounds off underground into a smooth CORESTONE, floating in its own decayed wrapping of crumbly, gritty rotten granite called GRUS. Joint spacing decides the survivors: closely jointed granite breaks into small blocks that may rot away completely; widely spaced joints leave big corestones intact.

Then comes the second stage: EXHUMATION. Sheetwash, streams and soil creep strip the soft grus away, and the corestones emerge — still stacked in the positions the old joint pattern gave them, now freestanding in the air as a tor. The deep rotting generally belongs to warmer, wetter climatic episodes and the stripping to drier ones, so a single tor is a two-climate archive. For the marks, the order is everything: joint-guided chemical rotting FIRST, underground; stripping and exposure SECOND, at the surface.

South African granite country is generous with examples: the stacked boulder koppies scattered through the Lowveld between White River and Hazyview, the balancing rocks of the Polokwane and Modjadjiskloof granites in Limpopo, and the corestone-strewn slopes of the Cape's granite fringe — while Spitzkoppe's aprons across the border mix domes and tor stacks in one view. That mixing is no accident, and it yields the closing rule: on one granite mass, wide joint spacing gives domes, close spacing gives tors. One rock, two crack-spacings, two landforms.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: joints, corestones and grus underground, exhumation afterwards, and the joint-spacing rule that chooses between dome and tor.

## Subtopic: The Concrete That Set Underground

Picture the old layered crust as a stack of timber boards, and picture magma as wet concrete being pumped up into the stack from below. Sometimes the concrete blasts right through the top — that is a volcano, another lesson. This lesson is about every pour that got stuck and set inside the stack. Each stuck shape has a name, and the names are only shapes.

The batholith is the full underground pour: a monster mass of granite with no known bottom, bulldozing across every board it meets. The laccolith is a pocket pour that pushed in between two boards and jacked the ceiling up into a blister — flat floor, arched roof. The lopolith is the pocket pour that went the other way: so heavy it bowed its floor downward into a saucer — and our Bushveld Complex is the heaviest saucer on Earth, loaded with platinum instead of cement. Then the thin runs: concrete squeezed up a vertical crack sets into a dyke — a buried wall cutting across the boards; concrete squeezed sideways between two boards sets into a sill — a buried shelf lying with them.

Now the two-word test that sorts all five on any diagram: does the shape lie WITH the boards, or CUT across them? Shelf, blister and saucer lie with the layers — concordant. Wall and monster cut across — discordant. Ask "lies with or cuts?" and the identification marks are yours before you have finished reading the question.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the five set shapes, the pour behind each, and the lies-with-or-cuts test.

## Subtopic: The Orange-Peel Mountain

Bring the buried granite up into the daylight and watch what freedom does to it. For its whole life the granite sat squashed under kilometres of rock, like a hiker pinned under a monstrous backpack. Erosion slowly unpacks the bag — strips the cover rock away — and the granite finally straightens its shoulders: it swells, just slightly, in every direction at once.

A swelling rock must crack, and granite cracks in the neatest way imaginable: in curved shells wrapped parallel to the surface, one inside the next, exactly like the layers of peel on an orange. Rain works into those curved cracks, blazing days and cold nights flex them, and eventually the outer peel loosens and slides off the mountain's shoulders in huge curved slabs. That shedding is exfoliation — the same word from the skincare bottles, meaning the same thing: lose the outer layer, reveal a fresher one beneath.

And here is the wonderful trick of it. A curved peel can only come off a curve — so every slab the mountain sheds leaves it ROUNDER than before. Granite does not crumble into shapeless rubble; it polishes itself, century after century, into a smooth bald dome. That is why the granite heads of the Lowveld shine above the trees after rain, and why Spitzkoppe rises out of the Namib like the back of a surfacing whale. From now on, every bare rounded rock summit you see should read like a receipt: buried granite — backpack removed — cracked into orange peels — peeling itself rounder forever.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the backpack coming off, the orange-peel shells, and why peeling perfects the dome instead of destroying it.

## Subtopic: Soap Blocks and the Secret of the Stacked Boulders

Those impossible towers of round boulders on granite koppies — who stacked them? You can solve the mystery at the kitchen sink with a crate of cube-shaped soap bars.

Pack the soap cubes into a crate in neat rows and leave the crate under a slowly dripping tap for a month. Water finds every gap between the cubes and works on each bar from its gaps inward — but not evenly. Every CORNER is dissolved from three sides at once, every edge from two, every flat face from only one. Corners go first, so each cube rounds itself into a ball, sitting in its own slippery soap-mush. Buried granite is the crate. The rock comes pre-cracked into blocks by joints; groundwater creeps along the joints for tens of thousands of years, rotting each block from its corners inward; and every block becomes a hard rounded corestone resting in its own crumbly rot — the gritty stuff called grus.

Then the reveal. Rain, rivulets and creeping soil carry the soft grus away, and the hard survivors are left standing exactly where the old joint pattern stacked them — a freestanding tower of rounded boulders. A tor. Nothing built it; everything around it was removed. And the sequence is where the marks live: rot FIRST, underground; strip SECOND, in the open air.

One final rule ties the whole granite story into a bow. Where the joints are far apart, the blocks are too big to round into balls — weathering can only smooth the whole mass into a dome. Where the joints crowd close, the blocks are small enough to become corestones and stack into tors. Same granite, same water, same time — the spacing of the cracks alone decides whether the hill ends up a whale's back or a giant's tower of stones. Some Lowveld koppies show both answers standing side by side.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the soap-bar corners, the two stages in their strict order, and the joint-spacing rule. Answer them, and no stack of balanced boulders will ever look like an accident to you again.
