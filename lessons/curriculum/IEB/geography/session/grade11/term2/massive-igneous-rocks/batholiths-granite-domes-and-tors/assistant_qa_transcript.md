**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: dolerite sills keep appearing in two different topics — they capped the mesas in the flat-strata lesson, and now they are back as members of the intrusion family. When a question mentions a sill, which lesson's answer does it want?

**Tutor:** Let the question's verb choose for you, because the sill honestly belongs to both stories and plays a different role in each. By ORIGIN, a sill is an igneous intrusion: magma driven horizontally between strata, concordant, coarse-grained from slow cooling. So when the question asks what a sill IS, how it formed, or asks you to label intrusions on a cross-section, answer from this lesson — emplacement, concordance, the dolerite composition. But by FUNCTION in the landscape, an old sill is simply the hardest layer in the stack it invaded. From the moment erosion reaches it, it behaves as cap rock: shielding mesas and buttes, holding cliff bands at one level along valley sides, forming the lip the Umgeni pours over at Howick. So when the question asks about landforms, scarp retreat or why a hill keeps its flat top, answer from the layered-landscape lesson and treat the sill as the resistant stratum in a differential-erosion story. The dyke lives the same double life — intrusion by birth, straight resistant wall by landscape career. One rock, two job descriptions; the wording of the question is the employer you answer to.

**Bianca:** Next one: laccolith and lopolith. The words are two letters apart and half the class swapped them in the practice test. Give us something that makes the swap impossible.

**Tutor:** Attach each name to the direction it bends its surroundings, and then rivet each to an anchor. LACColith — the blister. It pushed UP: magma inflating between two strata jacked the overlying layers into an arch, so the shape is a mushroom with a flat floor and a domed roof. LOPolith — hear the drop in the word. It sagged DOWN: an intrusion so massive that its weight bowed the strata beneath it into a saucer. Now the rivets. The lopolith owns the strongest anchor in South African geology: the Bushveld Igneous Complex north of Pretoria, the largest lopolith on Earth, whose layered saucer-edge is mined for platinum, chrome and vanadium in a great arc through Rustenburg and Mokopane — once that association is set, no exam nerves can flip it. The laccolith lacks a giant local celebrity, so pin it structurally: small enough to hide beneath a single dome-shaped hill, always concordant, discovered as an unexplained blister in otherwise orderly strata. Final safety check on any diagram: both are concordant, lying WITH the layering — if the body in the sketch cuts across the strata it is neither of them; a cutting wall is a dyke, and a cutting giant with no floor is the batholith. Blister up, saucer down, both between the layers.

**Bianca:** Third: in earlier grades exfoliation was taught as the sun's heat cracking rock surfaces. Now the lesson says the real cause is pressure release. Do we abandon the old explanation?

**Tutor:** Keep both, but promote the right one to the front, because they are two acts of one play and the deeper act must lead. Act one is structural: PRESSURE RELEASE. The granite crystallised kilometres down, squeezed by everything above it; over millions of years erosion carried that overburden away; the unloaded granite expanded outward, and the expansion opened the curved sheet joints lying parallel to the ground surface. This act is decisive because it manufactures the lines of weakness — without sheet joints there is nothing to peel, no matter how hard the sun works. Act two is the surface exploitation your earlier grades described: day-night temperature swings flex the outer shells, water enters the curved cracks and attacks the minerals or freezes on cold highveld nights, roots probe every opening — and the loosened sheets slide off one by one, which is the exfoliation you can actually watch, slab debris and all. So structure the answer as cause then trigger: unloading writes the sheet joints; heat and water then pry the sheets away. And spend one closing sentence on the consequence that carries the reasoning mark: because the sheets are curved, every peeling exposes a rounder surface, so the dome perfects its own shape as it erodes — the mountain that polishes itself.

**Bianca:** Last one: the two-stage tor theory says the boulders rounded off underground. One sceptic in our class wants to know how geographers can claim that when nobody was there to watch it.

**Tutor:** Salute the sceptic — that is precisely the question science exists to answer, and the tor evidence is good enough to convince a courtroom. Exhibit one: the process caught in the act. Fresh road cuttings and quarry faces through granite country regularly slice open the underground stage midway: rounded boulders still sitting in place inside the reddish, crumbly grus, below the surface, never yet exposed to the sky — corestones photographed in their original packaging. Granite belts in Limpopo and the Lowveld show such cuttings on ordinary roadsides. Exhibit two: the architecture of the stacks. Tor boulders stand in neat vertical columns and courses that reproduce the right-angled joint pattern of intact granite; boulders moved by floods or rolled downhill lie jumbled and unsorted. Order like that cannot be assembled in the open — it is inherited from cracks that only ever existed inside the buried mass. Exhibit three: the packaging itself. The gritty grus lying between and beneath tors is granite rotted by long chemical attack — decay that requires sustained contact with water inside enclosed cracks, conditions found underground and not on a breezy koppie summit. Put the three exhibits together: an intermediate stage visible in cuttings, an inherited geometry, and decay products demanding burial. The two-stage story — rot first below, strip second above — stops being a tale to memorise and becomes the only explanation that survives the evidence, which is exactly the argument your sceptic deserves.
