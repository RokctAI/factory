Hey, it's Bianca — massive igneous rocks, and suddenly you're expected to juggle five intrusion names plus domes, tors, corestones and grus. Breathe out. The whole topic fits into a concrete pour, an orange and a crate of soap.

The concrete pour sorts the intrusions. Full pour with no bottom: batholith. Pocket pour that jacked the ceiling up: laccolith. Heavy pour that bowed its floor down: lopolith — and ours, the Bushveld Complex, is the biggest on Earth and full of platinum. Concrete up a crack: dyke, a wall. Concrete between the boards: sill, a shelf. Then one two-word test wins every diagram: lies WITH the layers, concordant; CUTS across, discordant. Ask "lies with or cuts?" and the marks are banked.

The orange is the dome. Granite squashed under kilometres of rock gets its backpack removed as erosion strips the cover; it swells, cracks into curved shells like orange peel, and the shells slide off — exfoliation. Then write the sentence worth a mark every time: each shed peel leaves a rounder surface, so the dome polishes itself. Picture the bald granite heads of the Lowveld shining after rain.

The soap crate is the tors. Buried granite comes pre-cracked into blocks; water creeps along the joints and eats every block from the corners in — three sides at a corner, so corners lose first — rounding each block into a corestone sitting in crumbly grus. Erosion then strips the grus and the survivors stand stacked. The order carries the marks: rot first, underground; strip second. And the closer: wide joints make domes, close joints make tors — same granite, two landforms.

A pour, a peel, a soap crate, and the lies-with-or-cuts test. Four pictures and the entire topic is in your pocket. Now go and make those practice questions look easy — you've got this!
