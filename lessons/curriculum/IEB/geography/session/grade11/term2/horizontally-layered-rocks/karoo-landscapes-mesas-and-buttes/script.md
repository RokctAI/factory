# Part 1 — Expert

Every landscape is an argument between rock and weather, and in this session the rock argues from a position of perfect flatness. We are reading the scenery built on HORIZONTAL strata — the undisturbed, flat-lying layers that give the South African interior its tabletop skylines and give Lesotho its rampart of basalt. Four movements: first the layered rocks themselves and the single rule that governs them; then the family of flat-topped landforms from plateau down to pointed koppie; then the strange process by which cliffs retreat without ever softening; and finally canyon country and the map calculations that let you measure all of it on paper.

## Subtopic: Horizontal Strata and Differential Erosion

Sedimentary rock is born in sheets. Mud settles on a lake floor, sand spreads across a delta, layer covers layer, and each sheet is a STRATUM — strata in the plural. Where the crust has never been folded or tilted, the strata still lie today exactly as they were deposited: horizontal, parallel, stacked to enormous thickness. The South African interior is the textbook region for this. The KAROO SUPERGROUP — an immense pile of mudstones, shales and sandstones laid down over more than one hundred million years — floors most of the central plateau, and its layers remain almost perfectly flat.

Flat layers alone would make dull scenery. The drama comes from the fact that the layers are UNEQUAL in resistance. Mudstone and shale crumble readily under frost and rain. Sandstone holds out longer. And threaded through the whole pile are sheets of DOLERITE — magma that injected itself between the strata roughly one hundred and eighty-three million years ago and froze into horizontal sills of tough, crystalline rock. When weathering and erosion go to work on such an unequal stack, they strip the weak layers rapidly and leave the strong ones standing. Geographers call this DIFFERENTIAL EROSION, and it is the master process of this entire topic: nothing in this landscape makes sense without it.

The most important arrangement is a strong layer resting on weak ones. The strong layer becomes a CAP ROCK — a shield held over everything below it. As long as the cap survives, the surface it protects stays high and dead flat, and the sides of the landform stay steep. The moment the cap is punctured, erosion pours through the gap and the soft interior is scoured out. Flat top means intact cap; that equation is worth carrying through every question on this topic.

The same geometry can be built by fire instead of water. At the top of the Karoo pile, flood after flood of basalt LAVA poured across the land and hardened into stacked horizontal flows. Those flows now form the high plateau of Lesotho and the summit wall above KwaZulu-Natal — a BASALTIC PLATEAU, igneous in material but behaving in every respect like horizontal strata with a chain of caps.

The questions for this section are with you now: define strata and differential erosion, explain the work of the cap rock, and place the Karoo Supergroup and the Lesotho basalts as the regional examples.

## Subtopic: From Plateau to Pointed Koppie — the Landform Family

Differential erosion in flat strata manufactures one family of landforms, and the members are simply stages in a single life story. Begin at the beginning. A PLATEAU is a broad, elevated surface with a level top held up by a resistant cap and edged by steep slopes. The steep rim of a plateau is an ESCARPMENT, and southern Africa owns the grandest of them all: the Great Escarpment that curls around the interior, at its highest where the basalt cap of the Lesotho highlands drops nearly a vertical kilometre into KwaZulu-Natal.

Erosion cannot lower a capped plateau from above, so it gnaws inward from the edges. Streams cut back into the escarpment until an outlying piece of plateau is severed from the main mass. That detached, flat-topped remnant, still broader than it is tall, is a MESA — Afrikaans speakers in the Karoo simply say tafelberg, table mountain. Erosion keeps gnawing. The mesa loses width faster than height until the proportions flip: now the landform is taller than it is broad, a steep-sided tower carrying only a small flat crown. That is a BUTTE. The Upper Karoo near Steynsburg displays the pair side by side in the twin hills called Teebus and Koffiebus — the tea canister and the coffee canister — one a trim flat-topped mesa form, its neighbour whittled to a tighter, tower-like profile, both wearing dolerite caps.

Finally the cap loses the fight. Once the protective sill is breached and stripped away, the soft strata below are exposed on all sides and weather back into a smooth cone — a CONICAL HILL, the pointed koppie of the Karoo skyline. Spandau Kop, the elegant pyramid that stands guard outside Graaff-Reinet, is a famous example of the stage. After the cone, only the level plain remains. So the full sequence runs: plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill, plain — five ages of one landform, and a single Karoo viewpoint often shows several generations at once.

These landscapes are also lived in. The wide plains carry merino sheep and mohair goats; the clear, dry air above them has drawn the great radio and optical telescope projects to the Northern Cape; dolerite is crushed for road stone; the strata hold one of the richest fossil archives on Earth; and towns such as Graaff-Reinet and Nieu-Bethesda earn their living from travellers who come for the koppies themselves.

This section's questions are ready: define plateau, mesa, butte and conical hill by their proportions, place Teebus, Koffiebus and Spandau Kop, and give the five-stage sequence in order.

## Subtopic: Scarp Retreat and Back Wasting

Now slow the film down and watch how one edge of a capped landform actually erodes, because the process is stranger than it looks.

Focus on the cliff below the cap. In winter, Karoo nights fall well below freezing: water in the joints of the dolerite freezes, expands and levers blocks off the face. By day, fierce heating and cooling flex the rock and loosen more. Meanwhile every summer thunderstorm sends water tearing at the weak shale under the cap, carving it out so that the overhanging cap blocks lose support and crash down. Add it up and the remarkable result is this: the cliff never becomes gentler. It is rebuilt, storm after storm, century after century, at the SAME steep angle — just a little further back each time. The slope retreats parallel to itself. This is SCARP RETREAT, or parallel retreat, and the South African geomorphologist Lester King made these very landscapes world-famous by building his model of slope evolution on them.

Erosion of this style — slopes migrating sideways while holding their shape — is called BACK WASTING. Set it against its opposite, DOWN WASTING, in which a landscape is lowered from the top so that every summit sinks and every profile grows rounder and softer. Capped horizontal strata refuse to down-waste: the cap locks the summit at its height, so the only direction the landform can yield is inward from its edges. That is why a butte keeps its sharp cliff rim to the end of its life instead of slumping into a gentle dome.

Each retreating scarp leaves evidence behind it: a gently inclined surface of bedrock at its foot, sloping out towards the plain, called a PEDIMENT. Let scarps retreat towards each other from opposite sides of an upland for long enough and their pediments finally meet, merging into one vast, almost level erosion surface — a PEDIPLAIN. Stand anywhere in the Great Karoo and you are standing on exactly that: a pediplain hundreds of kilometres across, scattered with the mesas, buttes and cones that are the last surviving fragments of a higher, older land surface.

Work through this section's questions: scarp retreat as parallel retreat, the contrast between back wasting and down wasting, and the growth of pediments into a pediplain.

## Subtopic: Canyons and the Mapwork of Flat Country

Hand the same horizontal strata to a large, energetic river and the attack turns vertical. Instead of scarps nibbling sideways, the river saws directly downward, and the result is a CANYON — a deep, narrow valley walled by near-vertical cliffs. Because the walls slice through the whole layered stack, they read like a diagram: each resistant band stands out as a sheer rock face, each weak band wears back into a debris slope, and the wall descends in giant steps. The world's most famous example is the Grand Canyon of the Colorado; South Africa's own showpiece is the Blyde River Canyon on the Mpumalanga escarpment, where the river has sunk its gorge some eight hundred metres into the layered quartzites. Canyons grow deepest where uplift of the land REJUVENATES the river — steepening its course and restoring its cutting power — while the resistant strata hold the walls steep.

Now bring the topic to the topographic map, because reading this terrain from contours is a skill you will use in your final exams and in the field alike. Horizontal-strata country has an unmistakable contour signature. A mesa shows as a set of closed contours whose centre is strikingly EMPTY — the flat summit carries no contours at all — while around the rim the contours bunch so tightly they nearly merge: that crowding is the cliff. A butte gives the identical pattern shrunk to a small summit. A stepped hillside appears as alternating bands of crowded and widely spaced contours, one band per rock layer. A canyon announces itself as two parallel ribbons of tightly packed contours with the river squeezed between them.

Two standard calculations finish the toolkit, and both must be shown in full working. First, VERTICAL EXAGGERATION on a cross-section. Suppose the section uses a vertical scale of one centimetre to twenty-five metres. Twenty-five metres is two thousand five hundred centimetres, so the vertical scale is 1 over 2 500. Against a horizontal map scale of 1:50 000, the exaggeration is 50 000 divided by 2 500, which is 20 — the drawn profile is twenty times taller than reality. Second, GRADIENT: the height difference divided by the horizontal distance, both in the same units. If the land climbs one hundred and fifty metres over a horizontal distance of three thousand metres, the gradient is 150 over 3 000, which simplifies to 1 in 20 — a gentle slope, and on this terrain such an answer immediately tells you the line has crossed a pediment rather than a cliff.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: canyons with their named examples, the contour signatures of mesa, butte and canyon, and the vertical exaggeration and gradient calculations each worked in full.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with a paving slab, three shapes on a skyline, and a wall that walks — same landforms, same understanding, half the effort.

## Subtopic: The Paving Slab on the Sand Heap

Do this experiment in your head. A builder leaves a heap of soft building sand in a yard, and drops one concrete paving slab flat on top of it. Then the rainy season arrives. Everywhere the rain hits bare sand, it strips it away, layer after layer. But directly under the slab the sand cannot be touched — the slab takes every drop. Weeks later the yard is nearly level except for one thing: a proud column of sand standing untouched, wearing its concrete slab like a flat roof.

That yard is the Karoo. The sand is the soft shale and mudstone, laid down in flat sheets. The paving slab is DOLERITE — ancient molten rock that pushed in between the layers and set as hard as concrete. The rain, frost and wind are the demolition crew, and they always take the soft material first. That one habit — soft goes first, hard survives — is differential erosion, and it is the only rule this entire landscape obeys.

So read any Karoo skyline like the builder's yard. A dead-flat horizon means an unbroken slab is still lying up there on guard. A steep drop means the crew is busy eating the soft sand at an exposed edge. A lonely flat-topped hill is simply a sand column whose slab has not yet failed. And Lesotho with the high Drakensberg wall is the same trick performed with lava: sheet after sheet of hardened basalt stacked up like a pallet of slabs, protecting the highest ground in southern Africa.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the sand, the slab, and what a flat top always tells you.

## Subtopic: Box, Tower, Tent

Here is the whole landform family as three shapes you could draw in five seconds. The story starts with the unbroken high ground — the plateau, a full floor of protected land with steep edges, rimmed by the escarpment. The demolition crew works inward from those edges, and sooner or later a piece gets cut off from the main floor.

Shape one: the BOX. A cut-off chunk that is still wider than it is tall, flat lid firmly on — that is a mesa, the tafelberg of the Karoo. Shape two: the TOWER. Keep eroding the box and it narrows faster than it shortens, until it stands taller than it is wide with just a small flat cap up top — that is a butte. Near Steynsburg in the Upper Karoo the pair pose together as Teebus and Koffiebus, the tea tin and the coffee tin, two dolerite-capped hills that have become landmarks precisely because the shapes are so clean. Shape three: the TENT. The day the cap is finally punched through, the soft rock loses its roof and the weather rounds the whole hill into a smooth cone — the conical hill, the pointed koppie, like Spandau Kop standing outside Graaff-Reinet like a canvas tent pitched on the plain. After the tent collapses, only flat ground is left.

So the countdown runs: floor, box, tower, tent, flat — plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill, plain. Five ages of one landform. And the wonderful thing about the Karoo is that the generations do not queue politely one at a time: from a single farm gate you can often see a mesa, a butte and a cone sharing the same horizon, a whole family portrait in one view.

Great progress — the questions for this section are ready: match the three shapes to their names, say what event turns a tower into a tent, and give the countdown in order.

## Subtopic: The Wall That Walks Away

Last piece: how does the demolition actually happen? Here is the surprise — the cliffs never grow old. You would expect a hill, like an old mountain range, to sag and soften and round off with age. Karoo hills refuse. Their cliff faces stay just as steep after a million years; the only thing that changes is WHERE the cliff stands. The wall walks away — backwards, step by step, without ever losing its shape.

Watch one year of the walk. Winter: icy Karoo nights freeze the water inside the cracks of the cap rock; the ice swells and pops blocks off the cliff face like corks. Summer: thunderstorms rip into the soft shale beneath the cap, digging out its support so the next slab of cap breaks away. When the dust settles, there is a brand-new cliff — same height, same steepness — standing one step further back. Repeat for ages and the wall has walked kilometres across the country while staying a wall. That is scarp retreat, and shrinking a landscape sideways like this is back wasting. The opposite — grinding the tops down until everything is low and round — is down wasting, and the hard caps simply do not allow it here.

A walking wall leaves footprints. At its foot it leaves behind a smooth, gently sloping floor of rock called a pediment — the swept ground where the wall used to stand. When two walls walking from opposite sides finish eating the high ground between them, their swept floors join into one enormous plain, the pediplain — which is the Great Karoo itself, with the last boxes, towers and tents still standing on it like furniture nobody has carried away. And when a mighty river joins in, it cuts straight down instead of sideways and saws out a canyon with stepped walls — the Blyde River Canyon is our giant, and the Grand Canyon is the world's.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the wall that walks backwards, back wasting against down wasting, and the swept floors that merge into the great plain. Finish them, and every flat-topped hill you ever pass will read like an open history book.
