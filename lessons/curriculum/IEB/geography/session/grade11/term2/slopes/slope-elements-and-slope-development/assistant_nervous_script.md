Hey, it's Bianca — slopes plus GIS in one lesson can feel like two subjects ganging up on you. Relax. The whole thing is one cake, one stadium and one chessboard.

The cake is the country: flat top is the interior plateau, the steep side all around is the Great Escarpment — tallest at the Drakensberg — and the table is the coastal belt. Rivers leaving the top must jump the side, which is why the Tugela falls nearly a kilometre. Say top, side, table and the topography question is sorted.

The stadium is any hillside, four parts in fixed order: roof, wall, stands, pitch — crest, cliff, talus, pediment. Attach the shapes and the numbers: crest convex; cliff vertical, the free face that drops everything it loosens; talus straight at the angle of repose, about 25 to 35 degrees; pediment concave, under about 5 degrees. One trap to dodge: a soft hill with no hard rock band has no wall and no stands — just roof curving into pitch.

The walking part is slope retreat: the wall rebuilds itself backward, the stands follow, the pitch stretches forward, and the shape never sags. Given deep time, the pitches merge into the pediplain and the leftover hills stand as koppies. If a question asks for the rival model, offer decline — humid, deep-soiled slopes rounding off — then explain why our dry, capped, thinly covered landscapes choose retreat instead.

The chessboard is the GIS half. Spatial resolution is pixel size; spectral resolution is how many kinds of light the sensor reads. Vector is pins, string and fenced patches with fact cards — points, lines, areas, attributes. Raster is the chessboard, one value per square, perfect for heights and rainfall — and a chessboard of heights becomes a slope map in seconds.

Cake, stadium, walking wall, chessboard. Four pictures, the whole lesson. Now go and take those practice questions apart — you've got this!
