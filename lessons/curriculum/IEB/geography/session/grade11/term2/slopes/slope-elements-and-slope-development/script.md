# Part 1 — Expert

Mesas, cuestas, domes — every landform in this term has been described by talking about its slopes. This session promotes the slope itself to the main subject. Four movements: the three-part build of South Africa's surface, which sets the stage for every slope in the country; slope types and the four classic elements that stack from hilltop to plain; how each element behaves and how the whole profile moves through time by slope retreat; and the digital toolkit — resolution, raster and vector — with which modern geographers measure and map it all.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Topography — the Stage

Seen from space, South Africa sorts itself into three great structural units. The centre is the INTERIOR PLATEAU: a high, ancient erosion surface, mostly between 1 200 and 1 800 metres above sea level, carrying the Highveld grasslands, the Free State plains — Bloemfontein sits at about 1 400 metres — and the wide Karoo basin. Wrapped around the plateau runs the GREAT ESCARPMENT: the nearly continuous rim where the high country breaks off toward the sea. Its most dramatic stretch is the KwaZulu-Natal Drakensberg, where the basalt wall tops 3 000 metres; elsewhere it continues as the Nuweveld mountains behind Beaufort West, the Roggeveld rim of the western Karoo, and the forested escarpment edges of Mpumalanga and Limpopo. Between the escarpment and the coast lies the third unit, the MARGINAL ZONE: the folded Cape ranges in the south-west, stepped terrace country along the southern margins, and the hot, humid lowlands of the east coast.

This three-part architecture is why slopes rule South African geography. The plateau contributes gentle surfaces; the escarpment contributes the steepest slopes in the country — a drop of one to two vertical kilometres achieved within a few horizontal ones; and the marginal zone is the staircase between them. Rivers leaving the plateau must jump the rim: the Tugela hurls itself nearly 950 metres off the Drakensberg edge in one of the world's tallest waterfalls, and every major road from harbour to Highveld climbs through a pass.

Slopes themselves are classified first by SHAPE. A CONVEX slope bulges outward, growing steeper downhill, like the curve of a rugby ball. A CONCAVE slope hollows inward, steep at the top and gentlest at the base, like a skateboard ramp. A RECTILINEAR slope runs straight at one constant angle, like a ruler propped against a box. And most real hillsides are COMPOUND — several shapes stacked downslope, in South Africa overwhelmingly in one particular order, which the next section names.

Take this section's questions now: the plateau, escarpment and marginal zone placed and described, the Drakensberg as the rim's high point, and the four slope shapes separated.

## Subtopic: The Four Slope Elements

Slice a classic South African hillside in cross-section — a Karoo koppie, a spur below the escarpment — and four elements repeat in a fixed order from summit to plain. This four-part profile is the topic's signature diagram, and it reads from the top down.

First the CREST — also the summit or waxing slope: gently CONVEX, carrying little loose material, its rock slowly rounded by weathering. Second, wherever a resistant band outcrops, the CLIFF — the scarp, or FREE FACE: the steepest element, often near-vertical, built of bare exposed bedrock. The name free face is earned: any rock the weather frees from it falls away at once instead of lying where it loosened, so the face stays sheer and keeps producing debris — it is the hillside's supplier.

Third, catching that supply, the TALUS or DEBRIS SLOPE — scree in mountain language. It is RECTILINEAR, dead straight, because loose fragments settle at their ANGLE OF REPOSE — the steepest angle at which they can rest, generally about 25 to 35 degrees. The larger blocks halt high on the slope; the finer chips filter toward the bottom. The talus is a slope of transport: rock pauses here between cliff and plain.

Fourth, at the base, the PEDIMENT: a gently CONCAVE surface, typically under about 5 degrees, cut across solid bedrock and dusted with a thin veneer of sediment, curving away from the hill until it merges with the plain or the nearest drainage line. Water leaving the talus fans across the pediment as sheetwash, carrying the fine material outward. Fix the set with its shapes: crest convex, cliff vertical, talus straight, pediment concave. And note the honest exception — a hill without a resistant rock band grows no cliff and therefore no talus; soft-rock hills wear a smooth convex-into-concave profile with the middle elements missing. Where the full four occur, though, the order never varies.

This section's questions are ready: the four elements in order with their shapes, the free face explained by its name, and the angle of repose pinned to the talus.

## Subtopic: Slope Retreat — How the Profile Moves

The four-element profile is not furniture; it is machinery in motion, and its motion is SLOPE RETREAT. Weathering pries rock from the cliff; the cliff, losing material, is rebuilt slightly further back; the talus shifts to follow its supplier; and the pediment grows forward into the ground the other elements abandoned. In the classic account built on South African landscapes — Lester King's PARALLEL RETREAT, already met at the Karoo koppies — the profile keeps its proportions while it moves: the cliff remains steep, the talus holds its angle of repose, the pediment stays gentle. Nothing slumps; the entire staircase simply relocates backward, and the pediment left behind grows wider with every century.

Run the machine over geological time and the consequences build the country. Slopes retreating from opposite sides eat an upland from its edges; the pediments of neighbouring valleys finally touch and merge into a PEDIPLAIN; and the unconsumed remnants stand on it as koppies and INSELBERGS. The vocabularies of this term now interlock: a mesa or butte is slope retreat wearing a dolerite cap; pediment and pediplain are the floor the retreat sweeps out behind itself.

For balance, hold up the rival model. In humid climates with deep soils and thick vegetation, many geomorphologists describe slopes DECLINING instead: the whole profile lowers and rounds with age, cliffs decaying into smooth convex-concave curves — down wasting rather than back wasting. South Africa's semi-arid interior, hard caprocks and thin plant cover push the balance toward parallel retreat, which is why our landscapes hold their sharp edges for tens of millions of years. An answer that states both models and then argues from local conditions — dry climate, resistant caps, sparse cover — is an answer operating at the top of the mark range.

Slopes are also decisions people live with: houses, lands and roads keep to the gentle crests and pediments; the cliff and upper talus zones threaten rockfall; overgrazing strips the pediment's thin veneer and opens gully systems; and building at the toe of an active talus is an invitation the next lesson — mass movement — will explain in full.

Work through the questions for this section: the retreat cycle traced through all four elements, parallel retreat set against decline, and pediplain and inselberg attached to the story.

## Subtopic: GIS — Resolution, Data Types, Raster and Vector

This topic travels with the geographer's digital toolkit, because today slopes are measured, mapped and monitored by computer. A Geographic Information System stores the world as LAYERS — relief, drainage, roads, land use — that can be laid over one another and interrogated together.

Two resolutions decide what a satellite image can tell you. SPATIAL RESOLUTION is the ground size of a single pixel: at 30-metre resolution, a pixel covers a plot the size of a large school hall, so fields, dams and roads register but individual vehicles vanish; at 10 metres, farm tracks and small orchards appear. Finer resolution, smaller detail. SPECTRAL RESOLUTION is the number and narrowness of wavelength bands the sensor records — visible colours, near-infrared, thermal and more. Because every surface reflects the bands in its own signature, more and narrower bands let an analyst separate healthy veld from drought-stressed crops, bare soil from plough lands, clear water from algae.

The data inside a GIS comes in two architectures. VECTOR data describes the world as geometry: POINTS for features that live at a single position — a windpump, a survey beacon; LINES for features with length — rivers, roads, contours, pipelines; AREAS or polygons for features with extent — a farm, a reservoir, a fire-damaged block. Bolted to every object is its ATTRIBUTE data — the table of facts: the windpump's depth and yield, the road's surface type, the block's date of burning. RASTER data instead spreads a grid of identical cells across the land and stores one value per cell — height in a digital elevation model, brightness in a satellite band, millimetres in a rainfall surface. The division of labour: raster for phenomena that vary continuously, such as altitude and rainfall; vector for discrete objects with edges and names.

Aim the toolkit at this session's subject and it pays for itself. A digital elevation model — raster — becomes a slope-angle map of an entire district at one command; contours, rivers and farm boundaries ride above it as vector layers; and an analyst overlaying slope, soil and rainfall can flag every hillside too steep to cultivate, every gully-prone pediment, every road cutting that deserves inspection — which is precisely how conservation planners and municipal engineers now spend their mornings.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: spatial against spectral resolution, points, lines, areas and attributes sorted, and raster against vector with the right task for each.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same slopes with a wedding cake, a stadium and a chessboard — same content, same marks.

## Subtopic: The Country Is a Wedding Cake

Build South Africa as a one-tier wedding cake standing on a table. The flat top of the cake is the interior plateau — high, wide and level, with Bloemfontein sitting on the icing at about 1 400 metres. The vertical side of the cake, that sudden drop all the way around, is the Great Escarpment — and the stretch where the side stands tallest and sheerest, over 3 000 metres, is the Drakensberg. The table around the cake is the marginal zone, the coastal belt — crumpled at one corner where the Cape folded ranges wrinkle the cloth. Top, side, table: every journey from the coast to the Highveld climbs the side of the cake through a pass, and every river leaving the top has to jump — the Tugela jumps nearly 950 metres in one go.

Now zoom from the whole country to one hillside and collect the four slope shapes with your hands. Convex is the curve of a rugby ball — gentle at first, steepening as it rolls over. Concave is a skateboard ramp — steep entry, flattening to the floor. Rectilinear is a ruler leaned against a box — one angle, top to bottom. Compound is what real hills actually do: two or three of those shapes stacked downhill in sequence.

And South African hills love one particular stack. On your next long drive, read any Karoo koppie from top to bottom: a rounded cap, then a band of bare rock, then a straight ramp of rubble, then a long gentle skirt running out to the plain. Four shapes, one fixed order, every time — and naming them properly is the next section and a favourite diagram question.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the cake on the table, the jump off the top, and the shapes collected from ball, ramp and ruler.

## Subtopic: Roof, Wall, Stands and Pitch

Turn the hillside into a stadium and the four elements never get confused again. The CREST is the stadium ROOF: gently curved, convex, smooth — weathering slowly rounds it the way wind and rain age a dome. Below it stands the WALL: the CLIFF, or free face — sheer bare rock, the steepest element on the hill. It earns the name free face because everything the weather breaks free of it drops away instantly; nothing stays behind to soften it, so the wall stays vertical and keeps supplying rock to everything below.

Under the wall slope the STANDS: the TALUS or debris slope, straight as stadium seating. Every block the wall drops lands here, and the pile automatically arranges itself at the steepest angle loose rock can hold — the angle of repose, roughly 25 to 35 degrees, the same angle a dumped load of gravel chooses no matter how you tip it. Overload the stands and rock slides until the resting angle is restored — which is why this element is always rectilinear. Big boulders take the high seats; small chips filter down to the front rows.

And then the PITCH: the PEDIMENT, the gently curved concave floor at the base, under about 5 degrees, cut into solid rock with only a thin scatter of sediment on top, running from the foot of the stands out to the open plain. Sheets of rainwater fan across it, sweeping the finest material away. So the stadium works as a system: roof weathers, wall drops rock, stands catch and pass it down, pitch spreads it out. Chant the shapes — crest convex, cliff vertical, talus straight, pediment concave — and the diagram labels itself. One exam warning: a hill with no hard rock band builds no wall, and no wall means no stands — soft hills are just roof curving into pitch.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the four stadium parts, the gravel-pile angle, and which parts go missing on soft-rock hills.

## Subtopic: The Walking Hill and the Chessboard Map

Now the strangest idea in the topic: the stadium moves. The wall drops rock and is rebuilt a step further back; the stands shuffle backward after their wall; the pitch stretches forward into the vacated ground. And the shape never sags — wall stays sheer, stands hold their angle, pitch stays flat. The whole profile walks backward across the country like scenery wheeled offstage. That is parallel slope retreat. Give it fifty million years: hills walk apart, their pitches meet and weld into one continental floor — the pediplain — and the last unwalked remnants stand about on it as koppies and inselbergs. That is the Karoo in one sentence: the flat is the merged pitches, and every flat-topped hill is old plateau still in mid-walk.

Then the digital half, because the modern geographer maps all of this from a desk. A satellite image is a mosaic of pixels, and SPATIAL resolution is just the ground-size of each pixel — a 30-metre pixel captures the school but swallows the minibus; a 10-metre pixel finds the farm track. SPECTRAL resolution is how many kinds of light the sensor reads — including infrared bands where stressed crops and healthy veld glow differently, which is exactly how drought maps get made.

Finally, the two ways a computer stores a map: pins-and-string, or chessboard. VECTOR is the pins-and-string board from a detective film: a PIN for anything at a single spot — a windpump, a beacon; STRING for anything with length — a river, a road, a contour; a fenced-off patch for anything with area — a farm, a dam, a burnt block; and every pin, string and patch carries its little card of ATTRIBUTE facts — depth, surface, date. RASTER is the chessboard: a grid thrown over the landscape with one value in every square — height, rainfall, brightness. Crisp objects with edges want pins and string; smoothly changing things like altitude want the chessboard. The payoff for this topic: hand the computer a chessboard of heights — a digital elevation model — and it returns the steepness of every hillside in the district in seconds, the exact map a conservation officer or road engineer needs before the next wet season.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the walking stadium, the merged pitches, pixels and their sizes, and pins-and-string against the chessboard. Land these and you hold the oldest landscapes in the syllabus and the newest tools in one hand.
