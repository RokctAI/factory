# Part 1 — Expert

Last session the rock layers lay perfectly flat and the hills wore flat caps. This session we lean the layers over, and the whole landscape changes character: instead of tables and towers we get long, lopsided ridges marching across the country. Four movements again: what tilting does to strata and why every tilted ridge has two unequal faces; the three ridge types read off the angle of dip — cuesta, homoclinal ridge, hogsback; the layers bent full circle into basins and domes; and the mapwork skills that turn a contour sheet into proof of which way the rock leans.

## Subtopic: Tilted Strata, Dip Slopes and Scarp Slopes

No rock layer is deposited on a slant — sediment settles flat. Yet across much of South Africa the strata now lean. The crust does the leaning: colliding plates squeeze and fold the layers, uplift lifts one flank of a region higher than the other, and deep basins sag in the middle under their own filling. Layers that meet the surface at an angle are INCLINED or TILTED strata. The angle a tilted layer makes with the horizontal is its DIP, and the compass direction in which the layer descends is the dip direction — two terms you must be able to define in one line each.

Set differential erosion loose on a tilted stack of unequal layers and the familiar sorting begins: the weak layers are hollowed out into long valleys, the resistant layers stand up as long ridges. But here is the new idea — a ridge cut from leaning layers can never be symmetrical, because its two faces are built by two different rules.

One face is the DIP SLOPE. It is nothing more than the exposed upper surface of the resistant layer itself, stripped of its soft cover, running downhill at exactly the angle the rock dips. Walk down it and the same hard layer stays beneath your boots the entire way. The other face is the SCARP SLOPE. It slices ACROSS the stack, exposing the sawn-off ends of every layer — resistant band at the top, weak bands below — and because those weak bands wash out fast and undermine the hard edge above them, the scarp stands steep, often with a cliff at its crest and rubble beneath. Hold the contrast tight: the dip slope FOLLOWS one layer; the scarp slope CUTS through many. Almost every question on this topic is that sentence wearing a disguise.

Take this section's questions now: how flat-lying strata come to be tilted, dip defined as an angle with the horizontal, and the two slopes separated by their relationship to the layers.

## Subtopic: Cuesta, Homoclinal Ridge and Hogsback

Here is the classification, and it is nothing but one ridge photographed at three different lean angles.

Lean the strata gently — anything up to roughly 25 degrees — and you build a CUESTA: an emphatically lopsided ridge whose dip slope is a long, easy incline fit for orchards and gravel roads, while the scarp side drops away short and steep. Because a resistant layer crops out along a continuous line, a cuesta can run unbroken across a whole district; the sandstone ridge country flanking the Soutpansberg in Limpopo, where thick strata lean away to the north, shows the style on a grand scale.

Lean the strata harder, to somewhere between roughly 25 and 45 degrees, and the ridge sharpens into a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE. The asymmetry survives — the scarp is still the steeper face — but the dip slope is now a scramble rather than a stroll, and the crest narrows. South Africa's celebrated example is the MAGALIESBERG, the quartzite rampart running from Pretoria towards Rustenburg with its strata leaning steeply northward, and the parallel quartzite ridges of the Witwatersrand repeat the pattern through Johannesburg's suburbs.

Push the lean beyond roughly 45 degrees, with the layers approaching the vertical, and something is lost: the gentle side. Both faces now cut across the upturned strata at similar steep angles, the crest thins to a jagged blade, and the near-symmetrical result is a HOGSBACK — named for the bristling ridge of a wild hog's spine, and lending its name to the Eastern Cape mountain village of Hogsback below just such a crest.

Carry the classification as a lean-angle scale: to about 25 degrees, cuesta; about 25 to 45, homoclinal ridge; past about 45, hogsback. Quote the numbers as approximations, because the real content is the trend — the steeper the dip, the narrower the ridge and the more equal its two faces, until at near-vertical the asymmetry disappears altogether. All three ridges share one anatomy: a resistant layer at the crest, weak layers in the flanking valleys, and — until the lean passes 45 — a steep scarp face opposite a gentler dip face.

This section's questions are ready: the three ridges placed on the lean-angle scale, the Magaliesberg and Hogsback named, and the reason a hogsback has no gentle side.

## Subtopic: Cuesta Basins and Cuesta Domes

Strata do not always lean in a single direction. Bend the whole stack downward into a huge saucer, or arch it upward into a huge blister, and the cuesta principle curls into circles.

Sag the layers toward a central low and you have a CUESTA BASIN. Erosion excavates the weak layers as ring-shaped valleys and leaves the resistant layers standing as CONCENTRIC ridges around the centre. Now derive the slope directions instead of memorising them: every layer descends toward the middle of the basin, and a dip slope always faces the way its layer descends — so in a basin, the dip slopes face INWARD and the scarp slopes face OUTWARD, like the cut rims of stacked saucers. Basins carry a famous bonus: ARTESIAN water. Rain soaking into a tilted aquifer where it outcrops on the rim travels down the dip and gathers under pressure beneath the basin floor, so a borehole sunk near the centre can overflow without a pump — the mechanism behind the Great Artesian Basin of Australia and, closer to home, behind pressurised groundwater in parts of the Karoo sequence.

Arch the layers upward instead and you have a CUESTA DOME. Erosion usually breaks through the crown of the arch and guts the weak interior, once again leaving resistant layers as concentric ridges — but every layer now descends AWAY from the centre, so the directions reverse: dip slopes face OUTWARD and scarp slopes face INWARD, confronting one another across the excavated core. South Africa owns the planet's most dramatic example: the VREDEFORT DOME south-west of Johannesburg, where a colossal ancient impact heaved the strata into an arch that erosion has since etched into rings of ridges — a World Heritage Site you can trace on any satellite image.

The anchor for both structures is the derivation itself: the dip slope faces wherever the layers lean downward — inward in a basin, outward on a dome — and the scarp faces the opposite way. Re-derive it from a quick sketch every time and the directions can never betray you.

Work through the questions for this section: basin and dome geometry, the facing directions of the two slopes in each, and artesian water and the Vredefort Dome as the applications.

## Subtopic: Reading Tilted Country on the Map

On the topographic sheet, tilted country introduces itself through unequal contour spacing. A cuesta or homoclinal ridge draws its crest as a line with contours packed hard against one side — the scarp — and strung wide apart on the other — the dip slope. A hogsback packs the contours tight on BOTH flanks of a thin crest. A parade of parallel lopsided ridges separated by straight valleys is the signature of a whole leaning sequence, and concentric rings of ridge-and-valley whisper basin or dome.

Turn the visual impression into numbers with a GRADIENT on each side of the same crest. Suppose the crest reaches 900 metres, and the 750-metre contour lies 300 metres away on the eastern side but 3 000 metres away on the west. East: a drop of 150 metres over 300 metres is 150 over 300 — a gradient of 1 in 2, cliff-steep, the scarp. West: 150 metres over 3 000 metres is 1 in 20, an easy incline, the dip slope. The conclusion writes itself: the strata dip toward the gentle side, here westward. That little two-gradient comparison is the standard proof of dip direction, and it earns its marks only when both calculations are shown in full.

A CROSS-SECTION ruled at right angles to the crest turns the ridge into its telltale profile — a ramp meeting a cliff, the shape of a right-angled triangle lying on its hypotenuse. State the VERTICAL EXAGGERATION of your section: with a vertical scale of one centimetre to fifty metres, which is 1:5 000, against the 1:50 000 horizontal scale, the exaggeration is 50 000 divided by 5 000 — 10 times, enough to make the asymmetry unmistakable without caricaturing it.

Finish with the people, because settlement in tilted country is geology obeyed. Farms, plantations and homesteads climb the gentle dip slopes; towns, roads and railways thread the strike valleys between the ridges; the scarp faces stay wild, keeping their kloofs, proteas and breeding cliffs for raptors. Movement across the grain of the land funnels through the few places where rivers have sawn gaps — poorts — through the resistant crest: at Olifantsnek south of Rustenburg, the Hex River's gap through the Magaliesberg carries the main road and holds a dam in the same breach. Ask why the map's towns queue in lines and the rock answers.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: asymmetric spacing interpreted, the two gradients calculated and compared, and land use assigned to dip slope, scarp and valley.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with a sandwich, a car jack and an umbrella — same ridges, same marks, far less sweat.

## Subtopic: The Tilted Sandwich

Build a sandwich: bread, cheese, bread, ham, bread. Lay it flat on the plate — that was last session, flat layers, flat-topped hills. Now lift one end and prop it on a matchbox. Nothing inside the sandwich changed; only its angle did. That angle — between the leaning sandwich and the flat plate — is the dip.

The tilted sandwich already shows you both faces of every tilted ridge on Earth. Run a finger down the smooth top slice from the high end to the plate: one long, gentle ramp, riding on a single layer the whole way down. That is the DIP SLOPE. Now look at the cut end of the sandwich, where the knife went through: bread, cheese, bread, ham, all their sawn-off edges showing in a steep little wall. That is the SCARP SLOPE — steep because it slices across every layer at once. Four words carry the whole idea into any exam diagram: dip follows, scarp cuts.

And why does the landscape make ridges at all? Because not every layer in the real sandwich is soft. Some layers are biltong — tough, chewy, nearly indestructible quartzite — while others are soft cheese shale. Rain and rivers eat out the cheese into long valleys and leave the biltong standing as long ridges. It is the same differential erosion that built the Karoo koppies, simply running at an angle.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the tilted sandwich, the ramp and the cut end, and which face follows a single layer.

## Subtopic: Three Clicks of the Car Jack

Now slide a car jack under the high end of the sandwich and lift it in three clicks, watching the ridge rename itself at each stop.

Click one — a gentle lean, up to about 25 degrees. The top slice is still an easy ramp; you could push a wheelbarrow up it. The cut end stays short and steep. This comfortable, lopsided shape is a CUESTA, and whole districts of it flank the Soutpansberg, where the leaning sandstone makes ramp country big enough to farm.

Click two — the lean passes 25 degrees, heading for 45. The ramp steepens until climbing it means using your hands; the ridge grows narrower and sharper, though the cut end is still clearly the steeper face. That is a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE, and you have seen the national showpiece if you have ever driven between Pretoria and Rustenburg: the Magaliesberg is a quartzite sandwich jacked to exactly this setting, running for over a hundred kilometres.

Click three — past 45 degrees, the layers heaved nearly upright. Now the important thing happens: the ramp is gone. With the layers standing on end, BOTH faces of the ridge are cutting across sawn-off edges, both are steep, and the lopsidedness that defined the first two clicks vanishes. What is left is a thin, jagged, even-sided blade of rock — a HOGSBACK, spiky as the bristles down a boar's back, and there is a mountain village in the Eastern Cape named after the one above it.

That is the entire classification: click one cuesta, click two homoclinal ridge, click three hogsback. And the essay sentence that ties it together: the harder the layers lean, the narrower the ridge and the more equal its two faces, until near the vertical you cannot tell them apart.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the three clicks in order, the Magaliesberg placed, and why click three has no gentle side.

## Subtopic: Dishes, Umbrellas and the Gap in the Ridge

Sometimes the layers do not lean one way — they bend into a circle. Two objects sort out every such question. First, the satellite dish: a surface sagging toward its own centre. Layers bent like the dish make a CUESTA BASIN. Hold the dish in your mind and read the slopes straight off it: the smooth inner surface slopes down toward the middle, so the gentle dip slopes face INWARD, and the cut outer rims — the scarps — face OUTWARD. Basins hide a gift underground: rain entering a tilted layer at the rim slides down inside it to the centre and sits there squeezed under pressure, so a borehole near the middle can flow by itself. That self-raising water is called artesian water.

Second object: the umbrella. A surface arching over its centre, every side leaning down and away. Layers bent like the umbrella make a CUESTA DOME, and the slopes reverse exactly as the object tells you: dip slopes face OUTWARD down the umbrella's curve, scarps face INWARD across the middle — because erosion usually rips the umbrella's crown open and hollows out the soft core, leaving rings of resistant rock staring at each other. South Africa's world-famous one is the Vredefort Dome, an ancient impact that umbrella-ed the strata; from the air its rings look drawn with a compass. Never memorise inward and outward as a list — pick up the dish or the umbrella in your head and the directions read themselves off.

Last stop: the map and the people. One crowded contour side and one spacious side along a ridge is a cuesta or homoclinal ridge declaring itself; crowding on both sides of a knife crest is a hogsback; rings mean dish or umbrella. And people arrange themselves by the rock's rules: fields and farmhouses take the gentle ramps, roads and towns line up in the soft valleys, and everything that must cross the ridge — highway, railway, even a dam wall — funnels through the one gap a river has sawn, the way the road and the dam share the river's breach at Olifantsnek in the Magaliesberg.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — dishes against umbrellas, the slope directions read off each, and the crowded and spacious contour sides decoded. Master them, and every leaning ridge you pass will tell you its name and which way it is falling.
