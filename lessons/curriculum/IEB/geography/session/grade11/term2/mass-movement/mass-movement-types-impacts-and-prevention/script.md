# Part 1 — Expert

Weathering loosens rock; slopes give it somewhere to go. This session studies what happens when the loosened material actually moves: MASS MOVEMENT, the downslope journey of rock, soil and debris pulled by nothing but gravity. Four movements: the balance of forces that decides whether a hillside stands or fails; the six recognised kinds of movement, sorted by speed and water; the damage they do to land and to people, anchored in the KwaZulu-Natal disaster of April 2022; and the toolbox of measures that prevent failures or soften their blow.

## Subtopic: What Moves a Slope

Begin with the definition, because it carries marks in itself. MASS MOVEMENT — mass wasting in some books — is the downslope movement of weathered material under the direct pull of GRAVITY, with NO transporting agent involved: no river carrying it, no glacier, no wind. Two clauses, both essential. Whether the material creeps a few millimetres a year or thunders down at highway speed, if gravity alone moves it, it belongs to this topic.

Every slope is an unfinished arm-wrestle. Pushing for failure: gravity, whose downslope component grows with the SLOPE ANGLE — material resting near its angle of repose is a wrestler already at full stretch. Holding the line: friction between particles, the interlocking of grains, dry cohesion, and the living reinforcement of plant ROOTS knitting the soil together. A slope fails the instant the pushing side gains or the holding side weakens — so every trigger in this topic is one of those two changes in disguise.

WATER is the great traitor, because it plays both sides. A modest amount of moisture strengthens material — surface tension pulls damp grains together, which is why a rusk dipped briefly in coffee still holds its shape. But saturation switches teams: water fills every pore and pushes the grains apart, it adds tonnes of extra weight to the slope, and it greases the surfaces along which sliding can start, especially the smooth junction where soil meets bedrock. Hold the rusk under the coffee too long and it collapses off your fingers — and hold a hillside under a week of rain and it does the same. Most mass movement disasters are rain-delivered.

The remaining triggers complete the list. UNDERCUTTING — by a river eating its bank, waves attacking a cliff, or a road cutting sliced through a slope's toe — removes support at the very point that was holding everything above it. EARTHQUAKES and blasting shake grains out of their interlock. FREEZE-THAW in the high mountains levers rock off faces. And people supply their own triggers: stripping vegetation and its binding roots, stacking buildings and dumps on slopes, letting pipes and drains leak water into the ground, and carving steep unsupported platforms and cuttings.

## Subtopic: The Six Kinds of Movement

Geographers recognise six kinds of movement, and they arrange themselves like runners on a starting grid — slowest and driest on the inside lane, fastest and wettest on the outside. Classify any photograph by asking two questions: how fast, and how wet?

Lane one: SOIL CREEP, the tortoise. The entire soil blanket edges downhill at millimetres per year. The mechanism is a repeated shuffle: each wetting-and-drying or heating-and-cooling cycle lifts soil grains perpendicular to the slope, and gravity settles them back down vertically — a fraction downslope of their starting point. Nobody has ever watched creep happen; everybody has seen its evidence. Fence posts and old gravestones lean downhill, tree trunks curve at the base as the tree grows upright while its footing slips, hillsides wear little stepped TERRACETTES, and soil banks up against the upslope face of any wall in its path. Lane two: SOLIFLUCTION, creep's high-altitude cousin. Where the subsoil freezes solid, spring thaw turns the surface layer into waterlogged sludge that cannot drain downward — so it flows slowly over the frozen floor beneath, sagging downslope in tongue-shaped lobes. The summit plateaus of the Maloti and Drakensberg show the lobes clearly.

Lanes three and four slide. A LANDSLIDE is a coherent slab of rock or soil failing along a defined surface and sliding as one body — typically after saturation, and readily where a cutting has exposed strata dipping parallel to the slope. A SLUMP is the rotational special case: failure happens along a CURVED, spoon-shaped surface, so the moving block rotates as it descends — think of a scoop of ice cream turning as it comes out of the tub — leaving a crescent-shaped scar on the slope above and a block tilted back toward the hill below. Thick soils, embankments and cuttings are slump country.

Lane five: the ROCK FALL, the only free-faller. Blocks levered off cliffs by frost or heat expansion simply drop — bouncing, shattering and stacking into the talus at the cliff foot, which is why high passes hang warning signs and steel nets. Lane six, the outside lane: the MUD FLOW. Saturate debris far enough and it stops behaving like ground and starts behaving like a liquid — a dense slurry charging down valleys and gullies at terrifying speed, then setting hard around everything it swallowed. On slopes stripped by fire or clearing, a single cloudburst can launch one. Mud flows are the deadliest runner on the grid.

Hold the grid: creep and solifluction crawl; landslide and slump slide; rock fall drops; mud flow races — speed rising along the line, water rising with it.

## Subtopic: Impacts — KwaZulu-Natal and Beyond

Start the impact audit with the ENVIRONMENT. A failed slope loses in minutes the topsoil that took thousands of years to build: the scar is stripped to bedrock, and the fertile material ends up buried under sterile rubble at the slope foot. The sediment does not stop there — it chokes rivers, muddies the water, smothers the gravel beds aquatic life depends on, and settles into dams, stealing their storage capacity year by year. Fresh scars also become entry points for gullying, so one failure invites the next.

For PEOPLE, the impacts run the whole scale from slow nuisance to sudden catastrophe. Creep works quietly, tilting telephone poles, cracking foundations and nudging railway lines out of line — a maintenance bill that never closes. Rock falls close mountain passes: Van Reenen's Pass on the N3 and the Sani Pass switchbacks both live with falling rock. Slides and mud flows are the killers: they demolish houses, sever roads, water mains and power lines, and bury whatever stood in the runout zone. And the risk is not shared fairly. Where poverty forces families onto land that formal planning refused — steep, unstable hillsides — homes are built on hand-cut platforms with no retaining structures, greywater and pit latrines feed moisture into the slope, and the vegetation that once held the soil is long gone. The hazard is natural; the exposure is social.

South Africa's defining case study is the KWAZULU-NATAL DISASTER of APRIL 2022. A stalled weather system dumped more than 300 millimetres of rain on parts of the Durban metro within 24 hours — several months' rainfall in a single day. The soaked coastal hills failed in hundreds of places almost simultaneously: landslides and mud flows swept through formal suburbs and informal settlements alike, more than 400 people lost their lives, thousands of homes were destroyed or abandoned, and the damage to roads, rail, the port and water infrastructure ran to billions of rands. Every strand of this topic meets in that event: extreme rainfall as the trigger, steep deeply weathered hills as the setting, and unprotected settlement on hazardous slopes as the multiplier. For a smaller but sharply instructive case, the Merriespruit tailings disaster of 1994 near Virginia in the Free State showed the same physics in miniature: a rain-topped mine tailings dam liquefied into a mud flow that ran through the suburb below, killing seventeen people — saturation, slope and settlement in fatal alignment.

## Subtopic: Prevention and Minimisation

Defences sort into four families — reshape the slope, control the water, protect the surface, and plan the settlement — and serious protection uses all four together.

RESHAPE AND REINFORCE THE SLOPE: grade a dangerous cut back to an angle below its failure threshold; break long slopes into TERRACES that interrupt moving material; support the toe with RETAINING WALLS or GABIONS — those wire cages packed with rock stacked beside every highway cutting; pin loose faces to sound rock with anchor bolts; and hang steel MESH and catch fences where falling rock cannot be prevented, so it can at least be caught. High mountain passes combine several of these at once above their hairpins.

CONTROL THE WATER, because water writes most failure reports: cut-off drains along the crest intercept runoff before it enters the slope; perforated subsurface drains bleed water out of the soil before pore pressure builds; cracks are sealed and leaking pipes repaired quickly; and stormwater outlets are never, under any design, aimed at a slope face.

PROTECT THE SURFACE: vegetation is engineering that grows. Roots bind the soil, canopies break the force of rain, and transpiration pumps water back out of the slope — so established plant cover is preserved, burnt and cleared slopes are re-grassed immediately, and overgrazing on steep land is controlled.

PLAN THE SETTLEMENT: hazard mapping comes first — overlay slope angle, soils, drainage and rainfall in a GIS and the dangerous land maps itself; then zoning keeps housing, schools and clinics off the red zones; building regulations govern every cutting and platform; rainfall thresholds linked to early-warning systems trigger evacuation before slopes reach saturation — a system the Durban metro has been building since 2022; and communities learn the slope's own warning signs — new cracks opening, poles beginning to lean, doors and windows jamming as frames distort, and springs seeping from ground that was always dry. For settlements already sitting on hazardous slopes, honesty requires two options: engineered upgrading — drainage, retaining structures, safe platforms — or, where the slope cannot be saved, relocation carried out with dignity.

## Subtopic: Why Slopes Let Go

Picture every hillside as an arm-wrestle frozen in the middle. One arm is gravity, straining to drag every grain of the slope downhill, and it pushes harder the steeper the slope leans. The other arm belongs to the defenders: friction, the grip of interlocked grains, and plant roots lacing through the soil like the stitching in a rugby ball. While the defenders hold, nothing happens — for years, for centuries. Mass movement is the split second the gravity arm slams the other one down. And the definition is worth two marks on its own: material moving downslope under gravity, with NO river, glacier or wind doing the carrying. Gravity, alone.

The referee who fixes the match is water — and it cheats for both sides. Dip a rusk in your coffee for a second: it comes out darker but firm, because a little moisture pulls the crumbs together. Now hold it under for half a minute: it sags, lets go and drowns. Soil is the rusk. Lightly damp, it is stronger; saturated, its grains float apart, it weighs tonnes more, and every buried surface turns slippery. That is why the hillside that stood through fifty dry winters collapses in one soaked week — and why almost every disaster in this topic arrives with a rainfall report attached.

The other ways to lose the wrestle are quick to picture: chop at the supporting elbow — a river, the sea or a road cutting undercutting the slope's toe; jolt the table — an earthquake or blasting; or pull the defenders off the field — strip the plants and their roots leave with them. And people often do all three to one hillside at once: cut a platform, clear the grass, leak water from a cracked pipe. The slope keeps score silently, and settles it in one night.

## Subtopic: The Six Speeds of Falling Ground

Line the six movements up on a starting grid, slowest inside, fastest outside, and let the water rise as you walk down the line. Lane one: SOIL CREEP, the tortoise that never stops. The hillside's whole skin slips downhill slower than your hair grows. You cannot see it move; you can only read its trail — fence posts leaning like tired soldiers, tree trunks curved at the base because the tree grows straight up while its feet slide, tiny grass staircases across the slope, and soil piled up behind the top side of any wall. Lane two: SOLIFLUCTION, the tortoise in a freezer. High in the Maloti and Drakensberg the deep soil freezes solid; come the thaw, the surface layer turns to cold, soaked sludge that cannot drain down — so it oozes over the frozen floor in slow, hanging tongues.

Lanes three and four are the sliders. A LANDSLIDE is one slab of hillside resigning all at once — like a stack of bricks shooting off a tipper truck when the bed tilts past its limit: one mass, one slide surface, almost always after soaking rain. A SLUMP is the slide with a twist. The failure surface is curved like the inside of a spoon, so the block ROTATES on the way down — an ice-cream scoop turning out of the tub — leaving a bitten crescent scar above it and a block tilted backwards below it. Check road embankments after a wet winter and you will find one.

Lane five: the ROCK FALL — no sliding, pure drop. Frost levers a block off the cliff; it falls, bounces, shatters and joins the rubble heap below, which is why mountain passes bolt steel nets above the tar. Lane six, pole position: the MUD FLOW. Pour enough water into soil and it stops being land — it becomes a thick, boulder-loaded milkshake doing highway speed down the valley, then setting like concrete around whatever it caught. Fire-stripped slopes plus one cloudburst is the classic recipe, and it is the deadliest lane on the grid.

Say the grid aloud — creep, solifluction, landslide, slump, rock fall, mud flow — speed up, water up, and every photograph in every exercise takes its lane by itself.

## Subtopic: April 2022 and How to Fight Back

Now the case study to carry into every answer. April 2022, Durban and the KwaZulu-Natal coast. A stalled weather system wrings out more than 300 millimetres of rain in 24 hours — months of rain delivered in a day. The metro's steep, soaked hills give way in hundreds of places in one night: landslides and mud flows tear through suburbs and informal settlements, more than 400 people die, thousands of homes are destroyed, and the repair bill for roads, rail, the port and water systems runs to billions of rands. Then add the sentence that turns description into understanding: same rain, unequal risk. The families with the least choice lived on the steepest, least protected slopes — hand-cut platforms, no drains, no walls — and that is where the hills failed hardest. The rain was general; the danger was not.

Fighting back is four moves. Move one, RESHAPE: shave steep cuts back, step long slopes into terraces, and hold the toe with retaining walls and gabions — the rock-filled wire cages lining every highway cutting; where rock must fall, catch it with anchored mesh and fences, the way the high passes armour their hairpins. Move two, DRAIN: since water fixes the match, catch runoff in drains above the slope, bleed the slope itself with buried perforated pipes, fix every leak fast, and never point a stormwater outlet at a slope face. Move three, PLANT: roots are free reinforcement — grass and trees stitch the soil, drink its water and soften the rain's punch, so re-green every burnt or cleared slope at once. Move four, PLAN: overlay slope, soil, drainage and rainfall in a GIS and let the map paint the red zones; keep homes, schools and clinics off them; set rainfall thresholds that trigger evacuation warnings before saturation — as Durban has been building since 2022 — and teach every community the slope's tell-tale whispers: fresh cracks, leaning poles, jamming doors, water seeping where it never seeped before. And where people already live on the red zones, the honest choices are upgrade — drains, walls, safe platforms — or relocation done with dignity.
