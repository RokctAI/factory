# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a timed practice run through the theory half of Geography, built entirely from this session's own practice set — original questions written for today, each walked at examination pace with its mark allocation named. The allocation is the instruction: in this practice set, two marks buys one developed fact, and a four-by-two paragraph buys four full sentences. Answer every question in your head — better still, on paper — before the walkthrough gives it away.

## Subtopic: How This Practice Set Is Built

Know the shape of today's course before you run it. The practice set holds five stations. Station one is a quick-fire warm-up: ten single-mark items, half from Climate and Weather, half from Geomorphology, pure retrieval. Then four walked questions, one from each theory pillar — climate, geomorphology, settlement, economic geography — each built the way strong structured questions are always built: a two-mark definition to open, source-reading in the middle, explanation stacked above that, and a paragraph task at the top where insight lives. Every question in the set carries its marks in plain sight, and those numbers are instructions, not decoration: one developed fact per two marks, one full sentence per idea in a paragraph, and effort budgeted in proportion — a two-mark identification gets one line, never a page.

Three standing rules for the whole run. Rule one: a blank can never earn a mark, so answer everything; an educated elimination is a legitimate strategy on any objective item. Rule two: definitions are the cheapest marks in the subject — the concept in your own words, said cleanly, collects them. Rule three: read the instruction verb before anything else, because discuss, evaluate and suggest each pay in a different currency. That is the machine for today. Now feed it questions.

## Subtopic: The Quick-Fire Warm-Up — Ten Marks Before You Blink

Run the climate five first, at speed. Item one: the force that gives all large-scale weather systems their spin — one mark. Answer: the Coriolis force. Item two: the cloud type that delivers the violent rain of a cold front — one mark: cumulonimbus. Item three: true or false — in the Southern Hemisphere, air circles anticlockwise around a high — one mark: true. Item four: the local wind that flows up a warmed valley slope by day — one mark: the anabatic wind. Item five: choose the option — the moisture front over the interior separates, option A warm and cold air, option B moist eastern air and dry western air, option C plateau and coastal air, option D summer and winter air — one mark: option B.

Now the geomorphology five, same pace. Item six: the high ground separating two drainage basins — one mark: the watershed. Item seven: the drainage pattern that converges inward on a central pan — one mark: centripetal. Item eight: true or false — two streams of different order raise the order where they join — one mark: false; only two equal orders do. Item nine: the lowest level to which a river can erode its bed — one mark: base level. Item ten: choose the option — paired terraces stepping up a valley's sides are evidence of, A river capture, B braiding, C rejuvenation, D delta growth — one mark: C, rejuvenation.

The lesson inside the drill: warm-up items are retrieval, nothing deeper, and retrieval is trainable like vocabulary in a language — daily, aloud, fast. Ten marks of this kind open almost any theory work you will ever face, and they are bankable before real thinking begins. Train the vocabulary until the first ten arrive on reflex.

## Subtopic: Walking a Climate Question — Synoptic Chart in Hand

Now a full fifteen-mark structured question from the practice set, built on a described synoptic map — paint it in your mind as it is read. The map is dated late January. A tropical cyclone, named Cyclone Denga in this practice set, spins in the Mozambique Channel with tightly packed circular isobars around a very deep centre. The subtropical highs sit well south of their winter positions. Over the interior plateau lies a shallow heat low, and a broad feed of moist air curves in from the Indian Ocean.

Question one: identify the weather system in the Mozambique Channel — one times two, two marks. Answer: a tropical cyclone, and adding the evidence — closed circular isobars, no fronts, tropical latitude — makes the identification unshakeable. Question two: give two pieces of map evidence that the season is summer — two times two, four marks. Two developed facts: the highs have migrated south of the country, and a tropical cyclone exists at all, since these storms need late-summer sea temperatures; the heat low over the plateau is a third seal. Question three: describe the weather the Mozambique coastline should prepare for over the next day — three times two, six marks. Three developed facts in sequence: winds climbing to destructive speeds as the eye wall approaches, torrential rain from the spiral bands with flooding on the coastal lowlands, and a storm surge pushed ashore near landfall — then, briefly, the strange calm if the eye itself passes overhead before the winds return from the opposite direction. Question four: explain why this cyclone poses little direct threat to the Highveld — one plus two, three marks. The storm weakens rapidly over land as its warm-ocean fuel is cut, and the escarpment plus distance leave the interior with only the decaying system's rain.

Count the walk: two plus four plus six plus three — fifteen marks, and every answer was either read straight off the map or drawn from the cyclone story rehearsed since the start of the year. That is the anatomy of every structured theory question: observation, then explanation, priced by the mark.

## Subtopic: Walking a Geomorphology Question — River Capture on the Landscape

Next, a fifteen-mark geomorphology question from the set, built on a described sketch. Picture two rivers. River X plunges eastward off an escarpment toward the sea — short, steep, furiously erosive. River Y wanders across the plateau behind the escarpment edge — long, gentle, meandering southward. In the sketch's second frame, river X has gnawed its valley head backward through the escarpment, broken into river Y's channel, and swung river Y's upper course east into its own. A dry notch interrupts the plateau's edge, and river Y's lower course is left a thin trickle in a broad valley.

Question one: define river capture — one times two, two marks: the natural diversion of one river's headwaters into a neighbouring, more vigorous river by headward erosion. Question two: name the three features left at the sharp bend, in the dry notch, and along the shrunken lower course — three times two, six marks: the elbow of capture; the wind gap; the misfit stream. Question three: explain why river X was the captor and not the captive — two times two, four marks: falling steeply off the escarpment, river X held the greater gradient and therefore the greater erosive energy, and its headward erosion cut through the watershed until it intersected river Y's channel. Question four: state one consequence of the capture for each river — the remaining three marks: the captor gains discharge and cutting power, deepening its valley; the beheaded river loses discharge, and farms or settlements along its lower course lose the water they were built on.

Notice the craft of a sketch-based question: the drawing tells the story, and the marks pay for naming and explaining feature by feature. Whenever a diagram appears, annotate mentally in a fixed order — name it, place it, explain it — and be ready to draw in reverse: a labelled sketch of a captured drainage system earns its marks through clear labels, never through artistry.

## Subtopic: Walking a Settlement Question — The Emptying Platteland

The settlement question of the practice set is an eighteen-mark walk built on an original case-study extract, read as description: a maize-belt town in the western Free State has lost people for two decades. The district's grain farms consolidated and mechanised; the mill that once employed eighty people closed; the co-op store followed it. School leavers ride to Bloemfontein and Gauteng and do not return. The municipality is piloting two projects: a solar farm on degraded commonage, and a farm-stay tourism route through the district's historic sandstone farmsteads.

Question one: define rural depopulation — one times two, two marks: the long-term decline of a rural area's population as its people move away, mainly to urban areas. Question two: from the extract, identify two push factors driving the migration — two times two, four marks: mechanisation and consolidation shedding farm work, and the collapse of local employers and services — the closed mill and co-op — each named with a line of development. Question three: explain one consequence for the people who remain and one for the local economy — two times two, four marks: the town ages as the young leave, and remaining services thin further; each business that closes shrinks the customer base — the threshold — of the next, so closures cascade. Question four, the paragraph: in about eight lines, suggest how the municipality's two projects could slow the town's decline — four times two, eight marks, full sentences compulsory. A model line of reasoning: the solar farm brings construction and maintenance jobs that give school leavers a reason to stay; it also diversifies the town's income beyond maize, cushioning drought years; the farm-stay route draws outside spending into guesthouses, fuel and food; and together the projects rebuild the customer base that keeps the remaining shops and services above their thresholds. Four ideas, four sentences, two marks each — state the idea, then develop it, and never bullet it.

## Subtopic: Walking an Economic Geography Question — Corridors and Sectors

The economy question, walked at pace from a described resource: a table lists each sector's share of employment beside its share of gross domestic product — primary activities small in output but heavy in rural employment, tertiary the giant on both counts — and beside the table, a map marks the Coega Industrial Development Zone at the deep-water port of Ngqura on the Eastern Cape coast.

Question one: define an Industrial Development Zone — one times two, two marks: a purpose-built industrial estate linked to a port or airport, offering serviced sites and incentives to attract export-oriented manufacturing. Question two: use the table — which sector dominates the economy, and give one reason — one plus two, three marks: the tertiary sector, because an urbanised economy concentrates its work in trade, transport, finance and government services. Question three: explain two reasons why an export factory would choose Coega — two times two, four marks: the deep-water port alongside cuts the cost and time of shipping directly to world markets, and the zone's ready-built infrastructure and incentives lower the cost of setting up. Question four: account for Gauteng remaining the country's leading industrial region — three times two, six marks: it grew on the Witwatersrand goldfields, which concentrated capital, labour and railways; it still holds the country's largest market and deepest pool of skills; and its position at the hub of the national transport network lets it gather inputs and distribute products in every direction. Two plus three plus four plus six: fifteen marks, each answer cut to the size its allocation demands.

Step back and see the pattern across all four walks: a definition opens, source-reading follows, explanation builds, and insight closes. The resources change — a chart, a sketch, an extract, a table — but the staircase never does. Climb it the same way every time.

## Subtopic: The Paragraph Question — Where Insight Becomes Marks

Paragraph tasks decide strong finishes, so the run closes there. The contract in this practice set: eight marks as four ideas at two marks each, in full sentences, pitched where thinking is required. The recipe that survives exam nerves has four moves. Move one: read the instruction verb and obey it — suggest wants workable solutions, evaluate wants a judgement backed by evidence, discuss wants both sides weighed. Move two: before writing any prose, plan four ideas in the margin as four keywords. Move three: write one sentence per idea and develop each one — the second mark of every pair is earned by the because-clause. Move four: keep the topic's own vocabulary on the page; a paragraph about drought that never says food security, climate-smart or irrigation leaves vocabulary marks unclaimed.

One final rehearsal, spoken as a model. Task from the practice set: suggest strategies a drought-prone farming district could use to protect its food security — four times two, eight marks. Model paragraph: the district should promote climate-smart practices such as drought-resistant seed and minimum tillage, because these hold yields when rain fails. Small-scale farmers should receive support with inputs and storage, since their harvests feed local households directly. Water should be banked in good years through farm dams and repaired irrigation, which stretches supply across dry seasons. And school feeding schemes should be protected in the budget, because they guarantee children one nutritious meal even in the worst year. Four sentences, four developed ideas, eight marks — and the identical recipe answers every paragraph task the subject can offer, from heat islands to informal settlements. Run this session's questions once more against the clock in your head, and structured theory questions stop being a mystery and become a staircase you have already climbed.
