**Mandy:** Questions from the class after that practice run! First: when a set of questions starts with quick objective items, should we do those first or dive into the big subquestions?

**Tutor:** Take the quick items first, and take them fast — they are the on-ramp, and three reasons say so. First, they are pure retrieval: single marks that depend on vocabulary rather than reasoning, so they cost the least energy while your nerves settle. Second, they warm up exactly the memory the bigger questions will need: an item that asks you to name the cumulonimbus cloud primes the whole cold-front story before you meet it two pages later. Third, momentum is real — students who bank the opening marks attack the paragraph task with confidence instead of dread. Two disciplines while you sprint, though. Answer everything, because a blank can never earn a mark, and an elimination that narrows four options to two still doubles your chances. And keep your answers matched to the correct question numbers — the saddest losses in any marking are right answers standing beside wrong numbers. Sprint the warm-up, check the numbering, then climb the staircase with the engine already running hot.

**Mandy:** Next one: when a question says 'account for' or 'evaluate', do those verbs really change what the answer must contain?

**Tutor:** Completely — the verb is the contract, and each one pays in a different currency. 'Account for' means explain the causes of: accounting for Gauteng's industrial dominance wants reasons — the goldfields origin, the market, the transport hub — not a description of factories. 'Evaluate' and 'assess' demand a judgement: an evaluation with no verdict — effective or not, sustainable or not — has capped itself no matter how much it describes. 'Discuss' wants both sides weighed before a conclusion. 'Describe' wants characteristics only, no reasons required — and students burn minutes explaining inside a describe question, earning nothing extra for it. 'Suggest' wants workable solutions, and sensible creativity inside the syllabus is welcome there. My drill for the final stretch: for every practice question you attempt, underline the verb first and say aloud what currency it pays in — causes, judgements, characteristics or solutions. Ten seconds of verb-reading is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

**Mandy:** One from the middle: source-based questions scare people because the extract is always new. How do you answer on material you have never seen?

**Tutor:** By trusting that the source is new but the geography never is. Any unseen extract — a shrinking maize town, a flooded settlement, a new industrial zone — can only ask the syllabus, so every extract is a familiar concept wearing a new outfit. The method has three steps. Step one: read the questions before rereading the extract, so you know what you are hunting. Step two: mine the extract — when a question says identify two push factors from the extract, the answers are lying in the text, and naming the mechanised farms and the closed mill, each with a line of development, collects the marks; reciting generic push factors while ignoring the extract can forfeit the from-the-extract credit entirely. Step three: bolt the extract onto your framework — the Free State town is simply your rural depopulation chain wearing local clothes, so run the chain: causes, consequences for people, consequences for the economy, strategies. New outfit, same body. Practise on two or three news stories this week — any article about a town, a mine or a flood — and ask which question each could carry. That habit turns fear of the unseen into recognition.

**Mandy:** Last one: any advice for spreading effort across a long question so the paragraph at the end does not get squeezed?

**Tutor:** Budget by marks and let the allocations drive you — this is pacing, not clock-watching. In each walked question of today's set, the paragraph was the single richest item on the page, so it deserves the most deliberate treatment: four keywords planned in the margin before a sentence is written. The squeeze happens when someone spends paragraph-level effort on a two-mark identification early, then reaches the real paragraph exhausted and improvising. Guard against it with the tally habit from the practice run: as you finish each part, add up what you have banked — two plus four plus six — and check it against the printed total, so you always know what remains. And if a question stalls you, leave a clearly numbered gap and move on; a stalled mind restarts beautifully on an easy retrieval item. Steady banking beats heroic struggling in every timed setting there is — bank, tally, move.
