# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a practice run through a complete mapwork exercise, taken at examination pace on this session's own practice map — an extract written for today, described fully in words since no sheet lies in front of you. The terrain is imagined, but every skill is the real thing: map codes, coordinates, contour reading, the gradient calculation, cross-sections, interpretation and GIS, each question walked with its mark allocation named and its marking walked step by step. Build the landscape in your mind as it comes, and keep a pen moving.

## Subtopic: Meeting the Practice Map Extract

First, meet the ground. Our practice map, drawn at one to fifty thousand with a contour interval of twenty metres, shows the town of Kransfontein lying in a river valley beneath the wall of an escarpment. North-west of the town the land climbs steeply to a forested plateau edge called Uitkyk, where a trigonometrical beacon crowns the crest at one thousand five hundred and twelve comma six metres. The Doringspruit threads the valley floor, joined by finger-like tributaries off the high ground. Orchards line the lower slopes; a plantation darkens the plateau; suburbs climb one side of the valley while dense, regular blocks of housing crowd the other; a railway and a national road share the valley floor with the river. Alongside the map sheet sits an orthophoto extract of the town centre at one to ten thousand — five times larger in scale, photographic detail with contours drawn over it. Hold that picture; every question that follows stands on it.

Before any question, read the margin, because a map's edge is standing information: the sheet name and index code, the scale, the contour interval, and the magnetic declination note with its mean annual change. Skilled map readers meet the margin first, so that the paper starts on their terms — and that habit costs thirty seconds and pays all session.

## Subtopic: Question 1 — Ten Quick Marks on the Maps

The practice run opens with ten single-mark items testing the automatic skills, each answerable in seconds if the skill is reflex. Rehearse them at speed. Map codes: South African sheets are indexed by the degree square of latitude and longitude they occupy, with letter pairs narrowing the square down to one sixteenth — so our practice sheet's code begins with its degrees south and east, and the letters walk a grid of sixteen blocks; know how that walk runs. Contour reading: closely spaced contours mean steep slope, evenly spaced mean uniform, widely spaced mean gentle; a closed ring with heights rising inward is a hill; a V of contours pointing uphill marks a river valley; a long finger of high ground running out between two valleys is a spur. True bearing: protractor centred on the starting point, zero on true north, angle read clockwise to the target — one hundred and thirty-five degrees puts the target squarely to the south-east. Land use from conventional signs: rows of small green dots for the orchard, green with a tree symbol for the plantation, ploughed-field shading for cultivated land, black rectangles for the built-up blocks, the cross for a place of worship, the dashed red of the main road. Ten marks, one per item, pure recognition — and recognition is trainable. On any map you meet this term, spend five minutes naming what you see aloud; the ten quick marks of any mapwork exercise are built exactly there.

## Subtopic: Question 2 — Map Codes, Grid References and the Ridge Path

Question two of the practice set, five marks, asks three things. First: transcribe the sheet's index code from the margin — one mark, and the mark is for exactness: digits and letters in their printed order, nothing added. Second: give the coordinates of the trigonometrical beacon on Uitkyk — two marks. The method, spoken slowly: latitude first, then longitude, because we sit in the south-eastern quarter of the globe; read degrees and minutes from the printed grid, estimate the seconds as a fraction of the minute gap, and attach the hemisphere letters — south, then east — because a coordinate without its letters is an incomplete answer. Third: the map shows a walking path in block D four, climbing out of the valley toward the beacon, and the question asks what landform the contours reveal beneath the path — two marks. The contours there bulge outward in a long nose of high ground running from the plateau edge down between two tributary streams, and the path rides its crest: a spur, the walker's natural staircase, gentler than the valley heads on either side. Notice the grammar inside this small question, because it repeats everywhere: an alphanumeric block like D four finds the neighbourhood, coordinates pin the exact point, and the contour question checks that you can see the land's shape through the lines.

## Subtopic: Question 3 — The Average Gradient Calculation, Step by Step

Now the centrepiece, five marks: calculate the average gradient between point A, where the path crosses the one thousand two hundred and sixty metre contour on the lower slope, and the trigonometrical beacon at B on Uitkyk — and the instruction printed with it decides everything: show all calculations, because marks are awarded for the working. The formula is given: average gradient equals vertical interval over horizontal equivalent.

Walk it as the marking walks it. Step one, vertical interval: the beacon's height is printed as one thousand five hundred and twelve comma six metres; subtract the contour's one thousand two hundred and sixty; vertical interval, two hundred and fifty-two comma six metres. Step two, horizontal equivalent: the ruler reads four comma two centimetres between the two points; at one to fifty thousand each centimetre is five hundred metres, so four comma two centimetres is two thousand one hundred metres. Step three, substitute: gradient equals two hundred and fifty-two comma six over two thousand one hundred. Step four, divide until the top becomes one: one over eight comma three. State it in words as well as figures: one in eight comma three — for every eight comma three metres travelled horizontally, the ground rises one metre. A proper climb; your calves would confirm it.

Now the marking of this practice question, step by step: one mark for the correct vertical interval, one for the measured map distance, one for the horizontal equivalent, one for the substitution, one for the final ratio. And the marking carries tolerance: any measurement from four comma one to four comma three centimetres is accepted, and any final answer from one in eight comma one to one in eight comma five earns the answer mark, because your ruler and a marker's ruler will always disagree by a millimetre. The method marks protect you — but only if the working is on the page; a bare ratio gambles five marks on one line. The companion question, two marks, asks you to describe the route the path takes from A up to the beacon: it winds along the spur in long curves rather than attacking the slope head-on, following the gentler gradient of the crest and avoiding the contour-crowded valley heads — two descriptive points, two marks.

## Subtopic: Question 4 — Cross-Sections and Vertical Exaggeration

Question four, five marks, turns to the cross-section drawn between the beacon at B and the church spire in Kransfontein at C on the valley floor. The practice set shows three candidate profiles of the same line and asks which matches the stated vertical exaggeration — and then why vertical exaggeration is used at all.

Build the skill verbally. A cross-section slices the land along a line and draws the profile: height up the vertical axis, distance along the horizontal. Drawn at true scale, South African relief nearly vanishes — a two-hundred-metre climb across four kilometres of paper is a barely visible tilt — so the vertical scale is stretched. Vertical exaggeration equals the vertical scale divided by the horizontal scale, both written as fractions. Worked in our numbers: the section's vertical scale is one centimetre to fifty metres, which is one over five thousand; the horizontal scale is the map's one over fifty thousand. Divide: one five-thousandth over one fifty-thousandth is fifty thousand over five thousand — ten. The profile is stretched ten times, and the answer must say times: ten times, never one to ten. The final part completes the five marks: why exaggerate at all? Because stretching makes relief visible and comparable — while the reader must remember that every slope now looks steeper than the ground it stands for. Matching profiles to exaggerations is then pure reasoning: same two endpoints, same rise, but the higher the exaggeration, the taller and more dramatic the drawn hill. And the discipline repeats from question three: formula, substitution, calculation, answer with its unit — each line of working carries its own mark.

## Subtopic: Question 5 — Frost, Aspect and Streams: Theory Applied to the Map

Question five is the interpretation heart of the practice run, twelve marks, and it does what map interpretation always does: it reaches back into the theory syllabus and demands it standing on real contours. Three scenes from our practice map.

Scene one: the map marks a vegetable farm at M on the valley floor beside the Doringspruit, and the question asks why the farmer there fears clear, calm winter nights — four marks. This is the valley-climate chapter on the ground: on such nights the slopes radiate their heat away, cold dense air drains downslope as katabatic wind and ponds on the floor, a small inversion forms with milder air resting above the cold pool, and M becomes a frost pocket where seedlings die. The follow-up marks ask where farming and housing prefer to sit: in the thermal belt partway up the slope, above the cold pool and below the exposed crest.

Scene two: aspect and settlement — four marks. Kransfontein's valley runs roughly east to west, so one side faces north and one south. Southern Hemisphere rule: the north-facing slope drinks the midday sun, so it is warmer, drier and more desirable — and our map shows the established, larger-stand suburbs there. The shadowed south-facing slope is colder and damper, historically cheaper, and carries the denser, lower-income housing. The full answer gives the physical rule plus the social reading, because geography's habit is to find history written on a hillside.

Scene three: drainage — four marks. At a marked junction, two first-order tributaries meet: the stream below is second order, and it stays second order when another first-order joins, because only two streams of equal order can raise the order. The marked basin on the high ground reads high drainage density — many fine blue threads on steep, wet, impermeable ground — and the pattern in this dissected country is dendritic, a branching tree on uniform rock. Every one of these twelve marks is theory you already own, asked at a map location; the skill is hearing the chapter title behind the question.

## Subtopic: Question 6 — GIS to Close the Practice Run

The practice run closes with a GIS question worth eight marks, built on four ideas. Idea one: spatial versus attribute data, asked on this very map — two marks. Spatial data locates: the Doringspruit's line, the beacon's point, an erf's polygon. Attribute data describes what is located: the beacon's height of one thousand five hundred and twelve comma six metres, the road's surface, a stand's zoning. The distinction earns full credit when the definition arrives with a map example attached. Idea two: scale and the query — two marks: a question about one street corner needs large-scale data like the one to ten thousand orthophoto; a flood study of the whole Doringspruit catchment works at one to fifty thousand or smaller; choose the scale that matches the question, because detail invisible at small scale cannot be queried. Idea three: data layering — two marks: the lightbox picture, separate transparent layers for drainage, roads, cadastral boundaries and land use, stacked and switched on demand; name the layers a given decision needs and justify each — a new clinic wants the population, roads and cadastral layers. Idea four: buffering — two marks: draw a zone of stated width around a feature and test what falls inside — buffer the Doringspruit by one hundred metres to find flood-risk housing, buffer the school by five hundred metres to test walking access.

Close the run with the habit the whole session teaches: mapwork marks are method marks. Formula before numbers, working before answers, units always, evidence for every interpretation claim. The candidate who writes one in eight comma three with the working shown collects five out of five; the one who writes a bare ratio gambles everything on a single line. Run this practice map again tonight with your own hand doing the writing, and mapwork becomes the most predictable marks in the whole subject — in your final exams and in every test between now and then.
