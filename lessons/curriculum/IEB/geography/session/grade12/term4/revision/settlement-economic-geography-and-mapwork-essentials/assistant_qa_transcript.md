**Mandy:** Questions from the class! First up: threshold, range and sphere of influence — three terms that blur together under pressure. How do we keep them separate?

**Tutor:** Anchor all three to a single shop and they never blur again. Threshold is about the shop's survival: the minimum number of customers it needs to keep the doors open. A spaza shop survives on a few streets' worth of people; a specialist heart hospital needs millions behind it — which is exactly why spaza shops stand on every corner and specialist hospitals stand in two or three cities. Range is about the customer's legs: the furthest distance a person will travel for that service. Nobody drives an hour for a loaf of bread; people fly across the country for heart surgery. Sphere of influence is about the map: shade in the home of every customer a centre actually serves, and the shaded area is its sphere. So threshold counts customers, range measures the journey, and sphere shades the map. Then let them interlock into the hierarchy: high-order services carry big thresholds and long ranges, so only a few high-order centres can exist, each commanding an enormous sphere of influence. If you can explain why there are thousands of spaza shops but only a handful of specialist hospitals using those three words correctly, the whole hierarchy question is yours.

**Mandy:** Next: the city models. Do we need every model in detail, or does the South African one carry the weight?

**Tutor:** Hold the others in outline, but make the South African city your specialty, because it carries both the description and the interpretation. The outlines: the multiple-nuclei model says a city grows around several separate centres rather than one; the modern Western city adds edge cities and freeway rings; the developing-world city pairs a colonial core with informal fringes. But the South African city is where the depth lives, because its structure explains our urban problems. Describe it in layers: the central business district at the core; the established suburbs holding the well-serviced middle ground; industry strung along the rail and road corridors; and then the defining feature — townships and informal settlements on the far edge, separated from everything by the open buffer strips apartheid planners drew on purpose. Now draw the arrow from history to the present, because that is where insight marks sit: homes were placed furthest from work, so today's city carries long, costly commutes, strained trains and taxis, and peripheral neighbourhoods with the thinnest services. Every current issue — housing, transport, informal settlement growth — traces straight back to that layout. Layers first, then the arrow from past to present: that is a complete answer.

**Mandy:** One from the front row: in the calculation questions, if the final answer is wrong, is everything lost?

**Tutor:** No — and that should genuinely comfort you, because calculation marks reward the journey, not just the destination. In a gradient calculation the credit sits on the separate steps: finding the vertical interval, measuring the map distance, converting to the horizontal equivalent, substituting into the formula, and stating the final ratio. Measure slightly off but run the method faithfully, and the method still earns its share — everyone's ruler differs from everyone else's by a millimetre, and sensible marking allows for exactly that. The discipline that protects you is the four-line ritual: write the formula, show the substitution, show the calculation, state the answer with its unit. Two warnings to tape inside your pencil case, though. First, a bare answer with no working gambles everything on a single line — if that one number is off, there is nothing left to credit. Second, a missing unit turns a right number into an incomplete answer: four means nothing; four kilometres is an answer. Slow down, write the ritual, and let each line earn its own keep.

**Mandy:** Last one: GIS feels abstract next to rulers and contours. What is the minimum picture we need to walk in with?

**Tutor:** Three pictures and one sentence-pair — that is the whole entry fee. Picture one: the lightbox. A GIS stores the world as layers — one transparency for rivers, one for roads, one for property boundaries, one for land use — stacked so any layer can be switched on or off. A layering question wants that image plus a sensible pairing: overlay the drainage layer on the settlement layer to see which houses flood. Picture two: the moat. Buffering draws a zone of stated width around a feature and asks what falls inside — five hundred metres around a river to find flood-risk homes, a kilometre around a school to test walking access; planners live on this tool. Picture three: the question typed at the map. Querying asks the database to show, say, every stand larger than a thousand square metres zoned residential. And the sentence-pair: vector data stores the world as points, lines and polygons while raster data stores it as a grid of pixels; spatial data records where a thing is, attribute data records what the thing is like. Rehearse the three pictures and the pair aloud tonight, and the GIS questions become the friendliest marks in all of mapwork.
