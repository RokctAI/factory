# Part 1 — Expert

The final term belongs to revision, and this first sweep takes the two great pillars of physical geography together: Climate and Weather, then Geomorphology. A year of theory compresses into one sitting, organised the way strong revision always is — the working mechanism first, then the precise vocabulary, then the shapes that questions about each idea tend to take in your final exams. Write while you listen; a hand that moves remembers far better than an ear that only hears.

## Subtopic: The Three Weather Systems in One Sweep

Southern Africa's weather is run by three systems, and the fastest revision is to set them side by side.

First, the mid-latitude cyclone, also known as a frontal depression or temperate cyclone. It is born on the polar front, the battle line between thirty and sixty degrees south where cold air from the pole meets warm subtropical air and the two refuse to blend. Three ingredients build it: a strong temperature contrast across that front, rotation supplied by the Coriolis force — which in the Southern Hemisphere turns moving air to the left, so air winds clockwise into the low — and divergence in the upper air, where the jet stream drags air away aloft and lets surface pressure keep dropping. Its life runs through three acts: the first gentle wave on the front, the mature system carrying a cold front, a warm front and a warm sector between them, and finally occlusion, when the quicker cold front overtakes the warm front and hoists the warm sector off the ground entirely. What you must be able to narrate is the passage of the cold front over a coastal city: humid air and a north-westerly breeze before it arrives, a short violent spell of cumulonimbus rain as it crosses, and afterwards the tell-tale trio — wind now from the south-west, temperature lower, barometer rising. Because the whole global circulation slides north in winter, these systems deliver the Western Cape its winter rainfall.

Second, the tropical cyclone. Its nursery is a tropical ocean warmer than roughly twenty-six and a half degrees Celsius, between about five and thirty degrees of latitude, in late summer when the sea holds its deepest warmth. It needs three things: that warm water as an energy source, spin borrowed from the Coriolis force — absent on the equator, which is why no cyclone is ever born there — and calm upper-level winds so the storm's cloud tower is not torn apart. It matures through four named stages: disturbance, depression, storm — the moment it earns a name — and finally cyclone. Its architecture is famous: a still, cloud-free eye of sinking air, wrapped by the eye wall where the winds scream loudest, then curved rain bands spiralling outward. Steered westward by the trade winds, these storms strike Madagascar and the Mozambique coastline, and they collapse quickly once they cross onto land or over cool water, because the fuel line is cut. For a case study, hold one real storm in mind — Cyclone Idai, which devastated central Mozambique — with one social impact, one economic impact, and the management ladder of forecasting, warning, evacuation and resilient shelter.

Third, the subtropical anticyclones — the South Atlantic High, the South Indian High and the Kalahari High. A high is the temperament opposite of a storm: air descending and fanning outward, circling anticlockwise in our hemisphere, delivering stability, clear skies and quiet. Their slow seasonal migration writes the country's rainfall timetable, and they get the whole next section to themselves.

On a synoptic map the three systems give themselves away in one glance each: a low with fronts trailing from it is the mid-latitude cyclone; a tight circular knot of isobars in the tropics without any front is the tropical cyclone; a wide letter H with lazy, spaced isobars is the anticyclone. The questions for this section are with you now — the ingredients, the stages, and the one-glance identification.

## Subtopic: Anticyclones, Travelling Disturbances and Local Climates

Give the three highs their close-up, because they explain the interior's whole year. In winter the trio drifts north with the sun; the Kalahari High parks itself over the plateau, and its sinking air, combined with the plateau's altitude, presses a firm temperature inversion down below the level of the escarpment. The interior is capped: dry, stable, frosty, endlessly sunny — the reason a Highveld July is all blue sky and brown grass while the Cape is being rained on by passing fronts. In summer the highs slide south again, the inversion rises above the escarpment and loses its grip, moist air from the Indian Ocean pushes deep inland, and the interior's thunderstorm season opens. The South Indian High adds a habit worth knowing: it ridges in behind cold fronts along the south coast, feeding moist onshore air and gentle rain to the coastal strip.

Anticyclonic circulation also breeds three travelling disturbances. The moisture front is the invisible boundary across the interior between humid air from the east and dry air from the west; along it, on summer afternoons, lines of thunderstorms detonate, sometimes dropping hail onto Free State and Mpumalanga croplands. The coastal low is a small low-pressure cell that slips anticlockwise around the coast from west to east, bringing a day of low cloud and drizzle. Running ahead of it comes the berg wind: plateau air pouring down to the coast, compressed and heated as it descends, arriving hot and paper-dry — winter's fire weather along the southern coastline, often ended abruptly by the cold front that follows. The sequence berg wind first, cold front after is one of the most commonly examined chains in the topic.

Then shrink the scale. Valley climates on a clear winter night: hillsides radiate their warmth to the sky, the chilled dense air slides downslope as a katabatic wind and collects on the valley floor, and a small inversion builds — a cold pool below, a mild thermal belt partway up the slope, cooler air again at the crest. The floor gets the frost and the radiation fog, which is why fruit farmers in valleys like the Ceres basin set their orchards on the slope, not the flat, and why old farmhouses sit above the frost line. By day the flow reverses into an upslope anabatic breeze. Aspect stacks on top of this: in the Southern Hemisphere the north-facing slope catches the sun most directly, so it carries the vineyards, the crops and the priciest plots. And the city builds its own climate: dark surfaces, concrete mass, engine and machine heat create an urban heat island, sharpest on calm clear nights, capped by a dome of pollution that a steady wind smears into a plume. Fixes worth naming: parks and green roofs, tree canopy, efficient public transport, cleaner energy. Pause for this section's questions: the seasonal dance of the highs, the three disturbances, and a winter night in a valley narrated from floor to crest.

## Subtopic: Drainage Basins, Patterns and Profiles

Geomorphology starts with the drainage basin — every square metre of land whose water finds its way into one river and its tributaries, fenced by the watershed, with interfluves standing between neighbouring tributary valleys. The supporting vocabulary must be reflex: catchment, source, confluence, mouth, run-off, infiltration, groundwater, water table. Classify rivers by their flow calendar: permanent rivers run all year — the Orange does; periodic rivers run each wet season; episodic rivers, out in the arid west, run only after rare downpours; and an exotic river gathers its water in a wet region and then survives a crossing of dry country — the Orange again on its way to the Atlantic, and the Nile as the world's classic.

Drainage patterns are geology speaking, and questions ask you to translate. Dendritic: branches like an oak tree, on uniform rock that offers no steering. Trellis: tributaries joining at right angles across folded belts of alternating hard and soft rock — the signature of the Cape Fold Mountains. Rectangular: sudden right-angle bends where streams follow joints and faults. Radial: rivers running outward off a dome or volcanic cone like spokes. Centripetal: rivers converging inward on a basin or pan. Deranged: a directionless muddle. Parallel: streams running side by side down one steep uniform slope. Add drainage density — total stream length divided by basin area — which climbs where rain is heavy, rock is impermeable, plants are sparse and slopes are steep, and falls wherever infiltration soaks the water away. Stream ordering is pure mechanics: two first-order streams merge into a second-order; the order only rises when two streams of equal order meet.

Profiles finish the section. The longitudinal profile of a graded river — one whose energy exactly matches the load it must carry — is a smooth concave curve, steep at the source, nearly flat at the mouth. An ungraded profile is interrupted by knickpoints and waterfalls, each one being worn back as the river works toward base level — the lowest level to which it can cut, permanently the sea, temporarily a resistant rock bar or a dam wall. Cross profiles mature from the V-shaped notch of the upper course through a broadening middle valley to the wide flat floor of the lower course. Take this section's questions now — patterns matched to rock, the density controls, and grading defined in a single sentence.

## Subtopic: Fluvial Landforms, Rejuvenation and Catchment Management

Landform questions pay for identification plus formation, so rehearse both halves. Upper course, where vertical erosion dominates: waterfalls where a hard cap-rock overlies soft rock, rapids where resistant bands rib the channel. Middle and lower courses, where sideways erosion and deposition take charge: meanders, eroding their fast outer bank into a steep undercut and plastering sand onto the gentle inner slip-off slope; oxbow lakes, the sealed-off loops left when a flood slices through a meander neck; braided channels dividing around their own dumped bars; floodplains built layer by layer from flood silt; levees ridged up along the banks by repeated overtopping; and, where the river meets a sea too weak to sweep the sediment away, a delta.

Rejuvenation is the topic's plot twist. Give an old river new energy — lift the land, lower the base level, or swell its discharge — and it behaves young again, cutting vertically down through its own old work. The evidence list to memorise: a knickpoint, usually marked by a waterfall retreating upstream; paired terraces stepping up both valley sides like seats in a stadium; a valley-in-a-valley form; and incised or entrenched meanders, the river's old lazy curves now slotted deep into solid rock. Why it matters to people: terraces offer flat, flood-safe farmland and building ground; incised gorges offer dam sites, hydro-power and tourism — the Blyde River Canyon is the great local example. River capture is the second drama: a vigorous river gnaws headward through a watershed, breaks into its neighbour's valley and diverts the headwaters into its own channel. The evidence reads like a case file — the elbow of capture where the flow turns sharply, the dry wind gap notched through the ridge, and the misfit stream left trickling in a valley far too large for it. The captor grows stronger; downstream of the theft, farms, towns and wetlands lose their water.

Catchment management closes the theory. Human pressure loads river systems: eutrophication, where sewage and fertiliser feed algal blooms that strangle oxygen and kill aquatic life; overgrazing and deforestation stripping the soil that then silts the channels; settlement creeping onto floodplains. The Vaal system's recurring sewage and algae crises make a strong South African case study. Management answers come in threes: rehabilitate wetlands and re-vegetate banks, control grazing and cultivation near channels, and clear thirsty invasive alien trees through programmes such as Working for Water. One cause, one consequence, one strategy — that trio answers almost any catchment question. The final questions of this part are ready for you now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same year of work, lighter grip. The climate section becomes three personalities, the geomorphology section becomes one river's life story, and the close gives you a revision routine you can finish tonight.

## Subtopic: Three Storms, Three Characters

Let me introduce the three personalities who run our weather. The first is the winter visitor from the west — the mid-latitude cyclone. It forms out over the cold Southern Ocean and only reaches us in winter, because the whole weather machine shifts north and parks the Cape in its lane. It always leaves the same signature: muggy north-westerly air before it, one wild wet spell during it, and a cold south-westerly wind after it. Any answer about a cold front that forgets the wind swing is missing its ending.

The second is the summer bully from the warm sea — the tropical cyclone. It wakes only where the ocean is bath-warm, it spins only because the planet turns beneath it — which is why the equator, with no spin on offer, never breeds one — and at its centre sits the eye, an eerie calm room walled by the most violent winds on Earth. Cut it off from warm water, by landfall or a cold current, and it starves within days. That is why the Mozambique coast takes the full blow while our interior usually receives only the dying rain.

The third is the quiet giant — the anticyclone, and we live under three of them. A giant does not chase; it sits, and its weight is sinking air that warms and dries as it descends. That is a Highveld winter in one sentence: the Kalahari giant seated on the plateau, holding an inversion like a lid over the interior — sun, frost, dust, no rain. And when the giant's air slides off the plateau edge and rushes downhill to the sea, it compresses and heats on the way, arriving as the berg wind — the hot, dry, fire-fanning wind of the coastal winter. Learn the three characters and a synoptic map becomes a group photograph: spot the fronts, spot the tropical knot, spot the seated giant. Your questions for this section are with you now.

## Subtopic: A River's Whole Life in One Walk

Now walk one river from its first trickle to the sea, because most of fluvial geomorphology is simply its biography. In the mountains the river is an infant — tiny, loud and furious, spending all its energy cutting straight down. Its valley is a sharp V; its bed is a staircase of rapids, and every waterfall is just the infant leaping off a shelf of harder rock. In middle age the river relaxes and begins to wander, swinging in meanders that gnaw the outside of each bend and drop sand on the inside. Wander too extravagantly and a flood will one day cut straight across the neck of a loop, abandoning the bend as an oxbow lake — a crescent-shaped scar of the river's younger self. In old age, on the flats, the river shuffles under a full load of silt, building its own floodplain and raising levees along its banks, and at the coast it lays everything down in a delta, provided the sea is too tired to object.

Keep two plot twists in the story. Twist one is rejuvenation — the pensioner handed a fresh cup of strong coffee. Lift the land or drop the sea, and the old river attacks its bed like a youngster again, carving its own gentle curves down into rock. The result is unmistakable: a gorge that winds like a meander — an incised meander — with old valley floors stranded as terraces stepping up the sides. Twist two is river capture — open robbery. A stronger neighbour erodes backward through the ridge, breaks in, and walks off with the headwaters. The robbed river dwindles into a misfit, lost in a valley built for a giant, and the dry notch in the ridge — the wind gap — is the evidence left at the scene. Take this section's questions now: the three ages, the two twists, and the clues each twist leaves behind.

## Subtopic: The Marks You Can Bank Tonight

Revision only counts once it leaves your pen, so here is tonight's routine, built around this session's own practice tasks. First: from memory, draw the three synoptic portraits — a low trailing two fronts with its warm-sector wedge, a tropical spiral with its eye, a broad H with widely spaced isobars — and arrow the wind direction around each: clockwise into lows, anticlockwise around highs, the Southern Hemisphere's rule. Second: write the cold-front story in six short lines — two for before, two for during, two for after — and underline the wind swing. Third: recite the seven drainage patterns, each with a two-word geological clue attached — dendritic, uniform rock; trellis, folded belts; radial, dome; rectangular, jointed rock — because a pattern named without its rock reason is only half an answer.

Fourth: write one full paragraph answer. In this session's practice set, a paragraph task carries eight marks, built as four linked ideas at two marks each, always in full sentences — the first mark of each pair for stating the idea, the second for developing it with a because. Choose one tonight — the effects of an urban heat island, or what river capture does to the people downstream — and write exactly four sentences, one idea each. Fifth: turn your case studies into one-line flashcards: a named tropical cyclone with one social and one economic impact; a named stressed catchment with one cause, one consequence, one fix. And treat definitions as gifts: drainage basin, base level, rejuvenation, inversion — say each aloud in your own words until it fits in one breath. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the three portraits, the six-line front, and the four-by-two paragraph.
