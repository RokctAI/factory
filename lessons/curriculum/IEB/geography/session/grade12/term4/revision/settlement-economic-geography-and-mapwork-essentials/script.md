# Part 1 — Expert

Week two of the revision programme carries the human side of the subject and its toolkit: Settlement geography, the Economic geography of South Africa, and mapwork skills and techniques. These three belong together, because settlement and economy are the story of where South Africans live and work, and mapwork is how that story gets read off a sheet of paper. This session compresses each topic to its concepts, its South African examples, and the shapes that questions about it tend to take in your final exams — then closes with the calculation recipes that make mapwork the most practisable part of the whole subject.

## Subtopic: Site, Situation and the Rural World

Settlement geography opens with three definitions worth saying cleanly. A settlement is any place where people live, from one farmstead to a metropolis. Site is the actual ground a settlement occupies — the water, soil, slope and shelter of the exact spot. Situation is the settlement's position relative to everything around it — the rivers, routes, resources and neighbouring towns. A tidy way to hold the pair: site explains why the town began; situation explains why it grew.

Settlements classify in three ways. By size and complexity, the ladder climbs from isolated farmstead through hamlet, village and town to city, conurbation and megalopolis. By pattern, rural settlements — where most people work in primary activities such as farming — are either nucleated, with buildings clustered for shared water, safety and community, or dispersed, with farmsteads scattered across commercial farmland. By shape, the vocabulary is visual: round settlements enclosing a central stock kraal, linear settlements strung along a road, river or valley floor, and crossroad or T-shaped settlements sprouting where routes meet. Rural land use is the familiar trio of farming, forestry and conservation.

Then come the issues, where paragraph answers live. Rural-urban migration runs on push factors — drought, the mechanisation of farms shedding labour, thin services, degraded land — and pull factors, some real and some imagined: wages, schools, hospitals, the glow of the city. At the sending end the result is rural depopulation, and it unfolds as a chain: the young and skilled leave first, the remaining population ages, schools and clinics close as numbers fall, farm labour dries up, and the village shop fails because the minimum number of customers it needs has boarded the taxi. Strategies to slow the chain: rural development projects, agri-processing that adds local jobs, better roads and services, and land reform. Land reform itself stands on three legs worth naming precisely — restitution, returning land to those dispossessed after nineteen thirteen; redistribution, broadening ownership of land; and tenure reform, securing the rights of people who live on land they do not formally own. The section's questions are with you now: the three definitions, patterns and shapes with their reasons, and the depopulation chain from cause to strategy.

## Subtopic: Urban Settlements — Hierarchy, Structure and Issues

Cities demand vocabulary first, because five similar-sounding terms carry five different meanings. Urbanisation is a rising percentage of a population living in towns; urban growth is a rise in the absolute numbers; urban expansion is the outward spread of the built-up area; urban sprawl is that spread running uncontrolled; counter-urbanisation is the drift of people out of the big cities toward smaller towns. Classified by function, towns are central places serving their farming surroundings; trade and transport towns — break-of-bulk points where cargo changes from one transport mode to another, junction towns, gateway towns; or specialised towns, such as mining or resort towns.

The urban hierarchy ranks centres by the services they can support, and its three concepts interlock. Threshold population is the minimum number of customers a service needs to survive. Range is the furthest distance people will travel to use it. Sphere of influence is the total area a centre serves. Low-order services — bread, taxis, spaza goods — need tiny thresholds and appear on every corner; high-order services — specialist hospitals, universities, corporate head offices — need enormous thresholds and gather in a handful of metropoles.

Inside the city, land-use zones tile the map. The central business district sits at the historic core, where peak accessibility drives land values up and buildings upward with them. Around it lies the transition zone, the aging belt of mixed factories and flats where urban blight collects. Beyond that, residential zones sort by income; industry follows the rail and road corridors; decentralised nodes — outlying business districts and suburban malls — are pulled outward by congestion and cheaper land; and at the edge the rural-urban fringe interleaves city and farm. Know the models in outline — the multiple-nuclei city, the modern Western city, the developing-world city — but know the South African city deeply: a colonial-and-apartheid skeleton with the central business district at the core, formerly white suburbs holding the well-serviced middle ground, and townships and informal settlements pressed to the far periphery behind deliberately planned buffer strips of open land. That layout is not background history; it is the direct cause of today's urban issues — long, costly commutes, housing shortages, service backlogs, growing informal settlements, pollution and blight. Each issue answers to the same trio of cause, impact and strategy, with in-situ upgrading of informal settlements, bus rapid transit systems and inner-city renewal as the strategies worth naming. Attend to this section's questions: the concept ladder, the hierarchy terms, and the South African city described honestly.

## Subtopic: Economic Geography — Sectors, Farms, Mines and Factories

The economy sorts into four sectors: primary activities extract — farming, fishing, mining; secondary activities make — manufacturing and construction; tertiary activities serve — trade, transport, finance, tourism; quaternary activities know — research and information services. The twist worth mastering is the mismatch between a sector's slice of gross domestic product and its true weight. Agriculture's slice is small, around three percent, yet it anchors food security, exports and rural employment. Mining's direct share has slipped below a tenth, yet it still earns foreign exchange, feeds the power stations and supplies the beneficiation chain.

Agriculture divides into large-scale commercial farming — capital-intensive, mechanised, supplying export and home markets — and small-scale and subsistence farming, labour-intensive and land-hungry. Favouring factors: a spread of climates from the winter-rainfall Cape to the subtropical Lowveld, plus strong agricultural research and infrastructure. Hindering factors: drought and unreliable rain across most of the interior, a thin sliver of genuinely arable land, soil erosion, and insecurity on farms. Food security — enough affordable, nutritious food for everyone — is examinable three ways: define it, threaten it, defend it, with climate-smart farming, support for small-scale farmers and school feeding schemes as the defences.

Mining built the modern economy: the Witwatersrand gold rush summoned the railways, the migrant labour system and Johannesburg itself. Today the reliable examples are coal on the Mpumalanga Highveld feeding the power stations, platinum from the Bushveld Complex, and iron ore railed from Sishen to the port at Saldanha. Hindrances: depth and cost, unreliable electricity, labour unrest, declining ore grades.

Manufacturing concentrates in four core industrial regions, always named as a set: Gauteng — the PWV — the inland giant that grew on gold; Durban-Pinetown with its busy port and petrochemicals; Port Elizabeth-Uitenhage, the motor-assembly coast; and the South-western Cape around Cape Town, processing farm produce, textiles and technology. Policy tries to spread that map. An Industrial Development Zone is a purpose-built industrial estate tied to a port or airport, designed to attract export manufacturing — Coega, Richards Bay and Saldanha are the names to hold. A Spatial Development Initiative is a corridor of coordinated investment linking regions — the Maputo Corridor running from Gauteng to a neighbouring country's harbour is the flagship. Behind both sits the old tension of centralisation's economies of scale against decentralisation's relief. The informal sector closes the topic: unregistered, small-scale trade and services — street vendors, taxi operators, backyard mechanics — absorbing those the formal economy misses, vital as a survival net, held back by no credit, no premises and municipal harassment; the helping strategies are micro-finance, trading shelters and lighter regulation. This section's questions are ready: the four sectors and the output-jobs mismatch, the four regions as a set, zone versus corridor, and the informal sector's role.

## Subtopic: The Mapwork Toolkit — Calculations, Interpretation and GIS

Mapwork is the most practisable skill in the subject, because it runs on recipes. The scale facts come first: the standard topographic map is one to fifty thousand — one centimetre on the map is half a kilometre on the ground — and the orthophoto map is one to ten thousand, five times larger in scale, photographic detail with contours drawn over it.

The calculations, as recipes. Distance: measure in centimetres, multiply by the scale, and state the unit. Gradient: vertical interval over horizontal equivalent, both converted to metres, expressed as one in something. Cross-sections: plot the heights along a chosen line to reveal the land's profile, then vertical exaggeration — the vertical scale divided by the horizontal scale — which reports how stretched the drawing is, answered as so-many times. Magnetic declination: take the map's stated declination, multiply the mean annual change by the years elapsed, apply the change, then add the result to the true bearing for the magnetic bearing. Area: length in reality times breadth in reality, answered in square kilometres. Every recipe rewards the same discipline — formula, substitution, calculation, answer with its unit — because working shown is working credited.

Interpretation ties the map back to the two weeks of theory: reading site and situation from contours and rivers; explaining why the informal settlement crowds the floodplain while the golf estate takes the north-facing slope; recognising land-use zones from street patterns — the tight grid of the old central business district, the looping crescents of the suburbs, the dense regular blocks of the township; and spotting economic activity through conventional signs — cultivated land, orchards, mine dumps, factories strung along the railway. Then GIS, as ideas rather than software: data layering stacks separate themes — cadastral, drainage, land use — like transparencies; buffering draws a zone of stated width around a feature to test what falls inside; querying asks the database a question; vector data stores points, lines and polygons while raster data stores a grid of pixels; spatial data says where a thing is, attribute data says what it is like. The final questions of this part are with you now: the calculation recipes, the interpretation links, and the GIS vocabulary.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ground again, walked through one family's week — because settlement, economy and mapwork are really the story of where South Africans live, work and travel, and you already know that story from the inside.

## Subtopic: The Village, the Taxi and the City

Start in a village in the old homelands. It is nucleated — houses gathered close — because the water comes from one borehole and neighbours are safety. Why it stands exactly there is site: the spring, the level ground, the ridge that breaks the wind. The tar road that finally reached it is situation: the village's connection to everything beyond. Now watch cousin Lwazi leave. The district's farms mechanised and shed their workers — push. Johannesburg's wages, some real and some rumoured, kept calling — pull. His single taxi ride is rural-urban migration; repeat it ten thousand times and it becomes rural depopulation. The village school loses learners until it merges classes, the clinic cuts its days, and the general dealer locks its doors for good, because the minimum number of customers it needed — its threshold — is now living in Gauteng. The village greys, because the young always go first.

Lwazi steps off the taxi and reads the city like a map. The central business district: towers crowded onto the most expensive land, because everyone can reach it. The ring around it: tired factories and flats gone shabby — the transition zone, where blight lives. Strung along the highways: the malls and office parks that fled the congestion — decentralisation in action. And far out, beyond the strips of open ground the apartheid planners drew as buffers, the township and the informal settlement where his cousin's shack stands. That is the South African city model — a city drawn by history as much as by markets — and Lwazi's long, expensive commute is not an accident of it; it is the model working as designed, homes placed far from work. Take this section's questions now: push and pull in Lwazi's ride, threshold in the shop that closed, and the city read zone by zone.

## Subtopic: How the Country Earns Its Keep

Picture the economy as one household with four kinds of worker. The grandmother grows and digs — primary. The uncle builds and manufactures — secondary. The sister sells, drives and teaches — tertiary. The cousin researches and writes code — quaternary. The first trap in this topic hides right here: the grandmother's work looks tiny in the account books — agriculture is only a few percent of gross domestic product — yet the whole family eats because of her, and half the countryside works for her. Mining is the family's old inheritance: gold built the house — the railways, Johannesburg, the migrant labour system — and though the seam has thinned, coal still keeps the lights burning and platinum still pays the school fees.

The factories stand in four corners of the yard, and all four must be named together: Gauteng — the PWV — the inland engine; Durban-Pinetown at the busiest port; Port Elizabeth-Uitenhage assembling the cars; and the South-western Cape processing the harvest. Government tries to spread the work around the yard. An Industrial Development Zone is a fully prepared industrial site laid out at a port — Coega is the flagship — waiting for export factories to move in. A Spatial Development Initiative is an extension cord: a corridor of investment, like the one run from Gauteng to the neighbouring harbour at Maputo, carrying trade and jobs along its whole length. And when the formal household has no chair left at the table, people build their own: the vendor at the taxi rank, the backyard salon, the bakkie mechanic — the informal sector, uncounted in the books yet feeding the families the formal economy misses. Your questions for this section are ready: the four workers, the inheritance, the four corners and the extension cord.

## Subtopic: Five Calculations That Always Pay

Mapwork hides a run of marks that are pure recipe-following, so learn the five recipes as sentences you can say in the dark. Recipe one, distance: measure the centimetres, multiply by half a kilometre on the standard topographic map, and say the unit out loud — kilometres — because a naked number is an unfinished answer. Recipe two, gradient: height difference over ground distance, both in metres, spoken as one in twenty-five — meaning the land climbs one metre for every twenty-five you walk; check it with easy numbers: a rise of eighty metres across two thousand metres of ground is eighty over two thousand, which reduces to one over twenty-five. Recipe three, vertical exaggeration: the cross-section's vertical scale divided by the map's horizontal scale, answered as so-many times stretched. Recipe four, magnetic declination: years since the map's information was recorded, times the mean annual change, applied to the stated declination — then bolted onto the true bearing to give the magnetic bearing. Recipe five, area: real length times real breadth, in square kilometres.

Then the interpretation habit that turns map marks into free marks: never make a claim without pointing at evidence. Do not write that the settlement is rural — write that it is rural because the map shows scattered farmsteads, cultivated land and windpumps. Do not write that the factory site is good — write that it lies on flat land beside the railway with water from the river alongside. And keep the GIS ideas as three pictures: layers are transparencies stacked on a lightbox; a buffer is a moat of stated width drawn around a feature to ask what stands too close; a query is a question typed at the map. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: five recipes, one evidence habit, three GIS pictures. Master those and the mapwork marks are banked before the theory even begins.
