**Mandy:** Let's do the class questions! First one: urbanisation versus urban growth — I understand the definitions when I read them, but in data questions with tables of numbers I still pick the wrong one. Help?

**Tutor:** The definitions live in different columns of the table, and the moment you see that, data questions become mechanical. Urban growth lives in the ABSOLUTE column: the count of urban people. If the table shows a city's population rising from two million to three million, that is urban growth, full stop — a number got bigger. Urbanisation lives in the PERCENTAGE column: the urban share of the whole country. It rises only when cities grow FASTER than the countryside, because a percentage is a comparison, not a count. Now watch the case every data question is secretly built on: suppose the urban population climbs from two million to three million, but the rural population climbs proportionally just as fast. The urban count rose — growth — while the urban share stayed frozen — no urbanisation. So your drill at the table is: first find which column the question is pointing at, count or share; then, if it asks about urbanisation, check both rows, because you cannot judge a share by looking at cities alone. And keep the second pair on standby for the same trap: level of urbanisation is the percentage frozen at one date — the snapshot; rate is how quickly that percentage is changing between dates — the speed. Count, share, snapshot, speed: four different questions, and the table answers whichever one is actually being asked.

**Mandy:** Next question: for functional classification, do I really need all the town types with examples, or can I get away with central place plus one or two others?

**Tutor:** Learn the full set, because these questions are usually match-the-pairs, and one missing pair is one lost mark — but the set is smaller than it feels once you organise it as movement plus specialisation. The movement family all live where movement changes state, and each type answers one question: WHERE does cargo change vehicles — the break-of-bulk point, like Richards Bay, where coal steps from trains into ships and industry gathers to process what must be handled anyway; WHERE do routes meet — the junction town, like De Aar assembling itself around the railway crossing; WHERE do you cross between countries — the gateway town, like Komatipoort at the Mozambique door; WHERE do the mountains let you through — the gap town, like Ceres or Tulbagh in a Cape Fold passage. Four questions, four types, four towns. Then the central place stands alone as the shopkeeper: it exists to serve its surrounding region — the sphere of influence — with goods and services. And the specialised towns are simply famous for one job each, so learn them as a chant: Welkom mines, Secunda makes fuel, Hermanus hosts, Stellenbosch teaches, Bhisho governs. That is the entire classification: one shopkeeper, four movement types, five one-trick towns. Ten pairings, one minute of revision a day, and the matching question becomes the fastest marks on the page.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the group chat: why do CBD buildings have to be so tall? I wrote 'because land is expensive' and only got half the marks.

**Tutor:** Because you delivered the middle of the chain without its two ends — the full answer runs accessibility, then price, then height, and each link earns. Start at the true beginning: the CBD is the most ACCESSIBLE point in the city — every train line, taxi route and main road converges there, so every business that lives off customers and workers wants that address. Wanting is bidding: many bidders competing for a few hectares drive land values to the highest in the city — that is your expensive land, but now it has a cause attached. Then comes the consequence you stopped short of explaining: when the ground floor costs a fortune, the only economic response is to multiply the ground — build upward, stacking twenty or forty rentable floors above one unaffordable plot, which is why the skyline peaks exactly at the CBD and slides downhill in every direction as land gets cheaper. So the chain is: greatest accessibility, therefore highest demand, therefore highest land values, therefore tallest buildings. Four links, and a top answer adds the CBD's strange heartbeat as a flourish: crowded by day because everyone works and shops there, nearly empty by night because almost nobody can afford to sleep there. Write the chain, not the middle link, and the full marks follow.

**Mandy:** Last one: describe-the-zone questions give me a paragraph of clues and I have to name the land-use zone. Any strategy for not blanking?

**Tutor:** Ride the taxi. The zones come in a fixed order from the centre outward, so instead of treating the paragraph as a riddle, locate it on the journey — and each zone carries one giveaway word that names it. Tall buildings, expensive land, congestion by day, empty streets at night, every transport route converging: you have not left the terminus — CBD. Mixed and changing land use, old houses subdivided into flats and offices, light industry, decay next door to renovation: the ring where the city rebuilds itself — the transition zone, and the giveaway word is CHANGE. Housing that sorts by income, and in the South African city the deformation you must name: townships sited far out on the edge, buffer strips and railway lines separating them, long costly commutes — that clue set is the apartheid-planned residential pattern, with informal settlements attaching at the edges and along transport corridors. Flat land along railways, highways or a harbour, big premises, deliberately distant from the fancy suburbs: the industrial zone, giveaway BULK TRANSPORT. And a huge mall with office towers at a highway interchange far from downtown: a decentralised node, giveaway the ex-CBD functions that moved to the highways — think Menlyn or uMhlanga Ridge. Five stops, five giveaway words: convergence, change, the deformed gradient, bulk transport, the runaway mall. Put the paragraph on the route, find its stop, and the zone names itself before you finish reading.
