**Mandy:** Question time from the class! First one: spatial versus attribute data — I always second-guess myself on borderline cases. A dam's outline, the dam's name, the depth printed next to it, a contour line's height number. Can you settle all four?

**Tutor:** Settle them with one test: does this item tell me WHERE and WHAT SHAPE, or does it tell me a FACT ABOUT the thing? The dam's outline drawn on the map — that is position and shape, so spatial data, an area feature. The dam's name — a fact pinned to the feature, so attribute data. The depth figure — also a fact about the dam, so attribute again; numbers feel technical, but technical does not mean spatial. Now the tricky one, the contour's height number. The contour line itself, the drawn curve, is spatial — it shows where a particular elevation runs across the land. But the label reading one thousand two hundred, printed on the line, is attribute data: it is the fact attached to that line, telling you which elevation the line represents. Notice the pattern across all four: everything DRAWN is spatial; everything WRITTEN is attribute. The drawing is the where; the label is the what. When you meet a new borderline case, ask whether you could erase the item without moving anything on the map. Erase the name, the depth, the height number — the map's geometry stands untouched, so they were attributes. Erase the outline or the contour curve — geography itself vanishes, so they were spatial. That erase test has never returned a wrong answer.

**Mandy:** Next: buffering questions where the map shows an empty strip of land — how much do I have to write to get full marks? 'It's a buffer' feels too short.

**Tutor:** 'It's a buffer' is the password, not the answer — full credit wants the password, the purpose and the evidence, and each is one short sentence. Sentence one names the concept: the open land is a buffer zone maintained around the feature. Sentence two states the mechanism: a buffer is a zone of chosen width in which development is restricted, so that whatever lies inside is treated differently from what lies outside — that sentence proves you know the tool rather than the word. Sentence three points at the map and names the trouble being buffered, because a buffer is always FOR something: houses held back from the stream because the floodplain drowns foundations; the cleared corridor under the power line because nothing may stand in the servitude; the untouched band around the wetland because the town is protecting its sponge and filter. And if the question gives the buffer a width — one hundred metres from the stream — repeat the width in your answer; using the number shows you read the map rather than the textbook. So the recipe is: name it, define it, evidence it, and quote the width when one is given. Three sentences, occasionally four. Anything shorter is a password; anything longer is decoration.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the group chat: layering versus integration — they sound like the same thing to me. Both are about putting data together, aren't they?

**Tutor:** They live at opposite ends of the workflow, and a kitchen sorts them out instantly. Integration is the shopping: gathering ingredients from different shops — the property register from the municipality, the satellite image from the space agency, the census tables from the statistics office — and getting them all into one kitchen, prepared to one standard. In GIS terms, data from different sources and formats is brought into a single system and georeferenced to the same coordinate grid, so that everything can work together. Until that happens, nothing else is possible — you cannot lay a satellite image over a property map if the two speak different coordinate languages. Layering is the plating: once every ingredient is in the kitchen, you organise the information into separate themed sheets — drainage, transport, contours, cadastral — that stack in perfect alignment and can be shown or hidden at will. So integration is about SOURCES becoming one system; layering is about THEMES being kept separate inside that system. One combines origins, the other separates subjects — which is why they are not the same thing but partners: integrate first, layer second, and only then can you buffer, query and analyse. If you can write that sequence — integrate, layer, buffer, query, analyse — you hold the entire GIS workflow in five words, and workflow questions become a matter of reciting it.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the layer-combination redraw, I lost marks once for a 'displaced feature'. How do I make sure my redraw is exact under time pressure?

**Tutor:** Anchor before you draw — displacement happens when learners copy shapes instead of copying positions. The redraw works because all layers share one coordinate system, which means every feature has a fixed address inside the frame, and your only real task is delivering each feature to its address. Do it with reference points. Before drawing anything, study each given layer and fix its features against the frame itself: the stream enters at the middle of the left edge and exits at the bottom right corner; the woodland patch occupies the top right quarter, its lower edge at about the frame's midline. Those frame-relative addresses are your anchors. Then draw one layer at a time into the answer frame — never both at once — placing each feature by its anchors before shaping it: entry point, exit point, then the curve between them; corner positions first, then the outline. Where the features overlap in reality, let them overlap in your drawing; hesitating and nudging a stream aside so it misses the woodland is exactly the displacement that costs the mark, because the overlap IS the information. Finally, spend ten seconds checking each feature against its original with the frame-edge test: does it touch the same edges at the same places? Size and artistic quality earn nothing; position earns everything. Anchor, draw, overlap honestly, check the edges — do that and the redraw becomes the safest marks on the page.
