# Part 1 — Expert

More than half of humanity now lives in urban settlements, and roughly two out of three South Africans do. This session covers the urban half of settlement geography in four moves: how urban settlement began and how the world became urban; the precise vocabulary of urban growth, tested as definitions and as near-twin distinctions; site, situation and the functional classification of towns — central places, break-of-bulk points, junction, gateway and gap towns, and the specialised towns; and finally the internal anatomy of a city — the urban hierarchy and the land-use zones you must identify from a description, a map extract or an aerial photograph. Every zone is delivered here in words, as a journey you can hear, because that is exactly how questions will deliver them to you.

## Subtopic: From First Villages to an Urban World

Urban settlement has a birth story, and it is the logic, not the dates, that earns marks. Towns became possible only when farming produced a surplus — more food than the farming families themselves required. Surplus releases people from the land: the released become potters, traders, priests, soldiers and administrators, and they gather at defensible, well-watered meeting points to exchange goods and organise society. The first true urban settlements rose exactly there — in the great irrigated river valleys of the Nile, the Tigris-Euphrates and the Indus, where floodplain farming delivered surpluses reliably. State the recipe as a formula: surplus plus specialisation plus exchange equals a town.

For most of recorded history the urban share of humanity stayed tiny. The industrial revolution rewrote the curve: factories concentrate work at a single gate, and the towns holding the factories exploded into cities. Early in the twenty-first century the world crossed its great halfway line — more people urban than rural for the first time — and the urbanising frontier now runs through Africa and Asia, where urban growth is fastest on Earth. Hold the contrast that questions probe: developed countries urbanised early and slowly, so their urban LEVELS are high while their RATES are now slow; developing countries are urbanising late and fast — levels still lower, rates rapid. South Africa stands between the two: about two thirds urban and rising, with Gauteng as the country's strongest magnet.

Why it matters: urbanisation concentrates opportunity and strain at the same addresses — employment, universities and hospitals alongside housing shortages, informal settlements, congestion and service backlogs. That double face reappears in every urban question after this one.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the surplus recipe, the river-valley cradle, and the level-versus-rate contrast between the developed and developing worlds.

## Subtopic: The Vocabulary of Urban Growth

The syllabus lists seven terms, and they are tested both as clean definitions and as separations between near-twins. Learn each term together with its separator.

Urbanisation is the increasing PERCENTAGE of a country's population living in urban areas. It is a proportion, never a headcount — that is the trap it carries. Urban growth is the increase in the actual NUMBER of urban residents. The two can move apart: when town and countryside grow equally fast, city populations rise — urban growth — while the urban percentage stands still — no urbanisation. Every question opposing the pair is testing percentage against number, nothing more.

Rate of urbanisation is the SPEED at which the urban percentage climbs — high across the developing world today. Level of urbanisation is the percentage itself, frozen at a moment — high in developed countries. Speed against snapshot.

Urban expansion is the physical outward spreading of the built-up area — the city claiming more land. Urban sprawl is expansion gone wrong: low-density, unplanned or poorly planned outward spread — estates and malls leapfrogging along highways, ribbon development, farmland eaten, commutes stretched. All sprawl is expansion; not all expansion is sprawl, because compact, planned growth expands without sprawling.

Counter-urbanisation is the flow in reverse: people leaving large cities for small towns and rural districts — retirees and remote workers resettling in Garden Route towns and Overberg villages — pursuing quality of life, cheaper property and safety, enabled by cars, couriers and connectivity.

This section's questions are ready: percentage against number, speed against snapshot, expansion against sprawl, and the direction of counter-urbanisation.

## Subtopic: Site, Situation and the Functional Classification of Towns

Site and situation transfer into the urban world unchanged: site is the land the town is built on; situation is its position relative to routes, resources and other places. Cape Town's site is generous — sheltered ground below the mountain, fresh streams, a natural bay — and its situation, the resupply station on the sea route around Africa, made the site worth using. Johannesburg is the opposite lesson: a famously poor site with no harbour and no navigable river, overruled by a situation planted directly on the Witwatersrand's gold. Asked why a town is where it is, answer site first, then situation, and declare which of the two dominated.

Functional classification sorts towns by the work they perform, each type with its own definition. A central place exists to provide goods and services to the region around it — its sphere of influence. Small central places supply low-order goods bought often — bread, groceries, basic services; large ones add high-order goods bought rarely — specialist medicine, universities, corporate finance. A Karoo dorp serving its sheep-farming district is a central place in miniature.

Trade and transport towns grow where movement changes. A break-of-bulk point is where cargo must transfer between transport modes — ship to rail, rail to road — and Richards Bay, with its coal terminal shifting minerals from trains into bulk carriers, is a clean South African case: where cargo must be handled anyway, industry gathers to process it on the spot. A junction town grows where routes meet — De Aar assembled itself around a railway junction. A gateway town holds an entry between regions or countries — Komatipoort beside the Mozambique border crossing is a gate town in the literal sense. A gap town commands a natural passage through a mountain barrier — Ceres and Tulbagh sit at gaps in the Cape Fold ranges, collecting the traffic the mountains funnel toward them.

Specialised towns are famous for one job: mining towns like Welkom on the goldfields; industrial towns like Secunda, built around fuel-from-coal; resort towns like Hermanus, living off whales and holidaymakers; education towns like Stellenbosch, arranged around a university; administrative centres like Bhisho, seat of a provincial government. Pair the function with the town — the pairing is the mark.

Take the questions for this section now: site against situation with the Cape Town and Johannesburg contrast, the central place and its sphere of influence, and the trade-and-transport and specialised types with a South African example for each.

## Subtopic: Urban Hierarchy and Land-Use Zones: The Anatomy of a City

The urban hierarchy ranks urban settlements by size and by the order of services offered, and it obeys a see-saw rule: many small places offering low-order services; few large places offering high-order ones. At the base, hundreds of villages and small towns with general dealers and primary schools; in the middle, regional towns with hospitals and branch offices; at the summit, a few metropolises — Johannesburg, Cape Town, eThekwini — offering everything, including services available nowhere else. The mechanism in one sentence: the higher the order of a service, the larger the sphere of influence needed to sustain it, so the fewer the places able to offer it. That sentence answers every hierarchy question ever set.

Now walk the land-use zones from the centre outward — as a journey told in words, because that is how you will meet them. You begin in the central business district, the CBD: the functional heart. Land here is the most expensive in the city, so buildings climb to multiply floor space above costly ground — the skyline peaks exactly here. Banks, head offices, department stores and specialist retailers press together; every public transport line converges; the streets are jammed by day and strangely empty by night, because almost nobody can afford to live on this ground. The CBD's signature is a trio: highest land values, tallest buildings, greatest accessibility.

Step outward into the transition zone, the ring where the city rebuilds itself: grand old houses carved into flats and offices, light industry in old premises, buildings awaiting demolition beside blocks being renovated, decay and gentrification trading punches. Mixed, changing land use — the city's permanent construction site.

Beyond it the residential zones sort themselves by income, and here South African history speaks loudest. The classic model puts lower-income housing near the factories and the ageing inner city, with income rising outward to leafy high-income suburbs. Apartheid planning deformed that gradient: townships — Mdantsane, Mamelodi, Gugulethu — were deliberately sited on the far urban edge, walled off from the suburbs by buffer strips, railway lines and industrial belts, so the poorest residents inherited the longest, costliest journeys to work. Informal settlements attach where land can be found — on the edges and along the transport corridors.

Industry keeps its own ground: heavy industry stretched along railways, highways and harbours where bulk transport runs, on flat land far from high-value housing; light industry and modern industrial parks beside main roads and airports. And along the arterials and suburban crossroads stand the decentralised nodes — mall-and-office complexes like Menlyn, uMhlanga Ridge and Canal Walk — CBD functions that moved out to meet the highways and the high-income customers, draining some old city centres and forcing the renewal projects that try to refill them.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the see-saw rule, the CBD's signature trio, the transition zone's mixed change, the apartheid deformation of the residential gradient, and the pull of the decentralised nodes.

## Subtopic: Why Cities Exist and Why Everyone Is Moving to Them

Begin with a question that sounds too simple: why do towns exist at all? Because one season, long ago, farmers harvested MORE than their families could eat. That extra — the surplus — is the entire secret. With extra food in storage, not everyone must farm. One person can weave full-time, another can trade, another can keep order. And the non-farmers cluster at one convenient spot — a river crossing, a defensible rise, a harbour — to do business. Extra food, divided jobs, a marketplace: press the three together and a town precipitates. The first ones appeared where surplus was easiest to produce — the great irrigated valleys, the Nile chief among them.

For thousands of years, towns stayed small and scarce. Then the factories came, and a factory is a gathering machine: it pulls thousands of workers to a single gate every morning. Towns swelled into cities, cities became ordinary, and early in the two-thousands the world slipped past a quiet milestone — more people inside cities than outside them, for the first time in the species' history. South Africa crossed its own line long ago: roughly two in three of us are urban.

Carry one contrast with you. Wealthy countries finished their city-building rush generations back — their urban LEVEL is high, their SPEED now slow: a car already parked at the top of the hill. Developing countries are mid-rush — level still climbing, speed high: the car still accelerating up the slope. Level is where you stand; rate is how fast you are climbing. Two different questions, two different answers, never to be swapped.

Take this section's questions now: the surplus secret, the factory magnet, and the parked car against the climbing car.

## Subtopic: The Ladder of Shops and the Jobs Towns Do

The entire urban hierarchy fits inside one shopping trip. Bread and airtime: you walk to the corner spaza — every street has one. School shoes: a taxi ride to town — fewer of those. A matric-dance outfit or a specialist doctor: a longer trip to the big city — fewer still. And for the rarest services in the country — a transplant unit, a stock exchange — there is essentially one address. Feel the rule in your feet: the more ordinary the purchase, the nearer and more numerous the places selling it; the more special the purchase, the further you travel and the fewer places exist. Many small, few big — that see-saw is the urban hierarchy, and every hierarchy question is this shopping trip wearing formal clothes.

Now the jobs towns do, as one-line job descriptions. The central place is the shopkeeper town, serving the farms and villages around it — its customer area is the sphere of influence. The break-of-bulk town is where cargo changes vehicles: at Richards Bay the coal steps off trains and into ships, and because the cargo must be handled there anyway, processing industry sets up beside the terminal. The junction town lives where routes meet, feeding off everything that passes — De Aar and its railway lines. The gateway town keeps a door between countries — Komatipoort at the Mozambique crossing. The gap town sits in a mountain doorway — where the Cape Fold ranges permit only a few passages, towns like Ceres and Tulbagh collect everything the mountains squeeze through. And the specialised towns each carry one famous job: Welkom mines, Secunda makes fuel, Hermanus hosts holidaymakers, Stellenbosch teaches, Bhisho governs its province.

Site and situation ride along unchanged from the rural lesson, with one unforgettable pair. Cape Town: a lovely site — shelter, water, a bay — in exactly the right situation on the sea route. Johannesburg: a terrible site — no harbour, no real river — forgiven instantly, because the situation sat on the richest gold ever found. Towns forgive a bad site for a good enough reason. Say that sentence and you have understood the whole idea.

Your questions for this section are up: the shopping trip, the six job descriptions, and the city that forgave its site.

## Subtopic: A Taxi Ride Across the Zones

Final picture: climb into a taxi in the middle of any large South African city and ride to the edge. You will cross every land-use zone in the syllabus, in their proper order. Watch through the window.

Downtown, where you board, the towers are tallest and the pavements fullest — banks, head offices and flagship stores stacked on the priciest ground in the city. Why stack? Because land here is a tug-of-war: every business wants the spot all the taxis and trains converge on, so the ground costs a fortune, and the only way to pay a fortune for a floor is to pile thirty more floors of rent above it. That is the CBD: dearest land, tallest buildings, busiest days — and hollow nights, because almost nobody sleeps there.

Pull away from the centre and the city turns scruffy and fascinating for a few blocks: old houses split into flats and offices, panel beaters in Victorian shells, one building coming down as its neighbour is renovated. Half construction site, half waiting room — the transition zone, where the city is forever changing its mind.

Further out the houses begin, and the window shows a hard South African truth. In the textbook model, housing simply grows richer as you ride outward. Here, apartheid rearranged the seating: townships such as Mdantsane, Mamelodi and Gugulethu were pushed to the far edge, with railway lines, vacant buffer strips and factory belts inserted between them and the older suburbs — so the people with the least money were handed the longest, most expensive daily journeys. That planning wound is still measured every morning, in taxi fare.

You will pass the working land too: heavy industry stretched along the railways and highways — flat ground, bulk transport, and a polite distance from the garden suburbs — with tidy modern industrial parks nearer the airport. And just before the city thins into veld, one last stop: an enormous mall ringed by office towers — Menlyn, uMhlanga Ridge, Canal Walk. These decentralised nodes are fragments of the CBD that moved out to chase the highways and the spending power, which is precisely why some old downtowns emptied and now run renewal projects to win their tenants back.

Here come the final questions of the lesson: the tug-of-war that builds towers, the waiting-room ring, the rearranged seats, the factory belts, and the mall that ate the CBD's lunch. Replay the taxi ride once in your head, and any zone a question describes in words will introduce itself before the sentence ends.
