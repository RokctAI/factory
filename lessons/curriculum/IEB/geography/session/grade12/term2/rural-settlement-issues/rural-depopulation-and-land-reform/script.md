# Part 1 — Expert

The heaviest-scoring rural settlement questions are not about shapes on a map — they are about people leaving. This session assembles the full machinery of rural–urban migration: the push and pull factors acting on a single person at a single moment; rural depopulation with its causes, consequences and counter-strategies; the social justice layer that explains why rural poverty sits where it sits; and the three-legged land reform programme — tenure, redistribution, restitution — that questions return to year after year. Our running example is a familiar South African one: the thinning villages of northern KwaZulu-Natal and the young workers they send to Durban and Johannesburg.

## Subtopic: Push and Pull: The Engine of Rural–Urban Migration

Rural–urban migration is the movement of people from rural settlements to urban settlements, and the required explanation treats it as two forces pressing on the same person at the same time.

Push factors are the conditions at home that drive people away. In the South African countryside the list is painfully concrete: unemployment, as mechanised commercial farms shed workers decade after decade and communal plots cannot stretch to feed growing families; weak services — under-staffed schools, clinics reached only by taxi, gravel roads, intermittent water and power; drought and degraded veld cutting what farming can earn; plots already too small to divide again for the next generation; and, for the young especially, an absence of options — little work beyond the fields, no college nearby, and thin prospects all round.

Pull factors are the attractions of the city, real or believed: jobs and better wages, high schools and universities, hospitals, reliable electricity and piped water, the glamour of urban life, and — often decisive — relatives who went ahead and can offer a first bed and a first introduction. Hold onto the phrase real or believed, because strong answers point out that migrants frequently move on the perception of opportunity. The city does not always honour its advertising; many newcomers land in unemployment and informal housing on the urban fringe.

Who moves matters as much as why. Overwhelmingly it is the young, economically active adults — school leavers and workers in their twenties and thirties — and historically in South Africa the men moved first, drawn through the migrant labour system to the mines while families remained behind. That selectivity is the hinge of this whole lesson: when the young and able depart, the settlement they leave is thrown out of balance.

Pause here and take this section's questions: push against pull, the perception point, and the selectivity of who actually boards the taxi.

## Subtopic: Rural Depopulation: Causes and Consequences

Rural depopulation is the decline of the population living in rural areas — the visible outcome of out-migration persistently outrunning natural increase. Its causes are the push factors already listed, reinforced by two structural drivers that deserve their own sentences: the mechanisation and consolidation of commercial agriculture, which swaps workers for machines and merges small farms into vast ones; and the closure of rural services — when the school, the bank branch, the post office or the police station shuts, the next round of families leaves, which closes the next service. The spiral feeds itself.

Divide the consequences between the people who remain and the place itself. The people: the age–sex structure hollows out — children, the elderly and, in the old migrant-labour pattern, women remain, while the working-age middle is absent, leaving a population pyramid pinched at the waist. Households lean on social grants and on remittances — wages sent home from the cities. The dependency ratio climbs steeply: few earners carrying many dependants. And the skills leave first, because the educated and ambitious are the most mobile — the brain drain that makes teachers, nurses and local leadership so hard to keep.

Now the place: fields fall out of cultivation while the grazing nearest the settlement is flogged bare; homesteads stand empty and sag; the school closes for want of learners; shops lose their customers and lock their doors; and the consumer base that kept a small town's main street alive simply evaporates. Some maize-belt towns of the Free State platteland show the end state — a line of closed shopfronts serving a shrinking, greying population.

Before moving on, balance the ledger, because evaluation questions expect fairness: out-migration is not pure loss. Remittances flow back and raise new brick rooms beside old homesteads; pressure on exhausted communal land can ease; and some migrants eventually return carrying savings, skills and business experience. Heavy costs, modest compensations — a top answer sounds like an accountant, not a mourner.

This section's questions are with you now: the definition, the two structural causes, the hollowed-out population structure, and the balanced evaluation.

## Subtopic: Strategies, Social Justice and Access to Resources

If depopulation is a spiral, strategies are attempts to break it — and each measure should be aimed at a named push factor, which is exactly how the marks are structured.

Aim at unemployment: development that creates work where people already live — agri-processing plants that add value to local produce instead of railing it away raw; public works programmes hiring local labour to build and maintain rural roads and water schemes; and genuine support for small-scale farmers through finance, extension advice and access to markets. Aim at services: build and staff rural schools and clinics, electrify, lay on piped water, and maintain the connecting roads — every service that arrives deletes one reason to leave. Aim at opportunity: tourism that converts landscape into livelihood — game farms, cultural and heritage routes, trails and guest accommodation. The framing line that should open or close such an answer: a strategy succeeds when it switches off a push factor, because people rarely abandon a home that offers a living.

Then the social justice layer, which the syllabus names directly. Rural poverty in South Africa is not an accident of geography; it is the residue of deliberate planning. Access to natural resources — productive land and water — was racially rationed for a century: the Natives Land Act of 1913 restricted black land ownership to a small fraction of the country, and the former homelands afterwards concentrated large populations onto the least productive ground. Access to human-made resources was rationed the same way: rural districts received — and many still receive — less investment in schools, clinics, roads, communications and banking. Limited investment and missing infrastructure are not background scenery; they are active push factors, and they explain why depopulation runs hardest through the former homeland districts. Asked why rural poverty persists, the strong answer welds history to geography: the map of today's poorest rural districts still traces, to a large degree, the lines that apartheid drew.

Take the questions for this section now: strategies matched to the push factors they attack, and the historical roots of unequal access to resources.

## Subtopic: Land Reform: Tenure, Redistribution, Restitution

Land reform is the state's programme to correct the racially skewed pattern of land ownership and land rights inherited from the 1913 Land Act and apartheid. It stands on three distinct legs, and the marks are lost by answers that let the legs blur into each other. Learn each leg as its own sentence: the damage it repairs, and the way it repairs it.

Leg one: land tenure reform. Tenure is the strength of your right to be on the land you occupy. Millions of South Africans hold land weakly: labour tenants and farm dwellers on commercial farms, families on communal land under traditional administration, residents holding permits instead of title. Tenure reform strengthens those existing rights — shielding farm dwellers from arbitrary eviction, formalising rights on communal land — so that the people already using the land cannot simply be swept off it, and can invest in it with confidence. Tenure reform relocates nobody; it hardens the rights of those in place.

Leg two: land redistribution. This leg changes ownership patterns. It transfers farmland from the historically white commercial sector to black South Africans — emerging farmers, labour tenants, rural households — through assisted purchase and state support, long organised around the willing-buyer, willing-seller principle. Redistribution faces forward: it does not litigate the history of one particular farm; it asks who should have access to farmland now, in the name of equity and livelihoods.

Leg three: land restitution. This leg faces backward at specific, provable wrongs. People and communities dispossessed of land after the nineteenth of June 1913 by racially discriminatory laws — the forced removals, the cleared districts — may claim that specific land back, or accept financial compensation instead. Claims run through the Commission on Restitution of Land Rights and the Land Claims Court. The fingerprint of restitution: a named piece of land and a named historical removal.

The separating sentence that earns full credit: tenure secures the rights of people already on the land; redistribution transfers land to redress unequal ownership; restitution returns — or compensates for — specific land taken in specific past removals. And close the geographic loop: land reform done well is itself a depopulation strategy, because secure rights on productive land deliver exactly what migration promises — a livelihood — without anyone leaving home.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the three legs kept separate, the 1913 anchor date, and the link from land reform back to depopulation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same topic now, told at the scale where it actually happens — one household, one village, one decision made over supper.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen-Table Decision

Put the textbook down and sit at the table. A nineteen-year-old in a village in northern KwaZulu-Natal has just finished school. The family's plot fed her grandparents, but it cannot pay for data, taxi fare, clothes and a future. The only nearby work is seasonal cane cutting. The clinic is forty minutes away when the taxi comes. Meanwhile, in Durban, an aunt has a room available, and the street talks endlessly about jobs and wages down there.

Everything pressing on her from behind — no work, a plot too small to split again, thin services, a drought that made even the mealies unreliable — geographers call push factors. They shove. Everything glittering from the far end — jobs, colleges, hospitals, lights that stay on, the aunt's open door — those are pull factors. They tug. Migration happens on the night the shove plus the tug outweighs the comfort of home.

Add the honest detail that lifts an answer: the tug is partly a rumour. The city promises more than it pays. Plenty of new arrivals find no job and end up in a shack settlement on the edge of town — but a promise believed is enough to move a person, and answers earn credit for saying that migrants move on perception as much as on fact.

And look around the table at WHO is deciding. Not the grandmother — she stays. Not the two small cousins — they stay with her. It is the young and strong who go, as it has been since the mine buses first came for the men. Keep that picture in your head; the whole next section grows out of the empty chair she leaves behind.

Take this section's questions now: the shove, the tug, the rumour the city spreads, and who actually packs the bag.

## Subtopic: The Village After the Taxi Leaves

Come back to the village five years later and take attendance at the taxi rank on a Monday: schoolchildren, pensioners, and mostly women holding households together. The working-age middle is gone. Drawn as a population pyramid, the shape has its waist pinched in — one glance at the diagram tells you the young have left.

Now watch how each departure manufactures the next. The school's enrolment slips each January until the department merges it with a school two villages over. The bank closes its branch — too few customers. Then the post office follows. Each closed door deletes another reason to stay, so another family loads the bakkie, which closes the next door. Rural depopulation is not one dramatic event; it is a slow puncture, the village going soft the way a bicycle tyre does — no bang, just less every week.

Money still arrives, though. Wages sent home from Durban and Johannesburg — remittances — plus the grandmother's pension and the child grants keep the household upright, and you can spot the Durban money in the fresh brick rooms rising beside old rondavels. So audit fairly when you evaluate: the leak does send something back up the pipe. But no village lives forever on sent money — the fields lie unploughed, and the shopkeeper cannot survive on remittances that get spent at the mall in town.

How do you patch a slow puncture? Find each hole. No jobs? Bring the work here — a depot that processes local timber, sugar or fruit; road crews hired from the village; real backing for small farmers so the plot pays again. Thin services? Staff the clinic, electrify, fix the road — every arriving service is one less reason to go. Nothing for the young? Tourism can turn hills, heritage and battlefield routes into wages. And test every proposed strategy with a single question: which push factor does this switch off?

Your questions for this section are up: the pinched pyramid, the self-feeding spiral, the honest balance sheet, and matching each fix to its hole.

## Subtopic: Three Repairs for One Old Injustice

Why is the countryside poor to begin with? Not by accident. A century ago the law itself — the Natives Land Act of nineteen thirteen — squeezed black South Africans onto a sliver of the country, and apartheid then packed people into homelands on the driest, steepest, stoniest ground while spending the roads-schools-clinics money elsewhere. Today's map of rural poverty still shadows those old boundaries. The state's repair programme is land reform, and it carries three tools, each mending a different kind of damage. Keep them separate with three small scenes.

Scene one: a family has lived and worked on the same farm for three generations, yet nothing stands in their name — one letter could end it all. The repair here moves nobody; it strengthens their right to remain: papers, protection from eviction, formalised rights on communal land too. That is TENURE reform — same people, same land, firmer grip. Think of it as turning a promise made in the farmyard into a contract signed at the magistrate's office.

Scene two: in a whole district, the farmland belongs to a handful of owners, a pattern history built on purpose. The repair is to help the land change hands: the state assists black farmers to buy farms and to farm them, with finance and training attached. No one is pointing at a specific old crime on a specific farm; the aim is to share the asset fairly from now on. That is REDISTRIBUTION — new owners onto the land, for equity and for livelihoods.

Scene three: a community can stand at a fence and say, our families were removed from this land, in the removals, under those laws — after nineteen thirteen. They lodge a claim, prove the history, and receive that land back or money in its place, through the restitution commission and the Land Claims Court. That is RESTITUTION — a named place, a proven removal, a return or a payout. It is the only tool that reaches backward to one particular wrong.

Strengthen, share, return. Tenure strengthens the hold of people already on land; redistribution shares land more fairly; restitution returns what was specifically taken. Write those three verbs first, then unpack each in a sentence — that is the skeleton of a full-credit answer.

And here is the loop that closes the whole lesson: land reform, done properly, answers the kitchen-table decision itself. A family with secure rights on productive land already has the thing the city was promising — a living — with no one-way taxi required. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the three scenes, the three verbs, and the loop back to the village taxi rank. Answer them and this high-scoring topic is banked.
