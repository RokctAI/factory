# Part 1 — Expert

Every tap in South Africa opens onto a piece of high ground somewhere far away. We live in one of the driest countries on the planet, and the water in every reservoir was first gathered by a catchment — a stretch of land that collected the rain and leaned it toward a river. This session closes the river work with its management half: two special drainage patterns whose courses seem to defy the rock they cross, superimposed and antecedent drainage; the reasons drainage basins and catchment areas matter so much, and the reasons they are so badly looked after; the four major human impacts on river systems, led by eutrophication; and the management responses, finished with a South African case study of catchment repair in action.

## Subtopic: Rivers That Ignore the Rock — Superimposed and Antecedent Drainage

Under normal conditions, structure writes the drainage pattern. Uniform rock lets tributaries branch freely into a dendritic tree; alternating hard and soft bands force the right-angled lattice of a trellis pattern; a volcanic dome sheds radial streams like spokes. But the syllabus keeps two exceptions for grade twelve — rivers whose courses cut across the structure as if the rock were not there. In both cases the explanation lives in the past, not in the present geology.

Start with superimposed drainage. Picture an ancient folded landscape — ridges and valleys — buried completely beneath younger, flat-lying sedimentary layers. A river forms on that young cover and arranges its course to suit the cover alone; the buried folds are invisible to it. Over millions of years the river erodes down through the cover until the cover is gone, and the channel is lowered — let down — onto the old folded surface beneath. By then the course is fixed. The river keeps the path it inherited and simply saws through every hard ridge in its way, cutting deep transverse gorges where logic says it should have swung aside. In the southern Cape, rivers that rose on the old Karoo cover now slice clean through the Cape Fold ranges in spectacular poorts — think of the Groot River cutting Meiringspoort through the Swartberg — a course inherited from a cover that has long since been stripped away.

Antecedent drainage reverses the ages. Here the river exists first, flowing peacefully, when tectonic forces begin lifting a fold or a plateau directly across its path. If the uplift is slow enough, the river's vertical erosion keeps pace with the rising land, and the channel holds its line — a blade held steady while the block of wood is pushed up into it. The world's textbook giant is the Brahmaputra, which kept sawing while the Himalaya rose beneath it; the principle is the same wherever a river is older than the barrier it crosses.

Fix the difference in a single sentence for each, because the mark is for the mechanism. Superimposed: the structure is older — the river developed on a vanished cover and was lowered onto folds it never chose. Antecedent: the river is older — it cut downward as fast as the land rose across it. The field evidence is identical, a river passing through a mountain range in a deep gorge rather than around it, and that evidence has real value: such gorges become dam sites, transport corridors and dangerous flood bottlenecks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the normal rule that structure writes the pattern, the mechanism behind each exception, and the one-line age test that separates them.

## Subtopic: Why Catchments Matter — and Why They Are Poorly Managed

Definitions carry the first marks, so nail them down. A drainage basin is the total area of land drained by a river and all its tributaries. Call that same area a catchment when you think of it as a rain-collector — the surface that gathers precipitation and delivers it to the channel. The watershed is the ridge of high ground that separates neighbouring basins, the line where a falling raindrop must commit to one river or another. From these definitions flows the principle that governs the entire topic: everything done anywhere in a basin eventually registers in its river. A river is the summary of its whole catchment.

Why basins matter, point by point. Water supply first: cities do not drink from beneath their streets; they drink from distant funnels of high ground. Durban draws its water from the uMngeni system — the chain of Midmar, Albert Falls, Nagle and Inanda dams — which means Durban's water security is decided on farmland and hillsides more than a hundred kilometres inland. Flood regulation second: a vegetated catchment rich in wetlands takes a storm in slowly and releases it over weeks; a stripped catchment converts the same storm into an instant destructive peak. Ecology third: river, wetland and estuary form one connected living system fed by the basin. Economy fourth: irrigation, industrial cooling, hydropower and fishing all draw on basin yield. And running under all of it, scarcity: South Africa's average rainfall of roughly four hundred and sixty-five millimetres a year is barely half the global average of about eight hundred and sixty, so a wasted catchment cannot be replaced.

Now the required causes of poor management. First, fragmented control: a basin ignores every administrative boundary, yet each municipality and department manages only its own fragment, so responsibility for the whole system belongs to nobody — and a treatment works failing in one town contaminates the drinking water of every town downstream. Second, weak enforcement: the legislation is admired internationally, but monitoring, prosecution and follow-through are thin. Third, collapsing infrastructure: overloaded and broken sewage works discharge raw effluent daily — the uMsunduzi flowing through Pietermaritzburg, famous for its canoe marathon, regularly carries bacterial loads far beyond safe limits for exactly this reason. Fourth, competing demands: irrigators, industry, mines and households pull against one another, and the river's own ecological share is honoured last. Fifth, short-term choices: wetlands drained for development, ploughing to the water's edge, waste dumped where the next flood will collect it.

Attend to this section's questions before we continue: basin, catchment and watershed defined, the governing principle, three reasons basins matter, and three causes of their neglect.

## Subtopic: Four Human Impacts — Pollution, Overgrazing, Deforestation, Settlement

The first impact is pollution, and its flagship process is eutrophication — a chain you must be able to recite in order. Nutrients, mainly nitrates and phosphates, enter the water from fertilised lands, failing sewage works and household detergents. The enriched water triggers runaway algal growth — a bloom, frequently the toxic blue-green kind in South African impoundments. The floating bloom shuts out sunlight, killing the submerged plants beneath it. The algae then outrun their food and die in their turn, and bacteria decomposing that dead mass draw down the water's dissolved oxygen. Oxygen collapses, and fish and invertebrates suffocate; what remains is a green, foul-smelling, near-dead water body. Nutrients in, bloom, light blocked, die-off, decomposition, oxygen crash — that ordered chain is the definition. Roodeplaat Dam, north of Pretoria, ranks among the country's most enriched dams, its recurring blue-green blooms and floating hyacinth mats making it the example worth naming. Complete the pollution picture with industrial toxins, acid mine drainage — low-pH, metal-laden water seeping out of abandoned gold and coal workings — and plastic litter riding urban stormwater to the sea.

The second impact is overgrazing. Excess livestock strip the veld; the exposed soil is beaten into a sealed crust; infiltration falls and runoff accelerates, shaving off topsoil with every storm. Rivers choke with sediment, dams lose storage capacity year on year, and gully erosion carves dongas that bleed yet more silt into the system.

The third impact is deforestation and vegetation loss. Roots anchor soil and slow water; strip the indigenous cover and slopes discharge fast, muddy floods. South Africa adds its own complication: invasive alien trees — black wattle, eucalypts, pines — colonising watercourses transpire far more water than the vegetation they displaced, measurably shrinking streamflow before it ever reaches a dam.

The fourth impact is settlement. Urban surfaces seal the ground: rain that once soaked in now races off roofs and tar within minutes, so floods peak higher and faster while dry-season flows shrink. Informal housing pressed onto floodplains stands directly in the path of the next flood, and inadequate sanitation drains straight to the channel. Wetlands — the basin's filters and sponges — are drained and built over, and every new user tightens competition for the same fixed yield.

The questions on this section are with you now: the eutrophication chain in strict order, the route from bare veld to silted dam, the special problem of thirsty alien trees, and three ways urban growth injures a river.

## Subtopic: Management Strategies and the Working for Wetlands Case Study

Arrange the strategies from the basin's rim down to its mouth. Govern by basin: national water law divides the country into water management areas intended to be run as whole units, with catchment management agencies drawing every user — farmer, factory, municipality, household — around one table, because planning by municipal boundary manages fragments of a system that only works whole. Protect the source regions: a small fraction of the land, the high mountain catchments such as the Hottentots-Holland ranges and the Maloti-Drakensberg, generates a disproportionate share of national runoff; keeping those slopes vegetated, fire-managed and clear of invasive aliens protects everything downstream. Repair the sponges: conserve and rehabilitate wetlands, which filter pollutants, absorb flood peaks and sustain rivers through the dry months. Stop the pollution at source: repair and upgrade sewage works, enforce discharge licences, treat acid mine drainage, and keep vegetated buffer strips along channels to trap fertiliser and sediment before they arrive. Manage the land: correct stocking rates and rotational grazing, contour cultivation, and a firm rule against ploughing or building on banks and floodplains. Manage demand: fix leaking distribution networks, price water honestly, re-use and recycle, and guarantee the ecological flow the river itself requires. And educate, because a catchment is managed by everyone who lives in it or by no one at all.

For the case study, take Working for Wetlands, the national wetland rehabilitation programme that has been running for more than two decades. The problem: South Africa has already lost or degraded a huge share of its wetlands — drained for farmland, cut by erosion gullies, trenched and built over — and with each one the basin loses a sponge that stored floodwater, a filter that cleaned it, and a slow-release reservoir that kept rivers alive through winter. The method: teams work wetland by wetland, building gabion and concrete structures to halt head-cut erosion, plugging artificial drains so water spreads back across the wetland floor, re-shaping eroded channels and re-establishing indigenous wetland plants — deliberately labour-intensive work. The double dividend: each rehabilitated wetland returns its services to the catchment — cleaner water, gentler floods, steadier dry-season flow — while the hand-built method employs and trains workers from poor rural communities, with strong representation of women and young people. Ecological infrastructure restored and livelihoods created from one budget: name the programme, state the problem, describe the method, and give both dividends, and the case-study marks are secure.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: strategies ordered from rim to mouth, the case for governing whole basins, and the Working for Wetlands story told in five sentences.

## Subtopic: The Funnel Everyone Lives In

Hold one image for the whole topic: a vast, shallow funnel of land. Every river drains one. The funnel is every slope, farm, suburb, mall and mine whose rainwater, sooner or later, slides toward that river — the drainage basin, or the catchment when you picture it catching rain. The rim of the funnel, the ridge where a raindrop has to pick a side, is the watershed.

From the funnel comes the sentence that outranks every other sentence in this lesson: whatever happens anywhere in the funnel ends up in the river. A taxi rank's oil film, a farm's leftover fertiliser, a suburb's detergent — the funnel forwards all of it downhill. Test a glass of river water and you are reading a report on thousands of square kilometres of human behaviour.

Why does this matter so much here? Because South Africa gets roughly half the world's average rainfall, and the funnels are the national water supply. Durban is the clean example: the city's taps run on the uMngeni funnel — rain gathered inland and stepped down through Midmar, Albert Falls, Nagle and Inanda dams before it reaches the coast. A healthy funnel also behaves like a sponge: wetlands and grassy slopes drink the storm and let it go slowly, so the river neither explodes on Monday nor dies by month-end.

And why are the funnels struggling? Because no single authority owns one. A funnel sprawls across municipal and provincial lines, each authority tends only its own patch, and the sewage failure of one inland town becomes the drinking water of the towns below it. Add worn-out treatment works, thin policing of good laws, too many straws in one glass, and the quiet daily damage of building on wetlands and dumping in storm channels.

One last item for the map work: some rivers charge straight through mountain ranges in deep gorges instead of detouring. Two histories explain it. Either the river formed on a younger layer of rock that has since eroded away and was lowered onto buried mountains it never knew existed — superimposed drainage — or the river was already there and kept cutting down while the mountains rose underneath it — antecedent drainage. Lowered from above, or older than the uplift: ask who came first.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the funnel, its rim, its one commanding rule, why a dry country lives off its funnels, why nobody manages a whole one, and the two gorge histories.

## Subtopic: The Fish Tank That Turned Green

Now the pollution story's main event, told at aquarium scale. A classroom keeps a fish tank, and over the holidays someone doubles the feeding to be kind. The uneaten food dissolves and the water quietly turns rich. Algae wake up to a banquet and multiply until the water is opaque green. Below the green ceiling it is midnight, and the oxygen-making plants on the bottom die in the dark. Then the algae themselves crash — too many, too little left to eat — and the rot begins. Rot is done by bacteria, and bacteria breathe: decomposition strips the oxygen out of the water. The fish hang at the surface gulping, and by the end of the week the tank is still.

Scale that tank up to a dam and you have eutrophication — literally over-nourishment. The fish food becomes nutrients: nitrates and phosphates arriving from fertilised fields, leaking sewage works and washing powder. Everything else runs in the same order, and the order is the answer: feed, bloom, dark, die, rot, suffocate — six beats. South Africa's own green tank is Roodeplaat Dam outside Pretoria, where blue-green blooms and rafts of water hyacinth keep re-appearing. Asked to describe eutrophication, tell the tank story in river language, six beats, in sequence — and make sure the word oxygen appears, because the crash in oxygen is what actually kills.

The remaining polluters need one line each: factories contribute poisons; derelict mines ooze acid water carrying dissolved metals — acid mine drainage; and every urban storm drain feeds plastic to the nearest river.

Three more injuries to the funnel, quickly. Too many animals eat the veld to the ground — bare soil bakes into a crust, rain skids off it, and the topsoil leaves with the water, silting dams and tearing open dongas. Removing trees and indigenous plants repeats the trick — roots were the handbrake — with the local sting that invasive aliens like black wattle drink far more than the plants they replaced, shrinking the river itself. And the city seals its ground: tar cannot swallow rain, so storms become sudden brown torrents; shack settlements on floodplains sit in the flood's lane; and the wetlands that once sponged it all up lie under parking lots.

Take the questions for this section now: the six beats in order, the overgrazing chain, the thirsty aliens, and the sealed city.

## Subtopic: Spades, Sponges and the Water Factories

Fixing the funnel — and South Africa has a genuine home-grown repair story to tell.

Principles first, top of the funnel downward. Guard the water factories: a sliver of the country — high ranges like the Hottentots-Holland and the Maloti-Drakensberg — manufactures a huge share of the nation's river water; keep those slopes healthy and every dam below them wins. Rebuild the sponges: wetlands clean water, swallow floods and pay it out through the dry season — stop draining them and start repairing them. Police the pipes: mend the sewage works, hold factories to their discharge licences, treat the acid leaking from dead mines, and leave a living strip of vegetation along every channel to strain farm chemicals out of runoff. Farm softly: fewer mouths on the veld, rested camps, contour ploughing, and never turn soil at the water's edge. Waste less: chase the leaks, recycle, and always leave the river its own ration — a river pumped empty is not infrastructure, it is a ditch. And manage each funnel as one team, everyone at the table, because the water does not check whose boundary the pollution crossed.

Now the case-study story: Working for Wetlands, more than twenty years old. The villain is damage already done — wetlands drained for fields, sliced open by erosion gullies, trenched, tramped and built over, until the funnel's sponges stopped sponging. The plan is hands-on repair: crews with spades, gabion baskets and concrete plug the drains, stop the gullies eating up-valley, re-shape the flow so water spreads across the wetland floor again, and replant the indigenous cover. Deliberately labour-hungry — and that is the genius, because the programme pays twice. Once in water: a repaired wetland goes back to filtering, flood-softening and slow-releasing for the whole catchment below it. And once in people: jobs and training flow to poor rural communities, with women and young people at the front of the queue. Sponges restored plus livelihoods created, one budget line — the model answer whenever you are asked for a South African catchment management strategy. Five sentences deliver it: the name, its two-decade track record, the wetland-loss problem, the spade-and-gabion method, and the double dividend.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the fixes from factory to tap, the one-team-per-funnel principle, and the Working for Wetlands story with both of its payoffs. Take them, and the river section — processes, patterns and management — is banked.
