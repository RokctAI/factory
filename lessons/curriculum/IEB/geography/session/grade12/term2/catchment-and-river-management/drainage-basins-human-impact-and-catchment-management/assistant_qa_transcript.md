**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: I understand that superimposed and antecedent rivers both end in gorges through mountains, but in a written answer I keep swapping the definitions. Is there a foolproof way to keep them straight?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is a single question: who was there first? In superimposed drainage the structure came first — the folded mountains already existed, buried under a young flat cover of rock. The river innocently laid out its course on that cover, then eroded downward for millions of years until the cover was gone and the channel was lowered onto folds it never knew about, sawing gorges through them because its course was already fixed. In antecedent drainage the river came first — ante literally means before. It was flowing happily when uplift began raising land across its path, and because its downcutting matched the pace of the rise, it held its line, the way the Brahmaputra held its line through the rising Himalaya. So build both definitions around the age relationship and you cannot swap them: superimposed — structure older, river lowered from a vanished cover; antecedent — river older, cutting as the land rose. The scenery is identical, a discordant river in a transverse gorge, which is precisely why the marks sit in the mechanism and not in the picture.

**Mandy:** Next one: in the eutrophication chain, why do the algae dying make things worse? Surely once the algae die the dam should start recovering?

**Tutor:** That instinct feels right and is exactly wrong, and seeing why is the heart of the process. While the bloom is alive it is already doing damage — the green ceiling blocks sunlight and the submerged plants below die in the dark — but the true catastrophe is scheduled for after the bloom's death. When the algae crash, they leave behind an enormous mass of dead organic material, and dead material does not simply vanish: bacteria decompose it, and decomposition is respiration — the bacteria breathe dissolved oxygen out of the water on a massive scale. So the death of the bloom triggers the oxygen collapse, and the oxygen collapse is what suffocates the fish and invertebrates. That is why the chain must be recited in order: nutrients in, bloom, light blocked, die-off, decomposition, oxygen crash, suffocation. Leave out the decomposition step and your answer has no engine — the reader cannot see why anything died. And keep the word oxygen visible in your final sentence, because eutrophication kills by suffocation, not by poisoning; the toxic blue-green blooms of a dam like Roodeplaat are an extra hazard, not the defining mechanism.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the group chat: if Durban's water problems start a hundred kilometres inland, what can a city actually do about its own supply? It can't manage farms it doesn't own.

**Tutor:** That question has the right instinct — a city cannot fence its whole funnel — but the answer is that it does not have to own the catchment to invest in it. Follow the logic of the master principle: everything that happens in the uMngeni basin ends up passing Midmar, Albert Falls, Nagle and Inanda on its way to Durban's taps, so every rand spent upstream is spent on the city's own water. That is why modern catchment management is built on partnerships across the basin: the metro, the water utility, national programmes, farmers and conservation agencies co-funding the same upstream work — clearing invasive alien trees from the headwaters so streamflow returns, rehabilitating the wetlands that filter and steady the flow, supporting farmers to keep buffer strips and manage grazing so the silt stays on the land, and pushing hard on the failing treatment works whose effluent enriches the dams. The alternative is to treat everything at the bottom end, at the purification plant, forever, at climbing cost, while the dams silt up regardless. So in an essay, frame it as a choice of where to spend: expensive symptom treatment at the tap, or cheaper cause treatment across the funnel — and name the basin-wide partnership as the mechanism that lets a city reach land it does not own.

**Mandy:** Last question: for the case study, how do I stop my Working for Wetlands answer from turning into a vague paragraph about wetlands being important?

**Tutor:** By forcing it through five concrete moves, each one sentence, each earning its own credit. Move one, identity: name the programme — Working for Wetlands, a national rehabilitation programme with more than two decades behind it. Move two, problem: South Africa has lost or degraded a huge share of its wetlands — drained for fields, torn open by gully erosion, trenched and built over — so basins have lost the sponges that store floods, filter pollution and keep rivers alive in winter. Move three, method — and this is where vague answers die — describe the physical work: crews plug artificial drains so water spreads back over the wetland floor, anchor gabion baskets and concrete structures in the gullies to stop the head-cuts eating up-valley, re-shape eroded channels and replant indigenous wetland vegetation. Move four, first dividend: the repaired wetland resumes its services — cleaner water, softened flood peaks, steadier dry-season flow for everyone downstream. Move five, second dividend: because the method is deliberately labour-intensive, it creates jobs and training in poor rural communities, with women and young people strongly represented. Name, problem, method, dividend, dividend — five sentences, no vagueness possible, and the same skeleton will carry any catchment management case study you are ever asked for.
