**Mandy:** Class questions before we finish! First one: I drew a longitudinal profile in a test and lost marks for putting the steep part at the mouth. I still don't fully see why the curve has to be steep at the source and flat at the mouth — isn't a river fastest near the sea, where it's biggest?

**Tutor:** You have collided with the most common confusion in this topic — mixing up the slope of the land with the speed of the water — so let us pull them apart. The profile draws altitude against distance, nothing else. A river rises in mountains, so near the source it loses height quickly over a short distance: steep curve. By the lower course it is crossing nearly level plains, giving up its last few metres of altitude over hundreds of kilometres: flat curve. That is why the graded shape is concave — steep start, gentle finish — and drawing it the other way round describes a river that starts on a plain and ends up a mountain. Now, your speed instinct is actually respectable physics: despite the gentle gradient, the lower course often does flow efficiently, because the huge volume and the smooth channel reduce friction. But the profile question is never asking about speed — it is asking about the shape of the bed. So when you sketch, anchor two points first: source high on the left, mouth at sea level on the right, then join them with one smooth concave sweep. Steepness lives at the source; size lives at the mouth. Keep those two properties in separate pockets and the marks stop leaking.

**Mandy:** Next: undercut slope, slip-off slope, river cliff, point bar — four names for two banks. Is there a trick for never mixing them up under pressure?

**Tutor:** Attach every name to the water speed and the names sort themselves. The bend has one fast side and one slow side — that is the entire system. Fast water lives on the outside, because it swings the long way round; fast water has energy, and energy spends itself on destruction. So every destructive name belongs to the outer bank: it is undercut, hence undercut slope; the undercutting leaves a miniature cliff, hence river cliff. Slow water lives on the inside, on the short lazy path; slow water has no energy to hold its sediment, and dropped sediment builds things. So every constructive name belongs to the inner bank: the material slips out of the water and settles, hence slip-off slope; the settled material forms a bar at the point of the bend, hence point bar. Under pressure, run the chain in this order: which bank is this — outside or inside? Outside means fast, fast means erosion, erosion means undercut slope and river cliff. Inside means slow, slow means deposition, deposition means slip-off slope and point bar. Never memorise the four names as a list; derive them from the speed, and you also automatically earn the explanation marks, because the derivation is the explanation.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the group: my friend wrote a beautiful answer on how an oxbow lake forms, but only got half the marks because she apparently 'started too late'. What does that mean?

**Tutor:** It means her story opened at the flood, when the question paid for the whole film. An oxbow-lake answer is a chain of eight causes, and the first links are the quiet ones that feel too obvious to write: continuous erosion on the outer banks of both limbs of the loop, and deposition on the inner banks. Those two links are what narrow the neck — leave them out and the breakthrough has no cause; the flood in her answer smashed through a neck that her answer never thinned. From there the chain runs: the neck narrows from both sides; a major flood breaks through it; the breach, being shorter and steeper, becomes the main channel; deposition seals the two ends of the abandoned loop; the loop survives as an oxbow lake; and, given years, it silts and shrinks into a meander scar. Notice the two ends of the chain are the cheapest to forget: the erosion that starts everything and the scar that ends everything. So audit your paragraph with one test — does every event in it have a cause sitting in the sentence before? A breakthrough needs a thinned neck; a thinned neck needs outer-bank erosion; a sealed loop needs deposition. Eight links, each caused by the previous one, and no marker can find a gap to dock.

**Mandy:** Last question: on the map extract, how do I tell an ordinary meander from an incised meander quickly? They look like the same wiggle to me.

**Tutor:** They ARE the same wiggle — that is the point — so stop interrogating the wiggle and interrogate the contours around it. An ordinary meander lives on a floodplain: the land beside it is almost dead flat, so the contours stand far back from the channel, the map looks pale and open around the blue line, and you may spot the family beside it — a detached blue crescent that is an oxbow lake, or a marshy scar. An incised meander lives in a gorge: the same looping plan, but rejuvenation has driven the loops vertically downward, so the contours crowd hard against both banks, often packed so tightly they almost touch, and there is no floodplain, no oxbows, no room for the loop to wander. So the drill is: find the loops, then read the spacing of the contours on either bank. Far and sparse — floodplain meander, deposition landscape, lower-course story. Tight and crowded — incised meander, rejuvenation landscape, and the explanation must mention renewed downward erosion after a drop in base level or uplift of the land. One wiggle, two biographies, and the contours are the biographer.
