**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we wrap up! First one: I keep freezing on which number goes on top. Is it up over along, or along over up — and does it honestly matter if the ratio comes out the same pair of numbers?

**Tutor:** It matters completely, because the two orders make two different statements. The formula is vertical interval over horizontal equivalent — up over along, always — and the reason lives in what the final ratio promises. The form 1 : x is a sentence: for every 1 metre of climb, you travel x metres across the ground. The "1" is reserved for the climbing. Write 1 : 20 and you have said the land rises a metre for every twenty metres walked — true for our slope. Flip it to 20 : 1 and you have described a cliff that rises twenty metres for every single metre you move forward — a wall, not a hill. Same digits, wildly different landscape. So anchor the habit at the formula line: VI on top, HE below, and when you simplify, divide both by the top number so the 1 lands in the climbing seat. If you ever finish a calculation and the x is smaller than 1 — something like 1 : 0,05 — that is the inverted version waving at you: flip it back, and re-check your division while you are there.

***

**Mandy:** Next question: why does everyone go on about units? If I divide 250 by 5 I get 50, and 1 : 50 looks like a perfectly believable gradient to me.

**Tutor:** That is precisely what makes the mixed-unit trap so dangerous — the wrong answer looks respectable. Nothing about 1 : 50 announces that it is broken; it only breaks when you ask what the division actually did. You divided metres by kilometres, which is like calculating a soccer player's speed by dividing metres run by minutes on one line and seconds on the next — the machinery runs, a number falls out, and the number is meaningless because the two inputs were never speaking the same language. The honest division is 250 metres over 5 000 metres, which simplifies to 1 : 20 — and notice how far apart 1 : 20 and the fake answer sit: one is a slope a truck groans up, the other is nearly railway-flat. A whole grade of interpretation swings on that error. So build the conversion into your ritual: the moment a distance arrives in kilometres, write its metre value next to it before touching anything else — 5 km becomes 5 000 m on paper, immediately. Then run the sense check at the end: does the steepness match the contours you can see? Crowded contours with a gentle-looking ratio means go back and find the unit that lied.

***

**Mandy:** This one's from the group chat: if the ground between the two points dips down into a valley and then climbs again, isn't the answer just wrong? We ignored the whole valley.

**Tutor:** The answer is not wrong — it is honest about what it claims to be, and the word doing that work is average. Your 1 : 20 never promised to describe every footstep between beacon 89 and the spot height; it promised the overall relationship: total rise of 250 metres, spread evenly across the total 5 000 metres, as if the crooked ground were ironed into one straight ramp. Think of a bus trip where the driver crawls through roadworks and then speeds up on the open stretch — quoting the average speed for the trip is not a lie about the roadworks, it is a summary at a coarser scale. Same here. And the summary is exactly what most users want: a road planner asks whether the route as a whole is buildable; a hydrologist asks how much energy a river loses over a reach. When the detail matters — where exactly the steepest pitch sits — geographers reach for a different tool, the cross-section, which draws the real ups and downs. In a written answer, one sentence covers you: this is an average gradient, the total rise spread over the total distance, so local dips and climbs between the two points are smoothed out. Say that, and the valley is accounted for rather than ignored.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: I always run out of time in map work. Which parts of the gradient answer can I safely skip when I'm rushing?

**Tutor:** None of the four lines — and rushing is exactly when the layout saves you rather than slows you. Understand how the credit is spread: the formula line earns, the substitution with matching units earns, the simplification earns, and the ratio-form answer earns. Skip the formula and substitution to jot only "1 : 20", and you have gambled everything on one number: if a subtraction slipped anywhere upstream, you hold nothing, whereas the learner who showed the method keeps most of the credit even with an arithmetic slip at the end. The four lines also cost less time than you think — perhaps forty seconds once drilled — because they are always the same four lines: formula, numbers with units, divide by the top, ratio. What you can legitimately compress is the prose: the interpretation is one sentence, not a paragraph — the land rises 1 metre per 20 metres, a steep slope — and the checks are done with your eyes, not your pen: reverse-multiply 250 by 20 in your head, glance at the units, ask whether the steepness suits the contours. So the time-saving strategy is not skipping lines; it is drilling the four-line skeleton until it writes itself, and spending the saved seconds on the measurement, where a two-millimetre slip quietly costs a hundred metres of ground.
