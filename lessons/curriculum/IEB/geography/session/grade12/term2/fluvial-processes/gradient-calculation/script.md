# Part 1 — Expert

Average gradient is the piece of arithmetic that turns the look of a slope into a number that decides real things — where a road can go, what a farmer can plough, how a river will behave. This session assembles the skill from the ground up: the definition and the interpretation rule that decides the follow-up marks, the harvesting of the two ingredients from a topographic sheet, the line-by-line written layout that collects full credit, and the verification checks and classic traps. One worked problem runs through the whole lesson: calculate the average gradient between trig beacon 89, standing at 1 350 metres, and a spot height of 1 100 metres, where the two points lie 5 kilometres apart on a 1:50 000 topographic map.

## Subtopic: What Gradient Means

Gradient measures how steeply the land climbs or drops between two points. Contour spacing already gives you steepness as an impression — crowded lines mean a hard climb. Gradient upgrades the impression to an exact number.

Here is the definition. Gradient is the ratio of the vertical interval — the height gained — to the horizontal equivalent — the horizontal distance across which that height is gained. As a formula: gradient equals vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent. VI over HE. Up over along.

The answer is written as a ratio in the form 1 : x, and the meaning of that form is wonderfully physical: for every 1 metre you rise, you move x metres across the ground. A gradient of 1 : 4 is punishing — a metre of climb for every four paces. A gradient of 1 : 400 spreads that same metre of climb across four hundred metres of walking — the ground feels flat.

Which brings us to the interpretation rule that costs more marks than the arithmetic ever does: the BIGGER the number after the colon, the GENTLER the slope. 1 : 20 is far steeper than 1 : 200. The large number does not mean a large slope; it means your single metre of climbing has been thinned out over a longer walk.

Why the skill matters: beyond its regular place in map-skills questions, gradient is the spine of fluvial geomorphology — long profiles, stream energy, erosion and deposition all answer to it — and it settles practical disputes: can this hillside carry a road, a pipeline, a vineyard? Engineers think in gradients daily; for the next quarter hour, so do we.

Take this section's questions now: the formula in its correct order, the plain-language meaning of a ratio like 1 : 4, and the rule that ranks 1 : 20 against 1 : 200.

## Subtopic: Extracting VI and HE from the Map

Every gradient answer starts with harvesting two ingredients off the sheet. Ingredient one: the vertical interval.

The vertical interval is nothing more than the height difference: higher point minus lower point. Our anchors are the two most trustworthy height sources a topographic map prints. A trig beacon — the small triangle with its station number — marks a surveyed altitude: beacon 89 stands at 1 350 metres. A spot height — a dot with a printed number — marks another exact altitude: 1 100 metres.

So: VI equals 1 350 minus 1 100, which is 250 metres. Write the subtraction out in full in your answer — that visible working earns its own credit.

Ingredient two: the horizontal equivalent — the real horizontal distance between the points, expressed in the same units as the VI. Our problem states it: 5 kilometres. But rehearse the harvesting, because most questions make you measure. You would set a ruler between beacon and spot height and read the paper distance — on this 1:50 000 sheet, 10 centimetres. Multiply by the scale: 10 centimetres times 50 000 gives 500 000 centimetres of actual ground. Divide by 100 to reach metres: 5 000 metres. Divide by 1 000 to reach kilometres: 5 kilometres — matching what the question declared.

Now the step where answers die: units. The VI speaks metres; the HE arrived speaking kilometres. Dividing metres by kilometres produces gibberish — the two ingredients must share a unit before any division. Convert the HE: 5 kilometres is 5 000 metres. Only now, with 250 metres over 5 000 metres, is the calculation allowed to begin.

## Subtopic: The Calculation — Laid Out for Marks

Here is the full written layout. Each line is worth something, so each line goes on paper.

Line one, the formula: gradient equals vertical interval over horizontal equivalent. Stating it is normally the opening credit.

Line two, substitution with units showing: gradient equals 250 metres over 5 000 metres. Correct substitution — both values in the same unit — is the second credit.

Line three, simplification. The target form is 1 : x, so divide numerator and denominator by the numerator, 250. The top becomes 1. The bottom becomes 5 000 divided by 250 — and 250 goes into 5 000 twenty times, because 250 times 20 is 5 000. The fraction is 1 over 20.

Line four, the answer in ratio form: gradient equals 1 : 20. That is the currency the final credit accepts. A decimal such as 0,05, or the fraction abandoned as 250 over 5 000, holds the same value but is not in the required form and generally forfeits the mark.

Then interpret, because a follow-up almost always asks: 1 : 20 means the land rises 1 metre for every 20 metres travelled horizontally. Is that steep? For real terrain, yes — a cyclist stands on the pedals, a loaded truck drops to low gear, and a railway engineer walks away, since railways demand something gentler than roughly 1 : 50. One clear sentence like that turns arithmetic into geography.

Finally, the word average. Between beacon 89 and the spot height the actual land may sag into a stream valley and rear up again — the slope is anything but uniform. Our 1 : 20 smears the total rise evenly across the total distance: a straight-line summary of a crooked reality. If you are asked why the answer is called an average gradient, that is the reason.

## Subtopic: Verification, Traps and the Full Method

Before leaving any gradient answer, spend thirty seconds on three checks. Check one: sense. A 250-metre rise over a 5-kilometre walk should come out as ordinary hill country — if your x lands at 0,02 or at 20 000, a unit has slipped somewhere and the sense check has caught it. Check two: reverse the arithmetic — 250 times 20 gives back 5 000, so the simplification is sound. Check three: units — confirm both ingredients were in metres at the moment of division. Three passes and the answer is safe.

Now the traps, ranked by the damage they do. Trap one: mixed units — dividing 250 metres by 5 and offering a ratio built on that, when the 5 was kilometres; the honest division only works with 5 000. Trap two: inverting the ratio — dividing HE by VI the wrong way around and reporting 20 : 1, or dividing VI by HE and leaving the answer as 0,05. Anchor yourself: the vertical 1 metre owns the "1" position in the ratio. Trap three: subtracting the heights in the wrong order and dragging a minus sign through the working — gradient reports the size of the rise, so take the positive difference. Trap four: careless measurement on measure-it-yourself questions — at 1:50 000, an error of just 2 millimetres on paper is 100 metres of ground; measure to the nearest half millimetre.

The whole method in one breath: read both heights, subtract for VI; measure or read the distance, convert it to metres for HE; write the formula; substitute matching units; simplify to 1 : x; interpret in one sentence; run the three checks.

And notice where the skill travels: a river's long profile is a string of gradients, steep in the upper course and gentle near the mouth; cross-section questions ask for the gradient between two banks; settlement and development questions ask whether a slope can hold housing or a landing strip. One formula opens all those doors. Drill it until 1 : 20 appears without hesitation — few marks in the subject come faster.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same slope again, but this time we start at the school hall — one number for going up, one number for going along, and a way of saying "steep" your legs already understand.

## Subtopic: The Ramp and the Staircase

Picture the entrance to a clinic built on a slope. Straight ahead is a short staircase — six quick steps and you are at the door, breathing harder. Curling away to the side is the wheelchair ramp — long, lazy, doubling back on itself. Both routes lift you exactly the same height off the pavement. The only difference is how far you travel while being lifted. Hold onto that difference: it is the entire lesson.

Ask any slope one question: for every one metre I go UP, how many metres do I go ALONG? The staircase might answer one up for every two along. The ramp might answer one up for every twelve along. Geographers write that answer with a colon — one up, twelve along becomes 1 : 12.

Now for the trap nearly everyone falls into once. Which is steeper, 1 : 20 or 1 : 200? Instinct grabs the bigger number and shouts steeper — and instinct is wrong. Think of a single teaspoon of concentrated juice. Pour it into a small glass of water and the drink is strong; pour the same teaspoon into a full jug and you can hardly taste it. Same teaspoon, more water. Climbing works the same way: one metre of climb packed into 20 metres of walking is a proper slog, but one metre stretched across 200 metres disappears underfoot. Bigger second number, weaker slope. Every time.

Only now, with the idea secure, do the parts get their formal names. The "up" number is the vertical interval. The "along" number is the horizontal equivalent. The pair, written 1 : something, is the gradient.

Your questions for this section are with you now: staircase against ramp, what a ratio like 1 : 12 actually says, and which of two ratios is the steeper. Keep the teaspoon-in-the-jug picture and these are yours.

## Subtopic: Two Numbers Off the Map, Both Speaking Metres

The gradient needs its two numbers, and the map is holding both.

The "up" number first. Maps publish heights at points you can trust completely. A small triangle with a number beside it is a trig beacon — a spot a surveyor physically occupied and measured; its height is exact. A dot with a number is a spot height — exact as well. Our problem supplies one of each: beacon 89 at 1 350 metres and a spot height at 1 100 metres. The climb between them is the high one minus the low one: 1 350 minus 1 100 is 250 metres. Write that subtraction down — it earns credit all by itself.

Now the "along" number. This question donates it — 5 kilometres — but most questions make you earn it with a ruler. Lay the ruler between the two points: on this sheet it reads 10 centimetres of paper. The map has shrunk the world fifty-thousand-fold, so un-shrink it: 10 times 50 000 is 500 000 centimetres. One hundred centimetres to the metre gives 5 000 metres; one thousand metres to the kilometre gives 5 kilometres. The ruler and the question agree.

And now the single most dangerous moment in the topic, so slow down. The "up" number speaks metres. The "along" number is lounging around in kilometres. You cannot divide one by the other any more than you can find a price by dividing rands by cents — the answer lands a thousand times out and means nothing at all. Translate first: 5 kilometres is 5 000 metres. Now both numbers speak metres — 250 up, 5 000 along — and the division is legal. Build that translation into your hands as a habit and the most common gradient error in the country loses its power over you.

## Subtopic: Share It Out, Then Say It in One Line

All the hard thinking is behind you. What remains is a sharing sum, and you have shared before.

You hold 250 metres of climbing and 5 000 metres of walking. Asking "how many metres along for each single metre up?" is just sharing 5 000 out among 250 — exactly like asking how many R250 concert tickets a prize of R5 000 will buy. The answer is 20, and you can prove it backwards in a second: 250 times 20 is 5 000. So the slope climbs one metre for every twenty along, written 1 : 20.

Lay it out the way the marker pays, because every line is money. Line one, the formula: gradient equals vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent. Line two, the numbers in with their units visible: 250 metres over 5 000 metres. Line three, the share: divide top and bottom by 250, leaving 1 over 20. Line four, the answer dressed correctly: 1 : 20. Handing in 0,05, or the raw 250 over 5 000, is like paying at a till with a jar of loose five-cent pieces — the value is all there, but it is not in a form anyone will accept.

Then spend one sentence on meaning, because that sentence is often a mark on its own: the ground rises 1 metre for every 20 metres you cover — genuinely steep, the kind of hill where a minibus changes down, a cyclist stands up, and a railway simply is not built, because rail wants gentler than about 1 : 50.

One more word to own: average. Between the beacon and the spot height the ground is not a smooth ramp — it may dip through a donga, flatten out, then climb hard at the end. Your 1 : 20 ignores the wiggles and spreads the whole climb evenly over the whole walk, the way you might quote your average monthly spend on data even though some months were heavy and some light. That is why the answer is called the average gradient — and saying so answers the question whenever it is asked.

Three final guards. Both numbers in metres before dividing, always, or the units poison the answer. The "1" belongs to the climbing, never the walking — 20 : 1 is a different and wrong statement. And if your subtraction spits out a negative, keep only its size; a slope is exactly as steep going down as coming up.
