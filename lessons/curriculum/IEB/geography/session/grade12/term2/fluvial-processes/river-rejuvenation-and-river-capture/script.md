# Part 1 — Expert

Grade twelve fluvial geomorphology is ruled by two dramas: a river that recovers its youth, and a river that robs its neighbour. Rejuvenation and river capture carry heavy theory marks, and both are, at bottom, stories about energy — a river's energy budget torn up and rewritten overnight. This session works through rejuvenation's causes and its four signature landforms, then river capture's mechanism with its five-piece vocabulary, then what both processes mean for the people living beside these rivers, and finally the identification skills that let you read the evidence off long profiles, cross-sections and topographic maps.

## Subtopic: Rejuvenation — A River Made Young Again

The starting condition is the graded profile. Given enough time, a river planes its long profile — the source-to-mouth curve — into a smooth concave sweep: steep in the headwaters, flattening steadily to the mouth. Such a river is graded: energy and load in equilibrium, erosion balancing deposition along its length. The level governing all of this is base level — the lowest level to which the river can erode, which is ultimately the level of the sea it drains into.

Rejuvenation is the reversal of retirement: the river recovers erosive energy and behaves like a young river again, cutting vertically downward with real violence — because the relationship between river and base level has been disturbed. Sort the causes into the two required families.

Family one: base level falls — eustatic change when the movement belongs to the sea itself. During a glacial period, vast volumes of ocean water are imprisoned in ice sheets and world sea level drops; the river's mouth is suddenly stranded above the new sea surface. The lowest reach steepens sharply, velocity and energy climb, and the river begins trenching into its own floodplain, propagating the new steepness upstream.

Family two: the land rises — tectonic or isostatic uplift. The crust beneath the river is pushed up, as the southern African interior has been lifted more than once in its history, and every slope along the profile is steepened at a stroke. The outcome is identical: more speed, more energy, vertical erosion switched back on. A third, local trigger deserves its sentence: a sustained increase in discharge — a wetter climate phase, or the bonus water delivered by a river capture — also re-arms the river.

Compress the mechanism into one line: rejuvenation converts new height into renewed downcutting, and the junction where the new steep profile meets the old gentle one — the knickpoint — migrates upstream, opening the profile like a zip pulled from the mouth toward the source.

Take this section's questions now: base level and the graded profile, the two families of causes with their technical names, and the one-line mechanism with its migrating knickpoint.

## Subtopic: The Four Signatures of Rejuvenation

A rejuvenated river writes its confession into the landscape four times over, and questions ask you to name, explain and identify each signature.

Signature one: the knickpoint — the sharp break of slope in the long profile, usually announced by a waterfall or a run of rapids. Above the knickpoint the river still glides on its old gentle profile; below it, the channel has already been cut down to the new level. The fall gnaws at its own lip and retreats upstream year on year. Howick Falls, where the uMngeni steps off the old plateau surface and plunges toward its deepening gorge, is a fine South African face for the concept.

Signature two: terraces. Before the energy returned, the river had manufactured a floodplain. Renewed downcutting saws a trench through that floodplain, stranding the old valley floor on either side as flat benches perched above the new channel — paired river terraces, matching left and right at the same height like two shelves on facing walls. A second episode of rejuvenation cuts a second, lower pair, building a staircase of abandoned floodplains with the oldest step at the top. Terraces are magnets for farms, roads and settlement — flat, fertile and above flood level — and that human link is where application marks hide.

Signature three: the valley-in-a-valley. In cross-section the story appears as one nested shape: a fresh, steep-sided V incised into the floor of a broad, old valley. This nested form is the cross-sectional face of rejuvenation, and the terraces are nothing more than the surviving edges of the old valley floor flanking the inner trench.

Signature four: incised meanders. If the river was looping across its floodplain when the energy arrived, it does not straighten — it keeps its winding plan and cuts vertically, sinking its own loops into solid rock as gorges. The uMzimkulwana, winding through the deep sandstone trench of Oribi Gorge, shows an old river's shape carved with a young river's power. Separate the two varieties in a sentence each: entrenched meanders are symmetric in cross-section, cut straight down by rapid vertical erosion; ingrown meanders are asymmetric — one steep slope, one gentle — because slower incision allowed the loops to keep drifting sideways while they sank.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the four signatures named, the staircase logic of the terraces, the nested cross-section, and the entrenched-versus-ingrown distinction.

## Subtopic: River Capture — The Theft of a River

River capture — stream piracy in its more honest name — is the natural theft of one river's headwaters by another. You need the mechanism, the five labels, and the aftermath.

Set two rivers flowing roughly parallel with a watershed of high ground between them. One river holds the advantages — a steeper gradient, softer rock beneath it, or heavier rainfall on its side of the divide — and therefore superior erosive energy. Its weapon is headward erosion: every river extends its source backwards into the watershed, but the stronger river does so faster, boring through the divide toward its neighbour's channel season after season.

The capture itself is a single decisive moment: the strong river's headwaters cut through the last of the watershed and intersect the weak river's channel. Water obeys gravity alone — offered a lower, steeper exit, the weak river's upper course swings around and empties into the strong river. The headwaters have changed owners.

Now label the diagram, because the five-piece vocabulary is a standing question. The captor stream: the aggressor, now carrying its own discharge plus everything it stole. The captured stream: the weak river's former upper course, rerouted into the captor. The elbow of capture: the abrupt, near-right-angle bend where the captured water veers into the captor's valley — natural rivers do not corner like that, so an elbow on a map amounts to a confession. The misfit stream: the beheaded lower course of the victim, still trickling along its old broad valley but absurdly undersized for it, because that valley was cut by the far larger river it once was. And the wind gap: the abandoned length of valley between elbow and misfit — a dry notch through the high ground that once carried the river and now carries only wind.

The after-effects stitch this lesson's two dramas together: the captor, its discharge suddenly enlarged, gains energy and frequently rejuvenates — knickpoints and incision go to work up its valley. The victim, starved of flow, loses transporting power; its valley silts, swamps and chokes.

The questions on this section are with you now: headward erosion as the weapon, the five labels on the capture diagram, and the fates of captor and victim afterwards.

## Subtopic: Significance for People and Reading the Map

These two processes matter beyond the diagrams because they redistribute the two commodities settlement is built on: water and flat land.

Capture's human ledger. On the captor's side of the account: more water — stronger assurance for irrigation, town supply and potential hydropower; but also a newly energetic, incising river that can steepen banks and undermine bridge foundations. On the victim's side: a beheaded river delivers less water downstream — water tables sag, irrigation schemes shrink, and a farming valley or river town can watch the resource it was founded on dwindle away. The wind gap holds a quiet payoff of its own: it is a ready-made pass through high ground, and road and rail engineers have always preferred threading a wind gap to climbing a ridge.

Rejuvenation's human ledger. Terraces supply flat, fertile, flood-free platforms, which is why old settlements and roads line up along terrace edges in the world's great valleys. Knickpoint waterfalls offer hydropower sites and tourist economies — Howick's gorge viewpoint feeds a whole town's visitor trade. Deep incised gorges concentrate scenery and dam sites, since a narrow gorge makes a short, cheap dam wall — but the same gorge locks the water away below unreachable cliffs, makes every bridge expensive, and turns a flash flood into a lethal wall of water in the confined channel.

Now the identification drill, because these ideas arrive attached to maps and profiles. On the long profile: a graded river sweeps smooth and concave; a sharp step interrupting the sweep is a knickpoint — rejuvenation announced. On the cross-section: a V nested in a broader valley with benches stepping up the sides — valley-in-a-valley plus terraces. On the contour map: terraces read as stacked flat strips beside the river, each strip edged by a ribbon of closely spaced contours; incised meanders read as a winding course with contours crowded hard against both banks of every loop. For capture, audit the drainage plan itself: an unnatural elbow bend in an otherwise flowing course; a dry col or notch through a ridge with no stream in it — the wind gap; and beyond it a skinny stream adrift in an oversized valley — the misfit. Two or more of those clues together let you call the capture with confidence.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: one human consequence each for captor, victim, terrace and gorge, and the map clues for knickpoint, terrace, incised meander and capture.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same two dramas again — now starring a retired marathon runner with a winning ticket, and a neighbour digging toward your water pipe.

## Subtopic: The Old River Wins the Lottery

Give a river a life story. In its youth, up in the mountains, it is all energy — cutting downward, carving sharp V-valleys, throwing itself off waterfalls. In old age, near the sea, it has slowed right down: broad, unhurried, looping across a flat floodplain, nudging mud along instead of rolling boulders. Across its whole life it is smoothing its path from source to sea into one lazy curve, steep at the start and flat at the finish. When the curve is finished and everything balances, we say the river is graded — settled, comfortable, nothing dramatic left on its to-do list.

Every river also has a finish line it can never dig below: the level of the water it empties into, almost always the sea. That is base level — the floor of the river's world.

Rejuvenation happens when the floor drops away — or the ground is hoisted. Either way, the river wakes up much higher above its finish line than it was the day before. Higher above the finish line means steeper; steeper means faster; faster means powerful. The retired river is handed a young river's legs — a seventy-year-old marathon champion winning the lottery, hiring a coach, and turning up at the track again. The word itself says it: re-juvenation, made young again.

What can move the floor? Two things, and both are needed for full credit. The sea can fall — in an ice age, so much water is stacked into ice sheets that sea level sinks all over the world. Or the land can rise — the ground beneath southern Africa has been heaved upward more than once, tipping every river on the plateau into a steeper run. Sea down or land up: the gap widens either way, and the digging resumes.

And where does the new digging begin? At the bottom end, where the change happened — then it eats its way UP the river, the meeting point between new steep and old gentle crawling upstream year by year, like a tear spreading up a seam. Hold onto that crawling point: in the next section it gets a name and a waterfall to live in.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the lazy curve and its finish line, the two ways the gap can widen, and the direction the digging spreads. The old champion remembers every one.

## Subtopic: Four Clues the River Got Young

A river cannot win the lottery quietly. It leaves four clues lying around the landscape, and your job is to spot all four.

Clue one: the waterfall on the move. The crawling point where the new steep profile meets the old gentle one is the knickpoint, and the river marks it with a waterfall or rapids. Upstream of the fall the river still idles along its old self; downstream it has already cut to the new level. And the fall does not stay put — it chews its own lip and backs upstream year after year. Howick Falls on the uMngeni is the picture to hold: a river stepping off its old surface into the gorge it is still deepening.

Clue two: the old floors left as balconies. Before the win, the river had built itself a wide flat floodplain — its old ground floor. When the digging restarted, the river cut a trench straight down through that floor, leaving strips of it stranded on both sides as flat balconies above the new channel: terraces, matched left and right at the same height like the tiers of a stadium. A second win cuts a second, lower pair — a staircase of abandoned floors, oldest at the top. People adore terraces — flat, fertile, safely above the floods — which is why the farms and the old roads in any great valley sit exactly there.

Clue three: the valley inside a valley. Slice the valley crossways and the whole biography shows in one shape: a sharp young V-trench sunk into the floor of a broad old valley. The name is simply what the eye sees — valley-in-a-valley — and the terraces are just the old floor's leftover edges framing the new trench.

Clue four: the snake that sank. If the river was already looping in lazy curves when the energy arrived, it refused to straighten. It kept every loop and cut straight down, sinking its own curves into rock — incised meanders, a snake-shaped gorge with all its coils intact. The uMzimkulwana winding through the deep walls of Oribi Gorge wears the shape at full scale: an old river's plan, executed with a young river's violence.

Take the questions for this section now: the four clues, which clue crawls upstream, why the balconies come in matching pairs, and the snake that sank. Four clues, one verdict: the river got its youth back.

## Subtopic: The Neighbour Who Stole the Pipe

The second drama is river capture, and its other name confesses everything — stream piracy. One river steals another's water. This is not a metaphor; it is the literal event.

Picture two rivers flowing side by side with a ridge between them, like two properties separated by a boundary wall. One neighbour is stronger: steeper ground, softer rock to dig through, more rain landing on its roof. Now, every river slowly extends its source backwards, eating uphill into the ridge — headward erosion, the river's arm reaching back into the divide. The strong river's arm reaches faster. Season by season it tunnels through the boundary wall, aiming — although it does not know it — straight at the neighbour's water pipe.

Then the wall gives way. The strong river's headwaters break into the weak river's channel, and water, loyal only to gravity, makes its choice instantly: offered a lower and steeper exit, the weak river's entire upper course swings across and pours into the strong river. From that day the strong river runs double; below the break, the weak river is left holding a trickle.

Walk the crime scene and tag the five exhibits, because this labelled diagram is a standing favourite. The thief, now swollen with stolen water: the captor stream. The stolen upper course, flowing its new route: the captured stream. The sharp, unnatural corner where the stolen water turns into the thief's valley: the elbow of capture — rivers never voluntarily turn at right angles, so that corner is a signed confession on the map. The victim's surviving lower course: the misfit stream — a thin thread lost in an enormous valley, like a single violinist on a stage built for a full orchestra, because the valley was carved by the river it used to be. And the abandoned stretch between elbow and misfit: the wind gap — a dry notch through the high ground where water once flowed, now carrying nothing but wind, and very often a road, because engineers never refuse a free pass through a ridge.

The ending ties tonight's two dramas into one: the thief, suddenly rich in water, gains energy — and often rejuvenates, cutting downward with its new strength, complete with a knickpoint working upstream. The victim's starved valley silts up and turns swampy. And people inherit all of it: farms on the captor's side gain water security, while any town that grew along the victim's lower course watches its river and its boreholes fade.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the weapon of headward erosion, the five exhibits at the crime scene, the elbow as signed confession, and what the theft does to both rivers and the people beside them. Take them, and both dramas — the lottery and the robbery — are permanently yours.
