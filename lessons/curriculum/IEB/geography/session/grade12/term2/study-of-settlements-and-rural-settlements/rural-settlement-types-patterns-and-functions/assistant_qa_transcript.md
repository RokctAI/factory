**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First: site and situation — I know the definitions, but in map questions I still describe the wrong one. When the question says 'describe the site of settlement X', what exactly am I allowed to talk about?

**Tutor:** You are allowed to talk about the ground and nothing but the ground — pretend the rest of the map has been torn away, leaving only the little square the settlement stands on. On that square you may discuss relief: is it flat, gently sloping, on a spur? Drainage: well drained, marshy, beside a spring or borehole? Soil and rock, if evidence shows. Water on the spot: the river the settlement touches, the dam beside it. Aspect: north-facing warmth, shelter from the ridge. The moment your sentence needs anything beyond that square — the road to the next town, the distance to the city, the pass it guards — you have wandered into situation, and in a site question those sentences earn nothing. Here is a practical drill: before writing, draw a mental fence around the settlement symbol. Everything inside the fence is site vocabulary; everything outside is situation vocabulary. And when the question flips and asks for situation, reverse the fence: now the settlement itself is a mere dot, and you describe its position relative to routes, rivers, resources and other places — the junction it commands, the valley corridor it sits in, the towns it lies between. One more tip: wet-point and dry-point are SITE answers, because they describe why that exact ground was chosen — for the water rising there, or to escape the water flooding below. Fence in, fence out — that is the whole discipline.

***

**Mandy:** Next: dispersed versus nucleated — the descriptions are easy, but the question always adds 'account for the pattern' and that's where I go vague. What's the accounting the marks want?

**Tutor:** The accounting is land ownership first, with supporting reasons behind it — and the trick is to answer as if the land itself made the decision. Take dispersed. Why does one farmstead sit alone with kilometres of wheat around it? Because the farm is one large, privately owned unit, and a farmer must live on the land being worked — to run the kraals, watch the lands, manage the workers, respond when the pump fails at midnight. Large private property scatters people by its very geometry: one owner, one homestead, much land. Now nucleate. Why do homes cluster in a tight village with the fields around the outside? Because the land is held communally — no single family owns the fields, so no family needs to live out on them; instead people live together, for the defensive strength of numbers historically, for the shared water point, for the school, the church and the social life, and they walk out to the lands each day. So the model answer runs: pattern named, ownership stated, two or three supporting reasons attached. Dispersed — private farms, must live on the worked land. Nucleated — communal tenure, plus defence, shared water, community. If you remember only one line, remember the beans: flung across the floor when the land is private, gathered in one bowl when the land is shared. That single ownership link is usually the difference between the naming mark and the full accounting.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the group: the four shapes feel like pure memorisation. Round, linear, T, crossroad — is there a way to derive them instead of cramming them?

**Tutor:** Derive them from one question: what is the settlement organised AROUND? Every village shape is an answer to that question, which means you never memorise shapes — you read causes. If the organising centre is a protected middle — the cattle kraal, the community's gathering space — the homes arrange themselves in a ring around it, and you get the round village: protection and social structure drew the circle. If the organising feature is a line that flows — a road, a river, a canal, the foot of a ridge — every household wants frontage on it, so the homes queue along it one or two deep, and you get the linear village: beads on a string, drawn by access. If the organising feature is a junction, the logic sharpens: traffic slows, stops and turns at junctions, and paused travellers are customers, so trade plants itself exactly there and homes grow along the road arms. A side road ending against a main road gives two arms and writes a T; two roads crossing give four arms and write an X — the crossroad village. Same magnet, different road geometry. So in a question, work backwards: see a circle, ask what precious thing sat in the middle; see a ribbon, ask what flowed past the front doors; see a T or an X, ask what trade the junction concentrated. Name the shape, then speak its cause in the same breath — the name is one mark, the cause is the rest, and the cause is the part you can always derive.

**Mandy:** Last question: when a question hands me a whole map extract of a rural area and just says 'discuss the settlement', I freeze — where do I even start?

**Tutor:** Start nowhere creative — start with the five-move commentary, in order, every single time, because 'discuss' is not an invitation to wander; it is five specific questions wearing one word. Move one, classify: is this settlement rural? Say yes because the surrounding land use is primary — cultivated lands, orchards, plantations, grazing — and low density. Move two, pattern: are the dwellings dispersed or nucleated? Name it, then attach the ownership reason — scattered homesteads point to large private farms, a tight cluster points to communal land, shared water, community. Move three, shape, only if it clusters: circle around an open core, ribbon along the road or river, T or X at a junction — name it with its cause. Move four, function: read the land like a menu — mealie lands and cane mean crop farming, kraals and rangeland mean stock farming, ruler-straight dark plantation blocks mean forestry, a rest camp inside a reserve means conservation; and add the density rule, intensive farming packing settlement closer while extensive farming spreads it. Move five, site and situation: the ground under it — the dry shelf above the floodplain, the borehole in dry country — and the position around it — the junction, the route between towns. Five moves, roughly two sentences each, and you have a ten-mark discussion with nothing forgotten. Freezing comes from facing a blank; the commentary means you never face one — the structure is decided before the map is even printed.
