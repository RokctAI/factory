# Part 1 — Expert

Every settlement question opens with the same three-part toolkit: what a settlement is, where it sits, and why it took the shape it did. This session builds the toolkit piece by piece — site and situation as the two separate location questions; the classification ladder that climbs from a single farmstead to a metropolis; the rural patterns and the four village shapes that questions describe in words and expect you to name; and the land-use functions that explain why a rural settlement exists at all. The examples stay South African from start to finish: a Namaqualand farmstead crowded around its borehole, homesteads on the Eastern Cape hillsides, nucleated villages on communal land, and the timber villages of the plantation country.

## Subtopic: Settlement, Site and Situation

Open with the required definition: a settlement is any place where people live, together with the buildings and structures they use — everything from one isolated farmstead to the whole of Johannesburg qualifies. Geographers divide all settlements into two families. Rural settlements are small, low-density places whose people work mainly in primary activities — farming, forestry, fishing, mining. Urban settlements are larger, high-density places whose people work mainly in secondary and tertiary activities — manufacturing, retail, offices, services. Mark the dividing line carefully: it is dominant economic activity, not size. A mining village of two thousand residents doing primary work still belongs functionally with the rural family, while a small town living entirely off its shops and services has already crossed to the urban side.

Now the two location questions, and never let them touch. Site is the actual ground the settlement stands on — the physical character of the exact spot. Site vocabulary: relief, drainage, soil, water supply, aspect. A village on a gentle, well-drained, north-facing slope beside a permanent spring has an excellent site, and every term in that sentence is a site term. Situation is the settlement's position relative to its surroundings — other settlements, rivers, passes, routes, resources. A town standing where two national roads meet, midway between two cities, holds a commanding situation. Fix the contrast as under versus around: site is what lies beneath the settlement's foundations; situation is what lies around it and beyond the horizon.

Two special site terms carry their own marks. A wet-point site is selected because water exists precisely there in a dry region — the Namaqualand or Kalahari-margin farmstead planted on its borehole or spring is the standard picture. A dry-point site is the mirror case: in a wet, flood-prone district, the settlement retreats to higher, drier ground — Eastern Cape homesteads perched on the slope above a flood-prone valley floor, sleeping dry while their fields exploit the moist bottomland.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the definition of a settlement, the rural–urban dividing line, and the clean separation of site from situation with the wet-point and dry-point special cases.

## Subtopic: Classifying Settlements: Size, Pattern and Function

Settlements are classified three ways, and a single question can demand all three.

First, by size and complexity — the ladder. On the bottom rung, the isolated farmstead: one family, one working unit. Next the hamlet: a cluster of a few homesteads, perhaps a spaza shop, no true services. Then the village, where services first appear — a school, a clinic, a general dealer, a church. Above that the town, the city, and finally the metropolis — a giant such as Johannesburg or eThekwini, formed when growing cities fuse. Three quantities climb the ladder together: population, range of services, and complexity of functions. The rural family occupies the three bottom rungs — farmstead, hamlet, village; everything higher is urban.

Second, by pattern — how buildings arrange themselves across the land. The rural world runs on two master patterns. Dispersed settlement scatters individual homesteads far apart, each on its own land: fly over the wheat country of the Swartland or the maize districts of the highveld and you see a farmstead, then kilometres of cultivated land, then the next farmstead. Nucleated settlement gathers dwellings tightly around a central point — a water source, a church, a kraal — with the farmland surrounding the cluster; many villages on communal land take this form, as the traditional villages of southern Africa long have. Each pattern answers a question the land posed: dispersal follows large, privately owned farms, because the farmer must live on the land being worked; nucleation follows communal tenure, defence, shared water and the pull of community life.

Third, by function — the work the settlement performs. Rural settlements are usually unifunctional: one dominant activity, nearly always tied to the land. Urban settlements are multifunctional: dozens of activities behind one name. Bank the term unifunctional; a whole question type hangs on it.

This section's questions are ready for you now: the ladder from farmstead to metropolis, dispersed against nucleated with the reason behind each, and the unifunctional–multifunctional contrast.

## Subtopic: Rural Shapes: Round, Linear, T-Shaped and Crossroad

Four rural village shapes carry names you must produce on sight, because questions supply only a description or a bare sketch. Learn each as a word-picture.

The round village sets its dwellings in a circle or oval around a central open space. In the traditional southern African form, the homes ring a central cattle kraal: the herd sleeps protected in the middle, the households around it, the community's gathering space at the core. Defence and social organisation are the architects of this shape. Recite it: houses on the rim, working space in the heart.

The linear village is a settlement drawn out into a line, one or two buildings deep, along something that flows — a road, a river, a canal, the base of a ridge. Every household wants frontage on the routeway, so the village lengthens without thickening: beads threaded on a single string. A rural main road with its unbroken ribbon of roadside houses, or homesteads following a river terrace, shows the form. Its strength is access; its weaknesses are that services sit far from the two distant ends, and that the routeway brings noise and danger through the middle of home life.

The T-shaped village appears where a side road meets a main road and stops — a T-junction. Buildings line both arms, and seen from above the settlement itself writes the letter T. The crossroad village takes the next step: two roads intersect and continue, buildings colonise all four arms, and the settlement writes a cross with the junction at its heart. One cause drives both: a junction concentrates passing trade. Where traffic slows, stops and turns, the shop, the fuel pump and the taxi stop take root — and homes grow along the arms behind them.

Answer technique: name the shape, then immediately attach its cause — junction trade for the T and the crossroad, frontage on a routeway for the linear, the protected kraal and community life for the round. The name is one mark; the cause is the rest.

Take the questions for this section now: the four shapes as word-pictures, and the cause that built each one.

## Subtopic: Rural Functions and Land Use: Why the Settlement Is There

A rural settlement exists to work the land around it, so its function can be read directly off its land use. The syllabus names three big functions, each with a South African face.

Farming is the master function, split into two branches. Crop farming: the wheat lands of the Swartland, the citrus of the Sundays River valley, the sugar cane belt along the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the maize of the highveld. Stock farming: cattle in the bushveld and the eastern grasslands, sheep spread across the vast dry Karoo. The style of farming shapes the settlement that serves it: intensive farming on small, high-value plots — vegetables under irrigation, orchards — supports closer, denser rural settlement, while extensive farming spreads homesteads enormously far apart, because a Karoo sheep family needs thousands of hectares to make a living.

Forestry builds its own settlement type: plantation villages inside the planted timber country of the escarpment and the KwaZulu-Natal midlands, housing the teams who plant, thin and fell, with a sawmill settlement nearby to process the logs.

Conservation supplies the third function: settlements that serve protected land — staff villages and rest camps inside a park such as Addo Elephant National Park, and the gate settlements at reserve entrances — places whose land's job is protection and whose settlement's job is managing the wild land and hosting its visitors.

Now assemble the whole toolkit, because this is how it gets used. Handed a description or a map extract of a rural settlement, run the five-move commentary: classify it — rural, because the dominant activity is primary; state its pattern — dispersed or nucleated, with the land-tenure or water reason attached; name its shape if it clusters — round, linear, T-shaped or crossroad, with the cause; give its function — crop farming, stock farming, forestry or conservation, read from the land-use evidence; and comment on site and situation — the wet-point borehole, the dry shelf above the floodplain, the position on the route between towns. Five moves, each carrying marks, all drawn from this one session.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the three land-use functions with their South African regions, the link between intensive or extensive farming and settlement density, and the five-move commentary that answers any rural settlement question.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same toolkit again — this time built from things you already know: a plot and its neighbourhood, beans scattered or beans in a bowl, and villages read through a bus window.

## Subtopic: The Stand and the Street: Site versus Situation

Bring to mind a house you know well — home, or a grandmother's place. There are two completely different conversations about where that house is, and once you hear them as two conversations, site and situation never blur again.

Conversation one is about the plot itself — the actual ground under the house. Is the stand level or on a slope? Does storm water run off or pond against the wall? Does the foundation sit on solid ground or does the plaster crack? Is there water on the property — a tap, a tank, a borehole? Do the winter sun's rays reach the kitchen? Every one of those questions interrogates the exact patch of earth beneath the building. That whole conversation is SITE.

Conversation two never once mentions the ground: how long is the walk to school? How far to the clinic, the taxi rank, the shops? What lies nearby, and which routes pass the gate? That conversation — the house measured against everything around it — is SITUATION. Site is under the floor; situation is out of the window and down the street.

Now scale both conversations up to a whole settlement. In bone-dry Namaqualand, a farmstead presses close around the one place where water rises — a spring, a windpump over a borehole. The water made that exact spot buildable, so geographers call it a wet-point site: chosen FOR the water. Reverse the climate: in a wet region where the river spills over every summer, the homes climb to the higher, drier shelf above the floodplain — fields down on the rich wet flats, beds up where the flood cannot follow. That is a dry-point site: chosen to ESCAPE the water. It is the logic of a braai in the rain — close enough to the fire to cook, far enough back to stay dry.

Take this section's questions now: the two conversations, and which conversation the wet-point and dry-point choices belong to. Answer them the way you would describe that house you know.

## Subtopic: Scattered Mealies or One Pot: Dispersed and Nucleated

All the classifying in Part 1 compresses into two everyday questions: how big is the place, and do the homes spread out or huddle?

The size question is a ladder you have climbed in real life. One farmhouse with its sheds, alone — a farmstead. A few homesteads within calling distance, maybe a spaza — a hamlet. Add a school, a clinic, a church and a general dealer, and you are in a village. Keep adding people and services and you pass through town and city all the way to a metropolis like Johannesburg. The ladder's one rule: each step up adds people AND services together. The rural world lives on the bottom three rungs.

The huddling question has exactly two answers. Take a handful of dried beans. Fling them across the kitchen floor and they land scattered, each alone — DISPERSED settlement: one homestead here, the next a few kilometres on, which is precisely the look of commercial farming country, because each farmer owns a large private farm and has to live on it. Now pour the same beans into one bowl — NUCLEATED settlement: homes packed together in a cluster with the fields arranged around the outside. Villages on communal land are the standard case: the land belongs to the community, so people live together and walk out to work the fields. Scattered beans, private land; one bowl, shared land. That link — pattern to land ownership — is exactly the reason-mark the question is waiting for.

Then the function question: a rural place usually does ONE kind of work, nearly always working the land — unifunctional. A city does everything simultaneously — multifunctional. If most people around you earn from the land itself, you are standing in a rural settlement, whatever its population.

Your questions for this section are up: the ladder, the beans, and the one-function word.

## Subtopic: Reading a Village Like a Taxi Route

The four shapes stop being a list to memorise the moment each one attaches to a picture you already carry.

Round: the traditional homestead layout — dwellings standing in a ring with the cattle kraal at the centre. The circle wraps around what is precious: the herd sleeps guarded in the middle, the people around it, the meeting space shared at the core. Homes on the rim, wealth in the heart. If a question describes houses arranged in a circle around a central open space, answer round village, and give the cause: protection and community life.

Linear: recall any stretch of rural road where every single building faces the tar — house, shop, house, all in one long row with open land behind them. The village has taken the shape of the thing it clings to: beads on a string, because everyone wants a front door on the route. A river does the same work as a road, stringing homesteads along its dry bank. Long and thin means linear, and the cause is frontage — access to whatever flows past.

T-shaped and crossroad: readable straight through a bus window. Where a side road dead-ends against a main road, buildings gather along both arms and the village writes a letter T on the landscape. Where two roads cross and continue, buildings take all four arms and the village writes an X. The cause is the same for both: a junction is where traffic slows, stops and turns — and wherever travellers pause, someone sells to them. The shop, the fuel pumps, the taxi stop and the braai stand plant themselves at the junction, and the houses follow along the arms. The junction is a magnet; the shape is just the iron filings around it.

Finally, why does the village exist at all? Ask the land around it. Mealie lands, cane or orchards — a crop-farming settlement. Kraals, sheep and open rangeland — stock farming; and note the density rule: Karoo sheep country needs so many hectares per family that homesteads sit far apart, while small irrigated plots let people live close. Dark, ruler-straight rows of planted pine and gum — a forestry village serving the plantations and the sawmill. A rest camp and staff village inside a park like Addo — conservation, where the settlement's work is guarding land and hosting visitors.

Here come the final questions of the lesson: the four shapes matched to their pictures, the junction magnet, and the land-use clues that name a settlement's function. Read each one like a bus route you have travelled before, and rural settlement questions become marks you collect on sight.
