**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every single year: in a test, how do I stop myself confusing a mid-latitude cyclone with a tropical cyclone? They are both huge spinning lows.

**Tutor:** Build a two-column table in your head and check three rows. Row one, the energy source: a tropical cyclone is powered from below, by latent heat released from very warm ocean water, which is why it needs a sea surface warmer than about twenty-six degrees; a mid-latitude cyclone is powered sideways, by the temperature contrast across the polar front — it will happily deepen over cold water because contrast, not warmth, is its fuel. Row two, the structure: the tropical system is a nearly circular spiral with a clear, calm eye and no fronts at all; the mid-latitude system is asymmetrical, has no eye, and drags a cold front and a warm front behind it like two arms. Row three, the address and the season: tropical cyclones live between roughly five and thirty degrees of latitude and hit our region in late summer, coming down the Mozambique Channel; mid-latitude cyclones live between thirty and sixty degrees and reach the south coast in winter, riding the westerlies from the Atlantic. So if the chart shows fronts, no eye, and a position south of the country in July, the identification is finished before you have read the question stem. One system is a heat engine over a warm sea; the other is a battle along a temperature boundary — hold that sentence and the two will never blur again.

**Mandy:** Next one: on the station-model questions, how do I actually prove a town is sitting in the warm sector, instead of just guessing from where it looks like it is on the map?

**Tutor:** Treat the warm sector as a suspect with three pieces of evidence that must all agree. Evidence one is temperature: the station will read noticeably warmer than stations behind the cold front — often several degrees — and warm for the season. Evidence two is wind direction: inside the warm sector the circulation feeds air from the north-west, so the station's wind barb should point from the north-west quadrant; a station behind the front will already have swung to south-west. Evidence three is pressure behaviour: the warm sector lies ahead of the advancing low, so pressure is falling, and many charts note the tendency — a minus sign or a falling trace. If you can add a fourth clue, look at the cloud and weather symbols: warm-sector stations typically show overcast, drizzly, humid conditions rather than the hard showers of the front itself. Then write the answer as a chain, naming the station: warmer temperature, north-westerly wind, falling pressure, therefore located between the warm front and the cold front — the warm sector. Three agreeing witnesses and the conclusion stated: that is full marks, and it works on any chart they can print.

**Mandy:** Third question, from two people at once: everyone says winter is cold-front season for the Cape, but WHY does the season matter? What is physically different about the planet in July?

**Tutor:** What changes is the position of the overhead sun, and everything else follows like dominoes. In our summer the sun stands over the Tropic of Capricorn, and the whole belt system of the planet — the highs, the westerlies, the storm track — sits at its southernmost position, so the corridor the fronts travel lies south of the continent, out over the ocean, and the storms slide past without touching land. By July the overhead sun has moved to the Tropic of Cancer, and the entire machine follows it north by several degrees of latitude. The South Atlantic High and the South Indian High drift north, the westerly storm track drifts north with them, and suddenly the corridor between the highs runs across the southern tip of Africa. Same storms, same factory, same conveyor — the conveyor itself has simply been shifted so that its delivery route crosses the Cape. That is why the winter-rainfall region is winter-rainfall: not because winter creates the storms, but because winter parks the storm highway over the land. Say it that way in an exam — the pressure belts migrate with the overhead sun — and you have explained the seasonality of an entire climate region in one sentence.

**Mandy:** Last one, and it is a strange one: my aunt in Knysna says the hottest, driest, most dangerous fire days of her winter come right BEFORE a cold front. How can a rain storm cause a heatwave?

**Tutor:** Your aunt has noticed one of the most examinable connections in this whole topic: the berg wind. A day or so before the front arrives, the approaching low off the west coast and the high pressure still sitting over the interior plateau combine to drag air from the plateau down toward the coast. That air starts three thousand metres up, already dry, and as it descends it is compressed and warms at roughly one degree for every hundred metres of descent. By the time it spills over the coastal mountains onto towns like Knysna or Plettenberg Bay it can be over thirty degrees in mid-winter, with humidity through the floor. Hot, bone-dry, gusty, offshore wind — which is precisely the recipe for runaway fires, and it is why the devastating Knysna fires of June 2017 burned on a berg-wind day with a cold front bearing down on the coast. Then the front arrives, the wind swings south-west, the temperature crashes, and the rain finally falls on the fire. So the full exam answer links the two systems: the berg wind is not separate from the cold front, it is the front's herald — pre-frontal descending air off the plateau. A scorching winter day on the south coast is often the atmosphere's way of announcing that the storm is one day away.
