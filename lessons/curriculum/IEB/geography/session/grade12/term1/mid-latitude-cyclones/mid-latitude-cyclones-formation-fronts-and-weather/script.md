# Part 1 — Expert

The mid-latitude cyclone is the workhorse of South Africa's winter weather and one of the most rewarding systems to master for your final exams, because every part of it can be reasoned out from first principles. You will meet it under several names — frontal depression, extra-tropical cyclone, temperate cyclone — and they all describe the same thing: a vast spinning low that grows on the boundary between cold and warm air south of the country. In this session we establish where and why these systems develop, follow the three-stage life cycle from the first ripple to the final spiral, track the weather a cold front drags across a coastal town hour by hour, and close with the chart-reading and satellite skills that let you diagnose any system you are shown. Keep one governing idea in front of you the whole time: two air masses of different density meet along a line, refuse to mix, and the fight between them winds itself up into a storm.

## Subtopic: Where and Why Mid-Latitude Cyclones Form

The breeding ground is the belt between thirty and sixty degrees of latitude, over the ocean, in both hemispheres. The systems that matter to us are born over the cold waters far south-west of the country and are carried from west to east by the westerly wind belt. Fix that direction of travel first, because it organises everything else: a frontal system approaching George or Gqeberha always arrives from the Atlantic side and departs over the Indian Ocean side, and whatever the chart shows to the west of you today is your weather tomorrow.

Why does this belt make storms? Because it is where two profoundly different air masses face each other. Cold, dense air streams up from the polar south. Warm, moist air presses down from the subtropics. Where they meet lies the polar front — not a fence but a long, gently sloping surface along which the cold air slides beneath the warm. The two bodies of air keep their identities the way cold syrup keeps its layer beneath water: the denser one hugs the ground, and whenever the two are pushed together, it is always the warm, lighter air that is levered upward.

Development needs three ingredients working together. The first is the front itself — a sharp temperature contrast across a boundary. The second is rotation, supplied by the Coriolis force. In the Southern Hemisphere moving air is deflected to its left, so air rushing toward a centre of low pressure cannot travel straight in; it curves, and the whole circulation ends up turning clockwise around the low. State that carefully in any answer, because many textbook diagrams are drawn for the Northern Hemisphere, where the spin is the reverse. The third ingredient works from above: divergence in the upper air. The jet stream, a ribbon of extremely fast wind near the top of the troposphere, drags air away from the summit of the developing system. Removing air from the top lowers the pressure at the surface, and lower surface pressure pulls still more air spiralling inward. The system feeds itself.

For a full description in an exam answer, add the general characteristics: a low-pressure cell one to two thousand kilometres across with fronts attached, lasting several days, travelling eastward at roughly forty to fifty kilometres per hour, and arriving in families — one system following another along the same track, which is why a wet week in the southern Cape often brings three fronts in succession.

The questions for this section are with you now: the latitude belt and the direction of travel, the two air masses and the polar front between them, and the three ingredients of development with the Southern Hemisphere rotation stated the right way round.

## Subtopic: Stages of Development — Wave to Occlusion

The life cycle unfolds in three stages, and you should be able to sketch each one. Begin with the polar front lying roughly east to west across the ocean south of the country: cold air on its poleward side flowing one way, warm air on its equatorward side flowing the other, the two streams shearing past each other like traffic in opposite lanes.

Stage one is the initial stage, also called the wave stage. The shearing motion catches on the front and bends it, the way a steady wind puts a ripple into a long banner. Warm air bulges toward the cold side, cold air curls in behind the bulge, and at the crest of this small wave the surface pressure starts to fall. On a chart it is almost nothing — a kink in a long line — but the machinery has started.

Stage two is the mature stage, the one you must know in the greatest detail. The wave amplifies into an open system: a deepening low with two distinct fronts trailing from it like two arms. The leading arm is the warm front, where advancing warm air rides gently up over the retreating cold air ahead of it. The trailing arm is the cold front, where the following cold air drives in underneath and levers the warm air steeply upward. Between the two arms lies the warm sector — a wedge of warm, humid air caught between colder air ahead and colder air behind. The whole structure rotates clockwise and slides east.

Now build the cross-section, because a section through the mature system is a classic diagram question. Cut a line through the warm sector from front to back. At the leading edge the warm-front surface climbs at only about one in a hundred, so the warm air ascends slowly and spreads into layered cloud — thin cirrus far ahead, thickening through altostratus into deep nimbostratus — delivering hours of steady, soaking rain before the surface front arrives. Then comes the warm sector itself: mild, muggy, mostly overcast. At the rear, the cold-front surface is roughly twice as steep, near one in fifty, and it is advancing, so warm air is flung upward fast. Rapid uplift builds cumulonimbus towers: short-lived, violent showers, possible thunder, hail on occasion.

Stage three is the occlusion stage. The cold front travels faster than the warm front, and in time it catches up. The warm sector is pinched between them and lifted bodily off the surface — occluded literally means closed off. The chart now shows a single merged front spiralling into the low. With no warm surface air left to feed on, the temperature contrast collapses, and the system spins itself out and dissipates over a few days.

Attend to this section's questions before we continue: the three stages in their correct order, the full anatomy of the mature stage, and what precisely gets closed off at occlusion.

## Subtopic: Front Weather — Before, During and After

A favourite style of question places you in a specific town and asks you to narrate the weather as a cold front crosses it. Learn the answer as three scenes in time — ahead of the front, during passage, behind it — and anchor every scene with four instruments: thermometer, barometer, wind vane, and the sky itself.

Ahead of the front the town lies in the warm sector. The thermometer reads mild to warm for the season. The wind vane points into the north-west, drawing warm continental air toward the coast. The barometer is the tell-tale: pressure falls steadily, hour after hour, as the centre of the low approaches. Humidity climbs, the air feels close, and a wall of cloud builds slowly on the western horizon.

During passage the change is compressed into an hour or two. The cold wedge arrives and hurls the warm-sector air upward; cumulonimbus erupts overhead; rain arrives in hard, cold bursts, with thunder possible and snow on the high peaks inland. And the wind vane performs the single most examinable move in this topic: the wind backs sharply around from north-west, through west, to south-west. That wind swing is the fingerprint of a frontal passage. Any answer describing a cold front that leaves it out is missing the front's signature.

Behind the front the polar air owns the town. Temperature drops abruptly — commonly six to ten degrees Celsius lower than the morning. The barometer climbs quickly as high pressure ridges in behind the system. The wind holds cold and gusty from the south-west, and the sky breaks into fast-moving cumulus with bright, chilly sunshine between passing showers — the rinsed, sharp-edged day that follows a winter storm.

The warm front tells the same story in a gentler voice: a full day of warning as cirrus arrives and the cloud sheet lowers and thickens, then long soft rain, then milder air. South Africa, however, rarely receives the warm front itself. Our latitude clips only the northern edge of these systems, so the warm front usually slides past to the south, over open ocean, and what crosses the country is the cold front alone.

Now the human side, where case-study marks are earned. On the benefit ledger: these systems deliver the winter rainfall of the south-western Cape, filling storage such as the Theewaterskloof and Garden Route dams, while mountain snow banks water that feeds rivers through spring. On the damage ledger: gales tear roofing from informal housing; heavy rain ponds and floods low-lying settlements; storm swell hammers harbours and coastal roads; snow closes mountain passes such as the Outeniqua crossings and strands livestock; and cold snaps endanger people without shelter while electricity demand spikes. Management follows from foresight: because the systems are visible on satellite days out over the Atlantic, the weather service issues early warnings; municipalities clear storm-water drains before the rainy season; disaster teams stage blankets and shelter space in advance; farmers bring stock down from high ground before snow arrives.

Take this section's questions now: the three scenes with all four instruments, the reason the warm front seldom reaches us, and one benefit, one damage and one management response.

## Subtopic: Reading the Synoptic Chart and the Satellite Image

All of this earns marks only when you can extract it from a synoptic chart, so let us assemble the chart in words. A synoptic chart is a snapshot of surface weather at one moment. Isobars — curved lines joining places of equal pressure, labelled in hectopascals — are its skeleton: one thousand and twenty, one thousand and twelve, one thousand and four. Where isobars crowd together the pressure gradient is steep and the wind is strong; where they spread apart the air is nearly calm. A closed set of low values marked L south of the country is your cyclone. A cold front is drawn from it as a line studded with solid triangles, the triangles pointing the way the front is moving — for our systems, east to north-east. A warm front carries solid semicircles instead, and an occluded front carries triangles and semicircles alternating along the same line.

Staging a system from the chart is a three-step routine. A long front with only a slight kink is the initial stage. Two separate fronts meeting at the low with an open warm sector between them is the mature stage — confirm it from station data inside the sector, which will show warmer temperatures and north-westerly wind. A single merged line wound tightly around the low is occlusion.

Next, place the two permanent anticyclones, because the exam links them to the fronts. The South Atlantic High sits off the west coast and the South Indian High off the east coast. They matter in two ways. First, they set the corridor: a front can only slip eastward through the gap between the highs, and in winter, when both highs migrate north with the overhead sun, that corridor lies across the southern tip of the continent — which is exactly why the winter-rainfall region gets its frontal rain in winter and loses it in summer, when the corridor shifts south into the ocean. Second, the South Atlantic High supplies the aftermath: once the front is through, the high ridges in around the south coast, delivering the rising pressure, the clearing sky and the cool onshore south-westerly of the third scene.

On a satellite image the mature system announces itself as a comma of bright cloud: the comma's head curled around the low, its tail streaming away to the north-west along the cold front, often reaching from south of the country deep into the Atlantic. As occlusion proceeds, the comma head winds into an ever-tighter spiral. And remember the forecasting rule the westerlies enforce: today's chart to your west is tomorrow's weather overhead. A deep low with a trailing front sitting at ten degrees east tonight means the southern coastal towns should be checking their gutters.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the front symbols and the meaning of isobar spacing, the three-step staging routine, the winter migration of the highs, and the comma on the satellite image.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole storm again, in plain pictures: a rope between two crowds, three photographs, and one stormy Thursday on the Garden Route — the version that will still be with you when you sit down to write.

## Subtopic: Two Crowds at a Rope

Picture a long rope stretched across a sports field. One crowd grips it wrapped in blankets and beanies — that is the cold, heavy air pushing up from near the pole. The other crowd is in shorts and slops — the warm, light air pressing down from the tropics. The rope itself is the polar front. All year the two crowds shove against that rope, and they never blend into one crowd, because heavy and light do not blend: pour cold syrup into a glass of water and the syrup slides to the bottom every time. Cold air is the syrup. It stays low and pushes along the ground. Warm air sits on top — so whenever the crowds surge into each other, the warm air is the one that gets shoved upward.

And upward is where rain is made. Rising air cools; cooling air can no longer hold its moisture; the moisture condenses into cloud, and cloud thick enough becomes rain. Every winter storm on this coast begins with warm air being forced up at that rope.

Where does the spinning come from? The Earth is a merry-go-round turning under the field. In our half of the world, that turning nudges everything moving to its left, so air trying to rush straight at a low-pressure point ends up curving around it, and the storm winds up rotating clockwise, like bathwater circling a plughole. One last helper works from the roof: a ribbon of screaming-fast wind high above, the jet stream, acts like a chimney over a fire, drawing air up and away from the top of the storm. The more the chimney draws, the lower the pressure at the floor, and the harder the surface air rushes in.

So hold four images: a rope between blankets and slops, syrup that always slides under, a leftward nudge that sets the spin, and a chimney drawing from above. That is the entire engine of a mid-latitude cyclone.

Your questions for this section are with you now: which air is forced up and why, where the rain actually comes from, and which way the spin turns in our hemisphere. The syrup and the plughole will carry you through.

## Subtopic: The Storm's Life in Three Photographs

Now watch the storm grow up in three photographs, because the stages question rewards anyone who can draw them.

Photograph one: the rope develops a bend. The two crowds have been sliding along it in opposite directions, and the rubbing kinks the line — a shallow wave, warm air bulging into cold territory, pressure starting to sag at the tip. This is the initial stage, and it looks like almost nothing.

Photograph two: the bend has opened into a huge sideways V, and this is the frame to study hardest — the mature stage. The V has two arms with completely different manners. The front arm is the warm front, and it behaves like an escalator: warm air rides up over the cold air ahead in a long, slow, steady climb, so its rain is soft, grey and patient, lasting for hours. The back arm is the cold front, and it behaves like a snowplough blade: cold air ramming underneath and throwing the warm air violently into the sky, so its rain is short, loud and hard. Caught between the arms sits the warm sector — think of it as the filling in a vetkoek, warm and soft, wrapped in colder dough ahead and behind.

Photograph three: the chase ends. The snowplough moves faster than the escalator, and eventually it catches up. The two arms close together and the warm filling is squeezed up and out of the ground floor entirely. That is occlusion — the word simply means closed off. With its warm fuel lifted away from the surface, the storm has nothing left to burn, and it fades out like a braai fire at midnight.

Three photographs, then: the bend, the V with its vetkoek filling, the squeeze. Draw them once with the cold front wearing little triangles and the warm front wearing little half-moons, and the stages question is yours.

Take the questions for this section now: what each photograph shows, which arm is the escalator and which the snowplough, and what occlusion closes off.

## Subtopic: A Stormy Thursday in George

Finish with the question that turns theory into a story: describe what someone in George, on the Garden Route, experiences as a cold front passes. Tell it as one Thursday.

Thursday morning, before the front. The air is strangely warm and sticky for winter. The wind is coming out of the north-west — off the land, from the Karoo side. Out over the sea to the west, cloud is stacking into a grey wall, and the barometer at the surf shop has been sliding down all morning like a phone battery that will not stop draining. The old fisherman at the harbour looks at the sky and cancels his afternoon trip. He is reading the same signs a forecaster reads.

Thursday afternoon, the front strikes. Within an hour the sky shuts, rain hammers down in cold sheets, thunder mutters over the sea, and up on the Outeniqua peaks the rain is falling as snow. Watch the flags along the beachfront: they swing right around, from north-west to south-west — from the Karoo side to the ocean side. That swing is the storm signing its name, and no description of a frontal passage is complete without it.

Thursday night and Friday, after the front. The cold has moved in properly — jacket weather, six to ten degrees down on the morning. The barometer is climbing again as a great dome of high pressure shoulders in behind the storm. The sky tears open into hurrying white clouds with cold sunshine between showers; the mountains wear fresh snow; the town's dam creeps up a percentage point.

Why is this a winter story? Because in winter the whole global wind machine slides north with the sun, and the storm highway runs right over the southern coast. In summer the highway shifts south into the ocean and the storms pass wide of the land. And why does the story matter? The good: these storms are the water supply — the winter-rainfall region drinks from them. The bad: roofs lost to gales, streets flooded in low-lying neighbourhoods, mountain passes closed by snow, brutal nights for people without shelter. The wise response: act on the warning before Thursday, not after — clear the drains, secure the roofs, open the shelters while the storm is still a comma of cloud far out on the satellite picture.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: morning, afternoon and night of that Thursday, the wind swing that signs the storm's name, and why the storms only run this coast in winter. Tell the Thursday story and the marks will follow.
