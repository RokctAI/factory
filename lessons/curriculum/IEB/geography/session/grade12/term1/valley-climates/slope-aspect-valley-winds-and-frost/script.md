# Part 1 — Expert

Valley climates are the smallest weather systems in the syllabus and among the most dependable to answer, because the entire topic can be reasoned from two questions you can ask yourself in the exam room: which slope is facing the sun, and which way is the air draining tonight? This session builds the topic in four moves: slope aspect and the temperature split it carves into a South African valley; the daily rhythm of anabatic and katabatic winds; the night-time trio of inversion, frost pocket and radiation fog, with the thermal belt floating above them; and finally the human layer — why the orchard, the farmhouse, the highway and the low-cost housing project each occupy the positions they do.

## Subtopic: Slope Aspect — Which Side Gets the Sun

Aspect is the direction a slope faces. One word, and it decides the energy income of every square metre of that slope — and in a valley running east to west, it divides the two walls into two different climates.

Reason out the geometry for our hemisphere. South Africa lies in the Southern Hemisphere, so the midday sun stands in the NORTHERN half of the sky. A north-facing slope therefore tilts toward the sun: the rays arrive at a steep, nearly perpendicular angle, concentrating their energy onto each square metre. A south-facing slope tilts away: the same rays arrive at a shallow, glancing angle, spread thin across a wider surface, so each square metre collects less. Stack on the hours bonus — the north-facing wall is also lit for longer each day — and the ruling is permanent: in the Southern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes are the warm slopes, and south-facing slopes are the cool, shaded, moister slopes. In the Northern Hemisphere the ruling flips, and a favourite question is precisely that flip.

You can read the consequences through a windscreen in any Klein Karoo poort or along the Langkloof. The warm north-facing wall: drier soil, faster evaporation, thinner and grassier cover, the first slope to lose its snow. The cool south-facing wall: damper soil, denser and greener growth, forest patches hiding in the kloofs, snow lingering for weeks. Farmers treat aspect as a planting instruction — sun-hungry fruit and vines on the north-facing wall, plantations and dairy pasture on the shaded one.

Two precision points that earn their marks: the contrast between the walls peaks in winter, when the low sun makes the angle difference most extreme; and it peaks in east-west valleys, where one wall faces square north and the other square south. Turn the valley to run north-south and both walls share morning and afternoon sun, so the contrast fades.

Take this section's questions now: the definition of aspect, the Southern Hemisphere geometry that favours the north-facing wall, and the visible differences written on the two slopes.

## Subtopic: The Valley's Daily Breathing — Anabatic and Katabatic Winds

A valley breathes to a daily beat: air moves up the slopes through the day and drains back down them through the night. Both winds carry Greek names, and both the names and the mechanisms are examinable.

By day: the anabatic wind, the upslope breeze. Once the sun is up, the valley WALLS warm faster than the free air over the valley centre, because the rays strike the slopes directly. Air in contact with the warm rock and soil heats by conduction, loses density, and slips upward along the incline — a bubble sliding along a tilted ceiling. Through the afternoon a steady upslope breeze works the sunlit wall, strongest on the north-facing side because that wall is hottest. Anabatic air is rising air, and rising air can condense: the cauliflower cumulus that gathers on the peaks by mid-afternoon in summer is the anabatic system made visible.

By night: the katabatic wind, the downslope drainage. After sunset the slopes bleed heat to a clear sky by long-wave radiation. The air against them chills, gains density, and starts to slide downhill under gravity — a cold, quiet, persistent creep down both walls that gathers on the valley floor. Katabatic drainage runs strongest on clear, calm winter nights, when radiative cooling is fastest and no regional wind interferes.

Keep the asymmetry sharp: the anabatic wind is powered by heating and climbs; the katabatic wind is powered by cooling plus gravity and sinks. The memory hook from this lesson: anabatic ascends — two As; katabatic is gravity's wind, and gravity only ever pulls one way.

And where does all that cold drainage end up? It ponds. Cold air from both walls collects on the valley floor the way water collects in a basin, and that pond of cold air manufactures everything in the next section — the inversion, the frost pocket and the fog.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: each wind matched to its time, mechanism and direction, and the destination of the night's cold air.

## Subtopic: Night in the Valley — Inversion, Thermal Belt, Frost and Fog

Walk one clear winter night through to its morning, because this sequence carries the heart of every valley-climate question.

Evening: katabatic drainage pours cold, dense air onto the valley floor. By the early hours the coldest air in the whole valley lies at the BOTTOM, with warmer air resting above it — temperature now increases with height above the floor, the normal pattern inverted. This is the valley temperature inversion: a surface inversion built by radiation cooling and cold-air drainage.

Now climb the slope in your imagination and read the layers with your skin. The floor: coldest of all, often below freezing before dawn. The mid-slope: noticeably, sometimes astonishingly warmer — this band, perched above the ponded cold air yet below the radiatively chilled upper slopes, is the thermal belt, the warmest address of the night-time valley. Above it, the high slopes and the ridge cool off again toward the free-air temperature.

The cold pond on the floor breeds two dawn hazards. First, the frost pocket: wherever the ponded air falls below zero, vapour freezes onto every surface it touches, whitening the valley bottom while the mid-slope stays green — and any hollow that traps drainage, a depression, a dip behind a road embankment, an elbow in the valley, becomes its own private frost pocket; the hollows around Underberg below the Drakensberg are notorious for exactly this. Second, radiation fog: cool the moist ponded air to its dew point and its vapour condenses into droplets — fog created by radiative cooling, lying on the valley floor, densest at first light, then burning off from the top down as the sun gets to work. Anyone who has taken the N3 through the Midlands on a July morning, plunging from clear sunshine into a white river at every low crossing, has watched radiation fog drawing a map of the inversions.

The inversion is also a trap: smoke from early fires and dust climb into the warm lid, stop, and spread, which is why valley towns wear a grey morning blanket in winter — the same physics the urban-climates lesson scales up to a whole city.

The questions on this section are with you now: how drainage builds the inversion, where the thermal belt sits and why it stays warm, and the exact recipes for frost pockets and radiation fog.

## Subtopic: People in the Valley — Farming, Settlement and Sensible Planning

Every mechanism above converts directly into human decisions, and the standard phrasing — account for the location of the orchard, the farmstead, the settlement — is answered by laying people over the physics.

Farming first. Frost-tender crops — deciduous fruit, citrus, vines — belong on the THERMAL BELT, the frost-free shelf of the mid-slope. Put them on the valley floor and a single clear June night in a frost pocket executes the blossom and the year's income; push them too high and the growing season shrinks. That is why the apple and pear blocks of the Langkloof run in ribbons along the lower-middle slopes, and why aspect assigns the sides: sun-demanding table grapes and stone fruit take the warm north-facing wall, while orchard varieties needing winter chill, together with timber and pasture, can hold the southern aspect. The tough customers — pasture, lucerne, hardy vegetables — accept the risky floor, compensated by deep alluvial soil and the river. And when frost still threatens, farmers attack the inversion itself: wind machines that drag the thermal belt's warm air down into the trees, orchard heaters between the rows, even a helicopter held over a high-value block through a critical night — every one of them a deliberate stirring of the inversion structure, which is exactly the justification an answer should give.

Settlement obeys the same physics. Old farmsteads and many historic valley towns stand on the lower slopes — above the flood line, above the worst frost, on or near the thermal belt, with the valley walls breaking the regional winds. Roads and railways take the flat floor because engineering loves flat, and they accept the fog with it: winter chain collisions cluster on low-lying freeway dips, and that is where the fog alerts point.

Planning failure supplies the sharpest exam material. Low-cost housing and informal settlement pressed onto cheap valley-floor land occupy the coldest, foggiest, smokiest and most flood-exposed zone in the entire landscape — larger heating bills, more chest infections, worse flood losses, all loaded onto the households least able to carry them. Planning that reads the valley puts homes on the mid-slope shelf, keeps smoky industry out of the inversion pond entirely, and leaves the floor to fields, parks and the river's floodplain.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: crops assigned to floor, belt and each wall with reasons, the settlement logic of the shelf, and one planning failure with its correction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole valley again, armed with nothing but a torch, a basin and a scarf — the version that still works at midnight before the exam.

## Subtopic: The Sunny Wall and the Shady Wall

Stand in an east-west valley early on a winter morning. One wall is already golden and warming; the other is still grey and cold. The entire first section of this topic is just explaining that picture.

Here is the trick for getting it right every time. In South Africa the midday sun never stands overhead — it hangs in the NORTHERN sky. So the wall that FACES north is leaning toward the sun. Now take a torch and shine it straight at a wall: a small, bright, hot circle. Tilt the beam to a skimming angle: the same light smears into a long, pale ellipse. Straight beam, bright circle — that is the north-facing slope. Skimming beam, pale smear — that is the south-facing slope. One torch, two angles, and you have the whole physics of aspect.

Give the walls their nicknames. The north-facing wall is the stoep side of the valley — the side where you would sit in winter: warm, dry, first to shed its frost, dressed in grass, vines and sun-loving fruit. The south-facing wall is the pantry side — cool, damp, shadowed, green with forest and pasture, keeping its snow the way a pantry keeps things cold. Farmers planted the answer generations ago: drive the Langkloof and read it off the slopes — fruit sunning itself on one wall, pines and pasture keeping cool on the other.

Small print, worth real marks. The stoep-pantry contrast is strongest in winter, when the low sun makes the skimming angle most severe. It is strongest in valleys running east-west, whose walls face square north and square south; a north-south valley splits the sun more evenly and the contrast fades. And always declare your hemisphere — in Europe the stoep side faces south, and the flip is a favourite trick question.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the torch held straight against the torch held at a slant, the stoep side against the pantry side, and the winter and direction small print.

## Subtopic: The Bathtub That Fills With Cold Air

Now set the valley breathing, and let a bathtub carry the night shift.

Day first. The sun grills the valley walls, the walls warm the air lying against them, and warmed air climbs — so all day, soft breezes slide UP the slopes, hugging the mountainside the way heat shimmers up off a tin roof. That upslope day breeze is the anabatic wind — anabatic ascends, two As. It is also why little white clouds sit on the summits by afternoon: the slopes have been conveying warm moist air to the peaks since morning.

At sunset the valley changes direction. Under a clear sky the slopes fling their heat out to the stars and go cold fast. The air against them chills, and chilled air is heavy air — it stops floating and starts FLOWING, downhill, exactly like water. All night long, thin sheets of cold air trickle down both walls toward the bottom. That downhill night drain is the katabatic wind — gravity's wind.

And the valley floor? The floor is the bathtub. Cold air streams in off both walls like water down the sides of the tub, and by the small hours the tub has filled: a deep, still pool of the coldest air in the whole landscape lying on the floor, with warmer air resting on its surface. Look at what that has done to the temperatures: cold at the bottom, warmer above — upside down from the usual. That upside-down layering is the temperature inversion. And the warm layer floating just above the cold pool has a name every fruit farmer whispers with respect: the thermal belt — the snug shelf partway up the slope that stays warm all night, sitting above the cold bath like the rim of the tub above the waterline.

Take the questions for this section now: which wind blows when and why, the tub filling from both walls, and where the warm shelf sits. Hold the tub in your mind and no valley-night question can shake you.

## Subtopic: Frost on the Floor, Money on the Shelf

Finish the story at dawn — then place everything in the valley the way someone who understands it would.

The cold pool on the floor commits two kinds of mischief before sunrise. If it drops below zero, everything standing in it wakes up white: the frost pocket — the bathtub become a chest freezer. One such night in June can kill every apple blossom on the floor, a season's income erased before the kettle boils; the farmers around Underberg plan their whole layout around exactly that risk. If the pool is moist instead, cooling squeezes its vapour into droplets and the floor vanishes under radiation fog — the white river the N3 drops into at every low point on a July morning, thickest at dawn, gone by mid-morning as the sun stirs the tub. The pool also hoards smoke: every early fire in the valley sends its smoke up to the warm lid, where it stops and spreads into the grey blanket valley towns wear in winter.

Now do the placement. The money — orchards and vineyards — goes on the SHELF: the thermal belt, above the frost bath, below the cold heights. That is precisely the ribbon you see in the Langkloof: fruit blocks striping the mid-slope like a scarf wrapped around the mountain at exactly the height that stays warm. Sun-hungry crops choose the stoep side; plantations and pasture take the pantry side. The floor gets the hardy stuff — pasture, lucerne, vegetables — plus the road and the railway, because engineers need flat ground; just respect the fog down there, and drive like you know what a white river is.

Houses want the shelf too. Old farmsteads sit a little way up the slope — above the frost, above the floods, wrapped in the warm scarf. The planning tragedy, and the favourite evaluation question, is that low-cost housing so often lands on the cheap ground at the bottom of the tub: the coldest, foggiest, smokiest, most flood-prone patch in the valley, allocated to the families least able to pay for heating and clinic visits. The fix reads straight off the physics: homes on the shelf, smoke and industry out of the tub, the floor left to fields, parks and the river.

And when frost threatens the blossoms anyway, farmers cheat: towering fans that pull the shelf's warm air down among the trees, heaters between the rows, a helicopter beating the air over a precious block on a critical night — all of them stirring the bathtub so the freezer never sets.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the two dawn hazards with their recipes, what belongs on the floor, the shelf and each wall, and the planning mistake with its fix. Keep the torch, the tub and the scarf, and this topic is banked.
