Hi, Mandy here — loosen your grip on that highlighter. Valley climates is the kindest topic in climatology, because you can rebuild every fact from two pictures you already own.

Picture one: the torch. Shine it straight at the wall — small, hot circle. Tilt it — long, pale smear. That is aspect. In South Africa the midday sun sits in the northern sky, so the north-facing wall gets the straight beam: warm, dry, fruit and vines. The south-facing wall gets the smear: cool, damp, forest and pasture. Flip it for Europe, and say which hemisphere you mean.

Picture two: the bathtub. By day the sun heats the walls and breezes climb them — anabatic ascends, two As. By night the walls chill, cold air flows downhill like water, and the valley floor fills like a tub. Cold at the bottom, warm shelf floating above — that upside-down layering is the inversion, the shelf is the thermal belt, and the tub's mischief at dawn is frost pockets and radiation fog. Then everything human snaps into place: orchards and farmhouses on the shelf, hardy crops and roads on the floor, and never put housing at the bottom of the tub.

Tonight, draw the valley cross-section once: two walls, the cold pool, the warm shelf, the fog line. Label it in pencil, badly — five minutes. That one sketch answers almost every question this topic can ask.

You have seen frost on a low field and fog in a dip your whole life. Now you know why. Go prove it on the practice questions.
