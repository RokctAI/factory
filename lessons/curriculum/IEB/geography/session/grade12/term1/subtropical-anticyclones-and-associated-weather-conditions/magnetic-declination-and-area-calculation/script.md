# Part 1 — Expert

Alongside gradient and scale, two more calculations complete the mapwork toolkit, and both are pure recipe: magnetic declination, which reconciles the compass with the map, and area, which turns two measured distances into square units. Each is worth a handful of marks, each follows a fixed sequence of small steps, and each has one specific step where careless answers fall over. This session builds both recipes on fully worked examples of the standard kind: a topographic sheet whose declination note is eight years out of date and must be brought up to the present year, and a demarcated block on the orthophoto whose area is wanted in square metres. By the end, both calculations should feel less like geography and more like following a recipe you know by heart.

## Subtopic: Two Norths and the Angle Between Them

Every topographic sheet has to manage two different norths. True north is the direction to the geographic North Pole — the fixed point where all lines of longitude converge, and the north to which every map is drawn. Magnetic north is where a compass needle actually points: the magnetic pole, which does not coincide with the geographic pole and, inconveniently, wanders over the years as currents in the Earth's molten core shift.

Magnetic declination is the angle between those two norths — the standing correction between map north and needle north. Over South Africa the needle points to the west of true north, so our declination is always expressed as so many degrees and minutes west of true north. In the margin of every topographic sheet sits a declination note carrying three facts: the declination as measured in a stated year, the mean annual change, and the direction of that change — in our case westward, which means the angle creeps a little wider every year.

Why does this matter beyond the margin of the map? Because a bearing lifted off the map with a protractor is a true bearing, measured from true north — and it cannot be walked with a compass until it is translated. The relationship to memorise: magnetic bearing equals true bearing plus magnetic declination, for a declination lying west. Land surveyors, ship's navigators, game rangers and hikers all apply this correction daily, and the standard question tests whether you can update the printed declination to the current year before applying it.

One more foundation: angles in this topic are written in degrees and minutes, and these minutes have nothing to do with time. Sixty minutes of arc make one degree, just as sixty seconds of arc make one minute. Every piece of arithmetic in the topic leans on that sixty.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two norths defined, declination as the angle between them, the three facts in the margin note, and the sixty-minute rule.

## Subtopic: The Magnetic Declination Calculation

Now the calculation itself, laid out the way that collects every mark. The sheet's margin says: mean magnetic declination 17 degrees 53 minutes west of true north in 2018, mean annual change 4 minutes westward. The question asks: calculate the magnetic declination for 2026.

Step one: the difference in years. From 2018 to 2026 is eight years. Write it as a line of working: 2026 minus 2018 gives 8 years. Sometimes this difference is supplied in the question; recompute it anyway, because a wrong year count contaminates every later line.

Step two: the total change. Eight years at 4 minutes per year: 8 × 4 = 32 minutes. Attach the direction to the number the moment you write it — 32 minutes westward — because direction is what decides the next step.

Step three: apply the change. The declination lies west of true north and the drift is westward, so the angle is widening: add. 17 degrees 53 minutes plus 32 minutes gives 17 degrees 85 minutes. Now the step that sorts the prepared from the lucky: 85 minutes is more than a full degree, and no angle answer may hold sixty or more minutes. Carry sixty minutes up into one degree, leaving twenty-five minutes. The declination for 2026 is 18 degrees 25 minutes west of true north.

Write that final line in full — degrees, minutes, and the phrase west of true north. The direction is part of the answer, not a flourish; a mark is routinely reserved for it.

If the margin note ever gives an eastward annual change, the needle is swinging back toward true north and the westerly angle is shrinking: subtract instead. The decision is always read off the note, never assumed. Add when the drift carries the needle further west; subtract when it drifts back east.

Work this section's questions before moving on: the year difference, the multiplication, the add-or-subtract decision, and the carry that turns 85 minutes into one degree and twenty-five minutes.

## Subtopic: Area — Two Distances Multiplied

The area question demarcates a feature — an orchard block, a reservoir, a school precinct — and asks for its area in square metres or square kilometres. The formula is elementary: Area equals Length times Breadth. The marks are never for knowing the formula; they are for feeding it correctly.

Here is the iron rule: convert each measured distance into real ground units first, and only then multiply. Never multiply the two map measurements in centimetres and attempt to convert the square centimetres afterwards — a linear scale factor does not apply to an area, and that shortcut is the most reliable source of zero in the whole mapwork section.

Worked example on the orthophoto, scale one to ten thousand. Measured length: 4,2 centimetres. Measured breadth: 2,5 centimetres. Convert the length: at one to ten thousand, 4,2 centimetres stands for 42 000 centimetres on the ground, which is 420 metres. Convert the breadth: 2,5 centimetres stands for 25 000 centimetres, which is 250 metres. Only now multiply: 420 × 250 = 105 000. The area is 105 000 square metres. Since two rulers can disagree by a millimetre, a band of answers around the official figure is normally accepted — one more reason to measure to the nearest millimetre and keep the conversions clean.

On the topographic sheet, at one to fifty thousand, every centimetre represents half a kilometre. A block measuring 5 centimetres by 4 centimetres converts to 2,5 kilometres by 2 kilometres, and 2,5 × 2 = 5, so the area is 5 square kilometres. The recipe never changes — convert, convert, multiply — only the multiplier does.

Fit the unit to the feature and to the question. Square metres suit compact features on the orthophoto; square kilometres suit large tracts on the topographic sheet. When the question names a unit, finish in exactly that unit; switching units halfway through a calculation is where clean working turns into wreckage.

Take the questions for this section now: the iron rule of convert-first, both worked conversions, the multiplication, and the choice between square metres and square kilometres.

## Subtopic: Marks, Traps and the Answer Line

Both calculations are marked line by line, so give both the same three-line skeleton: the given values, the operation, and the answer with its unit — plus, for declination, its direction. Setting out full working is the cheapest insurance in the mapwork section: even a slip in arithmetic usually leaves the method marks standing.

Now name the traps so they cannot ambush you. Declination trap one: subtracting the annual change when the note says westward. The South African standard case is a westward drift on a westerly declination — the angle widens; add. Trap two: leaving 85 minutes standing in a final answer. Sixty or more minutes must always carry into degrees; 17 degrees 85 minutes earns the arithmetic marks and loses the answer mark. Trap three: omitting the phrase west of true north. Trap four: counting the years from the wrong starting point — the count runs from the year printed in the margin note, not from the year the map was surveyed or any other year mentioned in the extract.

Area trap one: multiplying centimetres by centimetres and converting afterwards — the square-centimetre dead end. Each side converts separately, first. Trap two: taking the two measurements from two different sheets — one off the topographic map and one off the orthophoto — when both must come from the same sheet at the same scale. Trap three: writing the wrong unit — metres instead of square metres, or square metres where square kilometres were demanded. The squared unit is part of the answer; say it aloud as you write it.

Finally, see where these two skills plug into the bigger machine. The declination correction sits underneath every compass-bearing and direction question, and behind real navigation in the field. The area calculation feeds land-use and settlement analysis — the hectares under irrigation, the growth of a housing development between two photographs. And both rest on the same foundation as scale, distance and gradient: turning a measurement on paper into a truth on the ground. Master that single conversion and four different mapwork questions become one skill in four costumes.

The closing questions of this part are ready: the marking layout, the four declination traps, the three area traps, and the downstream questions both skills feed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now both calculations again, told from the kitchen table: a compass that tells a small lie, minutes that behave exactly like the minutes on a clock, and a floor being measured for tiles.

## Subtopic: The Compass That Tells a Small Lie

Put a map flat on the table and lay an imaginary compass on top of it. The map points at true north — the top of the planet, the pole every map in the world agrees on. The needle could not care less about the map: it swings to its own north, the magnetic one, which from South Africa lies off to the west. Two arrows on one table, pointing at two different norths, with a wedge of angle between them.

That wedge is magnetic declination — the size of the compass's lie. And the lie will not even hold still: the magnetic pole drifts as the deep Earth churns, so the wedge stretches a few minutes wider each year. Ours is measured in degrees and minutes west of true north, and it grows slowly, which is exactly why the map's margin states the wedge as it was in some past year together with its yearly growth — and why the standard question asks what the lie measures now.

Why tolerate a lying needle at all? Because out on a farm road at dusk, the needle is the only instrument you have. The map speaks the language of true north; the compass speaks the language of magnetic north; declination is the dictionary between them. To walk a direction taken from the map, widen it by the wedge: magnetic bearing equals true bearing plus the declination, for a wedge lying west. Skip the translation and every hundred metres of walking drifts you further from the windmill you were aiming for.

The whole topic in one honest sentence: the needle lies west by a known, slowly growing angle, and your job is to know the size of the lie this year.

The questions for this section are up: the two arrows, the wedge between them, why the wedge keeps growing, and the translation sentence for bearings.

## Subtopic: Minutes That Are Not Time

The arithmetic of declination looks intimidating until you notice you already do it daily — on a clock face. Minutes of angle behave exactly like minutes of time: sixty of them make one whole. Sixty minutes make an hour; sixty minutes make a degree. Once that clicks, the rest is primary-school adding.

Run our example as if it were a clock problem. The lie measured 17 degrees 53 minutes back in 2018, and it grows by 4 minutes a year. From 2018 to 2026 is eight years — count the gap: 2026 minus 2018 gives 8. The growth: eight fours, 8 × 4 = 32 minutes. Add the growth onto the old lie: 53 minutes plus 32 minutes is 85 minutes. And just as no one ever says the time is half past ten seventy-five, no angle is ever left holding 85 minutes. Eighty-five minutes past the hour is twenty-five minutes past the next hour: sixty rolls over into one whole. So 17 degrees and 85 minutes rolls over into 18 degrees and 25 minutes. The lie in 2026: 18 degrees 25 minutes, west of true north — and say the west out loud, because it carries a mark.

Add or subtract? The margin note settles it. If the needle is drifting further west while the lie already lies west, the lie is growing: add — that is the standard South African case. If a note ever reports an eastward drift, the needle is swinging back toward the truth and the lie is shrinking: subtract. Read the note every time; guessing is how easy marks escape.

That is the entire calculation: count the years, multiply by the yearly change, add it on, and roll the minutes over like a clock. Four small moves, and they are the same four moves every single year.

Your questions for this section are ready: the clock rule for sixty, the eight fours, the roll-over from 85 minutes, and the add-or-subtract decision.

## Subtopic: The Rugby Field in Your Answer Book

Area is the tiling question. Nobody tiling a bathroom measures the floor on a photograph of the house — they measure the real floor's length and breadth and multiply. A map is precisely such a photograph, shrunk by a known amount, so the area question adds one step to the tiling job: un-shrink each side back to real size before any multiplying happens.

Do it with the worked numbers, on the orthophoto where everything is shrunk ten thousand times. The block's length on paper: 4,2 centimetres. Un-shrink it: 42 000 centimetres, which is 420 metres. The breadth on paper: 2,5 centimetres. Un-shrink it: 25 000 centimetres, which is 250 metres. Now tile: 420 × 250 = 105 000. The block covers 105 000 square metres.

Now feel the size rather than just writing it. A rugby field measures roughly 100 metres by 70 metres, which is 7 000 square metres. Our block is fifteen rugby fields laid out side by side — a serious piece of land, which is exactly right for a feature worth demarcating on an orthophoto. That is the sense check: had the answer come out at 1 050 square metres, barely a training pitch, or at ten million, a small city, the rugby-field test would have caught the smell immediately. Map-sized blocks land in the tens of thousands of square metres; farms and towns land in square kilometres.

And the one blunder that marking teams see every single year: multiplying the paper centimetres first — 4,2 times 2,5 — and then trying to rescue the squashed little number afterwards. It cannot be rescued. The shrinking was applied to each side separately, so the un-shrinking must be applied to each side separately, before the two sides ever meet in a multiplication. Convert the length. Convert the breadth. Then multiply. Three moves, in that order, always.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the tiling order of operations, the two un-shrinkings, the rugby-field sense check, and the paper-centimetre trap. Take them in the order they teach, and these two calculations become the most dependable marks in your entire mapwork answer.
