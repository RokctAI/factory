**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if maps and GPS both exist, why are we still learning to correct a compass in the first place? Is declination not just history?

**Tutor:** It is the opposite of history — it is the fallback layer under every modern system, and the questions test it because the professions still use it. A phone's GPS dies with its battery, loses signal in a kloof or under storm cloud, and a paper map plus compass weighs nothing and never needs charging — which is why hiking clubs, game rangers, the mountain rescue teams and ships' officers all still train the correction. But there is a deeper point: declination is not a fixed historical fact you can memorise once, because the magnetic pole is in constant motion. Southern Africa's declination changes measurably within a single decade — that is exactly why the sheet prints the value for a stated year plus an annual change, instead of one number. Anyone navigating from a map printed years ago must update the angle before trusting a bearing, and that update is precisely the calculation you are being taught. So think of the skill as reading an instrument whose error is known and predictable: the needle is wrong, but it is wrong by a documented amount that you can bring up to date with thirty seconds of arithmetic. Wrong-but-correctable beats blindly-trusted every time — in the field and in the exam.

***

**Mandy:** Next: in the worked example we added the change. Is there ever a real case where we would subtract, or is subtract just there to trick us?

**Tutor:** Subtract is real, and understanding when protects you from both directions of error. The rule is geometric, not memorised: ask whether the drift carries the needle further from true north or back toward it. Our standard case — a westerly declination with a westward annual change — widens the wedge, so you add. But the magnetic pole's wander is not one-directional forever; there are regions and eras where the needle swings back east, and a margin note reporting an eastward mean annual change on a westerly declination means the wedge is closing: subtract the total change. The arithmetic is identical — years times minutes per year — only the sign flips. Two safety habits make this automatic. First, always write the direction next to the change the moment you compute it: thirty-two minutes westward, not just thirty-two minutes. Second, before the final line, sanity-check the movement: if the drift is westward, this year's declination must be bigger than the printed one; if eastward, smaller. If your answer moved the wrong way, you subtracted when you should have added or the reverse — and you have caught it before the marker does. Read the note, write the direction, check the movement: those three habits make the trick version of the question impossible to spring on you.

***

**Mandy:** Third question: why does converting after multiplying give the wrong area? I multiplied 4,2 by 2,5, got 10,5, and figured I could just scale it up at the end. It felt so reasonable.

**Tutor:** It feels reasonable because it works for distance — and area is the place where that habit betrays you. Here is the machinery. When a map shrinks the world ten thousand times, every LINE shrinks ten thousand times. But a square patch of ground shrinks ten thousand times in length AND ten thousand times in breadth, so its area shrinks by ten thousand multiplied by ten thousand — one hundred million times. Your 10,5 square centimetres on paper therefore stands for 10,5 times one hundred million square centimetres of ground, not 10,5 times ten thousand. Scale up by the linear factor, as you did, and your answer is ten thousand times too small. Now watch the correct route dissolve the problem: convert each side first — 4,2 centimetres becomes 420 metres, 2,5 centimetres becomes 250 metres — and multiply real metres by real metres: 420 times 250 is 105 000 square metres. No squared scale factor ever appears, because you never multiplied paper by paper. That is the deep reason behind the iron rule: converting first keeps you working in one world, the real one. If you remember only one sentence, remember this: scale applies to lengths, so apply it while the numbers still are lengths.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: my working is always right but I keep bleeding single marks — a forgotten west here, a wrong unit there. How do I stop the leaks?

**Tutor:** With a proofread ritual, because those marks are not lost to ignorance — they are lost to finishing too fast. Build a three-point check that runs in under fifteen seconds. Point one: does the answer carry its label? A declination answer must end in the phrase west of true north; an area answer must end in a squared unit — square metres or square kilometres, matching what the question demanded. Read the question's last line again and copy its unit exactly. Point two: is the answer legal? An angle may never hold sixty or more minutes — if your minutes column reads sixty-plus, roll it over before moving on. An area should pass the sense check: compare it to a rugby field's seven thousand square metres and ask whether the size is believable for the feature. Point three: is the working visible? Three lines — given values, operation, answer — so that even a slip in multiplication leaves method marks standing. Then one habit above all: never do these checks in your head. Touch each line with your pen as you verify it. The physical act slows you exactly enough to see the missing west or the metres that should be square metres. Rituals beat resolutions — you will not become a more careful person by Friday, but you can adopt a fifteen-second checklist tonight.
