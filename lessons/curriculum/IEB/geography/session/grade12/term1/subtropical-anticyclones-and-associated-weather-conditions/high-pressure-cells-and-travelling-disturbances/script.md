# Part 1 — Expert

Storms get the attention, but on most days of the year South Africa's weather is being run quietly by three great cells of high pressure. They explain the sunshine, the deserts, the haze that hangs over winter towns, the freak hot days of a coastal July and the drumbeat of summer thunder on the plateau. In this session we name and place the three subtropical anticyclones, unpack the mechanisms by which they steer the country's climate through the seasons, meet the travelling disturbances that periodically break their hold — the moisture front with its line thunderstorms, the coastal low, and the berg wind — and finish by finding every one of these players on a synoptic chart. Carry a single mechanical truth through the whole lesson: an anticyclone is a column of sinking, spreading air, and sinking air warms, dries and destroys cloud. Everything else is that truth meeting the map of southern Africa.

## Subtopic: Three Highs by Name, Place and Character

South Africa lies near thirty degrees south, inside the subtropical high-pressure belt — the latitude where air that ascended at the equator returns to the surface. Three semi-permanent cells of that belt surround the country, and you need each one by name, position and personality.

Before naming them, nail down the rotation rule that costs more marks than any other in this topic: in the Southern Hemisphere, circulation is anticlockwise around a high and clockwise around a low — the mirror image of the Northern Hemisphere diagrams in many textbooks. Air spirals outward from a high, deflected left by the Coriolis force, and the result is an anticlockwise gyre.

Now the western cell: the South Atlantic High, anchored over the ocean off the west coast. Its outward, anticlockwise flow drives cool air and cold surface water northward along the west coast, sustaining the Benguela upwelling system. Cold sea means feeble evaporation, so the west coast gets fog banks instead of rain clouds — the reason Port Nolloth can sit grey and damp under mist yet receive almost no rainfall in a year, and the reason the Namib exists at all.

The eastern cell is the South Indian High, over the ocean east of the country. Its circulation sweeps warm, humid air off the tropical Indian Ocean and delivers it onto the east coast — the moisture tap for KwaZulu-Natal's sticky summers and, when the door is open, for thunderstorm rain deep into the interior.

The third cell is the land-based member: the Kalahari High, seated over the interior plateau. It differs from its ocean partners in being seasonal — dominant and surface-based in winter, weakened and lifted in summer. That seasonal pulse, as the next section shows, is the master control on interior rainfall.

All three share the anticyclone signature: subsiding air warming by compression as it descends, cloud evaporating, surface air diverging, the atmosphere stable and the sky clear. This is why South Africa is a country of sunshine statistics and water shortages — well over half of it classed as semi-arid.

Take this section's questions now: the three cells named and positioned, the anticlockwise rule for Southern Hemisphere highs, and the weather character that sinking air imposes.

## Subtopic: How the Highs Run South Africa's Seasons

Mechanism one: the inversion layer. Subsiding air over the plateau warms as it compresses, laying down a stratum in which temperature increases with height — an upper-air inversion, and in effect a ceiling on the country's weather. A thermal rising off the hot ground climbs only while it is warmer than the air around it; when it reaches the inversion it meets air warmer than itself, loses buoyancy and stops. No further ascent, no condensation, no cloud. The altitude of that ceiling is the whole story of interior rainfall. In winter the Kalahari High is powerful and the inversion lies low — beneath the level of the escarpment — so moist maritime air cannot climb onto the plateau at all, and the interior locks into cloudless days and frosty nights. In summer intense surface heating weakens the high, the inversion rises well above the escarpment, and humid air fed by the South Indian High streams in beneath it: afternoon thunderstorms follow almost on schedule. One ceiling, rising and falling with the seasons, explains why Bloemfontein freezes dry in July and rumbles with storms in January.

Mechanism two: the seasonal migration. The entire pressure belt tracks the overhead sun — northward in winter, southward in summer. Winter's northward shift does two things at once: it strengthens the block against moist air reaching the interior, and it uncovers the south-western corner of the continent so that mid-latitude cold fronts can swing beneath it and deliver the Cape's winter rain. Summer's southward shift reverses both: the highs stand guard south of the Cape and turn the fronts away — hence the Cape's dry summers — while the lifted ceiling lets the interior convect. One migrating belt manufactures both of South Africa's rainfall regions.

Mechanism three: the paired ocean currents. The South Atlantic High propels the cold Benguela current up the west coast: cold water, minimal evaporation, fog and desert shore. The South Indian High propels the warm Agulhas current down the east coast: warm water, vigorous evaporation, humid air and a green, well-watered coastline. Port Nolloth and Margate share a latitude and live in different climates, purely because of which high and which current sit offshore.

Mechanism four: ridging. Behind every cold front that clears the country, the South Atlantic High stretches a tongue of high pressure — a ridge — eastward around the southern tip of the continent. The ridge pushes cool, moist maritime air onshore along the south and east coasts, bringing showers and drizzle after the front and giving the southern coastal belt the rare privilege of rain in every season.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the inversion ceiling in winter versus summer, the migrating belt and the two rainfall regions, each current matched to its high, and the weather a ridging high delivers.

## Subtopic: The Travelling Disturbances — Moisture Front, Coastal Low, Berg Wind

Between the great stationary cells move three smaller systems that produce some of the sharpest weather in the country.

First, the moisture front and its line thunderstorms. In summer the interior hosts two very different air masses: humid tropical air curling in from the north-east under the influence of the South Indian High, and hot, bone-dry air over the western interior. The boundary between them — often visible from space as a ragged diagonal of cloud running roughly north-west to south-east — is the moisture front, also called the dry line. Along this line the moist air is levered upward, and thunderstorms detonate in a chain that can stretch for hundreds of kilometres: the line thunderstorms that batter the eastern plateau on summer afternoons with torrential rain, lightning, hail and flash flooding. The response is preparedness: weather-service alerts, lightning safety drills on farms and sports fields, hail netting over orchards, and storm-water systems maintained before the season, not during it.

Second, the coastal low. This is a shallow low-pressure cell pinned between the escarpment and the sea, which travels along the coastline like a bead on a wire — characteristically from the west coast around the south coast toward KwaZulu-Natal, running ahead of an approaching front. Ahead of the coastal low, air is dragged down off the plateau and blows offshore: hot, dry, gusty. Behind it, cool, moist marine air floods onshore: the temperature can fall ten degrees within an hour, low cloud rolls in, drizzle follows. When East London swelters through a strange hot morning and shivers under sea mist by evening, the coastal low has passed.

Third, the berg wind — the topic's showpiece, because it connects straight back to the anticyclone. In winter the strong interior high piles cool, dense air onto the plateau, some fourteen hundred to seventeen hundred metres above sea level. When a coastal low or an advancing front lowers pressure at the coast, that reservoir of plateau air drains seaward, plunging down the escarpment and compressing as it descends — warming at close to one degree Celsius for every hundred metres of fall. Descending a kilometre and a half, it arrives at the coast roughly fourteen degrees warmer than it left the plateau: a hot, desiccated, gusty offshore wind. Berg-wind days are the source of winter's strangest statistics — mid-thirties on the Eastern Cape coast in July — and they are the country's most dangerous fire days: cured winter fuel, humidity on the floor, and a strong wind to drive every spark. The George fires of late twenty eighteen raced through plantations on exactly such hot, dry offshore-wind conditions. Risk management is standard and examinable: total fire bans and firebreak preparation ahead of high-danger days, fire-danger warnings from the weather service, no open flames, and firefighting teams on standby.

The questions on this section are with you now: the moisture front and where its line storms strike, the coastal low's before-and-after contrast, and the berg wind's descending-air arithmetic with its fire connection.

## Subtopic: Reading It All on the Synoptic Chart

Everything in this lesson eventually appears as a question built on a South African synoptic chart, so run the identification drill.

The three highs first: hunt for closed isobars around cells labelled H. Over the Atlantic west of the country — the South Atlantic High. Over the Indian Ocean to the east — the South Indian High. Over the plateau itself, chiefly on winter charts — the Kalahari High. Central pressures typically read one thousand and twenty hectopascals or more, and a midwinter interior high can push beyond one thousand and thirty.

Next, date the chart by season without a date — a skill worth learning as a drill. Clue one, the interior: a strong closed high sitting on the plateau announces winter; a heat low over the north-western interior with humid north-easterly inflow announces summer. Clue two, the fronts: cold fronts brushing or crossing the south-western Cape belong to winter charts; fronts trailing far to the south while the highs ridge strongly poleward belong to summer. Clue three, the station data: interior dew points in the teens mean summer moisture; dew points near or below zero mean the dry winter lock. Any two clues agreeing gives you the season plus its justification in one move.

The ridging high: after a front exits, watch the South Atlantic High's isobars bulge eastward around the south coast — a hook of high pressure curling beneath the continent. That shape decodes as onshore south-westerly to south-easterly wind, falling temperatures, and post-frontal drizzle for the south and east coasts.

The coastal low and the berg wind travel as a pair on the chart: a small, tight low pressed against the coastline just ahead of a front, with coastal stations ahead of it reporting hot, dry offshore winds — plateau air descending — and stations behind it reporting the cool onshore change. Where you diagnose berg-wind conditions, the follow-up is prediction: state that the passage of the coastal low or front will bring an abrupt cool change, and give the mechanism.

The moisture front shows on the chart as a trough line across the interior with humid north-easterly air on its eastern flank and dry air to the west; on the accompanying satellite image it is the diagonal parade of cumulonimbus clusters across the summer plateau.

Close with prediction discipline: our weather travels broadly west to east, coastal lows run the coastline ahead of fronts, and ridging follows every frontal passage. Read the chart, name the systems, then say what tomorrow brings and why — the why is where the marks live.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: locating the three highs, dating the chart by season, the ridging hook, the coastal-low and berg-wind station signatures, and the moisture front's twin fingerprints.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with three guards, a pot lid and a hairdryer — the version built to survive an exam-room blank.

## Subtopic: Three Guards Around the Country

Think of South Africa as a property watched by three security guards. One guard stands offshore on the west side, out in the Atlantic. One stands offshore on the east side, in the Indian Ocean. The third sits inside the property, in the middle of the plateau. These are the three high-pressure cells — the South Atlantic High, the South Indian High and the Kalahari High — and a high-pressure cell is nothing more than a place where air is sinking. Sinking air is the enemy of weather: as it sinks it is squeezed, squeezing warms it, and warm descending air wipes cloud out of the sky the way a warm cloth wipes steam off a mirror. Three guards, all made of sinking air, is why this country runs on sunshine.

Each guard has habits. The west-side guard shoves cold water and cool air up the west coast. That is why the Atlantic at Langebaan numbs your feet in January, why the west coast does fog instead of rain, and why the land north of the Orange River mouth turns to desert. The east-side guard is generous: it ladles warm, wet ocean air onto the east coast, which is why the sea at Margate feels like bathwater and a February afternoon in Durban feels like breathing soup. The inside guard is the temperamental one — the Kalahari High. In winter it sits heavy and low over the interior and smothers everything. In summer it drifts up and loosens its grip, and the rain gets in. His story is the next section.

One rule before moving on, and it is the single most dropped mark in the topic: down here in the Southern Hemisphere, air runs anticlockwise around a high and clockwise around a low. Highs go against the clock. Repeat it until it is boring.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the three guards named and placed, why sinking air means blue sky, and which way air circles a Southern Hemisphere high. Let the guards hold the map for you.

## Subtopic: The Pot Lid Over Bloemfontein

Why is winter in Bloemfontein all frost and blue sky, while summer delivers a thunderstorm almost every afternoon? One kitchen picture answers everything: a pot with a lid.

The pot is the interior plateau. The lid is the inversion layer — a sheet of warm air, manufactured by all that sinking, lying over the country and blocking anything that tries to rise through it. Steam cannot leave a lidded pot; a growing cloud cannot punch through a layer of air warmer than itself.

In winter the inside guard is at full strength and screws the lid down low — lower than the escarpment, the huge step that rims the plateau. Moist sea air arrives at the bottom of the step, tries to climb, and finds the lid sitting below the top edge: entry refused. So the plateau stays dry — glittering cold mornings, white frost on the rugby fields, and all the dust and smoke of the winter towns trapped under the lid as that flat brown haze you see from the road at dawn. The haze is the lid made visible.

In summer the ground cooks, the inside guard weakens and floats upward, and the lid lifts far above the escarpment. Now the east-side guard's moist ocean air can pour up the step and onto the plateau. Wet air over baking ground with no lid overhead is a thunderstorm recipe, and the plateau cashes it in most afternoons — the towering four-o'clock storm, the smell of rain on hot dust. And where that incoming wet air runs into the dry desert air of the west, the storms organise into a queue along the boundary: the moisture front, with its line thunderstorms strung out like beads on a wire, the storms that can flood a town's streets in an hour.

The seasons themselves are the guards marching: the whole squad steps north in winter and south in summer, following the sun. When they step north, the south-western corner is left unguarded and the ocean storms sneak in to water the Cape. When they step south, they wall the Cape off and it dries out, just as the lifted lid lets the interior storm. One moving lid, one marching squad — both rainfall regions explained.

Take the questions for this section now: the lid low in winter and high in summer, why moist air cannot climb the step in July, and how the marching guards give the Cape its winter rain. Tell the pot-lid story out loud once and it is yours.

## Subtopic: The Hairdryer Wind and the Cool Change

Finish with the two travelling troublemakers that every family on the coast knows by feel, if not by name.

First, the berg wind — the hairdryer. Through winter the inside guard stacks air on the plateau, roughly a kilometre and a half above the sea. When a passing system tugs the pressure down at the coast, that stacked air breaks loose and pours over the escarpment edge, falling toward the shore. Falling air gets squeezed, and squeezed air heats: close to one degree gained for every hundred metres dropped. Fall fifteen hundred metres and the parcel lands about fourteen degrees hotter than it started — a scorching, utterly dry, gusty wind blowing from the land out to sea. That is the berg wind, the mountain wind: the reason East London can post a July afternoon in the mid-thirties while the plateau it came from sits near freezing. It is a hairdryer aimed at the coastline. And a hairdryer aimed at winter-dry bush is a fire machine: the country's worst blazes — the George plantation fires of twenty eighteen among them — have run before exactly this wind, with humidity through the floor and every ember airborne. Berg-wind protocol: total fire bans, firebreaks cut in advance, warnings broadcast, crews on standby.

Second, the coastal low — the cool change. Picture a bead threaded on a wire that runs along the coastline. The bead is a small low-pressure cell sliding along the coast, usually ahead of a cold front. In front of the bead, air is hauled down off the plateau — often the very hairdryer morning you just met. Behind the bead, cool damp sea air surges in: ten degrees gone in an hour, grey cloud, drizzle on the windows. The classic coastal whiplash day — school shirt soaked with sweat at eight, hoodie on by five — is nothing but the bead passing your town.

See how the whole topic locks together in one chain: the guards build the high-pressure world, the lid stores the dry plateau air, the bead tugs at the coast, the hairdryer fires ahead of it, and the cool change closes the door behind it. Tell it as that chain and no question in the section can surprise you.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the falling-air arithmetic that heats the hairdryer, the fire rules for a berg-wind day, and the before-and-after of the sliding bead. Finish them and the topic is banked.
