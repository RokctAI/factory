# Part 1 — Expert

Underneath every mapwork calculation lies one conversion: a distance measured on paper translated into a distance across real ground. This session builds that conversion from the meaning of the ratio scale itself, through a layout of working that earns each step its mark, on to winding routes, orthophoto sheets and the reverse calculation, and closes with the verification habits that expose a slipped decimal before anyone else can. One worked problem travels with us the whole way, in the standard form these questions take: the distance between two points on a 1:50 000 topographic map measures 7,4 centimetres — calculate the real distance in kilometres.

## Subtopic: What 1:50 000 Actually Means

The scale of a map is the fixed relationship between distance on the map and distance on the ground it depicts. South African topographic sheets use a ratio scale: 1:50 000, read aloud as one to fifty thousand.

Take the ratio literally: one unit of any length on the paper stands for fifty thousand of those same units on the ground. One centimetre on the map is 50 000 centimetres of actual landscape. One millimetre is 50 000 millimetres. Notice that the ratio owns no unit of its own — and that is precisely its strength. Measure in whatever unit you like, multiply by 50 000, and reality comes back to you in that same unit.

Now put a size to it: 50 000 centimetres is 500 metres — half a kilometre. On a 1:50 000 sheet, then, each centimetre of paper covers half a kilometre of country, and every two centimetres cover a full kilometre. Lay a ruler across the sheet and you are, quite literally, measuring the landscape.

Scale also appears on the sheet in two other costumes, and questions test whether you can move between them. The word scale spells it out in a sentence: one centimetre represents half a kilometre. The linear scale is the segmented bar printed along the bottom margin — built for stepping distances off with dividers or a paper edge, no arithmetic required. All three costumes carry identical information; the ratio is the one that plugs into a calculation.

One more distinction that regularly trips candidates: large scale versus small scale. A 1:10 000 map is a larger scale than a 1:50 000 map — which feels backwards until you treat the ratios as fractions: one ten-thousandth is a bigger fraction than one fifty-thousandth. Larger scale means a smaller slice of ground shown in richer detail. The orthophoto maps you use through Grade 12, printed at 1:10 000, are the large-scale companions of the topographic sheet.

## Subtopic: The Straight-Line Calculation

Now the worked problem, in the layout that banks every mark.

Step one: obtain the map distance. Our question supplies it: 7,4 centimetres between the two points. When the measuring is yours to do, set the ruler's edge exactly centre-to-centre on the two symbols and read to the nearest millimetre. Precision here is not fussiness: on a 1:50 000 sheet, each millimetre of sloppy measuring is fifty metres of ground error.

Step two: state the conversion the scale entitles you to. Real distance equals map distance multiplied by the scale factor. Put it down as a formula line: real distance = map distance × 50 000.

Step three: substitute and multiply, staying in centimetres for the moment: 7,4 centimetres times 50 000 gives 370 000 centimetres. Take the multiplication in two comfortable pieces: 7 times 50 000 is 350 000; 0,4 times 50 000 is 20 000; the sum is 370 000.

Step four: convert into the unit the question names — kilometres. Walk the metric ladder one rung at a time. Centimetres to metres: divide by 100, so 370 000 centimetres is 3 700 metres. Metres to kilometres: divide by 1 000, so 3 700 metres is 3,7 kilometres.

Answer: the real distance is 3,7 kilometres — with the unit attached. A bare 3,7 is an incomplete answer, and the unit routinely carries its own mark.

Once the long method is secure, a shortcut is worth holding in reserve: on a 1:50 000 sheet, halving the centimetres gives kilometres directly, since each centimetre is half a kilometre. Check it: half of 7,4 is 3,7. Same answer in one move. But use the shortcut as a verifier, not as your working — the written steps are what earn marks, and the halving trick works only at this one scale while the full method works at every scale ever printed.

## Subtopic: Curved Distances and Other Scales

Question setters lean on two favourite variations, and one method defeats both.

Variation one: the route refuses to be straight. "Determine the distance along the gravel road from the shop to the weir" — a road full of bends that no rigid ruler can follow. Use the string technique: press a length of string, or the edge of a strip of paper rotated segment by segment, along every curve of the route, mark the end point, then pull it straight against your ruler. Suppose the total reads 11,8 centimetres. From here nothing is new: multiply by 50 000 to get 590 000 centimetres, step down the ladder to 5 900 metres, and finish at 5,9 kilometres. The measuring tool changed; the mathematics did not blink.

Variation two: the sheet changes scale. Orthophoto maps arrive at 1:10 000. Put the same 7,4 centimetres on one of those and it un-scales by only 10 000: 74 000 centimetres, which is 740 metres — 0,74 kilometres. The identical paper measurement now spans a fifth of the ground, and that is exactly what larger scale, more detail, less area means in numbers. So never assume the factor: read the scale printed on the sheet in your hand, every single time. Questions that show the same two points on both sheets are checking precisely whether you matched each measurement to its own scale.

Then there is the reverse question: "the real distance between the two beacons is 3,7 kilometres; how far apart do they appear on the 1:50 000 map?" Drive the machine backwards. Convert 3,7 kilometres to 370 000 centimetres, divide by 50 000, and the map distance is 7,4 centimetres. Multiplication carries you from map to ground; division carries you home again. One relationship, used in both directions.

## Subtopic: Verification, Error Traps and Where This Skill Feeds

Verification first, because a ten-second check rescues more answers than an hour of revision. Spread your hand on a 1:50 000 sheet: a hand-span of roughly 10 centimetres covers about 5 kilometres. Our 7,4 centimetres produced 3,7 kilometres — comfortably under a hand-span, comfortably under 5 kilometres. It fits. Had the working spat out 37 kilometres, or 0,37 kilometres, the hand-span comparison would expose the runaway decimal on the spot. Build the reflex into your routine: convert, then ask whether a person could reasonably walk that answer in an afternoon.

Now the traps, each named so it cannot ambush you. Trap one, the most damaging: stumbling on the unit ladder — dividing by 1 000 on the centimetre-to-metre rung where 100 belongs, or stopping at metres when kilometres were demanded. Recite the rungs: divide by 100 into metres, divide by 1 000 into kilometres. Trap two: multiplying by the wrong factor because the question moved quietly from the topographic sheet to the orthophoto. The scale lives on the sheet — read it. Trap three: rounding the measurement before multiplying. Round 7,4 up to 7,5 and you have manufactured fifty metres of error before the calculation began; carry the full measurement through and round only at the end, if asked. Trap four: finishing in the wrong unit — 3 700 labelled metres is faithful arithmetic and a lost mark when the question said kilometres.

Where does the skill travel next? Into nearly every other mapwork calculation. Gradient needs the horizontal ground distance between two points — this conversion supplies it. Cross-sections need true distances along the section line. Area multiplies two of these converted distances together. Settlement and development questions dress it in context: how far must learners in the new extension walk to the clinic? Underneath each of them sits the same five-beat move you now own: measure, multiply by the scale, walk the ladder, state the unit, sense-check the size.

Make it physical tonight: take any topographic sheet, pick two trig beacons, a stretch of river, a farm dam. Measure, convert, then confirm against the linear scale bar at the bottom margin. When ruler, string and arithmetic all tell one story, these become the most dependable marks in your final exams.

The closing questions of this part are with you now — the hand-span check applied to a suspect answer, the four traps named and separated, and the downstream calculations that consume this distance as raw material.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same conversion again, told through a scale model, a marinade recipe and a piece of string — the version your hands already understand.

## Subtopic: The District, Shrunk Fifty Thousand Times

Stand on a koppie and look at the real thing for a second: the tar road, the river loop, the school roof flashing in the sun. A map is nothing more mysterious than that whole view built as a scale model and pressed flat — the entire district shrunk down evenly until it fits on one sheet of paper. Which means there is only one thing you truly need to know about any map: how hard it was shrunk. Know the shrink factor, and anything you measure on the paper can be blown back up to life size.

The number printed on the sheet is that factor. One to fifty thousand means the modelmaker shrank the district fifty thousand times. To go the other way — paper back to reality — multiply by 50 000. You are simply reversing the shrink.

Here is the quietly brilliant part: the factor works in any unit you choose. Think of a braai marinade written as one part sauce to five parts water. Mix it by the spoon for one chop or by the jug for the whole street — the recipe holds, because a ratio compares, it does not count. Map scale is the same kind of animal. Measure in centimetres, the ground answer arrives in centimetres; measure in millimetres, it arrives in millimetres. The ratio never quarrels with your ruler.

Now get the feel of the size. Fifty thousand centimetres is 500 metres — half a kilometre. One centimetre of paper: half a kilometre of ground. Two centimetres: a kilometre. Your standard 30-centimetre ruler, laid across the sheet, is spanning 15 kilometres of real South Africa.

The sheet announces its shrink factor in three ways, all in agreement. A sentence: one centimetre represents half a kilometre. A ratio: 1:50 000. And a printed bar along the bottom margin, divided into blocks with kilometre labels — the map world's pre-calculated till slip. Someone already did the multiplication for you, so you can walk a paper edge along a route, hold it against the bar, and read off kilometres without touching a calculator.

One last idea that feels wrong until it snaps into place. Think of pinching the screen on a maps app. Zoom right in and you can read one street's name — huge detail, tiny area. Zoom out and the whole province appears, but the streets vanish. The 1:10 000 orthophoto is the zoomed-in view: a larger scale, small patch, rich detail. The 1:50 000 sheet is zoomed out: smaller scale, wide country, less detail.

Take the questions for this section now: the shrink factor, the marinade rule that any unit works, the printed bar along the margin, and which sheet is the zoomed-in one. No rushing; your hands know this already.

## Subtopic: Multiply Once, Then Step Down Twice

Here comes the actual sum, and it is friendlier than its reputation, because you already run this exact arithmetic at the till.

We measured 7,4 centimetres between our two points. Un-shrink it: 7,4 times 50 000, in two easy bites — 7 times 50 000 is 350 000, and 0,4 times 50 000 is 20 000, so altogether 370 000. The ground distance is 370 000 centimetres. Absolutely correct, and absolutely useless in conversation. No taxi driver in history has quoted a trip in centimetres.

So we step the number down into human units. One hundred centimetres make a metre, the way one hundred cents make a rand — so 370 000 centimetres collapses into 3 700 metres, exactly as 370 000 cents collapses into three thousand seven hundred rand. Then the second step: one thousand metres make a kilometre, so 3 700 metres becomes 3,7 kilometres. And there it is — 3,7 kilometres, an answer a human can picture.

Write the unit down and say it as you write. A naked 3,7 is not an answer — 3,7 what? Kilometres. That one word is frequently worth a mark on its own, which makes it the cheapest word you will ever write.

This particular sheet also hides a lovely shortcut: since every centimetre is half a kilometre, just halve the centimetres. Half of 7,4 is 3,7. One move, same answer. Use it to check yourself — but present the full working in your answer, because the steps are what get paid, and halving works only on this one scale while the ladder works on every map on Earth.

That is the whole method, and it now has a name you can chant: multiply by the shrink factor, then step down the ladder — centimetres to metres to kilometres. Learn it once; it never changes again.

## Subtopic: Shoelaces, Zoomed-in Sheets and the Hand-Span Test

Three complications from real papers, and each one falls to something already in your pocket.

First: roads bend. A stiff ruler cannot follow the S-bends down a river valley, but a shoelace can. Press the lace — or the edge of a paper strip, pivoted bend by bend — along the whole wriggling route, pinch the end point, then pull it straight along your ruler. Say it reads 11,8 centimetres. The sum does not care how wiggly the road was: 11,8 times 50 000 is 590 000 centimetres, down the ladder to 5 900 metres, landing at 5,9 kilometres. New tool, same arithmetic.

Second: the sheet under your hand can change. On a 1:10 000 orthophoto, our same 7,4 centimetres un-shrinks by only 10 000: 74 000 centimetres, which is 740 metres — 0,74 of a kilometre. Same paper distance, one fifth of the ground, because the orthophoto is the zoomed-in sheet. Moral: glance at the printed scale before you reach for a multiplier, every time the page turns.

Third: the question sometimes runs backwards — here is the ground distance, find the map distance. Reverse the machine. 3,7 kilometres is 370 000 centimetres; divide by 50 000 and the points sit 7,4 centimetres apart on the sheet. Forwards multiply, backwards divide — and running your own answer backwards is the neatest self-check there is.

Now the free safety net. Spread your hand on the sheet: a hand-span is roughly 10 centimetres, which on a 1:50 000 map is about 5 kilometres — call it an hour's walk. Our 7,4 centimetres gave 3,7 kilometres: a bit under a hand-span, a bit under an hour's walk. The answer fits the hand. If the working had produced 37 kilometres, the hand-span test would smell the escaped decimal immediately — nobody strolls 37 kilometres before supper.

And that, genuinely, is the entire topic. Measure to the millimetre, never round before multiplying, use the scale printed on the sheet actually in front of you, finish in the unit that was asked, and give every answer the hand-span test.

Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the shoelace on a bending road, the zoomed-in orthophoto, the sum run backwards, and the hand-span test that catches a runaway decimal. Bank these, and gradient, cross-section and area are all standing on ground you have already secured.
