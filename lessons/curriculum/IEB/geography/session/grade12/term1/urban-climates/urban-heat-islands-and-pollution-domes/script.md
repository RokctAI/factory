# Part 1 — Expert

Every large city runs its own private climate. Pretoria's night air is measurably warmer than the bushveld an hour outside it, thunderstorms lean a little harder on the downwind edge of the metro, and on calm winter mornings a brown lid of dirty air hangs visibly over the skyline. This session works through urban climates in three movements: the element-by-element contrast between urban and rural air, with a mechanism for each difference; the urban heat island — definition, causes, effects and the strategies that shrink it; and the pollution dome and plume with the same four-part treatment. The command words that govern this topic are define, account for and suggest strategies, so every section is built to arm those three moves.

## Subtopic: Why City Air Differs from Farm Air

Set a city block beside a cattle farm an hour away and compare them element by element, attaching a mechanism to every difference — that is the standing format of this question.

Temperature: the city runs warmer, most dramatically at night — typically two to six degrees, occasionally more. The mechanisms fill the next section, because this difference carries its own famous name.

Surface materials: the farm wears soil, grass and crops — moist surfaces that reflect a reasonable share of sunlight. The city wears tar, concrete, brick and glass: dark, dry, low-albedo materials that reflect little, absorb most of the radiation striking them, bank that energy through the day and pay it back slowly through the night.

Moisture and humidity: rural ground breathes water. Soil evaporates and plants transpire, and both processes spend solar energy on converting water to vapour instead of on heating air. The city is waterproofed: rain hits roofs and roads, sprints down gutters into storm-water drains and leaves. With almost nothing left to evaporate, the sun's energy is spent almost entirely on heating, and urban relative humidity runs lower.

Wind: over open farmland the wind runs free and average speeds are higher. The city is an obstacle course of buildings that drags the general flow down — yet between tall buildings the remaining wind is squeezed and accelerated, so the city pairs an overall calmer, rougher airflow with gusty, funnelling street canyons. A corridor of towers can even manufacture its own draughts on an otherwise windless day.

Cloud, rain and fog: cities are slightly cloudier and wetter than their surroundings — a few percent more rain, concentrated over and just downwind of the built-up area. Two mechanisms drive it: heat-island convection lifts air over the warm city, and the city breathes out condensation nuclei — exhaust particles, smoke, dust — on which droplets form eagerly. Those same nuclei thicken urban fog, the historical smog of coal-burning towns.

Sunshine: the urban haze scatters and filters incoming radiation, so the city centre logs measurably fewer hours of direct sunshine than the farm — while still ending the day warmer.

Take this section's questions now: surface, moisture, wind, precipitation and sunshine, each difference paired with its mechanism, city against countryside.

## Subtopic: The Urban Heat Island — Definition and Causes

Definition first, ready to write: an urban heat island is the dome of warmer air standing over a city, in which temperatures run noticeably higher than in the surrounding rural areas, strongest at night and in winter, and peaking above the dense city centre. Now draw its temperature profile in words: the line rises gently from the rural fringe, climbs across the suburbs, runs high across the built core with small dips wherever it crosses a park or a stretch of open water, crests over the central business district, and slides down again on the far side. Those park dips are not decoration — they are the experimental proof that vegetation cools, and every reduction strategy leans on them.

The causes, one mark each, each with its mechanism.

Cause one: dark surfaces absorbing. Low-albedo tar and concrete soak up most incoming radiation, store it all day and release it all night — the city behaves like a bank that takes deposits in daylight and pays out after dark, which is precisely why the heat island is strongest at night.

Cause two: anthropogenic heat — warmth released by human activity itself. Engines, factories, air conditioners, heaters, stoves and several million warm human bodies leak heat into the urban air around the clock; in winter, with heating running hard, this source swells — one reason the island peaks in winter.

Cause three: the missing evaporation. Rain is piped away and vegetation is paved over, so almost no solar energy is spent evaporating water; virtually all of it heats surfaces and air. The rural energy budget buys evaporation; the urban budget buys temperature.

Cause four: geometry — the urban canyon. Vertical walls intercept radiation that open ground would bounce skyward, reflecting it wall to wall and down into the street; energy rattles around the canyon like a ball dropped into a stairwell, struggling to escape. At night, a street floor sees only a narrow ribbon of sky, so its stored heat has a tiny window through which to radiate away — streets cool far more slowly than fields.

Cause five: the pollution blanket. The haze dome absorbs part of the heat radiating upward from the city at night and returns a share of it downward — a miniature greenhouse layer fitted over the city alone.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the definition with its night and winter peaks, the profile with its park dips, and the five causes each tied to its mechanism.

## Subtopic: Heat Island Effects and How Cities Cool Themselves

Effects, sorted into groups an essay can use. Health and comfort: warm nights rob the body of its recovery window during a heat wave, so heat-stress illness and death climb — hardest among the elderly, infants and people who labour outdoors, and hardest of all in South African cities for families under corrugated-iron roofs, because a zinc sheet turns a dwelling into an oven that never cools. Energy and water: air-conditioning load surges on summer afternoons, stressing the national grid at its peak, while parks and gardens demand extra irrigation in the drier urban air. Weather: heat-island convection can sharpen afternoon thunderstorms over and downwind of the metro — part of the reason the Gauteng conurbation's storm and hail seasons bite so hard — and the same rising warm air props up the pollution dome. Ecology: city trees leaf earlier, pests that rural frost would kill survive the urban winter, and the seasons blur toward the core.

Reduction strategies — the suggest-strategies marks — organised so each strategy names the cause it attacks. Attack the albedo: light-coloured roofs and paving and reflective materials turn absorption back into reflection. Attack the lost vegetation: street trees, protected and expanded parks, green belts and planted roofs — vegetation cools twice, shading the surface and transpiring away energy that would have become heat, and the park dips in the profile are the evidence it works. Attack the sealed ground: permeable paving, retention ponds and urban wetlands hold water inside the city so evaporation can restart. Attack the anthropogenic leak: insulated, efficient buildings; public transport in place of one-per-car commuting; cleaner energy sources. Attack the geometry: space and orient new blocks to ventilate streets and keep the city's natural wind corridors open. South African municipalities supply ready examples — Tshwane's avenues of street trees, greening and tree-planting drives in Johannesburg's historically bare townships, coastal metros writing green corridors into their spatial plans — and naming one earns the local-example mark.

The questions on this section are with you now: effects grouped into health, energy, weather and ecology, and each strategy matched to the cause it undoes.

## Subtopic: The Pollution Dome and the Pollution Plume

Definitions, ready to write: a pollution dome is the dome-shaped mass of dirty air that collects over a city during calm, stable weather — the city's grey halo. When the wind picks up, the dome is dragged out downwind as a pollution plume — a long tongue of urban pollution smeared across the countryside. Dome and plume are one and the same pollution in two kinds of weather, calm and windy — a one-line distinction that regularly carries a mark.

Causes divide neatly: sources fill the dome, weather seals it. The sources: vehicle exhausts; industrial and power-station stacks; domestic coal and wood fires, especially through township winters at dawn and dusk; dust off construction sites and mine dumps; and seasonal veld fires. The seal: a temperature inversion. On a clear, calm winter night the ground cools fastest, leaving cold air lying beneath warmer air; a thread of rising smoke climbs only while it is warmer than its surroundings, meets the warm layer, loses its buoyancy and flattens out sideways as though pressed under glass. Winter's great anticyclone reinforces the seal from above: subsiding air lays a persistent regional lid over the interior plateau, which is why the smell of smoke owns winter mornings there and why zones such as the Vaal Triangle airshed have been declared national air-quality priority areas. Coastal industry writes the same story at sea level — the South Durban industrial basin, where refineries and factories sit pressed against residential streets, is the country's textbook pollution hotspot.

Effects: respiratory disease — asthma, bronchitis, chest infections — concentrated precisely where domestic burning meets the inversion, in the poorest communities, and that environmental-justice point deserves to be stated in plain words; smog and collapsed visibility; acid deposition downwind that eats building stone and acidifies soil and water; damaged crops and vegetation beneath the plume; and the dome doubling as a night blanket that feeds the heat island.

Reduction strategies split the same way the causes did. Cut the sources: extend electrification and affordable clean household energy so no family needs the evening coal fire; enforce scrubbers and filters on stacks through emission licensing under national air-quality law; tighten vehicle standards, clean the fuels, grow public transport; suppress dust on mine dumps. And work with the weather: ban open burning on stable winter days, issue air-quality alerts when inversions strengthen, and zone smokestack industry downwind of dense settlement rather than upwind of it. Monitoring stations in the priority areas and the South Durban basin now track the dome daily — citing either seals the local-example mark.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: dome against plume in one line, sources against the inversion seal, effects with the justice point, and strategies sorted into source-cutting and weather-wise.

## Subtopic: The City Is a Storage Heater

Why is town warmer than the farm? You have felt the answer with your own hand. Touch a face-brick wall an hour after sunset: still warm, like a loaf out of the oven. Touch a fence post on the farm at the same hour: cold. Cities are built almost entirely out of the warm-wall kind of stuff.

Think of an old storage heater — the kind that quietly charges up all day and then glows warm all evening. A city is a storage heater the size of a municipality. From sunrise to sunset the dark tar, brick and concrete drink radiation; from sunset to sunrise they breathe it back into the streets. A farm cannot behave this way, because a farm spends its sunshine differently: the ground is moist and the plants sweat. Every litre a field transpires is solar energy used up on evaporation instead of on heating the air — the same trick your skin plays when sweat dries and you feel cool. The city, though, cannot sweat. Its rain is rushed off roofs, down gutters, into drains and gone, leaving nothing to evaporate — so the sun's whole allowance is spent on heat.

Now add three smaller burners. First, the city's own machinery: every engine, stove, heater, air conditioner and warm human body leaks heat, millions of small radiators running around the clock. Second, the canyons: tall buildings bounce sunshine wall to wall and down to street level, and the energy rattles around like a ball dropped into a stairwell, finding no way out; at night the street can only cool through the narrow slot of sky between the rooftops. Third, the haze: the city's dirty air lies over it like a thin blanket, catching outgoing heat and handing part of it back.

Stack all of that and the city sits inside a bubble of warm air — warmest over the city centre, strongest at night and in winter. Its name: the URBAN HEAT ISLAND, an island of warmth in a sea of cooler countryside. And keep one detail loaded for the graph question: wherever the temperature line crosses a big park, it dips. Green patches stay cool. That dip is about to become an answer.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the storage heater, why the city cannot sweat, the stairwell canyons and the blanket, and the island with its park dips.

## Subtopic: Cooling the Island — Undo Each Cause

How do you cool a whole city? Use the cleanest trick in the topic: the causes were a list of problems, and the strategies are the same list run backwards. Take each problem and reverse it.

Problem one: dark surfaces banking sunshine. Reverse it: lighten the city. Pale roofs, light paving, reflective finishes. It is the parking-bay principle — everyone knows which car you can touch after a January afternoon: the white one, not the black one. Pale surfaces bounce energy away instead of banking it.

Problem two: the city that cannot sweat. Reverse it: reinstall the sweat glands. Street trees, protected parks, rooftop gardens. A tree cools twice — its canopy shades the tar, and its leaves transpire, spending sunshine on evaporating water rather than heating air. This is exactly why the temperature graph dips over parks: every park is a patch of working sweat glands, and Pretoria's jacaranda-lined avenues are the living local example. When the strategy question comes, trees and parks are the first answer, and the park dip is your proof.

Problem three: the sealed ground. Reverse it: let the water stay. Permeable paving that rain can soak through, ponds and wetlands folded into the parks — water kept in the city restarts evaporation, and evaporation is cooling.

Problem four: the million small heaters. Reverse it: leak less. Insulate buildings so heating and cooling work less; fill the taxi, bus or train instead of driving alone; switch to cleaner, more efficient energy.

Problem five: the stairwell canyon. Reverse it: build for breeze. Angle and space new towers so wind can rinse the streets, and never wall off the corridor the city's prevailing wind uses.

Then crown the essay with the why-it-matters sentence: hot city nights are not merely uncomfortable — in a heat wave they kill, and they choose their victims: the elderly, infants, outdoor workers, and families under zinc roofs in informal settlements, all while the summer grid strains at its peak. Cooling a city is health policy, justice policy and energy policy rolled into one.

Take the questions for this section now: each problem paired with its reversal, the double cooling of a tree, and who the heat hurts first.

## Subtopic: The Pot Lid of Smoke

The last piece is the city's grey halo. Every settlement makes smoke — exhaust pipes, factory stacks, and through the township winter, the evening coal stoves and imbawula fires that keep families warm. On a windy day the smoke is carried off and forgotten. But on a still, icy winter night, something closes over the city like a lid on a pot.

That lid is the temperature inversion, an old acquaintance from the valley and plateau lessons. On a clear calm night the ground sheds heat fast, so the coldest air ends up lying on the floor with warmer air resting above it. Follow a single thread of chimney smoke upward: it climbs while it is warmer than the air around it, reaches the warm layer, is suddenly warmer than nothing, and stops dead — spreading sideways as if pressed under a pane of glass. Multiply that by every chimney, brazier and exhaust in the metro and the city's smoke pools under the lid in a grey-brown dome: the POLLUTION DOME. Seen from the freeway at dawn in July, it is the brown smear lying across the skyline. When the wind returns, the dome is dragged out into a long dirty streamer across the countryside: the POLLUTION PLUME. One smoke, two weathers — calm builds the dome, wind draws the plume.

Who lives inside the dome? Mostly the people least able to escape it. The dome sits thickest exactly where households must burn coal and wood for warmth, and there the winter clinics fill with wheezing chests — asthma, bronchitis, small children first. In industrial pockets like the South Durban basin, factory fence lines and front doors share the same street, and the same lungs pay. Downwind, the plume delivers acid to crops, dams and buildings.

The lid itself is beyond our reach — winter's great anticyclone lays it on. So every strategy makes less smoke or times it better. Electrify and subsidise clean energy so no family needs the evening fire. Filter and scrub the stacks, with the air-quality law and its priority areas holding industry to it. Cleaner fuels, fuller vehicles, working public transport. No open burning on still winter days, when the lid is down. And draw the town plan wisely: heavy industry belongs downwind of the houses, never upwind of the townships.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: how the lid stops the smoke, dome against plume in one line, who the dome hurts first, and the strategies that shrink it. Close these out and the whole city-climate story is yours to keep.
