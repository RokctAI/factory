# Part 1 — Expert

The tropical cyclone is the most powerful storm the atmosphere can build, and for southern Africa it is a neighbour, not a headline from far away: the systems that spin up in the warm ocean east of Madagascar strike Mozambique, Madagascar and Malawi in most seasons, and their rain reaches into Zimbabwe and the South African Lowveld. This session assembles the whole system: the birth conditions and the regional names, the staged growth from a nameless cluster of thunderstorms to a named giant, the anatomy of eye, eye wall and rain bands with the exact weather each part delivers, and finally a southern African case study, the satellite identification and the management strategies that complete any answer. Underneath every fact, keep one idea running: a tropical cyclone is an engine that converts warm ocean water into wind, and everything about its life — where it is born, how it strengthens, why it dies — is the story of that fuel supply.

## Subtopic: Birth Conditions, Regions and Names

Tropical cyclones develop over warm oceans between about five and thirty degrees of latitude on either side of the equator — but never on the equator itself. Four conditions must be satisfied, and each one is worth stating with its reason.

Condition one: a sea surface of at least twenty-six and a half to twenty-seven degrees Celsius, with the warmth extending roughly fifty metres down. This is the fuel tank. Vigorous evaporation charges the air with water vapour, and when that vapour later condenses it hands back the energy it absorbed — latent heat, the power stroke of the engine.

Condition two: a position at least five degrees of latitude from the equator. On the equator the Coriolis force is zero, and without Coriolis deflection the air converging on a low simply flows straight in and fills it — nothing spins. That is why the band along the equator produces no cyclones and why no cyclone has ever crossed from one hemisphere into the other.

Condition three: converging surface winds, usually inside the easterly trade-wind belt, to begin the inward and upward flow — typically organising first as a loose cluster of thunderstorms called a tropical disturbance.

Condition four: weak wind shear — upper winds that differ little from the surface winds. Strong winds aloft slice the tops off the growing thunderstorm towers and scatter the released heat; quiet upper air lets the chimney stack straight and tall.

One machine, several passports, and the vocabulary is tested: hurricane in the North Atlantic and eastern Pacific, typhoon in the north-western Pacific, cyclone in the Indian Ocean and Australian waters. The storms that concern southern Africa are born in the south-west Indian Ocean, east of Madagascar, during the warm half of the year from November to April. Each season's storms take names alphabetically from a prepared list, so a storm beginning with A opened the season and one beginning with I came months later. In our hemisphere they rotate clockwise around a ferociously deep low, drifting east to west with the trade winds before curving away poleward.

## Subtopic: Stages of Growth and the Anatomy of the Storm

Growth is graded by sustained wind speed, through four stages. Stage one: the tropical disturbance — a disorganised cluster of thunderstorms loitering over hot water. Stage two: the tropical depression — the cluster acquires a closed circulation around a weak low, with winds still below about sixty-three kilometres per hour. Stage three: the tropical storm — winds between sixty-three and one hundred and eighteen kilometres per hour, and at this threshold the system is given its name. Stage four: the full tropical cyclone — sustained winds beyond one hundred and eighteen kilometres per hour and, in a mature system, the appearance of the eye. Above that, intensity categories climb with wind speed toward the devastating category five.

Now the anatomy, assembled from the centre outward, because the cross-section is a classic diagram question. Dead centre lies the eye: a calm core twenty to fifty kilometres across in which air is sinking rather than rising. Subsiding air warms and dries, so the eye stands clear, light-winded, sometimes blue-skied — the storm's cruellest trick, because it tempts people out of shelter halfway through.

Hard against the eye stands the eye wall: a closed ring of the tallest cumulonimbus on Earth, and the single most violent place in the storm. All the air that has spiralled inward over hundreds of kilometres finally turns upward here in a roaring sleeve; the eye wall holds the fastest winds — often beyond two hundred kilometres per hour — and the fiercest rain.

Outward from the wall, the spiral rain bands trail for hundreds of kilometres like the arms of a galaxy: curved belts of thunderstorm cloud separated by quieter corridors, so a place in the storm's path receives its rain in pulses that grow heavier and closer together as the core approaches.

Now run the engine through that structure. The warm sea evaporates; moist air spirals inward clockwise, accelerating; in the eye wall it rushes upward, condenses, and releases latent heat; the released heat lowers the central pressure further; the deeper low pulls the spiral harder. It is a loop that feeds itself. Spent air fans out at the summit, and a fraction sinks down the centre to make the calm eye. Central pressures can drop below nine hundred and sixty hectopascals — deeper than anything a mid-latitude chart of South Africa ever shows.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the four stages with their wind-speed boundaries, eye, eye wall and rain bands each tied to their weather, and the latent-heat loop that powers the machine.

## Subtopic: The Weather Sequence and Why the Storm Dies

Picture yourself in a low-lying coastal town — Quelimane on the Mozambique coast will do — with a cyclone bearing down. The sequence is best learned as a timeline you could narrate.

First, the warning day: long ocean swells arriving on a calm shore from a storm still hundreds of kilometres out; high thin cloud filming over and thickening; the barometer starting a slow slide; the wind freshening in pulses as the outermost rain bands brush past — squall, lull, harder squall.

Then the front half: the bands arrive faster and meaner, the wind climbs into destructive strength, rain turns horizontal and blinding — and the ocean itself rises. This is the storm surge: a dome of sea lifted by the extreme low pressure and bulldozed ashore by the wind. Across the history of these storms the surge, not the wind, has been the greatest killer, laying metres of seawater over flat coastal plains.

Then the eye: within the space of an hour, near-silence and open sky, with the pressure at its lowest. Stepping outside now is the storm's fatal invitation — the eye takes only twenty minutes to an hour to pass, and then the far eye wall lands with full violence and the wind blowing from the opposite direction, because the town is now on the other side of a clockwise-spinning wheel. Roofs and walls loosened by the first half are stripped by the reversed second half.

Then the back half and the long tail: the mirrored retreat of eye wall and bands, pressure recovering, seas easing over a day or two — while inland the rivers are only beginning to rise as the deluge drains off the catchments.

And why does the giant die? Starve the engine, three ways. Landfall is the classic execution: no ocean beneath it, no evaporation, plus surface friction grinding down the winds — though a dying storm still carries rain that can drown highlands far from the sea. Tracking poleward over water cooler than the threshold drains the tank just as surely. And strong wind shear aloft can dismember even a well-fuelled storm over a warm sea.

The questions on this section are with you now: the timeline from first swell to second eye wall, the storm surge and its death toll, the false calm with its wind reversal, and the three ways the engine starves.

## Subtopic: Case Study, Satellite Identification and Management

You need one southern African case study in full, and Cyclone Idai of March twenty nineteen remains the region's defining example. Idai began as a tropical depression that crossed into Mozambique and Malawi, soaking both — then reversed course, drifted back out over the warm Mozambique Channel, refuelled over that hot water, and intensified into a full tropical cyclone. It struck the coast at the port city of Beira, where the eye wall and a storm surge of several metres levelled much of the city — aid workers reported that ninety percent of Beira was damaged or destroyed. The storm then stalled inland and released weeks' worth of rain in days: rivers burst across central Mozambique, and in the mountains of eastern Zimbabwe, around Chimanimani, mudslides tore down slopes onto villages. More than a thousand people died across Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Malawi; hundreds of thousands were displaced; cholera followed the floodwater through wrecked sanitation; crops, roads, power and clinics were destroyed in one of the world's most vulnerable regions. Idai's return loop over the Channel is the perfect illustration of the fuel principle — back over warm water, a dying system restarted — and its inland toll shows that the rain can out-kill the wind. If your class prepared a different recent storm, the analytical skeleton is unchanged: name, date, track, impacts on people and environment, responses.

Identification on imagery and charts: a tropical cyclone on a satellite image is a dense, nearly circular disc of brilliant white cloud with spiral arms and, at maturity, a dark central dot — the eye. One look separates it from the mid-latitude system: tight circle with an eye, low latitudes, warm ocean — tropical; open comma with trailing fronts, poleward of thirty degrees — mid-latitude. On the synoptic chart, a tropical cyclone shows as tight rings of circular isobars around a named, intense low east of Madagascar or inside the Mozambique Channel, approaching the region from the north-east.

Management closes the answer, in three time frames. Before: early warnings and forecast tracks from the regional centres, cyclone-resistant building standards, mangrove belts conserved as living surge barriers, rehearsed evacuation routes to high ground, stocked emergency kits. During: shelter on high ground, stay inside through the eye, follow official broadcasts. After: safe drinking water first — cholera arrives with the floodwater — then clinics, power, replanting, and rebuilding to stronger codes. And add the sentence that shows real understanding: the hazard is natural, but the disaster is partly social — floodplain settlement, deforested slopes and weak warning systems turned Idai's wind and rain into a catastrophe that a wealthier, better-prepared coast might have withstood.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: Idai's track, refuelling loop and impacts, the one-look satellite distinction, and two strategies each for before, during and after.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same monster again, rebuilt from a boiling pot, a spinning skater and the most dangerous quiet moment in weather — the pictures that hold the facts still when the exam room goes loud.

## Subtopic: The Pot, the Spin and the Birth Rules

Begin at the stove. A pot of water boils, and steam climbs off it. The energy in that steam came up through the water from the plate below. Now blow the picture up to planet size: the tropical ocean at the end of summer is the pot, warmed for months until its top fifty metres sits above twenty-six and a half degrees, and the vapour streaming off it is the steam. Here is the one line of physics that unlocks the whole topic: when vapour condenses back into droplets, it repays every joule of heat it borrowed to evaporate. That repayment is latent heat, and latent heat is the cyclone's fuel. No hot ocean, no fuel, no storm. Hold that sentence; most of the lesson hangs from it.

But steam alone is not a storm — it needs spin. Watch a figure skater wind into a pirouette: arms wide, she turns lazily; arms pulled in, she blurs. Air racing inward toward a deep low does exactly that — but only if something first bends its path into a curve. The bender is the turning Earth itself, the Coriolis effect, and it has one dead spot: the equator, where its strength is zero. So the strip along the equator is a closed dance floor — no cyclone is born there, and none ever crosses it.

Two smaller rules complete the birth certificate. The winds high above must be gentle: strong upper winds knock the growing cloud tower sideways like a hand swiping a half-built tower of blocks. And the seed is always ordinary — a cluster of thunderstorms that happens to idle over the hot water long enough to organise itself.

Same creature, three passports: hurricane over the Americas' oceans, typhoon over Asia's, cyclone over ours. Our nursery is the Indian Ocean east of Madagascar, in our summer, November to April, with each season's storms named alphabetically down a prepared list — the A storm in early season, the I storm months deeper in.

## Subtopic: The Doughnut With the Hole in the Middle

Now shape the grown storm into something you can hold: an enormous doughnut of cloud, hundreds of kilometres across, with an eerie hole in the middle.

The hole is the eye. Air inside it is drifting gently downward, and sinking air dissolves cloud — so the eye is clear, nearly windless, occasionally even sunny: twenty to fifty kilometres of impossible peace at the centre of the most violent object in the atmosphere.

The ring of dough pressed around the hole is the eye wall — the worst address on the planet. Every stream of air that has spiralled inward across the sea makes its final turn here, straight up, in one howling sleeve of cloud. The fastest winds, the thickest rain and the tallest thunderheads all live in that ring.

Off the doughnut trail long curved arms — the rain bands — sweeping across the ocean like the arms of a slow-turning galaxy. A town in the path feels them as a rhythm: squall, breather, harder squall, shorter breather, each arm heavier than the last as the centre closes in.

Now march the shape over a seaside town and the famous sequence writes itself. The sea turns angry before the sky does — big smooth swells from a storm still far over the horizon. Then the arms, pulsing. Then the eye wall — the hours that test every roof. And then, without warning: stillness. Sunlight, even. Burn this into memory: that stillness is the hole in the doughnut, not the end of the storm. Within the hour the far side of the ring arrives at full fury with the wind blowing the opposite way — so whatever survived by leaning against the first wind gets hit from behind by the second.

One more monster swims alongside: the storm surge. Think of drinking from a straw — lower the pressure above the liquid and the liquid climbs. The cyclone's extreme low pressure lifts the sea surface the same way, and the wind then shovels that raised water onto the land: metres of ocean walking inland across a flat coast. The surge drowns more people than the wind kills. Low harbour cities fear the water, not the gale.

Take the questions for this section now: hole, ring and arms matched to their weather, the trap of the quiet middle, and the raised sea that does the real killing.

## Subtopic: Idai, the Fuel Gauge and Being Ready

Carry one storm into the exam as your story: Idai, March twenty nineteen. Idai started life as a mere depression that wandered over Mozambique and Malawi, dumping rain. Then it did the thing that makes it the perfect teaching storm: it drifted back out over the Mozambique Channel — back over the hot pot — refuelled on the warm water, spun up into a full cyclone, and drove into the port city of Beira. The eye wall and a surge of several metres wrecked most of the city in a night. Then the storm crawled inland and emptied itself: rivers spread across the plains of central Mozambique, and in Zimbabwe's Chimanimani mountains, hillsides stripped of trees let go and buried villages in mud. More than a thousand people died across three countries; cholera stalked the flooded settlements afterwards. Learn Idai as a fuel-gauge story: over warm water the needle climbs and the storm strengthens; over land or cool water the needle falls and the storm fades. Idai found a filling station on its doorstep — that is the entire physics of the topic told as one track on a map.

How do you recognise the monster from space? A tight white pinwheel with a dark dot at its centre — the eye. Its southern cousin from the Cape lessons looks nothing like it: an open comma with a long trailing tail, no eye, far further south. Pinwheel with a dot: tropical. Comma with a tail: mid-latitude. That single comparison line earns its marks almost every time the two systems share a question.

And because satellites see these storms days out, no one needs to be caught unprepared — which is where management collects its marks. Before: heed the warnings and the forecast track, know the route to high ground, keep an emergency kit, and guard the mangrove forests that tame the surge the way a windbreak tames a gale. During: stay sheltered, and never step out into the quiet middle — wait out the second half. After: clean water first, because cholera kills long after the wind has gone; then clinics, power, crops, and rebuilding stronger than before. Close with the sentence that turns a good answer into a complete one: the storm is nature's, but the size of the disaster is partly ours — floodplain shacks, bare hillsides and broken warning chains turned Idai into a tragedy that preparation could have shrunk.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: Idai's double life and what refuelled it, the pinwheel against the comma, and the before-during-after list with the golden rule about the quiet middle. Take them — in this topic, preparation saves lives as well as marks.
