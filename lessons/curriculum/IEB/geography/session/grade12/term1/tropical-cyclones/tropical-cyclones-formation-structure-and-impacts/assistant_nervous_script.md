Hi, Mandy here — breathe out. Tropical cyclones look like the scariest topic in climatology, but they are secretly the most learnable, because the whole thing is one fuel story told four times.

The birth question: warm ocean above twenty-six and a half degrees is the fuel, the spinning Earth is the ignition, and the equator is the one place the ignition never fires. The structure question: a doughnut — calm hole in the middle, furious ring around it, long spiral arms trailing off. The weather question: the doughnut marching over a town — swells, pulsing squalls, the ring, the deadly quiet hole, then the ring again from the other direction. The case-study question: Idai, the storm that drifted back over the warm Mozambique Channel, refuelled, and hit Beira — the fuel gauge drawn on a map.

Even the identification is friendly: pinwheel with a dot means tropical; comma with a tail means mid-latitude. One line, almost every year.

Tonight, do two small things. Draw the doughnut once and label hole, ring and arms with their weather. Then write Idai's track in four moves: inland, back to the Channel, Beira, the mountains. Fifteen minutes and the topic is caged.

You have watched these storms on the news since you were small. Now you know their engine. Go show the practice questions who understands the machine.
