# Part 1 — Expert

Between the farm gate and the shop counter stands the factory, and this topic gives it a full toolkit of ideas: what the secondary sector actually contributes, how to identify each industry type from its clues, the factors that decide where a factory settles, and the factors that hold industrial growth back. Every definition built here becomes a key for the next lesson, where South Africa's four industrial regions get unlocked one by one. The thread running through the whole session: why a fish cannery, a soft-drink bottler, a ferrochrome smelter and a software studio all answer the address question differently.

## Subtopic: What the Secondary Sector Contributes

The secondary sector — manufacturing, construction and electricity — earns roughly a fifth of South Africa's GDP, with manufacturing alone around twelve to fourteen per cent, and it employs about one worker in ten. But the percentages understate the sector, and four arguments explain why it punches above its share.

Argument one: value added. Manufacturing multiplies the worth of what the primary sector produces — chrome ore into ferrochrome, hides into shoes, deciduous fruit into canned peaches and juice. A country that ships its materials out raw and buys the finished goods back has sold cheap and bought dear; manufacturing keeps that difference inside the borders. This is the logic behind beneficiation — the standing policy of processing minerals at home before they leave.

Argument two: the quality of the jobs and their knock-on effect. Factory work is formal, year-round and skilled or semi-skilled, and every factory job props up others — the packaging supplier, the freight company, the canteen, the corner shops where wages get spent. Manufacturing's employment multiplier reaches further than any raw-material industry's.

Argument three: linkages. Manufacturing buys from farms, forests and mines — backward linkages — and hands the tertiary sector goods to move, sell and finance — forward linkages. It stands mid-river in the economy; strengthen the bridge and both banks feel it.

Argument four: export diversification. Manufactured exports, led by the vehicle plants at Rosslyn, East London, Durban and Kariega, spread the country's export risk so that no single commodity price can sink it. The vehicle industry, carefully nurtured by industrial policy for decades, is the showcase: South African-assembled vehicles ship to dozens of countries.

## Subtopic: The Types of Industries: A Field Guide

Questions in this topic hand you a clue — a product, a raw material, a habit of location — and ask for the industry type. Learn the guide as contrasting pairs.

Heavy versus light. Heavy industry moves enormous tonnages with enormous plant: the steelworks at Vanderbijlpark, the fuel-from-coal complexes at Sasolburg and Secunda, cement works, shipyards. It demands flat land, rail sidings, bulk water and serious power, and it keeps its distance from suburbs. Light industry makes small, high-value-for-their-weight goods — clothing, electronics, processed foods, furniture — from ordinary factory units in industrial parks along main roads, close to workers and buyers.

Raw-material-orientated versus market-orientated — the pair the transport-cost logic sorts. When the raw material is heavy, bulky or quick to spoil and SHRINKS in processing, the plant moves to the material: better to truck the light product than the heavy input. Fish canneries stand at St Helena Bay because fresh fish spoils within hours and a catch reduces to a fraction of its weight in cans; sawmills stand inside the Mpumalanga plantations; fruit canneries stand among the Ceres orchards. When the product is the difficult traveller — heavier, more fragile, more perishable than its inputs — the plant moves to the buyers. Soft-drink bottlers and bakeries sit in every city, because syrup concentrate and flour travel cheaply while crates of full glass bottles and fresh bread do not. The one-line test: ask which end of the process costs more to move, input or output — the factory always parks at the expensive end.

Footloose industry is anchored to neither end: its inputs and outputs are light, high-value or purely digital — software, electronics assembly, medical-device design. Such firms choose addresses for skilled people, connectivity and lifestyle, which is why they gather in metropolitan office parks rather than beside quarries. Ubiquitous industry appears wherever people live, because everyone buys the product and it travels badly — bakeries, dairies, brickworks; no sizeable town lacks its own.

Bridge industry is the name for break-of-bulk industry: factories that settle where cargo changes transport mode, working imports over as they land or exports before they ship. The vehicle plants beside the East London harbour are the picture to hold — components arrive by sea, assembled vehicles leave by sea, and the factory camps at the point where ship becomes train and truck.

## Subtopic: What Decides Where Industry Goes

Choosing an industrial site is a weighing exercise, and the syllabus list gives eight weights. Learn each with its question and a South African face.

Raw materials: how heavy, how far, how perishable? The cannery goes to the fish; the sawmill to the plantation. Labour: enough hands, the right skills, at survivable cost? Clothing factories historically followed abundant semi-skilled labour; design houses and tech firms chase scarce skills in the metros. Water: heavy industry drinks like a river — steel, pulp and paper, power generation — which is why the paper mills stand on KwaZulu-Natal's well-watered rivers and why the Vaal system, topped up by transfer schemes, waters Gauteng's industry. Energy: the ferrochrome smelters clustered near the Mpumalanga coalfields sit there because smelting devours electricity — a smelter locates on power the way a mill locates on grain. Transport: harbours, railways and highways collect factories; the corridor from Durban's port up to Gauteng is the country's industrial spine. Markets: market-orientated industry follows buying power, and Gauteng's dense, wealthy market is the strongest magnet in the country.

Then the two human weights. Political intervention: governments steer factories — the apartheid state once pushed industry to remote border areas for political reasons, and today's Special Economic Zones and Industrial Development Zones pull investment toward chosen sites with tax incentives and ready-built infrastructure, while policy certainty and functioning municipalities sit in the same pan of the scale. Competition and trade: a firm weighs who already holds the market, what tariffs protect or expose it, and which trade doors stand open — the African Continental Free Trade Area opens the continent's markets, while floods of cheap imported clothing have hollowed out local textile towns.

The favourite question shape is the scenario: a proposed factory, and you must weigh its location. Run the list — materials, labour, water, energy, transport, market, politics, competition — then name the two or three factors that dominate for THAT industry and say why. The weighing, never the mere list, earns the top marks.

## Subtopic: What Holds Industry Back

The factors hindering industrial development look like a list question but mark like an evaluation question, and South Africa's list is painfully concrete.

Electricity insecurity leads: load-shedding stops production mid-shift, ruins continuous processes, and teaches investors to price South African plants as risky. Infrastructure decay follows: failing rail freight forces cargo onto expensive roads, the big ports queue ships for days, and water cuts now reach industrial towns. Skills shortages: too few artisans, technicians and engineers leave the education pipeline, while emigration drains the experienced. Labour relations: long strikes and adversarial bargaining raise costs and uncertainty. Crime and corruption: stolen cable, extortion at building sites and rigged procurement all tax industry without appearing on any invoice. Global competition: cheap imported manufactures undercut local factories, clothing and textiles worst of all. Distance adds freight cost to everything South Africa sells across oceans. And the syllabus names two structural problems with their own logic: over-concentration — industry piled into four core regions, so congestion, land prices and dirty air climb there while the rest of the country waits for investment — and industrial pollution itself: the smoke of the Vaal Triangle, refinery fumes drifting over homes in south Durban, and a carbon-heavy production profile that green-minded trading partners increasingly tax at their borders.

Balance the account before closing, because evaluation weighs both pans: against the hindrances stand real strengths — the most diversified industrial base on the continent, deep banks and capital markets, strong universities, and trade access into Africa and beyond. A question asking you to account for slow industrial growth wants the hindrance list; a question asking you to evaluate South Africa as an industrial location wants both sides of the scale.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the hindrance list from load-shedding to pollution, over-concentration in one sentence, and the balanced evaluation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same toolkit through three things you already know: an ice-cream cone, a chip shop, and an honest list of why running a factory here is hard.

## Subtopic: Why the Factory Matters: The Ice-Cream Rule

Here is the whole secondary sector in one ice-cream cone. A dairy farmer sells a litre of milk for about seven rand. A factory churns that milk into a tub of ice cream and sells the tub for seventy. A beach kiosk scoops the tub into cones at twenty rand a scoop — well over two hundred rand a tub by the time the last scoop goes. Same milk, three prices — and the jumps between the prices are where wealth is actually created. The middle jump, raw thing into made thing, is manufacturing, and the money it conjures is called value added.

Now zoom out to a country. Sell your milk raw across the border and buy back imported ice cream, and you have hired another country's factory workers with your own money. Churn it yourself, and the factory wages, the tub-maker's contract, the freezer-truck jobs and the profit all stay home. That is the whole meaning of beneficiation — a grand word for a simple vow: stop exporting raw rocks and raw produce; do the value-adding here.

And a factory is a job machine with hidden gears. One ice-cream plant keeps the dairy farms selling, the packaging factory printing tubs, the cold-chain truckers driving, and the shops near the gate busy with workers' wages. Economists call those knock-on jobs multipliers; just picture gears — turn the factory gear and a dozen others turn with it. The showcase to name in an exam: the vehicle plants at Rosslyn, East London, Durban and Kariega, with a whole country of parts suppliers geared to their lines.

## Subtopic: The Chip-Shop Test: Where Does a Business Sit?

Every industry type in the syllabus answers one everyday question: what is more of a pain to move — what the business USES, or what it SELLS? Call it the chip-shop test.

The chip shop stands at the taxi rank, not at the potato farm, because hot chips die in twenty minutes and hungry commuters will not travel for them. It is market-orientated: parked beside its buyers. Soft-drink bottlers and bakeries pass the same test at city scale — concentrate and flour travel happily; crates of heavy full bottles and fresh bread do not, so the bottling lines and ovens sit in the cities that drink and eat them. Now flip it. A fish cannery stands at St Helena Bay on the West Coast because fresh fish is a fast-spoiling, weight-losing raw material that shrinks into a light, long-lasting can — you ship the tin, never the fish. That is raw-material-orientated: parked beside its ingredients. One question — which end is heavier, bulkier or quicker to rot — and the factory always sits at the difficult end.

Some businesses shrug at the question entirely. A software studio's raw material and product both move down a cable, so it settles wherever its skilled people want to live — footloose, tied to neither end. Some businesses are simply everywhere, because everyone buys them fresh and they travel badly — every town has its bakery and its brickworks — ubiquitous, like school shoes. And some businesses camp at the harbour gates, where ship-cargo becomes truck-cargo: the vehicle plants beside East London's harbour take components off the ships and send finished cars back onto them — bridge industries, working right at the break-of-bulk point.

Size sorts the final pair. Heavy industry is the giant — the steelworks, the fuel-from-coal plants — needing flat land, bulk water, its own rail siding and serious electricity, kept far from front doors. Light industry is the quiet neighbour — clothing, furniture, food — renting a unit in the industrial park beside the highway. Meet any industry in a question and ask five things: heavy or light, which end does it park at, or is it footloose, everywhere, or at the bridge? Five questions, and every type gives up its name.

## Subtopic: Choosing an Address, and What Makes It Hard

Placing a new factory is choosing where to live — a checklist you weigh, never just recite. Are the ingredients close, or cheap to bring in? Are there workers with the right hands and heads, at wages the business can carry? Is there bulk water and dependable electricity — remember that a smelter is essentially electricity made solid, which is why the ferrochrome furnaces crowd the coalfields where power is generated. Can trucks, trains and ships reach the gate — highway, railway, port? Are the customers nearby — because for bread and bottled drinks, the market IS the address? Then the human questions: is the state dangling tax breaks and ready-made infrastructure in a special zone, inviting you in — and who is already selling what you plan to sell, or dumping it cheaper from abroad? For any real factory, two or three of these questions outweigh all the rest — name which, and why, and you are doing exactly what evaluating location factors means.

Now the honest half: why is running a factory in South Africa hard? The list writes itself from the news. The power goes — a line stopped mid-shift can lose the whole batch. The trains limp — so freight rides expensive trucks and the ports back up. Skills are short — the pipeline produces too few artisans and engineers. Strikes stop the floor. Cables vanish in the night. Cheap imports undercut the local clothing factory before its doors open. And the two big structural ones the syllabus names: over-concentration — nearly everything squeezes into four regions, choking them with traffic, land costs and smog while the rest of the country goes without investment — and pollution itself, the Vaal Triangle's brown haze and the refinery air of south Durban, which sickens neighbours and now costs money at climate-minded borders as well.

Finish on the balance, because fairness is a marking skill: against all of that stands the most complete industrial machine in Africa — factories, banks, universities and open trade doors no neighbour can match. Weigh both hands: what drags industry down, and what still holds it up.
