# Part 1 — Expert

South African industry packs itself into four core regions, and this session studies two of them in depth: the Gauteng region — historically the Pretoria–Witwatersrand–Vereeniging area, the PWV — and the Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage region on Algoa Bay, whose cities today carry the names Gqeberha and Kariega. We map all four regions first, then work the two chosen case studies through a standard grid — location factors, main activities, what favours and what hinders them, and their economic and social impacts — and we close with the policies designed to push industry beyond the four: Spatial Development Initiatives with their corridors, and Industrial Development Zones inside the present Special Economic Zone framework, with Coega as the flagship.

## Subtopic: The Four Core Regions and the Chosen Two

Name the four with their anchors. First, Gauteng — the PWV: the inland colossus running from Pretoria across the Witwatersrand down to Vereeniging and the Vaal, carrying by far the largest slice of national manufacturing. Second, Durban–Pinetown in KwaZulu-Natal: industry wrapped around the busiest harbour in the southern hemisphere's Africa. Third, Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage on Algoa Bay: the coastal home of the motor industry. Fourth, the South-Western Cape around Cape Town: a diversified spread of consumer industries — food and wine, clothing, printing and publishing — feeding a comfortable regional market, with a port and a tourism economy alongside.

See the pattern before the detail: one region stands inland, made by minerals; three stand on the coast, made by harbours. That single organising sentence — minerals built Gauteng, harbours built the rest — arranges every comparison you will ever be asked to draw. And see what the packing means: these four regions hold most of the country's manufacturing between them, which is exactly the over-concentration problem the previous lesson defined, and the entire reason the SDI and zone policies at the end of this session were invented.

For each case-study region, carry a four-row grid, and hear every question as one of its rows: WHY there — the location factors; WHAT happens there — the main activities; HOW it fares — the factors favouring and the factors hindering; and WHO feels it — the economic and social impacts, both positive and negative. Four rows, two regions: that matrix is the entire topic.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the four regions with their anchors, the minerals-versus-harbours sentence, and the four rows of the case-study grid.

## Subtopic: Gauteng — the PWV: South Africa's Industrial Heart

Row one, why there. Gauteng's location factors read like the economy's birth certificate. Gold along the Witwatersrand pulled capital, workers and railways up onto the highveld. Coal from the nearby Mpumalanga and Vaal fields supplied cheap power. Water — the region's one missing ingredient — was piped in by engineering: first the Vaal Dam, later the Lesotho Highlands scheme carrying mountain water across a border to the plateau. The rail network laid for the mines turned the region into the national transport hub, now topped by the air-freight capacity at OR Tambo. The mines banked generations of skills and engineering know-how. And the region's own population grew into the richest consumer market on the continent, so industry serving that market feeds on itself. Hold the irony: the founding factor — gold — is nearly mined out, yet the infrastructure, skills and market it gathered keep the region on top. Industrial regions outlive their reasons.

Row two, what happens there. Iron and steel at Vanderbijlpark and Vereeniging with the Vaal Triangle's heavy engineering around them; fuels and chemicals at Sasolburg on the region's rim; vehicle assembly at Rosslyn outside Pretoria; machinery, metal goods, food processing and printing; and stacked above the factory floors, the densest cluster of banks and corporate head offices in Africa. Gauteng is the country's workshop and its office block in one skyline.

Row three, how it fares. In its favour: the giant market, the deep skills pool, the converging transport, and the agglomeration economies of thousands of firms feeding one another. Against it: no port — every export and import pays the long overland leg to Durban; water that must be pumped uphill from another country's mountains; load-shedding and rail decay biting hardest where machinery is thickest; congestion, costly land, and the polluted air of the Vaal Triangle.

Row four, who feels it. Economically the region produces a third or more of national GDP, pays the biggest share of the country's taxes, and draws work-seekers from across the subcontinent. Socially it pairs opportunity with the starkest inequality in the country — Alexandra's shacks facing Sandton's towers across a highway is the standard image — while informal settlements outgrow their services and the industrial south's dirty air falls on the poorest streets.

This section's questions are with you now: the six location factors, the activities from steel to head offices, the no-port handicap, and the Sandton–Alexandra image.

## Subtopic: Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage: The Motor Town on Algoa Bay

Row one, why there. A natural harbour on Algoa Bay made the settlement a trading gateway; railways ran inland to the Karoo, whose wool and hides fed the first processing industries; flat, affordable land waited beside the bay; and because the local market was small, the region grew by serving national and export markets through its port. Then came the decisive twentieth-century ingredient: global vehicle makers chose it — Ford and General Motors historically in Port Elizabeth, Volkswagen at Uitenhage — planting the motor industry that now defines the region. The modern deep-water port at Ngqura completes the location story.

Row two, what happens there. Vehicles above everything: Volkswagen's plant at Kariega ranks among the largest car factories in Africa and ships hatchbacks to Europe; Isuzu assembles bakkies; and around the assembly halls spreads a dense belt of component makers — with catalytic converters a regional speciality, the thread that ties this region to the platinum case study. Beyond the motor core sit tyre plants, textiles, food processing, and the port logistics that move it all.

Row three, how it fares. In its favour: generations of automotive skill, export contracts secured under the state's automotive support programmes, two harbours including Ngqura's deep water, and expansion room next door at Coega. Against it: dependence on a single industry — when world car demand slumps or a model contract lapses, the whole region feels the tremor; distance from the Gauteng market; water insecurity, with Nelson Mandela Bay's recent droughts pushing the taps close to failure; load-shedding across assembly lines; and unemployment far above the national average even in good years.

Row four, who feels it. Economically the motor cluster earns billions in exports and anchors the Eastern Cape's formal economy. Socially it offers well-paid, unionised work to those inside the fence, while the province around it remains the country's poorest — the factory gate and the township beyond it, prosperity and poverty sharing one postcode.

Take the questions for this section now: the harbour-rail-investment chain, the Volkswagen and Isuzu anchors with the catalytic-converter link, the one-industry risk, and the fence-and-township contrast.

## Subtopic: Spreading the Map: SDIs, Corridors, IDZs and SEZs

Four regions holding most of the industry is a problem with a policy answer, and two instruments must be known by name.

Spatial Development Initiatives — SDIs. The recipe: choose a corridor with untapped potential, pour public and private investment into its whole artery — roads, rail, ports, pipelines — and let the upgraded route draw industry, agriculture and tourism into districts that apartheid planning starved. The flagship is the Maputo Development Corridor, upgrading the road and rail line from Gauteng through Mpumalanga to the Mozambican harbour at Maputo — handing the port-less inland core a second, shorter door to the sea and waking the economy along the route. Others worth naming: the Lubombo SDI toward northern KwaZulu-Natal and the Wild Coast SDI in the Eastern Cape, both resting on tourism and agriculture, and the West Coast zone with its minerals and fishing.

Industrial Development Zones — IDZs, today folded into the wider Special Economic Zone — SEZ — framework. The recipe: fence a fully serviced industrial estate beside a port or airport, provide purpose-built infrastructure, tax incentives, duty-free inputs for export production and one-stop state services, and use the package to land export manufacturers who would otherwise never come. The zones to know: Coega beside the deep-water port of Ngqura — the largest, hosting vehicle assembly, agro-processing and energy projects; East London — automotive-centred, serving the Mercedes-Benz plant; Richards Bay — minerals and metals beside the bulk-cargo port; Dube TradePort at King Shaka airport — high-value goods that travel by air; and Saldanha Bay — oil, gas and marine engineering. Since the SEZ legislation, zones reach inland too, but the classic examples remain the coastal four plus Dube.

Now evaluate, because evaluation is always the follow-up. Successes: Coega and East London hold real factories and thousands of real jobs, and the Maputo corridor carries genuine freight. Limits: the zones cost heavily for each job created, some stand part-empty years after opening, and gate-side incentives cannot outweigh load-shedding, port queues and distance when an investor runs the numbers. The balanced verdict: SDIs and IDZs steer some growth to new places and are the right shape of policy, but they shift industry at the margin — the four-region pattern still stands.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the SDI corridor logic with Maputo as flagship, the IDZ recipe with its five named zones, and the balanced verdict.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic as a story you can retell: one long train, four engines, the engine that outlived its gold, the car town by the bay, and the plan to couple new engines further down the line.

## Subtopic: Four Engines Pulling One Train

Picture South Africa's economy as one enormous train, and notice that nearly all its pulling power comes from just four engines. Engine one sits inland on the highveld: Gauteng — the old PWV, for Pretoria, Witwatersrand, Vereeniging. Engine two is Durban–Pinetown, built around the busiest harbour on the subcontinent. Engine three is Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage on Algoa Bay — today's Gqeberha and Kariega — the one that builds the cars. Engine four is the South-Western Cape around Cape Town, hauling food, wine, clothing and books for its own comfortable coaches.

Now the one-sentence secret of the whole map: minerals built the inland engine; harbours built the coastal three. Gauteng exists because gold dragged everything up onto the plateau; the other three exist because ships have to dock somewhere, and factories camp where the cargo lands. Say that sentence in any comparison answer and you have drawn the map in words.

Why does it matter that there are only four? Because a train with all its engines coupled in a few places overheats there — traffic, expensive land, dirty air — while the far coaches crawl, and the people in them leave to find work near the engines. That is over-concentration, last lesson's problem, now wearing its map. Keep it in mind, because this story ends with the government trying to bolt new engines onto the quiet stretches of the line.

And keep the case-study grid folded in your pocket, four questions per engine: why THERE? what does it PULL? what helps and what HURTS? and who RIDES — who gains and who pays? Every case-study question you will meet is one of those four questions in formal dress.

## Subtopic: The Engine That Outlived Its Gold

Gauteng is the strangest industrial giant anywhere, and its strangeness is precisely what you are expected to explain. It has no port — a factory in Kempton Park sends its exports six hundred kilometres downhill to Durban before they smell the sea. It has no great river — its water is pumped up from the Vaal Dam and even from the mountains of a neighbouring country, Lesotho, like a house drinking through a very long straw. By every textbook rule of location, nothing this size should stand on that plateau. So why does a third of the economy sit up there?

Because of what the gold left behind. A century ago gold called everything up the hill — money, workers, railways, power stations burning the coal next door. And once assembled, all of it became its own reason to stay. The mines faded, but the railways still meet there, the skills still live there, the biggest and richest crowd of customers in Africa still shops there, and thousands of factories trade parts and services with one another every day. The engine outlived the gold that built it — put that sentence in an essay and the person marking knows you understand.

What does the engine pull? The heavy loads: steel at Vanderbijlpark, fuels and chemicals at Sasolburg, machinery and metal goods, cars at Rosslyn — and stacked above the factories, the banks and head offices, because Gauteng is the workshop and the office at the same time.

What strains it? Exactly its missing pieces and its crowding: every export pays the long haul to the coast; the borrowed water must keep flowing uphill; load-shedding bites hardest where the most machines stand; the roads jam; and the Vaal Triangle's smoke settles over the poorest suburbs. Who rides? The region pays the nation's largest tax bill and carries its most jobs — and still, Sandton's glass towers stare across a highway at Alexandra's zinc roofs. Wealth and want, one window apart: that image is the social-impact answer.

Your questions for this section are up: the no-port, borrowed-water strangeness, the outlived-its-gold explanation, the heavy loads it pulls, and the window between Sandton and Alexandra.

## Subtopic: The Car Town, and Starting New Engines

Now the second case study, the friendly-sized one: Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage. Its story retells itself. A good natural bay gave it a harbour; railways ran inland to fetch the Karoo's wool and hides; and then, in the century of the motor car, the global manufacturers picked it — Volkswagen settled at Uitenhage, today's Kariega, and others gathered in Gqeberha. The region became the country's car town: the Volkswagen plant is among Africa's biggest, sending hatchbacks to Europe; Isuzu builds bakkies; and around them stretches a belt of parts factories — including the catalytic converters that carry Rustenburg's platinum into the world's exhaust systems. Port, rail, chosen by car makers: six words, whole location story.

Its weakness is its fame turned inside out: one industry. When the world pauses its car-buying, or a model contract ends, the whole town holds its breath. Add drought scares in the taps, load-shedding across the assembly line, and the country's poorest province at its back — good wages inside the plant fence, deep unemployment outside it. Strength and fragility in the same postcode: state both and you have evaluated.

Final chapter: starting new engines along the quiet line. Two tools, each easy to picture. Tool one, the SDI — Spatial Development Initiative: pick a promising stretch of the route and upgrade the entire road-and-rail artery, so businesses spring up along it the way shops spring up along a new taxi route. The flagship is the Maputo Corridor: a fast route from Gauteng through Mpumalanga to Maputo's harbour — a second door to the sea for the port-less giant, and a wake-up call for every town on the way. Tool two, the IDZ — Industrial Development Zone, now part of the Special Economic Zones family: build a ready-made industrial estate at a port, plug in the power, roads and cranes, sweeten the deal with tax breaks, and invite export factories to move straight in — a fully furnished flat for industry. Names to carry: Coega beside the deep port of Ngqura, right next to the car town; East London, serving the Mercedes-Benz plant; Richards Bay for metals; Dube TradePort at Durban's airport for cargo that flies.

Has it worked? One honest breath: partly. Coega and East London hold real factories and real jobs, and the Maputo route hauls real freight — but the zones are expensive, some sit half-empty, and no tax break beats load-shedding and queuing ports in an investor's spreadsheet. The four engines still pull most of the train; the new ones are coupled, but small. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the car town's six-word story, the one-industry risk, the two tools with their flagship names, and the honest verdict. Retell the story once tonight — four engines, the giant that outlived its gold, the car town, the new engines — and this case-study essay becomes the friendliest question in your final exams.
