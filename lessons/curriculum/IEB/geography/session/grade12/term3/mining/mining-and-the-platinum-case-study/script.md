# Part 1 — Expert

Modern South Africa was put together around holes in the ground: diamonds at Kimberley from 1867, gold along the Witwatersrand from 1886, and today the richest platinum deposits on the planet in the Bushveld. This session covers the whole mining story: its contribution and significance to South African development, then and now; the platinum case study — location, favouring and hindering factors, and economic contribution; the balanced accounting of mining's costs; and the mapwork skills bolted onto this topic — spotting mining activity on a topographic sheet, grid referencing, and the symbol code the map uses to speak.

## Subtopic: How Mining Built and Still Carries South Africa

Start with the big historical claim, because you will be asked to defend it: mining is the foundation of the modern South African economy. The evidence links up like a chain. The mineral strikes pulled money and people onto empty veld — Johannesburg leapt from farmland to the largest city on the subcontinent within a generation, entirely on gold. The mines needed to move rock, so railways were driven from the ports into the interior to serve them; they needed power, so a coal-fired electricity system grew to feed them; they needed drills, explosives, boots and credit, so South African manufacturing and banking grew up as mining's suppliers — the JSE itself was created to raise money for the goldfields. And the migrant labour system, drawing men from across southern Africa to the shafts, redrew the region's settlement patterns and family life — a social imprint the settlement work earlier this year already traced.

Now the present tense. Mining directly earns under ten per cent of GDP and employs under half a million people — but it carries a far larger share of the country's merchandise export earnings. Platinum group metals, coal, iron ore, gold and manganese sit year after year among the biggest exports, and that foreign income pays for the oil, machinery and electronics South Africa buys from the world. Add the multipliers — every mining wage supports suppliers, transporters and the shops where it is spent — and add the royalties and taxes that flow to the state. Whole towns stand on it: Rustenburg on platinum, eMalahleni on coal, Kathu on iron ore, Kuruman on manganese.

The honest picture needs the other column too: mining's costs. Environmental — acid water seeping from old gold workings into rivers, dust off the dumps, sinkholes in dolomitic ground, scarred land and tailings mountains. Social — the migrant-labour legacy, dangerous work and lung disease, and the shock waves when prices fall and shafts close, leaving one-purpose towns stranded. The strongest answers carry both columns — the contribution and the bill — and that balance is what a critical discussion means.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the evidence chain for the foundation claim, today's contribution measured three ways, and the cost column.

## Subtopic: The Platinum Case Study: Location and Favouring Factors

Platinum is the case study, and South Africa is its world capital: the country holds the majority of the planet's known platinum group metal reserves and leads global production.

Location first, and precisely. The platinum sits in the Bushveld Igneous Complex — a vast, ancient body of molten rock that cooled and solidified in layers, shaped like an immense saucer beneath the northern interior. Its mineral-bearing layers reach the surface in curved arcs called limbs: a western limb through the Rustenburg and Brits districts of North West; an eastern limb through Limpopo and Mpumalanga around Burgersfort and Steelpoort; and a northern limb near Mokopane in Limpopo. The capital of the platinum belt is Rustenburg — fix the pairing: specialised mining town, platinum. Inside the layered rock, the metal concentrates in two thin horizons — the Merensky Reef and the UG2 chromitite layer — which the mines chase underground for kilometres.

Then the favouring factors, family by family. Geological: enormous, high-grade reserves lying in continuous, predictable layers — an endowment almost no other country owns. Historical and technical: over a century of deep-mining skill inherited from the gold industry, so the engineers, drilling technology and refining capacity were already in place. Infrastructural: the Bushveld lies within reach of Gauteng's heartland — its railways, power lines, water schemes and skilled people extend to the platinum belt with ease. Labour: a long-established mining workforce. Market: persistent world demand — platinum group metals are essential in autocatalysts, the exhaust-cleaning converters fitted to petrol and diesel vehicles, and are prized in jewellery, chemical catalysts and, increasingly, hydrogen fuel cells. South Africa sells something the global car industry cannot yet do without.

Take the questions for this section now: the Bushveld Igneous Complex with its three limbs and two reef names, and the five families of favouring factors.

## Subtopic: Platinum's Contribution and Its Hindering Factors

Count the contribution properly. Platinum group metals rank at or near the top of South Africa's mineral export table almost every year. The industry employs on the order of one hundred and seventy thousand people directly — the largest employer within South African mining — and every one of those wages supports dependants and businesses along the platinum belt. It anchors the provincial economies of North West and the eastern limb districts; Rustenburg grew into one of the fastest-growing cities in the country on the back of it. It draws major capital investment, pays royalties and taxes, and feeds the beneficiation agenda — smelters and refineries at Rustenburg and elsewhere process ore locally, adding value before export, while policy pushes for still more downstream processing, including hydrogen-economy plans built on platinum catalysts.

Now the hindering factors, sorted into two baskets. Basket one, the physical and technical: the reefs are thin — commonly around a metre — and getting deeper, so the work is hot, labour-intensive and stubbornly hard to mechanise; costs climb as shafts follow the dipping layers down; and the ore demands complex, electricity-hungry smelting. Basket two, the economic and social: expensive, unreliable power — smelters stall when the supply fails, and load-shedding has repeatedly cut output; world prices that swing with the global car market and can sink below production cost; labour conflict — the platinum belt endured the sector's longest strikes, and the Marikana tragedy of 2012 stands in this history; rising wage and input costs; deteriorating rail and water infrastructure; and a long-term demand question, because battery electric vehicles carry no autocatalyst — though hydrogen fuel cells may convert some of that lost demand into new demand. And the communities around the shafts still wait for housing, services and jobs, so the industry works under heavy social expectation.

When this content is examined, the verb is usually discuss or evaluate: contribution on one side, hindrances on the other, and a conclusion that platinum remains a pillar of the economy — a pillar under strain, needing dependable power, labour peace and deeper beneficiation to stay upright.

This section's questions are ready: the contribution counted in exports, jobs and beneficiation, and the two baskets of hindering factors.

## Subtopic: Mining on the Map: Identification, Grid References and Symbols

Mapwork bolts this topic to the topographic sheet, and three skills carry the marks.

Skill one: recognising mining on the map. A mine announces itself with a cluster of signs. The printed word first — Mine, Myn, Colliery, Quarry, Diggings — the sheet often simply says so. Mine dumps and slimes dams: pale, geometric mounds and rectangular dams with edges too straight for nature — straightness is the giveaway. Excavations and open workings drawn with toothed or hachured depression symbols. Buildings and headgear clustered in otherwise open country, with road and rail spurs running at them — ore is heavy, and a railway that leaves the main line to dead-end at a works is powerful evidence. Power lines converging on one site, because mines are gluttons for electricity. And near a mature mine, the settlement clue: a grid-planned town laid out beside the workings. In an answer, quote at least two pieces of map evidence, each pinned to its grid reference.

Skill two: the referencing itself. A six-figure grid reference splits every map square into tenths: read the eastings first, then the northings — along the corridor, then up the stairs — estimating tenths within the square. Drill it until the order is automatic, because a reversed pair loses the mark even when your finger found the right feature. Alphanumeric references — the letter-number blocks such as C4 used on some sheets and orthophoto maps — ask only for the block name. Always answer in the exact format the question demonstrates.

Skill three: the symbol code as a system. Blue for water, green for vegetation, brown for contours and cultivation, black and red for human constructions — and the legend is printed on every sheet, so check rather than guess. The human symbols around mines earn the most questions: the thick red national route, thinner secondary roads, the black railway with its station ticks, power lines striding on pylon dots, and built-up shading. A classic integrated question pairs a photograph with a map extract of the platinum belt and asks: name the economic activity, give map evidence with grid references, and explain why the activity is located here — and that last part comes straight from the case study you now carry: the Bushveld's layered treasure, the nearby infrastructure, the workforce, and a world that wants the metal in every exhaust.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the mine's signature on the sheet, the corridor-then-stairs referencing order, and the symbol families by colour.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same story from ground level — a country that dug its way into existence, a giant buried saucer, and a treasure map that cannot keep its secret.

## Subtopic: The Country That Dug Itself Into Existence

Ask why South Africa's biggest city stands where it stands. No navigable river, no harbour, no lake — Johannesburg exists because in 1886 somebody stumbled onto the edge of the richest gold deposit on earth, and within a few years the bare highveld wore a city. Kimberley's diamonds had rehearsed the pattern twenty years earlier: find the treasure, and everything else arrives at a run.

That "everything else" is the actual lesson. Heavy rock had to move, so railways were laid from the coast to the interior — the skeleton of today's rail map. The mines needed power, so power stations rose on the coalfields. They needed drills, dynamite, boots and loans, so factories and banks grew up as their suppliers — the JSE was founded precisely to fund the goldfields. Workers travelled from across southern Africa, and towns, townships and the whole migrant labour pattern followed them. Pull any thread of the modern economy backwards — the railways, the power grid, the stock exchange, Johannesburg itself — and the thread ends at a mine. That is what "mining is the foundation of the economy" actually claims: not that mining is today's biggest earner, but that the house stands on it.

Today mining brings in a bit under a tenth of the country's income directly and employs a few hundred thousand people — but hold the key part: it stocks a huge share of the export stall. Platinum, coal, iron ore, gold — sold to the world, they fetch the foreign money that pays for the petrol, phones and machines in the country's shopping basket. Mining is South Africa's stall at the world's market: what sells off that stall settles the bills for everything the household cannot make itself.

But digging leaves marks the land keeps. Old workings leak acid water into rivers, dumps breathe dust over townships, and when a mine dies, its one-purpose town is left stranded on the veld. An honest answer holds both pages of the ledger: the foundation AND the bill.

Take this section's questions now: the city with no river, the everything-else that mining dragged into being, the market-stall picture of exports, and the bill the land presents.

## Subtopic: The Buried Saucer Full of Platinum

Now the star of the case study. Deep beneath the bushveld north and west of Pretoria lies a colossal, ancient saucer of once-molten rock — the Bushveld Igneous Complex. As it cooled, its minerals settled into flat bands, the way silt settles into layers at the bottom of a still dam — and two of those bands, one named the Merensky Reef and one named the UG2, are loaded with platinum. The saucer's rim breaks the surface in three arcs, and the mines queue up along them: the western arc through Rustenburg, the eastern arc near Burgersfort and Steelpoort, and a northern arc by Mokopane. Rustenburg is the capital of platinum country — weld those two names together permanently.

Why is South Africa the platinum superpower? Count the ingredients of the winning recipe. Ingredient one: the deposit — most of the world's known platinum in this single saucer, lying in neat, predictable bands. Ingredient two: a hundred years of deep-mining craft inherited from gold — the engineers and the drills were already in the country. Ingredient three: the saucer sits near Gauteng, so the railways, power lines and skilled people were practically next door. Ingredient four: a hungry world — nearly every petrol and diesel vehicle on earth carries a pinch of platinum in its exhaust to scrub the fumes, and jewellers and the new hydrogen technologies want it too. Rich deposit, inherited craft, next-door infrastructure, hungry market: four ingredients, one superpower.

But the recipe is brutal to cook. The platinum bands are thin — about a metre, waist-height — and deep, so picture extracting the jam layer from the middle of a giant cake, kilometres underground, in heat, mostly with hand-held drills, because machines struggle in a tunnel you cannot stand up in. Then the surface troubles: power cuts that freeze the smelters, world prices that dive whenever car sales dive, wounding strikes — the platinum belt carries the memory of Marikana — and a question mark over the future, because battery electric cars need no platinum while hydrogen cars would need plenty. Treasure in the saucer, struggle at the surface: that tension IS the platinum case study.

Your questions for this section are up: the saucer with its three arcs, the four ingredients, and the thin, deep bands that make the treasure hard to lift.

## Subtopic: Reading the Treasure Map

Last skill: finding the mine on the map sheet — easier than it sounds, because a mine cannot hide. It is the loudest, untidiest thing on any topographic map; you only need to know its signature.

Hunt for shapes nature refuses to make. Nature never draws straight lines — so pale rectangular mounds and perfectly square dams are mine dumps and slimes dams, piled and pumped by machinery. Hunt for opened ground: the small toothed symbols of pits and quarries. Hunt for heavy transport aiming at apparently nothing: a railway spur that abandons the main line and dead-ends at a knot of buildings in open veld — rock is heavy, and only trainloads of something valuable justify rails to nowhere. Hunt for power lines converging like extension cords feeding one ravenous machine. And very often the sheet simply confesses in print: Mine, Myn, Colliery, Quarry. In an answer, give two or three of these clues and pin each one to its grid reference — evidence plus address, every time.

The address system is the six-figure grid reference, ordered by the chant: along the corridor, then up the stairs. Easting first — the numbers growing to the right — then northing — growing upward — estimating tenths inside the square. Corridor, then stairs, without exception; swap them and the mark is gone even though your eyes were on the right spot.

And the map speaks in a colour code as fixed as a school uniform: blue is always water, green is vegetation, brown is the land's shape and its cultivation, black and red are the things people built — roads, railways, buildings, power lines. Do not panic-memorise what the printed legend hands you for free; your job on the day is speed, not recall.

Now assemble the whole lesson on one question: a map extract of the eastern platinum belt near Steelpoort. You spot the straight-edged dumps, the rail spur, the printed word Mine, and you quote each with its reference. Asked what is mined and why here, you answer from the saucer: platinum, in the Bushveld's layered reefs, served by the region's rail and power, feeding the world's exhaust pipes. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the shapes nature refuses to make, the corridor-and-stairs chant, the uniform code of colours, and the saucer standing behind the map. Land these, and mining becomes the topic where theory and mapwork pay you twice for the same knowledge in your final exams.
