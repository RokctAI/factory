# Part 1 — Expert

Agriculture earns barely three per cent of South Africa's GDP and yet sits at the heart of this term's work, because it powers three of the syllabus's most dependable question families: the small-scale versus large-scale contrast, the maize case study, and food security. This session works all four stations in turn: what agriculture genuinely contributes to the economy, the two farming worlds sharing one country, maize from the highveld lands to the export terminal, and the food security framework with its influencing factors and repair strategies. Keep one constraint burning in the background: only about twelve per cent of South Africa's land is arable, and rain is unreliable over most of it — that single fact explains half of everything that follows.

## Subtopic: What Agriculture Really Contributes

Open with the paradox you must argue in both directions. On the GDP scoreboard agriculture is tiny — around two to three per cent. But you are expected to dismantle the lazy conclusion that tiny means unimportant, and the dismantling uses five levers. Lever one: food supply — the sector feeds more than sixty million people, and if this contribution fails, none of the others matter. Lever two: employment — around five per cent of the formal workforce plus a large seasonal and informal army, concentrated in rural districts where other work barely exists; whole valleys live on farm wages. Lever three: linkages — agriculture is the input line of the agro-processing industry, a substantial slice of manufacturing: maize mills, sugar refineries, fruit canneries, wine cellars, abattoirs. Measure the whole value chain instead of the farm gate and agriculture's real weight multiplies several times. Lever four: exports — citrus, wine, table grapes, wool, macadamias, and maize in surplus years; farm exports earn foreign exchange, and South Africa ranks among the world's top citrus exporters. Lever five: rural stability — farming anchors the countryside, slows depopulation and keeps the small towns breathing; think back to the settlement work, because farm jobs are the brake on that downward spiral.

The products sort into two markets. The home market takes the staples: maize — the national staple — wheat, sugar, vegetables, poultry, beef and dairy. The export market takes the high-value harvest: citrus and deciduous fruit from the Western and Eastern Cape and Limpopo, wine from the Cape winelands, wool and mohair off the Karoo, sugar from KwaZulu-Natal, avocados and macadamias from the subtropical northeast.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the five levers against the small-percentage argument, and the home-market versus export-market split.

## Subtopic: Two Farming Worlds: Small-Scale and Large-Scale

South African farming runs as two systems living side by side, and the contrast between them is asked directly.

Large-scale commercial farming: roughly thirty to forty thousand commercial units working most of the country's farmland. Its character: big farms, deep capital — tractors, centre-pivot irrigation, precision technology — hired labour, scientific inputs, production aimed squarely at the market and at export, and full access to finance, insurance and marketing chains. This system grows the overwhelming bulk of the food that reaches shops.

Small-scale and subsistence farming: hundreds of thousands of households, concentrated in the former homeland areas, working small plots — often communal land under traditional tenure. Its character: family labour, thin capital and inputs, production mainly for the household pot with an occasional surplus sold locally, sharp exposure to drought, and blocked access to credit — communal land cannot easily stand as collateral for a loan — plus long distances to markets, extension advice and irrigation.

The verbs that arrive with this content are compare and evaluate. Compare along five clean lines: farm size, capital and technology, labour source, market orientation, and land tenure. Evaluate with balance: large-scale farming delivers the national food supply and export income, but sheds workers as it mechanises and concentrates ownership in few hands; small-scale farming carries millions of livelihoods and keeps people on the land, but low yields leave each household one bad season from hardship. Then the policy bridge closes the answer: support for emerging farmers — finance, extension services, secure tenure through land reform, market access — aims to walk households from subsistence toward commercial production, connecting the two worlds instead of picking one.

This section's questions are with you now: the five comparison lines, the balanced evaluation, and the collateral problem that keeps small farms small.

## Subtopic: The Maize Case Study: From Highveld Field to Export Terminal

Maize is South Africa's staple food and its biggest field crop by area, and it is the case study to know cold. Learn it in three layers: where, why, and what it gives back.

Where: the Maize Triangle — the summer-rainfall grain country of the interior plateau, sweeping across the Free State, North West and the Mpumalanga highveld, with the Free State the largest producer. Anchor the corners roughly at the western Free State grainlands, the country around Lichtenburg in North West, and the highveld around Ermelo in Mpumalanga.

Why there — the favouring factors, each worth a mark: summer rain of roughly five hundred to eight hundred millimetres falling in the growing season, exactly when the plant drinks; long, hot, frost-free summers; deep, fertile, well-drained soils spread over gently rolling country that giant machinery loves; and the support web already standing — silos, railway lines, mills, and the futures market that prices each season's crop. Add the human capital: generations of grain-farming skill and a research establishment breeding drought-tolerant cultivars.

What hinders — every favouring list casts a shadow: rainfall variability and recurring drought, because highveld summer rain is thunderstorm rain and some seasons it simply stays away; hail and early frost; erosion-prone soils where practice is careless; input costs — diesel, fertiliser, seed — climbing faster than the maize price; and a shifting climate dragging rainfall patterns in directions the farmers cannot follow.

The contribution: maize feeds the country twice. White maize becomes mealie meal, the staple of household plates; yellow maize becomes the feed floor under the poultry, egg, dairy and beef industries — so every chicken portion and every egg carries maize inside its price. Around the crop stand the millers, the feed makers, the food processors and the grain-belt towns with their silos; and in surplus years South Africa acts as the subcontinent's grain shed, exporting maize to its neighbours and earning the region's trust along with the income.

Take the questions for this section now: the triangle's three provinces, the favouring factors with their shadows, and maize's double life as human staple and animal feed.

## Subtopic: Food Security: The Framework, the Factors, the Fixes

Food security, in the standard definition, exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food for an active and healthy life. Load-test every clause. ALL people — a national average hides hungry households. AT ALL times — secure in the drought year and at month-end, not only after good harvests. PHYSICAL AND ECONOMIC access — the food must be nearby AND affordable; either failure alone breaks security. SUFFICIENT, SAFE, NUTRITIOUS — enough calories, safely stored, properly varied.

That definition exposes South Africa's sharpest paradox, which you must be able to state in two sentences: the country is food secure at NATIONAL level — it normally grows enough staples and imports any shortfall — yet millions of HOUSEHOLDS are food insecure, because unemployment and poverty put adequate food out of financial reach. National sufficiency, household hunger: the binding constraint is access, not production.

The influencing factors group naturally in threes. Environmental: drought and flood cycles, climate change, soil degradation, water scarcity — and always the twelve per cent arable constraint. Economic: unemployment and poverty, food price inflation, input costs, and the exchange rate that prices imported wheat and fuel. Social and political: population growth, illness — including HIV/AIDS — weakening farming households, land reform uncertainty cooling investment, and instability elsewhere in Africa disrupting regional supply chains.

The repair strategies work best matched to the failure each one fixes: support for small-scale and emerging farmers — extension, finance, irrigation, secure tenure — so households grow more of their own food; school feeding schemes and social grants, striking the economic-access failure directly; food gardens at homes, schools and clinics; cutting post-harvest losses with better storage and transport; research into drought-tolerant cultivars; and, at national scale, protecting the commercial sector's productivity while diversifying water sources. Keep one named example ready: the National School Nutrition Programme feeds around nine million learners on school days — an access-side intervention that also holds children in school.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the definition clause by clause, the national-versus-household paradox, the factor triads, and the strategies matched to their failures.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same content carried by three pictures: a supper plate you can read like a map, two farms touching at one fence, and a riddle about hunger in a country with full silos.

## Subtopic: The Country That Runs on Pap

Start at the supper table. Pap, a piece of chicken, chakalaka, maybe an egg on top. Now run the plate backwards like a film. The pap was mealie meal, which was white maize, which stood in a highveld field last summer. The chicken — and the egg — came from birds raised on feed mixed mostly from yellow maize. So maize is on that plate at least twice: once where you can see it, and once hidden inside the chicken and the egg. Run the same film for the whole country and you understand why maize outranks its percentages: it is the floor under the national diet.

Where does it all grow? Put a finger on the middle of the South African map — the high, flat interior — and sweep it from the Free State across into North West and over to the Mpumalanga highveld. That sweep is the Maize Triangle, the country's grain basket. Why exactly there? Because maize is a summer-rain crop with a clear shopping list, and the plateau ticks every item: rain in the growing months — roughly five hundred to eight hundred millimetres; long hot summers with no frost until the crop is in; deep soils on land flat enough for combine harvesters; and the support machine already parked next door — the tall silos along the railway, the mills, the co-ops. A harvest this size needs infrastructure the way a stadium needs turnstiles.

But highveld summer rain is thunderstorm rain, and a thunderstorm keeps its own diary. Some seasons it arrives on schedule; some seasons the whole triangle stands under a hot, empty sky. Drought is the standing threat, with hail, late frost, pricey diesel and fertiliser, and a slowly drifting climate lined up behind it. Planting maize is burying money in October and betting the farm on the clouds.

The stakes are national. In a good year South Africa fills its own bowls and sells the extra to its neighbours; in a drought year the price of mealie meal climbs in every shop, and the poorest homes feel it first, because pap is the food of tight budgets.

Take this section's questions now: the maize hiding twice on the plate, the triangle under your finger, the crop's shopping list, and the thunderstorm's diary.

## Subtopic: Two Farmers, One Fence

Picture two farms meeting at a single fence. On the one side: three thousand hectares of maize, a shed full of tractors, a centre-pivot irrigator walking its slow circle, hired workers, a bank loan, a delivery contract with the silo company, and a farmer watching the maize price on a phone. On the other side: two hectares of communal land, a family raising maize and vegetables mainly for its own pot, hand tools and one borrowed tractor-day for ploughing, no title deed and therefore no loan — the bank cannot take communal land as security — and, in a kind season, a small surplus sold at the taxi-rank market.

That fence runs through the whole of South African farming, and its formal name is large-scale commercial versus small-scale subsistence agriculture. Compare across five fingers: size — thousands of hectares against a few; capital — machines and irrigation against hand tools; labour — hired workers against the family; market — feeding the nation and the world against feeding the household; tenure — title-deed ownership against communal rights. Five fingers, five comparison marks.

Now be fair to both sides, because fairness is a marking skill. The big farm delivers: it is the reason supermarket shelves stay full, exports flow and the nation as a whole does not run short — but its machines employ ever fewer hands per hectare, and its ownership sits with a few. The small farm sustains: it holds millions of families, anchors village life and keeps rural South Africa peopled — but its yields are low, and one failed season falls straight onto the family's own plate. Neither farmer is the villain; the two systems are doing different jobs.

The way forward is a bridge over the fence, not a winner: help small farmers become bigger farmers — training and extension officers, affordable credit, secure land rights so the plot itself can back a loan, irrigation, and a road to real markets. Each support knocks out one difference between the two sides.

Your questions for this section are up: the five-finger comparison, the fair verdict on both farms, and the bridge that policy is trying to build.

## Subtopic: Full Silos, Empty Plates

Here is the riddle the whole topic funnels into: how can a country's silos stand full while children in that same country go to bed hungry? Solve it and food security is yours.

The definition first, slowly, because every word carries a mark: food security means ALL people, at ALL times, can physically REACH and actually AFFORD enough safe, nutritious food for a healthy life. Treat it as a checklist. All people — the average is not allowed to hide anyone. At all times — the drought year and the last week of the month count. Reach and afford — a stocked shop is worthless to a family with an empty purse; that family is food insecure in front of full shelves. Enough, safe, nutritious — filling up on cheap starch alone fails the last test; that is hidden hunger.

Now the riddle unties itself: South Africa is food secure as a COUNTRY — commercial farms and imports keep the national supply adequate — but millions of HOUSEHOLDS are food insecure, because with unemployment above thirty per cent the food is present and the money is not. The problem is not the farms; it is the wallets. Production security at the top, access insecurity at the bottom — those two sentences are the spine of any food security answer.

What pushes households toward hunger? Pack the causes into three bags. The nature bag: drought, floods, tired soil, a shifting climate. The money bag: unemployment, food price inflation, costly transport and inputs. The people bag: more mouths each year, illness draining working families, insecure land rights. Three bags, quickly unpacked in any answer.

And each fix aims at a particular bag. School feeding — the National School Nutrition Programme reaching around nine million learners — and social grants strike the money bag directly. Food gardens at homes, clinics and schools shorten the road from soil to plate. Backing small farmers — seed, water, advice, secure tenure — grows the household's own supply. Better storage and transport stop food dying between field and shop. Drought-tolerant cultivars answer the nature bag. Match fix to bag and the strategy paragraph writes itself.

Here come the final questions of the lesson: the riddle and its two-sentence solution, the checklist definition, the three bags, and the fixes aimed at each. Own these, and the agriculture and food security questions in your final exams become marks you collect on the way in.
