**Mandy:** The classification questions trip people up when a business seems to live in two sectors at once. A wine estate grows grapes, makes wine and runs a restaurant — so which sector is it?

**Tutor:** None of them — because we never classify the business; we classify each ACTIVITY inside it. A modern company is a bundle of activities wearing one name. On your wine estate: growing the grapes is primary — raw fruit taken from nature. The cellar fermenting and bottling is secondary — a transformation has happened, raw grapes became a manufactured product. The restaurant and the tasting room are tertiary — services sold to visitors. Questions are built around exactly this trap: a scenario will describe a fishing company that catches hake — primary — and also cans it — secondary — in the same sentence, and the marks go to whoever splits the sentence. So when a case feels double, split it on paper: name each activity, run the test — raw from nature is primary, transformed is secondary, service delivered is tertiary, knowledge handled is quaternary — and file each activity separately. And learn one boundary in advance: processing is secondary even when it happens on the farm. Pressing olives, drying fruit, pasteurising milk — the syllabus line is that extraction ends at raw, and any transformation, however small, has crossed into secondary.

**Mandy:** Next: the twin pie charts, money share next to jobs share. I can describe that the slices differ, but my answers keep coming back half-marked. What does the full-mark version say?

**Tutor:** Half marks means you described the gap without explaining its mechanism, and the mechanism always has two halves — value of output and intensity of labour. Take agriculture's classic pair: around three per cent of GDP against five per cent or more of employment. The full-mark answer names both halves. Half one: raw farm output is cheap — unprocessed wheat, fruit and wool earn little compared with banking or manufactured goods, so the money slice stays thin. Half two: farm work still needs many hands — planting, picking, packing — at low wages, so the jobs slice stays fat. Then flip both halves for finance: a small team of highly skilled people can move billions, so value per worker is enormous — fat money slice, thin jobs slice. Close by stating the rule as a rule: sectors with low value per worker carry more employment than GDP; sectors with high value per worker carry more GDP than employment. Description, two-part mechanism, general rule — climb all three rungs and the full allocation is yours. And when a question says "suggest reasons" in the plural, give at least two; the plural is an instruction, not decoration.

**Mandy:** The unemployment figure — over thirty per cent — how do I use it in an answer so it works for marks instead of just sounding grim?

**Tutor:** Make it do analytical work, because nearly every economic geography question this term secretly points at it. Three disciplined uses. Use one: as the WHY behind policy. Whenever you are asked why the state pushes agri-processing, mineral beneficiation, tourism growth or industrial zones, the bottom answer is always jobs — South Africa needs sectors that absorb labour, and each policy tries to create employment where raw exports create almost none. Quoted there, the figure earns its place. Use two: as the tension inside mechanisation. Farms and mines mechanise to survive competition — production holds while jobs fall — so the very trend that protects output deepens unemployment. Naming that tension is the kind of evaluative sentence long paragraph answers are built from. Use three: as the doorway to the informal economy. Where formal work is scarce, people build their own — street trade, transport, home services — so informal work is the shock absorber of South African unemployment, and including it makes any employment answer more credible. One precision habit finishes it: say "the official unemployment rate exceeds thirty per cent, and is far higher among young people" — the youth clause shows you understand the structure of the problem, not just its headline number.

**Mandy:** Last one: when a question asks me to comment on the reliability or the limitations of economic statistics, what is actually expected on the page?

**Tutor:** A short, specific list of what the numbers cannot see — and the blind spots of economic statistics can be prepared before you ever see the table. Blind spot one: the informal economy. GDP and employment counts capture registered, taxed activity, so the container salon, the backyard mechanic and the unregistered childminder slip through, and both output and work are undercounted — heavily so in South Africa. Blind spot two: subsistence production. Vegetables grown and eaten at home never cross a market, so rural households look poorer in the data than at the table. Blind spot three: averages bury distribution. GDP can rise while most households feel nothing, so pair any growth claim with the question of who received it. Blind spot four: shifting definitions. The official unemployment rate counts only active job-seekers, while the expanded rate adds those too discouraged to keep looking — and the gap between the definitions is many percentage points, so the table's definition changes its meaning. Blind spot five: money across time. Rand values compared across decades without inflation adjustment mislead, and so does comparing a boom year with a recession year. Two or three of these, one sentence each, tied to the actual source in front of you, is a complete comment. The skill is not distrusting data everywhere — it is knowing exactly where its eyesight stops.
