# Part 1 — Expert

Term three points Geography at the economy itself: how South Africa makes its money and where its people actually work. The scaffolding for everything that follows is the four economic sectors, so this session builds that scaffolding carefully: what each sector is, anchored in South African examples; what GDP and GNP measure and how the sectors share them; why the employment picture refuses to match the money picture; and the calm routine for reading the tables, pie charts and graphs that carry every question on this topic. Hold one contrast in your head from the start: agriculture earns only around three rand of every hundred rand the economy produces, while the services together earn roughly two thirds — and explaining that contrast is where the marks sit all term.

## Subtopic: The Four Sectors: What Each One Does

The primary sector takes raw materials straight from nature. Think of the merino farmer running sheep across the Karoo, the hake trawler working out of Saldanha Bay, the timber plantations of the KwaZulu-Natal midlands, the iron-ore mine at Sishen in the Northern Cape. The test never changes: if the job ends with something still raw — wool, fish, logs, ore, cane — the work is primary, no matter how big the machines or how famous the company. Mining sits in this sector, and mining is the primary activity on which South Africa's whole modern economy was originally raised.

The secondary sector takes those raw materials and TRANSFORMS them: manufacturing, processing, construction and power generation. The mill in Cape Town spinning Karoo wool into fabric, the sugar mill on the KwaZulu-Natal coast crushing cane into sugar, the steelworks at Vanderbijlpark, the plant assembling cars in East London, the crews building a new school, the wind farm in the Eastern Cape feeding the grid — all secondary. The defining word is transformation: raw material in, changed product out, and value added in between. That phrase, value added, is the sector's entire purpose — a bale of raw wool earns little, while the same wool woven into winter coats earns many times more.

The tertiary sector makes no physical product at all; it delivers SERVICES: retail and trade, transport, banking and insurance, tourism, teaching, nursing, government administration. The minibus operator, the attorney, the guesthouse owner below the Drakensberg, the cashier, the traffic officer — all tertiary. This is the giant of the South African economy, carrying most of the money and most of the jobs.

The quaternary sector is the knowledge tip of the service world: research, information technology, data analysis, actuarial work, the software cluster around Stellenbosch, a university team modelling climate risk. Some textbooks fold it into tertiary; your syllabus names it separately, so treat it as its own sector — the one that works with information and ideas rather than goods or routine services.

Chain the sectors through a single product and the frame locks in. The Karoo farm shears the wool — primary. The mill weaves it into cloth — secondary. The boutique sells the coat and the courier delivers it — tertiary. The fibre-research station at Grootfontein breeding a finer-woolled sheep — quaternary. One fleece, four sectors, and every worker in the country belongs to exactly one link.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the four definitions, the transformation keyword, and the wool chain from paddock to laboratory.

## Subtopic: GDP, GNP and What Each Sector Contributes

Two ways of sizing an economy, separated by a single letter. Gross Domestic Product, GDP, totals the value of all final goods and services produced INSIDE a country's borders in a year, regardless of who owns the producers. Domestic means inside the fence: a British-owned bank running branches in Sandton produces inside South Africa, so its output belongs to South Africa's GDP. Gross National Product, GNP, follows ownership instead of location: everything produced by a country's own citizens and companies, wherever on the planet they operate. A South African supermarket chain's stores in Zambia count in South Africa's GNP — and in Zambia's GDP. Keep the rhyme: domestic is the ground, national is the passport.

Now the shares, learned as rough proportions rather than precise decimals. Agriculture contributes only around two and a half to three per cent of South Africa's GDP. Mining, despite its history and its export muscle, sits below ten per cent. The whole secondary sector — manufacturing, construction, electricity — earns roughly a fifth. And the tertiary sector, from banks and shops to schools and tourism, earns comfortably over sixty per cent, close to two thirds. One sentence captures the strangeness: South Africa carries the sectoral shape of a developed economy — services dominant, primary small — inside a society still struggling with developing-world unemployment and inequality. That paradox is worth an essay paragraph whenever it fits.

The pattern also has a direction. As economies mature, their weight slides from primary to secondary to tertiary: first most people work the land, then factories take over, then services outgrow everything. South Africa has walked that road — diamonds and gold built the cities, manufacturing grew up behind the mines, and services now dwarf them both. But carry a standing caution: a small GDP share never proves a sector unimportant. Agriculture at three per cent still feeds sixty million people, supplies the entire food-processing industry, and earns export income; mining still underwrites export earnings and whole provincial economies. GDP share is one lens on importance, never the only one.

This section's questions are ready for you now: the ground-versus-passport separation, the approximate shares, and the caution about small percentages.

## Subtopic: Employment: Where the Jobs Actually Are

The money numbers and the jobs numbers tell different stories, and the space between them is where data questions live.

Jobs first. The tertiary sector employs by far the most South Africans — shops, transport, government, security, domestic work, finance. Agriculture employs a noticeably larger share of workers than its small money share suggests — around five per cent of formal jobs, plus a great deal of seasonal and informal farm work — because much of farming still runs on human hands. Mining's workforce keeps shrinking, now well under half a million, as machines replace miners and old gold shafts close. Manufacturing carries roughly one worker in ten.

Now the mismatch. Agriculture's slice of employment is close to double its slice of GDP: many workers, modest money, because raw produce sells cheaply and farm wages are low. Finance is the exact reverse: a huge slice of GDP produced by comparatively few, well-paid people. State the governing rule in full, because full statements earn full marks: labour-intensive sectors hold a bigger share of employment than of GDP; capital-intensive sectors hold a bigger share of GDP than of employment. Whenever a table shows a sector's two percentages disagreeing, that rule is the explanation being asked for.

Two South African realities frame every answer. First, unemployment: with the official rate above thirty per cent — and dramatically worse for young people — the deepest economic problem is not which sector leads but the shortage of work in all of them, which is why each case study this term ends by asking what an industry does for jobs. Second, the informal economy: the container hair salon, the street-corner fruit seller, the backyard panel beater, the unregistered childminder — work invisible to official statistics yet feeding millions of households. An employment analysis that forgets informal work has undercounted the country, and saying so earns evaluation marks.

Take the questions for this section now: the employment ranking, the labour-intensity rule, and the two realities behind the tidy percentages.

## Subtopic: Reading the Numbers: Tables, Graphs and the Questions They Carry

Questions on economic structure never arrive naked — they arrive dressed in data: a percentage table, a pie chart, a line graph running across decades. The marks go to the reader with a disciplined order.

Four steps, always the same. Step one: title, units and date before anything else — percentages of GDP, percentages of employment and rand values support completely different conclusions, and a pie from 1990 beside a pie from 2023 is a question about change, not about either year. Step two: name the largest and the smallest categories out loud — the obvious sentence is the first mark, so write it. Step three: hunt the comparison — what grew, what shrank, where two datasets contradict each other. Step four: attach a reason from the syllabus — mechanisation emptying farms and mines of workers, financial services swelling, ore bodies thinning.

Rehearse the two classic shapes. Shape one: twin pie charts for the same year, GDP by sector beside employment by sector. The question wants the gap — agriculture fatter on the jobs pie, finance fatter on the money pie — closed with the labour-intensity rule. Shape two: a half-century line graph with the primary line sagging and the tertiary line climbing. The question wants the development story: economies shift their weight from primary through secondary to tertiary as they mature, and South Africa's lines trace it faithfully.

And one calculation to have cold: percentage into rand. If GDP is about seven and a half trillion rand and agriculture earns about three per cent, then agriculture's contribution is seven and a half trillion multiplied by three hundredths — about two hundred and twenty-five billion rand. A tiny share and a mountain of money at the same time — write both halves down, because the contrast is usually the entire point of the question.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the four-step order, the two classic data shapes with their governing rules, and the percentage-to-rand calculation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same economy again, told through a loaf of bread, a hundred-rand note, and a till slip.

## Subtopic: One Loaf of Bread, Four Kinds of Work

Pick up a sliced loaf in any shop. Every kind of work South Africa knows helped put it there, and there are only four kinds.

Somebody GREW the wheat — a farmer in the Swartland pulled it out of soil, sun and winter rain. That is primary work: growing, fishing, forestry, and digging things out of the ground. The test fits in your palm: if the job leaves something raw in your hands — a wheat kernel, a fish, a log, a lump of ore — the work is primary. A mine is simply a farm for rocks, harvesting what nature already made.

Somebody CHANGED the wheat — the mill ground it to flour and the bakery turned flour into bread. That is secondary work: taking raw stuff and making it into something new. Milling, baking, welding, sewing, building, generating power. The magic word is making — and watch what making does to price. A handful of wheat is worth cents; a branded sliced loaf costs real money. That gap is called value added, and it is the reason countries fight to do their own manufacturing instead of shipping raw materials out cheap and buying finished goods back dear.

Somebody MOVED and SOLD the loaf — the delivery van, the supermarket shelf-packer, the cashier, and the bank that carried the money when the card tapped. None of them made anything; they provided services. That is tertiary work: shops, taxis, teachers, nurses, banks, tour guides, government offices. Stand in any shopping centre and look around — nearly every job in sight is tertiary, which quietly tells you what kind of economy South Africa has become.

And somewhere in a laboratory, a plant scientist is breeding a wheat that shrugs off drought. Work done purely with knowledge and information — research, software, data — is quaternary work, the thinking tip of the service world.

Grow it, make it, move-and-sell it, think it up. Four verbs, four sectors, and every job in the country answers to one of them. Take this section's questions now: the four verbs, the raw-in-your-hands test, and the price jump that manufacturing creates.

## Subtopic: Sharing One Hundred Rand Among Four Workers

Now, how big is each kind of work? Shrink the whole economy to a single hundred-rand note and pay the four workers their real shares.

Here is the shock. The farmer — the one who feeds all sixty million of us — walks away with about three rand. The miner, from the industry that built Johannesburg, takes less than ten. The factory worker collects about twenty. And the service workers — the banks, shops, taxis, schools, hospitals, hotels and government offices together — pocket roughly sixty-six rand of the hundred. Two thirds of South Africa's earnings go to people who never grow or manufacture a single physical thing.

Before that feels unjust, two corrections. First: a small share is not a small importance. The farmer's three rand FEEDS EVERYBODY at the table — no wheat, no bread, no country — and farm produce is the raw material the food factories and supermarkets depend on. The miner's share arrives heavy with export earnings. Judge a sector by its percentage alone and you will misjudge it, and questions reward the learner who says so. Second: the hundred-rand note measures money, not people. Count HEADS instead of rands and the shares move — farming employs about double its money share, because farm work needs many hands at modest pay, while a bank is the mirror image: enormous money, few people. One line holds the whole idea: many-hands sectors are bigger in jobs than in money; big-machine, big-money sectors are bigger in money than in jobs. Nearly every table question this term is that line wearing numbers.

One last pair, split by a single letter. GDP counts everything produced inside South Africa's borders — inside the fence — whoever owns the producer. GNP counts everything South African-owned, anywhere on earth. The British-owned bank in Sandton: South Africa's GDP, Britain's GNP. The ground versus the passport.

Your questions for this section are up: the hundred-rand shares, the two corrections, and the fence-versus-passport pair.

## Subtopic: Reading the Numbers Like a Till Slip

Every data question in this topic hands you something to read — a table, a pie, a graph — and panic is optional. Read it the way you check a till slip at the door of the shop: calmly, in order, obvious things first.

First, the top of the slip: which shop, what date, what currency. On a data source that means the title, the units and the year. Money or jobs? Percentages or rands? 1995 or 2024? Half of all data mistakes are cured by the heading the reader skipped. Second, say the loud things: the most expensive item and the cheapest. On a pie chart: "The tertiary sector holds the largest share; agriculture the smallest." That sentence feels too easy to earn a mark — it earns a mark; write it. Third, find the story: what changed since the last slip, which line looks wrong. Two pies from different years — the story is the change between them. A money pie beside a jobs pie — the story is the gap: agriculture's slice fatter on the jobs side, finance's fatter on the money side. Fourth, explain with a reason from the lesson: machines replacing farm and mine labour, services swelling as an economy matures, many hands versus big machines.

If a calculation appears, it will be a percentage of a big number, and the out-of-every-hundred trick handles it. Per cent means out of every hundred rand. If the economy is seven and a half trillion rand and agriculture earns three per cent, then agriculture takes three rand of every hundred — and three of every hundred, stretched across seven and a half trillion, is about two hundred and twenty-five billion rand. Tiny slice, enormous sum — write down both, because that contrast usually IS the question.

Keep two honest sentences loaded for evaluation marks, true of every dataset this term: the figures cannot see the informal economy — the container salons and street traders sustaining millions — and a sector's percentage is not its importance, or farming would matter least in a country that cannot skip a single harvest. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the till-slip reading order, the gap between the two pies, the out-of-every-hundred trick, and the two honest sentences. Read the numbers calmly and the data questions in your final exams become the friendliest marks on the page.
