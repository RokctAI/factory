# Part 1 — Expert

Every day, millions of South Africans are in motion — to work and back, to boarding school and back, to harvest and back — and every year, hundreds of thousands move in a deeper sense: they change where they live. Movement of the second kind rewrites the geography of two places simultaneously, the place emptied and the place filled. This session builds the classification system for population movements, assembles the push-and-pull machinery that explains them, and then works the two case studies at the heart of this topic: the internal stream out of the rural Eastern Cape into Gauteng, and the international streams converging on South Africa, led by the movement from Zimbabwe.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Moves — A Vocabulary for Migration

Begin with the strict definition, because it draws the line every classification question tests. MIGRATION is a permanent or semi-permanent change in a person's PLACE OF RESIDENCE. Residence is the key: a learner travelling to school, a nurse on a night shift and a family on holiday are all moving, but no home address changes, so none of them is migrating. Geographers group those returning movements as CIRCULATION — repeated, temporary journeys that end back at home: the daily commute, the weekly cycle of a boarder, the seasonal round of fruit pickers moving with the picking.

Genuine migration is then classified along three axes.

Axis one asks WHERE. INTERNAL migration stays inside one country — Limpopo to Tshwane, a farm to the district town. At the receiving end the mover is an in-migrant; at the sending end, an out-migrant. INTERNATIONAL migration crosses a state border, and the terminology doubles: the same person EMIGRATES from the country left behind and IMMIGRATES into the country entered — one journey wearing two labels, depending on which country is describing it. Internal migration also has named streams. Rural to urban is the giant in developing countries, and it powers URBANISATION — the growing percentage of a nation living in towns and cities. Urban to rural is its modest reverse current. Rural to rural covers moves like farmworkers shifting between districts, and urban to urban covers moves like leaving East London for Cape Town when a better post opens — the commonest type in wealthy economies.

Axis two asks WHY — chosen or compelled. VOLUNTARY migration is decided freely, usually chasing work, wages, study or family. FORCED migration offers no real alternative: flight from war, persecution or disaster. Learn the legal ladder inside the forced category: a REFUGEE has crossed an international border escaping persecution or conflict; an ASYLUM SEEKER has applied for refugee recognition and awaits the decision; an INTERNALLY DISPLACED PERSON is fleeing the same dangers while still inside their own country. South African history supplies its own forced movements within a border: apartheid removals that demolished communities such as Cato Manor in Durban and South End in Gqeberha, and relocated millions of people into the homelands.

Axis three asks HOW LONG. PERMANENT migration intends never to return; TEMPORARY migration does. The defining South African version of temporary movement was contract labour: for generations, mineworkers spent eleven months of every year on the mines and one month at home, repeating the cycle for a working lifetime. Geographers call that rhythm circular or oscillating migration, and it created households anchored in two places at once.

## Subtopic: Push and Pull — Why People Move

The explanatory engine for any migration question is PUSH AND PULL. PUSH FACTORS are the defects of the origin — conditions that expel. PULL FACTORS are the attractions of the destination — conditions that invite. No migration has only one side: something is always pushing while something else pulls, and a complete answer names both.

Organise the factors into four families. ECONOMIC: joblessness, poverty wages, shrinking farm plots and landlessness push; employment, better pay, markets and the scale of a metropolitan economy pull. Across the world and across history, economic motives power most migration — people overwhelmingly move toward work. SOCIAL: weak schools, remote clinics and absent services push; universities, hospitals, electricity, and above all family and friends already established at the destination pull. That final pull compounds over time: settled relatives provide a bed, advice and job leads, making each subsequent move cheaper and safer — the mechanism called chain migration, and the reason streams widen rather than fade. POLITICAL: conflict, persecution, discrimination and instability push; peace, security and rights pull. ENVIRONMENTAL: drought, flooding, exhausted soils and a shifting climate push; dependable rainfall and productive land pull.

Then add the two refinements that mark out a thoughtful answer. First, migration is a weighed decision, not a reflex. Between origin and destination stand INTERVENING OBSTACLES: transport costs, sheer distance, visas and border posts, and the emotional price of leaving people behind. The move happens only when the expected gain still outweighs the cost after the obstacles are counted. Second, the decision runs on PERCEPTION. Migrants respond to the destination as they imagine it — assembled from a relative's phone calls, photographs and social media — and imagination flatters. The real city holds unemployment queues and backyard rooms as well as opportunity. That gap between the imagined and the actual destination is why streams keep flowing into cities whose job markets are already saturated.

Pause for this section's questions: push against pull across the four families, the obstacles in between, and the role of perception.

## Subtopic: Case Study — Rural Eastern Cape to Gauteng

Now run the machinery on South Africa's biggest internal stream: from the rural Eastern Cape — and comparable former-homeland districts of Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal — toward Gauteng and the other metropolitan areas.

Any serious answer starts with the history, because this stream was engineered before it was chosen. Apartheid's homeland system packed the majority population onto a small fraction of the land — the former Transkei and Ciskei among the designated territories — where soils were poor, industry was absent and services minimal. On top of that, the migrant labour system turned movement into the region's main export: men left on eleven-month contracts to the Witwatersrand mines, slept in single-sex hostels, and posted wages home, while pass laws prohibited their wives and children from joining them. When the legal barriers fell in the 1990s, a century of restrained movement was set loose, and whole families could finally migrate together.

State the pushes, and notice they are the homeland inheritance restated: rural joblessness far beyond the national rate, fragmented plots too small to feed a household, distant clinics and struggling schools, and almost nothing to keep a matriculant at home. State the pulls as the mirror: Gauteng's unmatched concentration of employment, functioning hospitals and better schools, and — the decisive multiplier — home-village networks already rooted in the townships and inner city, so that a new arrival from a village outside Mthatha steps off the bus into a web of relatives who know where the work and the rooms are.

Then argue consequences for BOTH ends, gains and losses at each — that structure is the essay. For the Eastern Cape origin: remittances arrive monthly and hold households upright, and migrants who return bring skills and savings; against that, the province exports its young and able, leaving villages where grandmothers raise grandchildren on grant money, fields lie unploughed, and the population pyramid has a missing middle. For the Gauteng destination: a continuous supply of young, motivated labour feeds the economy, and migrant earnings and enterprises enlarge it; against that, people arrive faster than houses, so backyard shacks and informal settlements multiply, schools and clinics overflow, and too many job-seekers chase too few openings. And the stream never fully cuts its roots: many migrants keep the rural homestead, send money every month, ride home each December on the N2, and speak of retiring to the village. The monthly transfer and the December taxi are part of the migration itself.

## Subtopic: Case Study — Crossing Borders into South Africa

Within southern Africa, South Africa is the destination that outweighs all others — the regional economy that draws the region's people. The single largest inbound stream comes from ZIMBABWE.

The causes are stark. After 2000, Zimbabwe's economy went into freefall: hyperinflation destroyed the currency until prices doubled in days, formal jobs all but vanished, and hospitals and schools decayed — an overwhelming push compounded of economic ruin and political intimidation. South Africa exerted the region's strongest pull: the biggest job market for thousands of kilometres, a long land border, family and church networks already in place, and even low-end wages that convert into meaningful support for relatives at home. Estimates put the Zimbabwean-born population in South Africa above a million — some holding work or study permits, some recognised as asylum seekers, many undocumented.

Classify the stream carefully, because precision earns marks: it is MIXED MIGRATION — voluntary economic migrants, genuine refugees and asylum seekers, and people who are several of those things at once, travelling the same routes. It flows alongside older channels: Basotho and Mozambican miners recruited under formal contract schemes for over a century, and longer-distance refugees and traders from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia and Ethiopia, many of whom build shops and businesses in South African townships.

Weigh the consequences on both sides of the border. For the sending countries: money sent home is a lifeline that feeds families and, in Zimbabwe's case, props up the national economy itself; yet every departing teacher, nurse and artisan deepens a brain drain that makes recovery slower. For South Africa: migrants staff farms, building sites, kitchens, security firms and hospitals, start businesses, and supply skills the economy is short of; yet the arrival of many work-seekers into a country already carrying high unemployment sharpens competition for jobs, housing and services in the poorest neighbourhoods — exactly where hardship already concentrates. That pressure has repeatedly turned toxic: XENOPHOBIC violence against foreign nationals erupted notably in 2008 and again in 2015, killing people, torching shops and shaming the country regionally. An honest case-study answer names the phenomenon, its victims and its cost.

## Subtopic: Every Move Answers Two Questions

Stand on the platform of a long-distance bus depot on a Sunday evening and watch the passengers. Most are loops: workers heading back for the week, learners returning to residence — they will all be home again soon. Their movement is circulation, the returning kind, and it changes no address. But look at the young woman with a full suitcase, a one-way ticket and an address in Johannesburg written on her phone. Her residence is about to change. She is the migrant.

Once you know it is migration, two questions sort every case. Question one: was a BORDER crossed? Stay inside the country — Qumbu to Johannesburg — and it is internal migration. Cross into another country and it becomes international, where one traveller carries two names at once: emigrant in the country being left, immigrant in the country being entered. One journey, two dictionaries.

Question two: was it CHOSEN? A move for work, study or marriage is voluntary. A move to escape war, persecution or disaster is forced — and the forced side has its own precise ladder. A refugee has already crossed a border fleeing danger. An asylum seeker is waiting for the paperwork that says refugee. An internally displaced person is running from the same danger without ever leaving their country.

Then add the word South Africa contributed to the science: oscillating. For about a hundred years, men from the Eastern Cape, Lesotho and Mozambique lived a pendulum life — eleven months in a mine hostel, December in the village — swinging between two homes for a whole career. Never quite arrived, never quite returned. That pendulum financed the goldfields and rearranged millions of families, and its softer version still ticks today: the Midrand security guard whose payslip lives in Gauteng while his family, his cattle and his December live outside Qumbu.

## Subtopic: The Tug-of-War Decision

Why would anyone pack up a life? Picture the would-be migrant as the marker knot in the middle of a tug-of-war rope. On one side, home — but this team scores by pushing: the farm that no longer feeds everyone, the closed mine, the clinic forty kilometres away, the matric certificate with nowhere to go. On the other side, the destination pulls: advertised jobs, a college, streetlights, safety, and a cousin with a spare mattress. When the destination's pull beats home's push-assisted grip, the knot slides — and a person moves. The two teams are push factors and pull factors, and every full answer fields players from both.

Each team runs four kinds of player. Money players — hunger for wages versus wages on offer — and money wins most matches played anywhere on earth. People players — schools, clinics, and especially the family who moved first. Every relative already in Tembisa strengthens the pull, because they shrink the fear: somewhere to sleep, someone who knows which factory is hiring. That is why streams snowball — every successful migrant recruits the next. Safety players — persecution and conflict push with more force than anything else in the game; peace pulls. And land-and-sky players — failed rains and tired soil push; watered ground pulls.

But the rope drags through mud — the obstacles. Bus fare and moving costs. Distance itself. For border-crossers, passports, permits and queues. And the heaviest drag of all: leaving a mother, a child, a home language. The knot only slides when the pull, after all that friction, still wins.

Finish with the truth that explains the puzzle of overfull cities: the migrant is being pulled by a PICTURE. The destination in their head was painted by a cousin's best photographs and proudest stories, and it is sunnier than the real place, which also contains retrenchments and rented backrooms. People answer the picture, not the unemployment statistics — and so the buses stay full even when the jobs are scarce.

Questions on the tug-of-war are up now: the two teams, the four kinds of players, the mud on the rope, and the too-sunny picture.

## Subtopic: The Letter Home

If you want to understand what migration does to places, follow one object: the money that travels home at month end. A generation ago it was a registered letter with folded notes; today it is a supermarket till transfer or a banking app. Millions of these transfers move every month — Gauteng to the former Transkei, South Africa to Zimbabwe, Lesotho and Mozambique — and tracing a single one answers every consequences question this topic can set.

Land where the money lands: a village household. The transfer becomes maize meal, a school uniform, airtime, a grandmother's prepaid electricity — very often the household's largest income source. That is the origin's great gain, together with the skills and savings that come back when a migrant returns for good. Now scan the village for what the money cannot replace: the absent generation. Nobody between eighteen and forty at the funeral on Saturday. Fields turning to grass. A clinic that cannot keep a nurse. The origin gains the remittance and loses the people who earn it — and a full answer holds both truths at once.

Now run the transfer backwards to where it was sent: the city. The city's gain walked in on two feet — a young worker, often skilled, always determined: the bricklayer from Qumbu, the nurse from Bulawayo, the trader from Addis Ababa now running a spaza shop. Economies are stacked out of exactly such arrivals; Gauteng was built by them and still is. But the city strains under the same arithmetic: arrivals outrun houses, so backyard rooms and informal settlements spread; desks, clinic queues and taps fall behind; and at the bottom of the job market, competition turns fierce in the very streets with the least to spare. Twice in recent memory — 2008 and 2015 — that pressure detonated into xenophobic attacks on foreign nationals, with lives lost and livelihoods burned, a cost the country is still counting.

So the answer pattern is a grid of four: origin gains — remittances, returning skills; origin loses — its working-age people; destination gains — labour, energy, enterprise; destination strains — housing, services, social peace. Four boxes, filled, with the Eastern Cape or Zimbabwe attached as your named example — and no consequences question in this topic can catch you.
