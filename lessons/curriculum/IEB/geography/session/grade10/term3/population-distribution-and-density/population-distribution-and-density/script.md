# Part 1 — Expert

Humanity has never covered the planet evenly. Whole subcontinents hold more people than entire hemispheres of ocean, ice and desert, and within a single country the contrast can be just as extreme. This session gives you the two technical terms geographers use for that unevenness, trains the density calculation until the units are automatic, organises the physical and human causes into a framework you can write from, and then turns the framework onto South Africa itself — a country whose biggest concentration of people sits far from any coastline, for reasons that are only partly geographical.

## Subtopic: Distribution and Density — Two Different Ideas

Start with the distinction, because everything else in the topic rests on it, and questions are deliberately written to test whether you keep the two ideas apart.

POPULATION DISTRIBUTION is the PATTERN of where people are found across an area. It is a description in words: even or uneven, clustered or scattered, hugging the coast, following a river, thinning out towards the interior. When you answer a distribution question you are painting a word-picture of a map.

POPULATION DENSITY is a NUMBER: the average count of people for each unit of area, almost always expressed as people per square kilometre. It measures crowding and says nothing about arrangement.

Here is a demonstration that they are genuinely independent. Imagine two rural wards, each covering one thousand two hundred square kilometres, each home to seventy-two thousand people. Divide people by area in both cases and you get sixty people per square kilometre — the densities are identical. But in the first ward the people live on smallholdings spread fairly evenly across the whole area, while in the second every single resident lives in one fishing town tucked into a corner, and the rest of the ward is empty veld. Identical density, opposite distributions. The number answers how many; the pattern answers where.

Build the describing vocabulary now, because it is what distribution answers are made of. DENSELY POPULATED: many people packed into a small area. SPARSELY POPULATED: few people thinly spread over a large one. UNINHABITED: nobody at all, as on ice caps, the harshest deserts and the highest ranges. For the shape of a pattern, three words do most of the work: CLUSTERED, also called nucleated, where settlement bunches around a point; LINEAR, where it strings out along a road, a river or a shoreline; and DISPERSED, where separate homesteads stand apart across open country.

Take this section's questions now: the two definitions held apart, the two-ward demonstration, and the vocabulary for describing a pattern.

## Subtopic: Calculating Density — and What It Hides

The arithmetic is one line; the marks come from the units and from knowing what the answer cannot tell you.

POPULATION DENSITY equals TOTAL POPULATION divided by TOTAL AREA, in people per square kilometre.

Work one through. A district holds one hundred and eighty-nine thousand people on one thousand four hundred square kilometres. Divide: one hundred and thirty-five people per square kilometre. Then write the unit. An answer of one hundred and thirty-five with no unit attached is an unfinished answer, and markers treat it as one.

Now scale it up to the country. South Africa's population has passed sixty-three million, on about one comma two two million square kilometres of land, which averages out at just over fifty people per square kilometre. Set two provinces against that average. KwaZulu-Natal packs roughly a fifth of the nation into a strip of well-watered eastern land, giving it a density of around one hundred and thirty people per square kilometre. The Northern Cape sprawls over nearly a third of the country yet carries a density of under four. Between those two provinces the difference is more than thirtyfold, and the national average of fifty describes neither of them.

That is the first limitation, and stating it in an answer signals real understanding: density is an AVERAGE, and an average buries its extremes. A national figure blends near-empty Bushmanland with the packed streets of Umlazi and pretends they are one place. The smaller the area you calculate over, the more honest the number becomes; over a whole country it can actively deceive.

The second limitation concerns the land itself. Ordinary arithmetic density divides people by ALL land — dune, mountain, salt pan, everything. Two territories with the same figure may face utterly different pressure if one is mostly farmland and the other mostly rock. For that reason geographers also calculate a density per unit of ARABLE land, which reveals how many mouths each productive hectare actually has to feed.

Pause for this section's questions: the calculation with its unit, the provincial contrast, and the two limitations of the density figure.

## Subtopic: Physical Factors Affecting Distribution

Sort every cause under one of two headings, physical or human, and structure written answers the same way. The physical list first.

RELIEF. Level and gently rolling land invites ploughs, foundations and roads, so it fills with people. Steep, broken terrain resists all three — soils slide thin, access is hard, building is expensive — which is why the high interior of Lesotho and the Drakensberg escarpment carry so few people compared with the lowlands beneath them. In hot latitudes, though, altitude can attract rather than repel, because highland air is cooler and healthier.

CLIMATE. No single physical factor matters more. Where temperatures are moderate and rain arrives reliably, farming thrives and the great population belts of the world have grown. The repelling extremes are just as clear: the Namib and the Sahara turn people away with drought, Siberia and the polar fringe with cold that freezes the growing season, and the Congo basin's rainforest with leached, hungry soils, impenetrable growth and disease.

WATER SUPPLY. There is no settlement without fresh water, full stop. People gather at rivers, springs, lakes and dependable boreholes, which is why ancient dense populations line the Nile and the Ganges, and why in arid country the map so often shows a linear thread of villages tracing a single river.

SOILS. Deep fertile soil feeds many people from each hectare and so supports dense rural settlement. The volcanic soils of Java and the delta silts of Bangladesh carry some of the heaviest rural densities on the planet; sandy, thin or salt-poisoned ground carries almost nobody.

NATURAL RESOURCES. A mineral deposit is fixed in the earth, so the people must travel to it. Mining plants dense settlement in places every other factor argues against.

VEGETATION AND DISEASE. Dense forest obstructs clearing and movement, and disease organisms have shaped the map too — malaria historically kept farmers out of low-lying African river valleys for generations.

ACCESSIBILITY. Natural harbours, navigable rivers and easy passes through mountains funnel trade, and where trade concentrates, people concentrate.

Your questions on this section are with you now: the seven physical factors, each paired with a place it attracts and a place it repels.

## Subtopic: Human Factors and South Africa's Pattern

HUMAN and ECONOMIC factors sit on top of the physical canvas, and in a modern economy they usually have the last word.

WORK leads the list: people migrate to jobs, and jobs cluster where mines, factories and offices cluster. TRANSPORT ROUTES pull settlement into corridors along highways, railways and ports. SERVICES — schools, clinics, piped water, electricity, network coverage — make places liveable and their absence empties them. GOVERNMENT AND HISTORY redraw the map through land laws, forced relocation and the placing of capitals and industries. SOCIAL BONDS — family, language, faith, community — anchor people where pure economics would not.

Now apply the whole framework to South Africa, in the order that earns marks: state the pattern, point to the evidence, then explain it.

The pattern is sharply uneven. A moisture line divides the country: east of the region where annual rainfall drops below roughly five hundred millimetres the land is comparatively well settled, while the western two-fifths lie sparse. On top of that divide sit four great urban concentrations. GAUTENG is the giant and the puzzle — an inland province with no harbour, no navigable river and unremarkable grassland, yet holding around a quarter of all South Africans on less than two percent of the land. The answer lies underground: the gold of the Witwatersrand, struck in the eighteen-eighties, summoned workers, then the factories that supplied the mines, then the banks that financed the factories, and the whole structure outlived the gold itself. The DURBAN region grew on the country's busiest harbour and the industry bolted to it. CAPE TOWN grew from the oldest anchorage into a centre of services, tourism and government. The GQEBERHA and EAST LONDON coast grew on harbours and vehicle manufacturing.

One more feature demands explanation, because the land cannot supply it: remarkably high RURAL densities in parts of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and the eastern margins, on soil that could never support such numbers by farming. These are the former homeland territories — areas such as QwaQwa, Venda and Gazankulu — into which apartheid legislation compressed the majority of the population on a fraction of the land. Those densities are a political inheritance written onto the landscape, and they still drive the stream of people moving toward the cities.

The empty quarter completes the map: the Karoo, Bushmanland, the Kalahari margins and Namaqualand, all rationed by scarce, unreliable rain, with vast farms, scattered hamlets and the occasional mining town.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: the human factors listed, the four urban concentrations explained, and the former-homeland densities accounted for.

Now the same ideas with a bus, a beach and a drive up the west coast.

## Subtopic: The Bus and the Beach

Picture sixty passengers packed into one bus on a hot afternoon. Now put the same sixty people onto a long open beach. Nothing about the people changed — only the space did. That squeezed feeling in the bus and that lost feeling on the beach is DENSITY: people divided by space, nothing more.

Now the part everyone misses, so watch closely. Keep all sixty on the beach and arrange them twice. First, spread them out along the waterline, one towel every fifty paces, end to end. Then call them all over to crowd around the tidal pool at one end, leaving the rest of the sand bare.

Count people: sixty both times. Measure the beach: same beach. The density has not moved. But the map of the beach looks completely different — and that arrangement is DISTRIBUTION.

Lock it in like this. Density is a NUMBER and it answers one question: how crowded? Distribution is a PICTURE and it answers a different question: where is everybody? If a question asks for people per square kilometre, it wants the number. If it asks you to describe where people are, it wants words — bunched at the pool, strung along the water, empty in the middle.

The describing words are a short list. Dense: many in little space. Sparse: few in lots of space. Clustered: gathered at a spot. Linear: following a line, like the towels along the waterline or towns along a river. Dispersed: scattered apart like farmhouses in the Karoo.

And one warning about the number. It is an average, and averages flatten the truth. South Africa averages a little over fifty people per square kilometre, and that figure describes nowhere real — it is silent Bushmanland and buzzing Umlazi mashed into one made-up place.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the bus and the beach are coming to you right now: which idea is the number, which is the picture, and why the average misleads.

## Subtopic: Why the Richtersveld Stays Empty

Try this thought experiment. You are dropped into an empty country and told to pick a place to live, guided only by the land. Whatever you find yourself checking for — that is the list of physical factors, and you already carry it in your head.

WATER comes first, every time. No water, no people; it is as blunt as that. Drive through the Richtersveld in the far north-west and you can watch the rule operating: baking rock, almost no rain, and almost nobody — until you reach the Orange River, where a thin green line of irrigated farms and little settlements clings to the water. Dry country does not scatter its people; it strings them along the river like beads.

FLAT GROUND next. Level land ploughs easily, builds cheaply and takes a road without a fight. The Richtersveld's shattered mountains offer none of that, and neither do the high Maloti peaks across the country — which is why the valleys below them hold the people the mountains refuse.

BEARABLE WEATHER. Enough rain to grow food, heat you can work in, cold that does not kill the harvest. The world's empty zones are the places where weather says no: too dry, too cold, or rainforest where the soil gives up as soon as the trees fall.

GOOD SOIL. Rich earth feeds more people per field, so deep-soiled farmland fills up while sand stays bare.

Then two wildcards that overrule everything above. MINERALS: the ore cannot come to you, so you go to it — Kathu exists in the middle of the dry Northern Cape purely because iron ore lies beneath it, and diamonds once pulled thousands to the bleak coast at Alexander Bay. And ACCESS: a sheltered bay or a gap through the mountains gathers trade, and people gather on trade — Saldanha's deep natural harbour is the reason a steel-and-ore port sits on an otherwise quiet stretch of west coast.

Now stand back and look at South Africa. Rain falls generously in the east and fails in the west, and the people follow the rain almost exactly. Before you mention a single mine or city, the land has already drawn most of the map.

Your questions on the land are up now: water, relief, climate and soil as attractors and repellers, and the two wildcards that override them.

## Subtopic: Follow the Jobs

Here is the one thing rain and soil cannot explain, and it is the loudest fact on the South African map.

The country's biggest crowd of people lives in Gauteng — a province with no beach, no harbour, no great river, and grass like anywhere else on the highveld. Judged purely by the land, it should be ordinary farming country. Instead, about a quarter of the nation lives on less than two percent of the ground. Why?

Because in the eighteen-eighties, prospectors hit the richest gold reef ever found, and one discovery set off a chain that never stopped. Gold needs miners, so miners streamed in from every direction. Miners need houses, food, shops, schools and transport, so towns boomed. Mines need drills and pumps, so engineering works opened. Industry needs money, so banks and offices stacked up. Rail lines were laid to feed the mines, and every later business rode on those same rails. Today the reef is largely mined out — and the crowd remains, because jobs, roads and people, once gathered, hold one another in place.

That is the master rule of human geography: people chase work, and work grows where people already are. Watch the same rule run elsewhere. Richards Bay was a quiet lagoon until a deep harbour was built there in the nineteen-seventies; the harbour drew coal exports, the exports drew smelters, and the smelters drew a city. eMalahleni sits on coalfields, and the power stations and collieries around it did exactly what the gold did for Johannesburg, on a smaller scale. Ports like Durban and Cape Town ran the chain in the older way: harbour brings trade, trade brings factories, factories bring jobs, jobs bring people.

And then there is the pattern that neither jobs nor rain explains: crowded rural districts in the old homeland areas of the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal, where far more people live than the thin farmland can feed. That is not geography's doing — it is history's. The law once fenced the majority of South Africans into those small territories, and the crowding they carry today is inherited, not chosen. It is also the engine pushing workers from those districts to the cities, year after year.

So when you describe South Africa's population map, say three things and you have said it all: full east and empty west, because of rain; four great city clusters, because of gold and harbours; and crowded rural pockets, because of history.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — why Gauteng exists where it does, how the harbour towns grew, and why the rural east is crowded.
