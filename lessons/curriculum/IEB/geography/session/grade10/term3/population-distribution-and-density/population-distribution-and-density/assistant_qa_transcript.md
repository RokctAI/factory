**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the lesson said the density formula is easy, so where do people actually lose marks on it?

**Tutor:** Almost never on the division — on everything wrapped around it. The first leak is the unit: an answer of one hundred and thirty-five means nothing until you write people per square kilometre after it, and an answer without a unit is treated as incomplete. The second leak is mismatched units going in: if the question gives you an area in hectares or the population in thousands, convert before you divide, and show the conversion, because method marks live in that line. The third leak is interpretation. A calculation question is very often followed by a comment question, and the expected comment is that your answer is an average — it flattens the contrast between crowded and empty parts of the area, so the smaller the area you calculate over, the more honest the figure. And the fourth leak is choosing the wrong tool entirely: if the question asks you to describe where people are, no calculation is wanted at all — that is a distribution question and it needs words. Read the question stem first, decide whether it wants the number or the picture, and only then pick up the calculator.

**Thandi:** Next: the lesson called climate the strongest physical factor, but then spent ages on Gauteng, which has nothing special about its climate. Which one actually decides where people live?

**Tutor:** Both, but at different scales, and saying that clearly is what a top answer does. Zoom out to the whole country and climate is plainly in command: rainfall thins westward and so do people, which is why Bushmanland and the Karoo are nearly empty while the eastern seaboard is full. Physical factors set the outer limits — they decide where people CAN live. But zoom in to one region and a single human fact can trump the whole physical list. The Witwatersrand's gold could not be moved, so people moved to it, and each arrival made the next arrival more likely: miners needed shops and schools, mines needed workshops, workshops needed banks, and the railways built for the ore carried everything that came after. That self-reinforcing pile-up is why Gauteng still dominates long after the gold stopped paying. So the framework for any explain-the-pattern question is layered: physical factors sketch the broad canvas, human factors paint the detail, and where they conflict in a modern economy, the human factors usually win. An answer that keeps those two scales separate reads like a geographer wrote it.

**Thandi:** This one came up in our study group. The old homeland areas are crowded, but crowded rural places usually mean good farmland — and the lesson said the land there is poor. How do both things stay true?

**Tutor:** They stay true because the crowding was never produced by the land — it was produced by law. Under the segregation and apartheid land measures of the twentieth century, the majority of South Africans were legally restricted to designated reserves that later became homelands, territories like QwaQwa, Venda and Gazankulu, chosen for political convenience rather than farming quality. Large populations were held on small areas of mostly marginal land, so the usual rule — dense rural settlement signals fertile soil — simply does not apply there; the signal was overridden. The consequences are still visible in three ways. Ecologically, too many people and livestock on thin soil produced overgrazing and erosion. Economically, the land cannot feed its population, so households depend heavily on wages sent home from the cities and on social support. And demographically, those districts export working-age adults to Gauteng, Durban and Cape Town in a steady stream, which hollows out the middle of their population structure. Whenever a South African population map shows you density that farming cannot explain, reach for history — an answer that stops at rainfall and jobs has missed the third layer.

**Thandi:** Last one: when a question shows a population map and says describe and explain the distribution, what should the first sentence be?

**Tutor:** A single overview sentence that names the pattern before any detail: the distribution is uneven, with population concentrated in the east and along the southern coast, and very sparse across the western interior. That opening line earns its mark on its own and sets up everything after it. Then work in fixed moves. Locate the dense areas by name and compass direction — a concentration in the north-eastern interior, ribbons of settlement along the eastern seaboard. Locate the sparse areas the same way — very low densities in the west and north-west. Then attach a reason to each observation, pairing one physical cause with one human cause wherever you can: the east is watered and the west is not; the interior cluster grew on mining and industry, the coastal ones on harbours and trade. Two habits lift the answer further. Quote the map's own key — writing over one hundred people per square kilometre shows you read the legend rather than guessed. And never write here or over there; use compass directions, because the marker sees only your words, not your finger on the map.
