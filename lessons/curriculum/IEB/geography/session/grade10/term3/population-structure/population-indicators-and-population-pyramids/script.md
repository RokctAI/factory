# Part 1 — Expert

Hand a geographer five numbers and one graph, and they can tell you a country's past, presence and probable future. The five numbers are the population indicators — birth rate, death rate, total fertility, infant mortality and life expectancy — and the graph is the population pyramid, which compresses an entire nation's age and sex structure into a single silhouette. This session defines each indicator with exam precision, explains the forces that move it, then teaches you to build, read and interpret pyramids — ending with South Africa's own numbers and its own distinctive shape.

## Subtopic: The Vital Statistics — Birth, Death and Natural Increase

Indicators exist to make unfair comparisons fair. India records vastly more births than Botswana for the trivial reason that it holds vastly more people; to compare like with like, geographers express the vital events as a rate per thousand people per year.

The CRUDE BIRTH RATE counts live births per thousand people in one year. South Africa currently records about nineteen per thousand. Mali, near the top of the world table, exceeds forty per thousand. South Korea, near the bottom, has fallen below six. The label crude means the rate is computed across the entire population — infants, teenagers, pensioners, everyone — with no adjustment for who could actually bear children.

The CRUDE DEATH RATE counts deaths per thousand people per year; South Africa's stands near nine. Handle death rates with suspicion, because they conceal a famous trap: several of the world's healthiest countries post HIGHER death rates than much poorer ones. Italy, with superb hospitals, runs a death rate above ten per thousand, while Uganda, far poorer, sits below six. The explanation is age structure, not medicine: Italy is full of the elderly, and the elderly die at higher rates in even the best care, while Uganda's population is overwhelmingly young. A low crude death rate may prove nothing except youth.

Subtracting gives the RATE OF NATURAL INCREASE: births minus deaths, per thousand. For South Africa, nineteen minus nine leaves ten per thousand; divide by ten and the growth rate is about one percent a year from vital events alone. The word natural is a fence around the calculation — migration is deliberately excluded, so a country with healthy natural increase can still lose population if emigration is heavy enough.

What moves these rates? Birth rates run high where children are field hands and old-age insurance, where many infants die so parents bear extra as a hedge, and where girls leave school early and contraception is out of reach. They fall as women's education advances, as child survival becomes normal, and as urban life turns children from earners into expenses. Death rates fall with clean water, immunisation, sufficient food and functioning clinics — and climb again with epidemic, famine and war.

Take this section's questions now: the three rates defined per thousand, the natural increase calculation, and the age-structure trap inside the death rate.

## Subtopic: Fertility, Infant Mortality and Life Expectancy

Three deeper indicators complete the panel, each seeing something the crude rates miss.

The TOTAL FERTILITY RATE is the average number of children a woman would bear across her lifetime under current patterns — and it is the best single forecast of future growth. Memorise the threshold: 2,1, called REPLACEMENT LEVEL. Two children replace two parents; the extra decimal allows for children who never reach adulthood. Sustained fertility above 2,1 eventually grows a population; below it, the population eventually contracts, however wealthy the country. Somalia's fertility runs above six. South Korea's has collapsed below one — the lowest ever recorded for a nation — and Spain hovers near 1,2, which is why both face shrinking futures. South Africa's has slid from about six children per woman in the 1960s to roughly 2,3 today: a fraction above replacement, still easing downward.

The INFANT MORTALITY RATE counts babies per thousand live births who die before their first birthday, and development economists treat it as the single most revealing statistic a country produces — because keeping infants alive demands that everything works simultaneously: safe water, adequate food, cold-chain vaccines, staffed clinics, mothers educated enough to use them. The Central African Republic loses more than seventy babies per thousand; Iceland and Finland lose fewer than two. South Africa has brought its rate down to around twenty-five per thousand — far better than the 1990s, yet still an order of magnitude above the world's best.

LIFE EXPECTANCY is the years a newborn can expect to live under today's conditions. Switzerland exceeds eighty-four. South Africa stands near sixty-five, and the path of that number is a national story worth telling in any essay: at the height of the HIV and Aids epidemic in the early 2000s, before treatment was widely available, South African life expectancy sank to around fifty-three years; the antiretroviral programme that followed — now the largest in the world — restored more than a decade. Remember also that life expectancy is an average with a heavy anchor: infant deaths drag it down disproportionately, so a low figure never means nobody grows old.

A final discipline for written answers: never classify a country from a single indicator. Argue from two or three in agreement — high fertility with high infant mortality and short life expectancy tells one consistent story; any lone number can lie.

## Subtopic: Reading a Population Pyramid

The population pyramid is the one graph this topic demands you both read and describe in words, so learn its anatomy verbally.

Construction first. Down the centre of the page runs a vertical axis. Off that axis, at each level, extend two horizontal bars back to back: the LEFT bar counts males, the RIGHT bar counts females. Each level is a five-year AGE COHORT, stacked in order: nought to four at the bottom, five to nine above it, ten to fourteen next, climbing cohort by cohort to the very old at the summit. Bar LENGTH shows the number — or percentage — of people of that age and sex. Children therefore form the base, elders the peak, and the outline of the whole stack is the shape you interpret.

Three habits unlock any pyramid. Habit one: inspect the BASE. A broad base signals a high recent birth rate; a base narrower than the bars above it signals that births are declining right now. Habit two: follow the SLOPE of the sides. A steep inward taper from cohort to cohort means many people are lost between age groups — high mortality; near-vertical sides mean most citizens survive into old age. Habit three: hunt for IRREGULARITIES. A notch — one cohort abruptly shorter than its neighbours — records a past catastrophe: war, epidemic or famine that killed members of that generation or suppressed the births that would have created it. A bulge records a baby boom or an inflow of migrants of that age. And a longer female bar in the top cohorts appears almost everywhere, because women outlive men.

The pyramid also feeds a calculation: the DEPENDENCY RATIO. Split the population into YOUNG DEPENDANTS, nought to fourteen; the ECONOMICALLY ACTIVE, fifteen to sixty-four; and AGED DEPENDANTS, sixty-five and older. Add the two dependant groups, divide by the economically active, multiply by one hundred — the answer is dependants per hundred workers. Work one: if thirty percent of a nation is under fifteen and six percent is over sixty-four, the dependants total thirty-six percent against sixty-four percent active; thirty-six divided by sixty-four times one hundred gives about fifty-six dependants per hundred workers. A wide base loads the ratio with children, straining schools and clinics; a heavy top loads it with pensioners, straining pensions and hospitals.

Your questions on this section are ready now: how the bars are arranged, the three reading habits, and the dependency calculation.

## Subtopic: Three Pyramid Shapes and South Africa's Own

Interpretation questions revolve around three classic silhouettes, each a chapter in a national story.

The EXPANSIVE pyramid is a genuine triangle: a very broad base tapering rapidly to a needle peak, every cohort visibly smaller than the one beneath it. It belongs to countries with high birth rates and high or recently high death rates: swarms of children, few elders, brisk growth, short life expectancy — the profile of Mali, Uganda and Angola. Its future is preloaded: today's giant child cohorts will become tomorrow's giant parent cohorts, so the population keeps expanding for decades even after family size starts falling. That built-in continuation is population momentum.

The STATIONARY pyramid rises with almost vertical sides — cohorts of nearly equal length from childhood into late middle age, narrowing only near the top where old age finally cuts in. Births roughly balance deaths, growth idles near zero, life expectancy is long. Australia and Denmark approximate the shape.

The CONSTRICTIVE pyramid is the counter-intuitive one: the base is NARROWER than the bars above — pinched at the bottom, swollen through the middle, substantial at the top. In words: the country contains fewer young children than young adults, because fertility has dropped below replacement. The population is ageing and headed for contraction — Japan, Italy and South Korea are the textbook cases — and the coming problem is arithmetic: each decade shifts the swollen middle upward, leaving fewer workers beneath more pensioners.

Now hold South Africa's pyramid up to the light. It is TRANSITIONAL — caught between expansive and stationary. The base remains fairly broad, since fertility at about 2,3 still exceeds replacement, but the bottom cohorts have stopped outgrowing the ones above them. A pronounced bulge fills the twenties and thirties. Above that, the sides taper faster than a wealthy country's would, carrying among other things the scar of the Aids years, which cut into the cohorts now reaching middle age. The topmost bars lean female, as they do worldwide. The reading: South Africa is moving from a young, fast-growing population toward a slower, older one — and at this moment it holds an unusually large working-age share relative to its dependants. Demographers call that window a demographic dividend, and it pays out only if the economy can employ the bulge that creates it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three shapes matched to countries, the meaning of a pinched base, and the reading of South Africa's own pyramid.

## Subtopic: A Country's Report Card

Treat the five indicators as a report card that every country carries, with five subjects written on it.

Subject one, births: how many babies per thousand people per year — the birth rate. Subject two, deaths: how many deaths per thousand per year — the death rate. Subtract deaths from births and you have natural increase — the speed the population grows all by itself, before a single suitcase is packed. South Africa: roughly nineteen births, roughly nine deaths, so about ten per thousand — one percent a year.

Then the three subjects that dig deeper. Fertility: how many children the average woman raises in a lifetime. Carry one number out of this course forever: 2,1. Two children stand in for two parents, and the decimal covers the children who never grow up. Above 2,1 a country's future grows; below it, the future shrinks — money cannot argue with it. South Korea has fallen below one child per woman, and its classrooms are already emptying.

Infant mortality: out of every thousand babies born alive, how many never reach their first birthday. This is the strictest subject on the card, and the most honest, because a baby survives only if everything works at once — the water, the food, the vaccines, the clinic, the mother's schooling. A good score here means the whole system is passing.

Life expectancy: the years a newborn can expect. And here lives the favourite trick question: a LOW life expectancy does not mean nobody gets old. It is an average, and every lost baby hauls the average down. A country where half of all babies die but survivors reach eighty averages out around forty — while its villages are full of grandparents.

Close with the golden caution: never grade a country on one subject. Italy's death rate is higher than Uganda's — not because Italian hospitals fail, but because Italy is old and Uganda is young. Always ask WHO makes up the population before trusting any rate.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: the five indicators in plain words, the number 2,1, and the Italy-and-Uganda trap.

## Subtopic: The Pyramid as a Family Photograph

Imagine photographing the whole country at once — sixty-odd million people — but arranged by a strict photographer. Everyone lines up in rows by AGE: babies and toddlers in the front row, five-to-nines in the second, ten-to-fourteens in the third, and so on, row upon row, until the eldest citizens stand at the very back. One more instruction: in every row, males gather on the left of the centre aisle, females on the right.

Now climb a ladder high above the crowd and look straight down. The OUTLINE of that arrangement is the population pyramid. A packed front row shrinking to sparse back rows: a land of children. Rows of nearly equal length front to back: ages evenly spread. A front row SHORTER than the rows behind it: a nation that has slowed its baby-making.

That is the entire graph decoded. Each pair of bars is one row of the photograph — a five-year age group — males drawn left of centre, females right, youngest at the bottom, oldest at the top, longer bar meaning more people. No further mystery.

And the photograph is also an archive. A row oddly SHORTER than its neighbours — as if a bite were taken from the crowd — marks a generation thinned by war, epidemic or famine, either killed or never born. A row that bulges beyond its neighbours marks a baby boom or a wave of migrants who arrived at that age. And in the back rows the right-hand side outnumbers the left, because women outlast men nearly everywhere.

Finally, slice the crowd into three blocks. The front block, under fifteen: too young to work. The back block, sixty-five and up: retired. The wide middle block earns for all three. The front and back are the dependants, and the question every treasury asks is blunt: how many dependants must each hundred workers carry?

Questions on the photograph are up now: who stands where, what a short row means, and the three blocks.

## Subtopic: Wide Base, Narrow Base — What the Shape Tells You

Three shapes to know on sight. Picture them as stacks of crates in a warehouse.

Stack one: a true pyramid — an enormous bottom layer, each layer above smaller, finishing in a point. The EXPANSIVE shape. Translation: children everywhere, elders scarce, population sprinting. Mali, Uganda, Angola. And read the future hidden inside it: today's colossal bottom layers become tomorrow's colossal parent layers, so the sprint continues for decades even if families begin shrinking immediately. That is momentum, and it is why growth outlives the birth rates that caused it.

Stack two: a column — layers of almost identical width from the floor to well past the middle, narrowing only near the ceiling. The STATIONARY shape. Births balance deaths, the population cruises at a steady size, and most citizens see old age. Think Australia or Denmark.

Stack three should make you look twice: the bottom layer is SMALLER than the layers resting on it — pinched at the floor, bulging in the middle, weighty on top. The CONSTRICTIVE shape. It means the country holds fewer small children than young adults: families have dropped below replacement. Japan, Italy, South Korea. Roll this stack forward thirty years in your mind: the bulging middle rises to become the heavy top, the pinched floor rises to become a thin middle — many pensioners balanced on few workers. That is why such governments plead, pay and advertise for babies.

And South Africa's stack? Between the pyramid and the column, and shifting. Our bottom layers are still broad — fertility of about 2,3 keeps us above replacement — but they have stopped broadening. A conspicuous bulge fills the twenties and thirties. The taper above runs steeper than a rich country's, still carrying the mark of the Aids years across the middle-aged layers. For now, the country enjoys a rare arrangement: an outsized working-age middle relative to children and pensioners — the demographic dividend, a one-time opening that converts to prosperity only if that bulge finds work.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the three shapes, what a pinched base predicts, and where South Africa's stack fits.
