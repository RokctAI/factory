# Part 1 — Expert

No human being, no drill and no machine has ever reached even the bottom of the crust. The deepest borehole on record, the Kola Superdeep in the Russian Arctic, stopped a little past twelve kilometres; the deepest mine on earth, Mponeng near Carletonville, reaches about four — and the centre of the planet lies 6 371 kilometres down. On a classroom globe, everything humans have ever touched is thinner than the varnish. Yet geographers can state the thickness, composition, temperature and physical state of every internal layer with confidence. This session builds that picture layer by layer, separates the two kinds of crust, and then answers the question that separates strong candidates from average ones: how we actually know, because the seismic evidence is examinable in its own right and everything in plate tectonics stands on it.

## Subtopic: The Layers by Composition — Crust, Mantle, Core

Divided by composition, the earth has three layers, and one master rule runs through all of them: temperature, pressure and density all climb as you go down.

The CRUST is the rigid, rocky outer shell, and its defining fact is how little of the planet it is — between roughly 5 and 70 kilometres thick, well under one per cent of the earth's radius. Even at Mponeng, four kilometres down, miners are still near the top of it, though the rock face there is already hot enough to need refrigerated air. The base of the crust is a sharp boundary called the MOHOROVIČIĆ DISCONTINUITY — mercifully shortened by everyone to the Moho — identified because earthquake waves abruptly accelerate the moment they cross into the denser rock below.

Beneath it lies the MANTLE, about 2 900 kilometres of dense silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium, mostly a rock called peridotite. It makes up roughly 84 per cent of the earth's volume, which makes it the giant of the three layers. Fix its physical state in your mind now, because this is the single most heavily penalised mistake in the topic: the mantle is SOLID rock. It is not an underground ocean of lava. Within its upper portion, though, from about 100 to 250 kilometres down, lies the ASTHENOSPHERE, a zone where the rock sits so close to its melting point that it behaves plastically. Think of road tar on a January afternoon in Musina: solid enough to stand on, yet soft enough to hold a footprint by evening. Rock in the asthenosphere creeps in exactly that way, and that slow creep is what allows tectonic plates to move at all.

At about 2 900 kilometres the GUTENBERG DISCONTINUITY marks the top of the CORE, a sphere of iron and nickel in two parts. The OUTER CORE, from about 2 900 down to 5 150 kilometres, is LIQUID, and the slow churning of that molten metal is the dynamo that generates the earth's magnetic field. The INNER CORE, from 5 150 kilometres to the centre, is SOLID — and it hands you the topic's best paradox. It is the hottest place in the planet, somewhere between 5 000 and 6 000 degrees Celsius, roughly the temperature of the sun's surface, yet it stays solid because the weight of everything above squeezes the iron so ferociously that it cannot loosen into a liquid.

Take this section's questions now: the three layers with their depths, the discontinuities named, and the state of each layer given with its reason.

## Subtopic: Continental Versus Oceanic Crust

The crust is not a single material. It comes in two varieties, and every contrast between them pays off later in the term.

CONTINENTAL crust carries the traditional name SIAL, from its dominant silica and aluminium. It is thick — typically 30 to 50 kilometres, swelling to 70 beneath major mountain belts — granitic in composition, and comparatively LIGHT at a density of about 2,7 grams per cubic centimetre. It is also immensely old. The Makhonjwa Mountains around Barberton in Mpumalanga contain rocks more than three billion years old, among the most ancient surviving pieces of crust found anywhere, and the granite beneath Johannesburg is a similar antique.

OCEANIC crust is SIMA, from silica and magnesium. It is thin — a mere 5 to 10 kilometres — basaltic, DENSER at around 3,0 grams per cubic centimetre, and startlingly young. Nowhere on the planet is the ocean floor older than about 200 million years, because oceanic crust is endlessly manufactured at mid-ocean ridges and endlessly consumed at subduction zones. Continents are the earth's archive; the sea floor is a page that keeps being torn out and rewritten.

That density gap carries one enormous consequence: wherever oceanic and continental crust are driven together, the DENSER oceanic slab sinks beneath the lighter continental one. Subduction is not a rule invented by textbooks — it is simply buoyancy doing its work.

Now the second way of slicing the planet, and you must be able to use both. The layers above were defined by COMPOSITION. Defined instead by MECHANICAL BEHAVIOUR, the outer earth divides into the LITHOSPHERE — the rigid shell made of the crust PLUS the cold, stiff uppermost mantle, about 100 kilometres thick in total, broken into the tectonic plates — and the plastic asthenosphere beneath, which the plates slide across. So a plate is never just crust: it is crust with a slab of rigid mantle welded underneath.

Your questions on this section are with you now: sial against sima on thickness, composition, density and age; why no ocean floor is ancient; and the lithosphere distinguished from the crust.

## Subtopic: How We Know — Seismic Evidence

Now for the evidence, because a claim about a place nobody can visit is worthless without it.

Every large earthquake fires waves through the entire body of the planet, and the two types of BODY WAVE behave differently in different materials. P-WAVES, the primary waves, are compressional: they squeeze and stretch the rock along their direction of travel. They are the fastest, arrive first, and pass through SOLIDS, LIQUIDS AND GASES alike. S-WAVES, the secondary waves, are transverse: they whip the rock from side to side across their path. They are slower, arrive second, and they are completely unable to cross a LIQUID, because a liquid has no rigidity with which to pass on a sideways shear.

Here is what the worldwide network of seismic stations records after every major earthquake. Beyond about 103 degrees of angular distance from the epicentre there is an S-WAVE SHADOW ZONE: across the whole far side of the planet, S-waves simply never arrive. Not weakly — not at all. Only one thing stops an S-wave dead, and that is liquid. The vanished S-waves are the proof that the outer core is molten.

P-waves complete the map. They do cross the core, but they are strongly REFRACTED — bent — at the moment they pass from solid mantle into liquid outer core, and that bending sweeps them away from a band of the surface between roughly 103 and 143 degrees, the P-wave shadow zone. The width of that band fixes the depth of the core-mantle boundary with remarkable precision. And faint P-wave arrivals detected inside the shadow zone can only be explained by yet another bend at a deeper boundary — the LEHMANN DISCONTINUITY — which is how the solid inner core was discovered.

Three independent lines of support close the case. Density: the planet as a whole averages about 5,5 grams per cubic centimetre, yet surface rocks average only about 2,7, so the deep interior must be far heavier than anything at the surface — metal, in other words. Magnetism: a planetary magnetic field demands circulating liquid metal, and the outer core supplies exactly that. Meteorites: iron meteorites are understood to be core fragments of shattered planetary bodies, and their iron-nickel composition matches what the earth's core must contain.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: P-waves against S-waves, the two shadow zones and what each one proves, and the three supporting arguments.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Interior in Words — and the Exam Traps

Cross-section questions ask you to label or describe the interior, so practise delivering it in one fixed order: name, depth range, composition, physical state, one distinguishing feature. Crust — surface to between 5 and 70 kilometres, silicate rock, solid, occurring in two varieties. The Moho. Mantle — down to 2 900 kilometres, iron-magnesium silicate rock, solid but plastic within the asthenosphere, the largest layer by volume. The Gutenberg discontinuity. Outer core — to 5 150 kilometres, iron and nickel, liquid, source of the magnetic field. The Lehmann discontinuity. Inner core — to the centre at 6 371 kilometres, iron and nickel, solid under crushing pressure, hottest of all at 5 000 to 6 000 degrees.

Then guard against the four classic traps. TRAP ONE: calling the mantle molten. It is solid rock; only the asthenosphere deforms plastically, and even that creeps rather than flows freely. Magma occurs in isolated pockets, never as a worldwide sea. TRAP TWO: reasoning that the inner core must be liquid because it is hottest. State depends on pressure as well as temperature, and at the centre pressure wins the argument. TRAP THREE: treating crust and plate as the same word. A plate is lithosphere — crust plus rigid upper mantle. TRAP FOUR: swapping sial and sima. Continental crust is thick, light, granitic and ancient; oceanic crust is thin, dense, basaltic and young — and it is the density, nothing else, that drags it downward at a subduction zone.

Hold this framework and the rest of the term clicks into place: plates of lithosphere, powered by heat from a core nobody will ever see, gliding on plastic rock nobody will ever touch — every step of it resting on the seismic evidence you can now cite. The final questions of this part are with you now: the cross-section delivered in order, and all four traps stated correctly.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same planet again — through a peach, a farm dam, and the trick for choosing a watermelon.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is a Peach

Bite a peach down to the stone and you are holding a scale model of the earth, and a better one than you might think.

The SKIN is the crust. Feel how thin it is against the rest of the fruit — that is honestly generous. The real crust runs from about five to seventy kilometres deep on a planet six thousand three hundred and seventy-one kilometres to the middle. Scaled to a peach, the crust is thinner than the fuzz. Every city, every farm, every mine and every ocean sits in that skin.

The FLESH is the mantle, and exactly as in the peach it is most of the fruit — about two thousand nine hundred kilometres of it, roughly eighty-four per cent of the planet's volume. Now the fact that learners get wrong more than any other: the mantle is NOT liquid. It is solid rock through and through. One band near the top is simply soft. Picture a slab of chocolate left in the cubbyhole on a long summer drive: still a solid bar, still snaps if you are quick, but leave it leaning and it slowly slumps. That slumping band of nearly-melted rock is the asthenosphere, and it is the reason the plates can drift.

The STONE is the core, and this stone has two parts. Its outer part is LIQUID iron and nickel, stirring slowly, and that stirring is the planet's generator — it is the whole reason a compass needle knows where north is. The very centre is a ball of SOLID iron at five to six thousand degrees, as hot as the surface of the sun. Why does the hottest spot in the planet not melt? Because the weight of the entire earth is pressing on it, and under that much squeeze the iron cannot spread out into a liquid. When heat and pressure argue, pressure wins.

Skin, flesh, stone. Crust, mantle, core. Questions on the peach are with you now — the three parts, their thicknesses, and which are solid and which is liquid.

## Subtopic: The Cork Raft and the Roof Sheet

The skin of the earth comes in two materials, and the difference between them decides which one dives when they collide.

Picture a farm dam. Floating on it is a thick raft of cork, and beside it a thin sheet of corrugated roofing iron. The cork raft is far thicker, but cork is LIGHT, so it rides high with most of its body above the waterline. The iron sheet is thin, but iron is HEAVY, so it floats low, barely breaking the surface.

The cork raft is continental crust. Thirty to fifty kilometres thick, up to seventy under the big mountain ranges, built of granite-type rock, light at about two comma seven, and ancient — the rocks of the Makhonjwa Mountains near Barberton have been sitting there for more than three billion years, and the granite under Johannesburg is nearly as old.

The roof sheet is oceanic crust. Just five to ten kilometres thick, made of basalt, heavier at about three comma zero, and never old — no sea floor anywhere on earth has survived longer than about two hundred million years, because the ocean floor is a conveyor that keeps making new floor at one end and swallowing old floor at the other.

Now push the raft and the sheet together across the dam and watch. The iron slides under the cork. Every single time. Nobody legislated that; the heavier one simply sinks. That is subduction, and it is why the deepest ocean trenches hug the edges of continents.

One more idea before the questions. When geographers say PLATE, they never mean the crust alone. A plate is the crust PLUS the cold rigid top slice of the mantle stuck to its underside — about a hundred kilometres of solid rock altogether — sliding around on the soft chocolate-slab layer below. The crust is the paint; the plate is the paint together with the plank it is painted on. Your questions on the dam are up now: thick-and-light against thin-and-heavy, which one dives and why, and what a plate actually includes.

## Subtopic: Thumping the Watermelon

Be honest about the problem first: nobody has ever been down there, and nobody ever will be. The deepest borehole ever drilled managed about twelve kilometres out of six thousand three hundred and seventy-one. So where does anyone find the nerve to announce that the outer core is liquid?

The same place you find the nerve to announce that a watermelon is ripe without cutting it open. You thump it, and you judge the inside by the sound that comes back. The earth gets thumped for us — every large earthquake is the knock — and the listening is done by thousands of seismic stations spread across the planet.

Each big earthquake sends two kinds of wave right through the globe. The P-wave is a squeeze, a push-and-pull travelling forward, and it will pass through anything: rock, liquid metal, anything. The S-wave is a sideways whip, and sideways is its weakness. Drag your hand sideways across a table top and the table drags back — solids resist sideways movement, so the wave gets passed along. Try the same sideways sweep through bathwater and your hand just glides. Liquid cannot carry a sideways shake at all.

So here is the discovery. After a major earthquake, the stations on the far side of the world all report the same strange thing: the S-waves never arrive. Not late, not faint — missing entirely, across a huge patch of the planet. Something in the middle of the earth swallowed them, and the only thing that swallows an S-wave is liquid. The molten outer core was discovered by silence.

The P-waves fill in the rest. They make it through, but they BEND hard where they cross into the liquid core — the same reason a spear-fisher aiming straight at a fish will miss, because light bends where air meets water. That bending leaves a quiet ring on the surface where almost nothing arrives, and the width of the ring measures exactly how deep the core begins. A few faint arrivals inside the quiet ring, bent one extra time, betray the solid iron ball at the very centre.

And three everyday clues agree. Weigh the planet and it averages twice what its surface rocks weigh, so something monstrously heavy hides inside — metal. A compass works, and only churning liquid metal can run that magnet. And iron meteorites, falling from space, look exactly like shrapnel from the cores of broken planets.

Thump, listen, conclude. The final questions of the lesson are coming to you now — the two waves and what each can cross, what the missing S-waves prove, and the three clues that seal the case.
