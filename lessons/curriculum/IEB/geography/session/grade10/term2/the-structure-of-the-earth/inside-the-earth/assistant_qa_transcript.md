**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the outer core makes the magnetic field — but why should a geography learner care? What would actually change for us if that liquid metal stopped moving?

**Tutor:** More than you would think. The magnetic field is the planet's invisible shield: it deflects the stream of charged particles constantly blowing off the sun. Without it, that solar wind would strip lighter gases from the upper atmosphere over geological time and would reach the surface far more strongly — Mars, which lost its internal dynamo long ago, also lost most of its atmosphere, and that is no coincidence. There are everyday consequences too. Compasses, obviously, but also the navigation of animals: turtles, some birds and even certain bacteria orient themselves by the field. And the field is not fixed — the magnetic poles wander measurably year by year, and the rocks record that the field has flipped completely many times in the past. Those recorded flips, locked into cooling lava on the sea floor, become crucial evidence in the next lessons on plate tectonics. So the churning outer core is not a piece of trivia about a place you will never visit; it is actively protecting the atmosphere you are breathing and quietly writing the data that proves the continents move.

***

**Thandi:** Next question: you said the inner core is five to six thousand degrees. Nobody has ever been past twelve kilometres — so how can anyone possibly measure a temperature down there? It sounds like a guess.

**Tutor:** It is an inference, but a tightly constrained one, and the difference matters. Three methods triangulate it. First, gradient: in deep mines we measure directly that rock temperature climbs as you descend — at Mponeng the rock face reaches about sixty degrees only four kilometres down — and that measured gradient, corrected for how rock conducts heat, can be projected downward. Second, laboratory physics: we know from experiment at what combinations of temperature and pressure iron melts. Seismology tells us the outer core is liquid iron and the inner core is solid iron, and the pressure at each depth can be calculated from the weight of the overlying layers. So the temperature at the boundary between them must sit almost exactly on iron's melting curve at that pressure — the state change itself is the thermometer. Third, modern experiments squeeze tiny iron samples between diamond anvils to core pressures, lasers heat them, and the melting point is observed directly. All three approaches converge on the same band, five to six thousand degrees. So the number is not plucked from the air; it is pinned between a measured gradient, a known boundary, and laboratory iron.

***

**Thandi:** Here is one that puzzled a few of us: if both kinds of crust float on the mantle, why do the continents stand kilometres higher than the ocean floor instead of everything levelling out?

**Tutor:** Because floating obeys density, and the two crusts float differently — the principle is called isostasy. Continental crust is thick and light, so like a thick cork raft it rides high, with deep roots pushed down into the mantle and a large freeboard standing above. Oceanic crust is thin and dense, so it floats low, and water collects on top of it — which is really why the oceans are where they are. The crust is not resting on liquid, remember; the plastic asthenosphere simply yields over long timescales, so blocks of crust settle to their balance level as if in an extremely slow fluid. And isostasy is not finished business. Where the last ice age loaded Scandinavia with kilometres of ice, the crust was pressed down; the ice is gone and the land is still rising, a centimetre or so a year, rebounding like a raft after a passenger steps off. Mountains work the same way: as erosion strips the Drakensberg, the lightened block keeps easing upward. So the height difference between a continent and an ocean floor is not an accident of history — it is a floating equilibrium, continuously maintained.

**Thandi:** Last one: the ocean crust is only about seven kilometres thick, and our mines already go four kilometres down. Why has nobody just drilled those last few kilometres and finally touched the mantle?

**Tutor:** People have tried — seriously — and the obstacles are worth understanding. Drilling the ocean floor means operating through several kilometres of water first, from a ship that must hold position over an unmarked point while a drill string kilometres long turns beneath it. Then the rock fights back. Temperature climbs steeply near a ridge, and past a few hundred degrees drill bits blunt, electronics fail, and the borehole itself starts to squeeze shut, because rock at depth deforms plastically — the same property you learned about in the asthenosphere, arriving early. Pressure forces drilling mud and fluids to behave unpredictably, and each bit change means pulling the whole string up and threading it back down the same narrow hole. Scientific ocean-drilling programmes have pushed holes more than a kilometre and a half into oceanic basalt, and reaching the Moho remains a live ambition, but the last stretch is the hardest ever attempted. And notice the lovely irony: the seismic methods from this lesson have already given us the mantle's density, state and depth to good precision — the waves made the journey no drill has yet survived. The drill would confirm; the physics already knows.
