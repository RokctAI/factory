**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: animals — half of us have heard that dogs and chickens go strange before an earthquake. Is there anything real in that, or is it just stories?

**Tutor:** There is a kernel of physics wrapped in a great deal of story. The kernel is the P-wave. It arrives first, it is the weakest of the three waves, and many animals feel vibrations that people ignore — so in the seconds between the P arrival and the destructive surface waves, animals genuinely may react before the humans around them notice anything. That is not prophecy; it is faster hearing of an event already underway, and it buys seconds at most. The stories, though, claim animals sense earthquakes hours or days ahead, and that has never survived careful testing. The difficulty is one-sided memory: when a dog howled and an earthquake followed, everyone retells it; the thousand nights the dog howled and nothing happened are forgotten. Controlled studies that log animal behaviour continuously find no reliable signal ahead of rupture. Interestingly, engineers borrowed the honest part of the idea: early-warning networks are, in a sense, electronic animals — instruments that detect the harmless P-wave and broadcast an alert that outruns the slower surface waves. So the fair summary for an exam is: animals may respond seconds early to real waves, but no method, animal or instrument, predicts an earthquake before the fault actually slips.

**Thandi:** Next one: if the mines cause most of our tremors, how does mining make an earthquake at all? There is no plate boundary under Klerksdorp.

**Tutor:** Correct — and that is exactly why the distinction matters. A natural tectonic earthquake needs plate-driven strain; a mining tremor needs only stressed rock and a sudden change in its support, and deep gold mining provides both. Remember that at three or four kilometres down, the overlying rock presses on every tunnel with enormous force. Mining removes reef and leaves voids, and the stress that the removed rock used to carry is instantly transferred to the pillars and faces beside the void. Where that concentrated stress exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock bursts or an old healed fault in the region is nudged past its friction limit and slips — the same elastic rebound you learned, but with the trigger supplied by excavation rather than plate motion. That is why the tremors cluster around active mining districts, why they are shallow, and why even moderate magnitudes are felt so sharply at the surface — a shallow focus delivers its energy almost undiluted, as the Orkney event of 2014 showed when it damaged hundreds of houses. Mines manage the risk by backfilling voids, leaving support pillars, monitoring micro-tremors continuously, and pulling crews out when the small events swarm. The mechanism is the lesson's mechanism; only the finger on the trigger differs.

**Thandi:** Here is one about the scales: if Mercalli depends on what people say they felt, is it not hopelessly unscientific? Different people exaggerate differently.

**Tutor:** Individually, yes — collectively, no, and the distinction is the whole trick. A single witness is unreliable: one person's "terrifying" is another's "mild". So intensity is never assigned from one account. Surveys collect hundreds or thousands of reports across a region, and each grade on the scale is anchored to observable facts rather than feelings: crockery breaking, sleepers waking, chimneys falling, walls cracking. Whether a chimney fell is not a matter of temperament. When the mass of reports is mapped, the exaggerations average out and clean concentric zones of intensity appear — and those maps carry information no seismograph can supply. A magnitude number tells you the energy at the focus; the intensity map tells you where that energy actually hurt people, and its odd bulges expose the patches of soft ground that amplified the shaking — the very places to avoid rebuilding hospitals. There is also a practical modern form: online "did you feel it" surveys turn thousands of phone reports into an intensity map within minutes of an event. And for pre-instrument history, human accounts are all we have: the size of the great Lisbon earthquake of 1755 is estimated from written damage reports — Mercalli reasoning applied backwards. So: unscientific witness by witness, robust in bulk.

**Thandi:** Last one: a tsunami is just a big wave, and surfers ride huge waves at Jeffreys Bay every winter. Why is a tsunami so much deadlier than any storm swell?

**Tutor:** Because the two waves are different animals wearing the same word. A storm swell, however tall, is a surface disturbance: the wind has piled up the top few metres of water, the energy sits near the surface, and each wave carries water a short distance before it breaks and drains back. A tsunami is the whole ocean moving. When the sea floor jerks upward at a subduction zone, it lifts the entire column of water above it — from the bed to the surface, kilometres deep — and that column's energy travels outward at airliner speed. In deep ocean the tsunami is a broad hump less than a metre high; ships ride over it unaware. The transformation happens at the coast: as the water shallows, the wave slows and all that deep energy has nowhere to go but up and forward. What arrives is not a breaking curl but a rising flood that keeps coming for many minutes, penetrating kilometres inland, then drains with equal violence carrying debris and people out to sea — and it repeats, because a tsunami is a train of waves, often with the largest not first. That is why the 2004 Indian Ocean disaster killed on coastlines thousands of kilometres from the fault. The survival rules follow directly: if the ground shakes hard near a coast, or the sea suddenly withdraws, move to high ground immediately and stay there until authorities clear the all-clear — never go down to watch the exposed sea bed.
