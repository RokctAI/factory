# Part 1 — Expert

An earthquake is stored energy escaping — strain that rock has been accumulating for decades or centuries, released in seconds as shaking. This session pins down where that energy is stored and where it emerges, the three waves that carry it, the two very different scales that measure it, and the question that outranks all the others in this topic: why an earthquake of one size leaves a few hundred dead in one country and hundreds of thousands in another. In 2010 a magnitude 8,8 event in Chile released roughly five hundred times the energy of the magnitude 7,0 event in Haiti the same year — and killed a tiny fraction of the people. Explaining that comparison properly is where the highest marks in this topic live.

## Subtopic: Focus, Epicentre and Elastic Rebound

Begin with the mechanism, because every later fact hangs on it. Plate movement loads the rock on either side of a fault with STRAIN. The rock deforms elastically, storing the energy the way a drawn bow stores the archer's effort, while friction keeps the fault clamped shut — sometimes for centuries. The moment arrives when the stored strain finally beats either the strength of the rock or the friction locking it: the fault slips, both sides lurch back toward their unstrained shape, and the energy leaves as vibrations racing outward. That is ELASTIC REBOUND, the standard explanation you must be able to give in sequence.

Two positions must never be confused, because the pair is tested constantly. The FOCUS — also called the hypocentre — is the point INSIDE the crust where the rupture starts and the energy is first released. The EPICENTRE is the point on the SURFACE vertically above the focus. Shaking is usually fiercest at the epicentre and fades with distance from it.

Focus depth is sorted into three classes: SHALLOW, surface to 70 kilometres; INTERMEDIATE, 70 to 300; DEEP, 300 to 700. Depth is destiny. A shallow focus delivers its energy to the surface almost undiluted, which is why the world's lethal earthquakes are overwhelmingly shallow ones; a deep focus spends most of its energy on the journey up. Deep events occur only at subduction zones, where a cold slab carries brittle rock far down into the mantle.

Expect company around the main shock. FORESHOCKS sometimes run ahead of it; AFTERSHOCKS trail it for days or months as the crust resettles — and they routinely finish off structures the main shock only cracked, which is why damaged buildings are kept empty afterwards.

Geography of occurrence: earthquakes crowd the plate boundaries — the Ring of Fire, the Mediterranean-Himalayan belt, the mid-ocean ridges, the East African Rift. South Africa sits mid-plate, so natural events are uncommon; most of the tremors felt around the gold mines of the Witwatersrand and the North West are triggered by deep mining, and the 2014 event near Orkney, at magnitude 5,5 the strongest of that mining region's earthquakes, damaged hundreds of homes in nearby townships.

## Subtopic: Seismic Waves and Locating the Epicentre

The energy travels as three wave types, and their fixed arrival order is itself a measuring tool.

P-WAVES — primary — are compressional: the rock is squeezed and released along the direction of travel, like the jolt that runs down a line of coupled rail trucks when the locomotive shunts. Fastest of the three at about six kilometres per second in the crust, first to arrive, and able to cross solids, liquids and gases.

S-WAVES — secondary — are transverse: the rock whips from side to side across the travel direction, like the wave that snakes along a garden hose when you flick the end. Roughly half the P-wave's speed, second to arrive, and stopped dead by liquids, which have no rigidity to hand a sideways motion onward.

SURFACE WAVES — L-waves — roll along the ground itself rather than through the planet. Slowest, last to arrive, largest in amplitude — and responsible for most of the destruction, because they heave and sway the ground under structures.

The recording instrument is the SEISMOGRAPH; its output, the SEISMOGRAM, is a line that first twitches at the P arrival, kicks harder at the S arrival, then swings widest as the surface waves pass.

Now the location technique. P and S waves leave the focus together but travel at different speeds, so the INTERVAL between their arrivals stretches with distance — the further the station, the longer the S-minus-P gap. Read the gap and you have the distance. But distance is not direction: a station 300 kilometres from the epicentre knows only that the epicentre lies somewhere on a circle of radius 300 kilometres. One circle is not an answer; two circles still cross twice; THREE circles intersect at a single point. That is TRIANGULATION, performed with at least three stations, and it is how every epicentre on earth is fixed.

## Subtopic: Richter and Mercalli — Two Different Measurements

Treat these as two rulers measuring two different things, because that is exactly what they are — and confusing them is the classic error here.

The RICHTER SCALE measures MAGNITUDE: the energy released at the focus, computed from the amplitude on a seismogram corrected for distance. Each earthquake therefore has exactly ONE Richter value, no matter where it is felt or how badly, and the scale is open-ended at the top.

The scale's trap is that it is LOGARITHMIC. One whole step up means TEN TIMES the recorded ground motion and roughly THIRTY-TWO TIMES the energy. Work the comparison you should carry into any exam: magnitude 8 against magnitude 6 is two steps, so ground motion ten times ten — one hundred times larger — and energy about thirty-two times thirty-two, close to ONE THOUSAND times more. The phrase "slightly stronger" for a two-step difference is simply wrong. Calibration points: below about 3, generally unfelt; above 7, a major earthquake; the record is the magnitude 9,5 event in Chile in 1960. Very large modern events are reported on the moment magnitude scale, tuned to agree with Richter but more faithful at the top end.

The MERCALLI SCALE measures INTENSITY: the observed effects at one particular place, graded I to XII in Roman numerals from human reports and visible damage — II, felt by a few people at rest; V, sleepers wake and crockery breaks; VIII, chimneys and poorly built walls fall; XI, few masonry structures left standing, rails bent; XII, damage total.

The examinable consequence: ONE earthquake owns ONE Richter number but MANY Mercalli numbers — high rings of intensity near the epicentre grading outward to low ones. And two towns at equal distance can score differently, because intensity answers to local ground, focus depth and building quality: loose sediment and reclaimed land amplify the shaking savagely, while firm bedrock passes it on lightly. Richter reports the event; Mercalli reports what the event did to you.

## Subtopic: Effects, Vulnerability and Preparedness

Divide the effects cleanly. PRIMARY effects come straight from the shaking: the ground ruptures along the fault, and buildings, bridges, roads and buried pipes tear and collapse. SECONDARY effects grow out of the primary ones: fires ignited by severed gas lines and electrical cables; landslides and rockfalls off steep slopes; LIQUEFACTION, where water-saturated loose ground momentarily behaves as a liquid so that intact buildings lean and settle — whole suburbs of Christchurch in New Zealand were undermined this way in 2011; dam failures and floods; polluted water and disease; homelessness and long economic disruption. And when the focus lies under the sea floor and the bed is thrown vertically, the displaced water column becomes a TSUNAMI, racing across oceans at airliner speed and rearing up in shallow coastal water — the Indian Ocean tsunami of 2004 remains the defining example.

Now the top-mark question: why do equal magnitudes kill so unequally? Because deaths are governed by VULNERABILITY, not by magnitude.

BUILDINGS AND ENFORCEMENT. Shaking does not kill; failing structures do. Chile's strictly enforced engineering codes in 2010 kept collapse rare through a magnitude 8,8; Haiti's unreinforced concrete the same year failed catastrophically at 7,0.

DENSITY AND URBANISATION. The nearer and larger the city, the higher the toll — especially informal housing packed onto steep or soft ground.

WEALTH AND PREPAREDNESS. Money buys monitoring networks, drilled schools, equipped rescue teams, hospitals with generators, and insurance to rebuild; poverty buys none of these, and recovery drags for years because rebuilding competes with every other need.

TIMING AND WARNING. An event in the small hours finds people asleep inside the very structures that fail.

GROUND. Bedrock towns ride out what sediment towns cannot.

Reduction, then. PREDICTION stays out of reach — scientists map high-risk zones, estimate decade-scale probabilities, and watch foreshocks, ground tilt, water levels and radon, but no one can issue a date. The working strategy is PREPAREDNESS, in three layers. Engineering: flexible frames, cross-bracing, foundations tied to bedrock, light roofs, and base isolation — great rubber-and-steel bearings that let the ground slide beneath a comparatively still building. Planning: zoning that keeps schools, hospitals and dense housing off faults and soft ground, with building codes that are inspected, not merely printed. People and systems: rehearsed drop-cover-hold drills, emergency water and torches, known family meeting points, gas shut-off knowledge; seismometer networks that broadcast a few seconds' warning ahead of the surface waves — enough to halt trains and open fire-station doors — and tsunami alerts for coastlines.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same shaking through a snapped branch, a starter's gun, and one stone thrown into a dam.

## Subtopic: Snapping the Branch

Take a dry branch about as thick as your thumb and start bending it over your knee. Nothing happens at first — except that it is happening: the branch is loading up with your effort, storing it, pressing back against your hands harder and harder. Keep going. More. Then CRACK — the branch gives way, both halves spring violently straight, and the snap stings your palms.

You have just run a complete earthquake. The push of the plates is your hands. The locked fault is the wood holding on. The years of quiet are the long slow bend — nothing visible, everything loading. The snap is the fault slipping, and the sting in your palms is the stored energy arriving at the surface as shaking. The rock does not explode; it springs back. Geographers call it elastic rebound.

Two addresses come out of this, and both are compulsory. The FOCUS is the underground point where the crack actually let go. The EPICENTRE is the ground-level point directly above it — if the rock gave way five kilometres beneath the school hall, the epicentre is the school hall.

Why does depth matter so much? Bury a firecracker at the bottom of a full rain tank and set it off: a dull thud at the top. Tape it just under the lid instead: the lid jumps. A shallow earthquake is the firecracker under the lid — its energy reaches the surface almost unspent, and those are the events that flatten towns. The deep ones arrive worn out.

One warning to keep: after the main snap, the ground goes on adjusting for days or weeks — aftershocks — and those smaller snaps push over the walls the first one loosened. That is why nobody re-enters a cracked building early.

## Subtopic: The Starter's Gun and Three Cell Towers

When the fault lets go, the energy leaves as three runners, and they never finish together.

First across the line is the P-wave — a push-and-pull squeeze, the jolt that runs down a line of rail trucks when the engine bumps them. Fastest, and it goes through everything: rock, water, even the liquid core.

Second is the S-wave — a sideways whip, the wave that travels down a garden hose when you flick it. About half the speed, and it drowns in liquid.

Last comes the surface wave, rolling along the ground itself like swell coming into a beach. Slowest of the three — and the demolition crew, because it moves the ground furthest.

Now the distance trick. At an athletics meeting, the timekeepers at the finish line watch the starter's gun: they see the puff of smoke instantly, and the bang wanders over a moment later. The longer light beats sound, the further away the start line is. The earth runs the same race with its own two signals: P and S leave the focus at the same instant, and the GAP between their arrivals at a station announces the distance — short gap, close event; long gap, far one.

But knowing you are two hundred kilometres from something is not knowing where it is: it could be anywhere on a circle two hundred kilometres around you. So borrow the trick your phone uses. A phone is located because SEVERAL cell towers each measure their own distance to it, and only one spot agrees with all of them at once. Seismic stations do exactly that: three stations, three circles, one crossing point — the epicentre. Triangulation.

## Subtopic: One Splash, Many Ripples — and Staying Alive

Here is the picture that keeps Richter and Mercalli apart forever.

Throw one stone into a farm dam. The SPLASH is a single event with a single size — that is RICHTER, the magnitude, one number fixed at the source. The RIPPLES are what arrive at each point of the shoreline — tall near the splash, gentle by the far bank, barely a shiver in the reeds. That is MERCALLI, the intensity: a different reading at every place. One splash, one Richter number; many shorelines, many Mercalli numbers.

Richter hides a trap in its counting. Each step up the scale is not one unit worse — it is ten times the ground movement and about thirty-two times the energy. So an 8 against a 6 is not "two worse": it is ten times ten, a hundred times the shaking, and thirty-two times thirty-two, about a thousand times the energy. Never write "a bit stronger" about a two-step gap.

Mercalli is written in Roman numerals, I to XII, because it is a ladder of observations: pictures swinging, then windows breaking, then chimneys down, then streets of rubble. And notice the ripple rule: the same earthquake scores high rings near the epicentre and lower rings outward — plus odd patches where soft ground makes the ripples taller, the way the shallow muddy end of the dam always shows the biggest waves.

Now the part this topic exists for. Earthquakes do not kill people; buildings do. Chile and Haiti proved it in the same year: Chile's monster released around five hundred times the energy of Haiti's earthquake, yet Haiti buried hundreds of times more people — because one country builds, inspects and drills, and the other could not. Same planet, same physics, different vulnerability.

And since no scientist on earth can put a date on the next event, everything useful happens beforehand. Engineers float buildings on rubber-and-steel pads, brace the walls, tie foundations to bedrock. Planners keep schools and hospitals off the fault line and off jelly-soft ground. Families rehearse: drop, get under something solid, hold on; water, torch and shoes by the bed; one agreed meeting place. Seconds of electronic warning stop the trains; sirens clear the beaches ahead of a tsunami.
