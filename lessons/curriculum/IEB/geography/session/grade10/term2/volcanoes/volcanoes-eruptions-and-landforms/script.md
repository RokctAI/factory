# Part 1 — Expert

A volcano is a rupture in the crust through which molten rock, gas and shattered fragments escape to the surface. This session follows that material from its birthplace in the mantle to the landforms it finally leaves, answers the topic's central question — why one volcano leaks quietly while another destroys itself in an afternoon — separates the features built above the surface from those built beneath it, and closes with the ledger of dangers and rewards that persuades millions of people to farm on the slopes anyway. Mainland South Africa has no active volcano, yet volcanic rock wrote much of our scenery: the ramparts of the Drakensberg, the flat-topped koppies of the Karoo and the diamond mines all take their place in this lesson.

## Subtopic: From Magma to Lava — and Why Some Eruptions Explode

Two words first, because they are the same substance at two addresses. MAGMA is molten rock while it remains BELOW the surface; LAVA is that identical material the moment it emerges. Both hold dissolved gases — chiefly water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide — and it is the gas, wanting out, that powers every eruption.

Magma is generated in three settings you already know. At DIVERGENT boundaries the separating plates relieve pressure on the mantle below, and rock that stayed solid only because it was squeezed begins to melt. At CONVERGENT boundaries the descending slab sweats out its water, and that water lowers the melting point of the mantle wedge above, brewing magma beneath the overriding plate. And at HOT SPOTS a column of abnormally hot mantle rises under the middle of a plate and scorches through it — Hawaii is the textbook case, and Tristan da Cunha, the remote South Atlantic island whose entire community had to be evacuated when its volcano erupted in 1961, is a hot-spot volcano with a South African connection, since the islanders' nearest neighbours are ourselves.

Being lighter than the rock around it, magma rises and pools in a MAGMA CHAMBER some kilometres down, until pressure drives it up a pipe toward daylight.

Then everything depends on a single property: VISCOSITY — how strongly the melt resists flowing — which is set mainly by SILICA content.

LOW-silica magma is BASALTIC: extremely hot, around 1 100 to 1 200 degrees Celsius, and thin enough that gas bubbles slip out of it freely as it rises. Its eruptions are EFFUSIVE: lava wells up, streams away for kilometres, and stacks into broad, gently sloping country.

HIGH-silica magma — andesitic or rhyolitic — is cooler and stiff, stiff enough to seal its own vent like a cork. The gas cannot leave, pressure climbs for years, and when the seal finally fails the eruption is EXPLOSIVE, hurling out PYROCLASTIC material: ash, cinders and volcanic bombs.

Classify activity as well: ACTIVE volcanoes have erupted in recorded history and may again; DORMANT ones are quiet but still connected to magma; EXTINCT ones have lost their supply for good.

## Subtopic: The Structure of a Volcano and the Main Types

Assemble the anatomy from the bottom upward, the order a labelling question follows. The MAGMA CHAMBER is the deep reservoir. The main VENT, or pipe, climbs from it to the summit, where it flares into the CRATER, a funnel-shaped hollow. The CONE around the vent is the stacked product of past eruptions. Side pipes that break out through the flanks build SECONDARY or PARASITIC CONES on the volcano's shoulders. And when a truly enormous eruption drains the chamber, the unsupported summit founders into the void, leaving a CALDERA — a basin many times wider than any crater. Crater Lake in Oregon fills such a collapse: a ring of cliffs around a lake ten kilometres across, where a whole mountaintop used to be.

The magma dictates four main volcano types.

SHIELD VOLCANOES are the work of runny basalt. Because each flow travels far before freezing, the pile grows immensely wide and only gently high — slopes of a few degrees, spreading like a bronze shield laid flat. Kilauea in Hawaii, one of the most active volcanoes on earth, is the standard example, erupting rivers of lava rather than explosions.

COMPOSITE VOLCANOES — stratovolcanoes — rise at subduction zones from alternating tempers: an explosive phase buries the slopes in ash, a calmer phase seals the ash beneath a coat of lava, and hundreds of such double layers build the tall, steep, elegantly concave cone of the postcards. Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines and Mount St Helens in the United States are composite volcanoes, and their history is exactly why these are the deadly type.

CINDER or ASH CONES are the modest ones: nothing but loose pyroclastic fragments heaped around a vent into a steep little cone, rarely a few hundred metres tall, with an oversized crater. Parícutin in Mexico famously began in a farmer's field in 1943 and built its cone within months.

DOME volcanoes grow from lava too stiff to travel at all: it swells over the vent in a steep bulging mound that cracks, collapses and rebuilds itself — the fitful dome that regrew inside Mount St Helens' crater after its great eruption is the type in action.

## Subtopic: Extrusive and Intrusive Landforms

Volcanic landforms sort into two families by one question: did the magma reach the surface?

EXTRUSIVE landforms are built in the open air. The cones and craters above belong here, together with LAVA FLOWS and LAVA PLATEAUX. A plateau is what happens when very fluid basalt issues not from one mountain but from long FISSURES — cracks kilometres in length — flooding the land sheet upon sheet until a level-topped upland stands stacked like reams of paper. South Africa owns a monumental example: as Gondwana began tearing apart about 180 million years ago, fissure eruptions drowned the interior in basalt, and the surviving stack is the high Drakensberg and the Lesotho plateau — which is why that skyline runs in level ramparts and giant steps instead of peaks. Hot springs are extrusive relatives too, where deeply circulating groundwater returns heated to the surface, as it does at Badplaas in Mpumalanga and at Goudini near Worcester.

INTRUSIVE landforms crystallise UNDERGROUND, and surface only after erosion strips off their cover. Five are required.

A BATHOLITH is a vast body of magma, generally granite, frozen deep in the crust; unroofed by erosion it weathers into rounded domes — the smooth granite masses of the Cape Peninsula, whose giant rounded boulders shelter the penguin colony at Boulders Beach, are the exposed roof of such a body.

A LOPOLITH is a saucer-shaped intrusion sagging at its centre; the Bushveld Igneous Complex north of Pretoria is the planet's largest, and it stores the richest platinum and chromium reserves on earth.

A LACCOLITH is a dome-shaped intrusion that has heaved the strata above it into a blister.

A SILL spreads BETWEEN existing rock layers, lying parallel with the bedding; a DYKE slices ACROSS the layers, standing steep or vertical. The Karoo is veined with both in dolerite, injected during the same event that fed the Drakensberg. Dolerite shrugs off weathering far better than the soft shales around it, so an exposed sill becomes armour plating on a hill — which is why the Karoo plain is studded with flat-topped koppies, each capped by its dolerite lid, with the sheared-off columns of the Valley of Desolation outside Graaff-Reinet as the most theatrical display. An exposed dyke weathers out instead as a long straight ridge of dark rock striding across the veld.

A VOLCANIC NECK or PLUG is the magma that froze inside the pipe itself; when the soft cone erodes away the plug remains as a lonely tower. Our most precious pipes are of KIMBERLITE — at Cullinan east of Pretoria and Venetia in Limpopo — which hoisted diamonds up from mantle depths.

## Subtopic: Hazards, Benefits and Management

Rank the hazards by lethality. LAVA FLOWS bulldoze property yet seldom kill, since most can be outwalked. PYROCLASTIC FLOWS are the executioners: avalanches of incandescent gas, ash and rock that sweep downslope faster than any vehicle and incinerate whatever they touch — one such flow from Mount Pelée destroyed the town of St Pierre on Martinique in 1902 within minutes, leaving almost no survivors. ASH FALLS crush roofs, smother crops and grazing, foul water, injure lungs, and paralyse aviation — the 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull in Iceland closed European airspace for days. LAHARS are volcanic mudflows of ash mixed with rain or meltwater; they pour down valleys and bury towns far from the summit, as one buried Armero in Colombia in 1985 with terrible loss of life. Add toxic gases, landslides, tsunamis from island eruptions, and the year or two of global cooling that follows truly great eruptions when ash and sulphur veil the stratosphere.

Yet people return, and for reasons that make economic sense. Weathered ash and lava produce some of the world's most FERTILE SOILS, rich in potassium, phosphorus and magnesium — the crowded farmland of Java and the vineyards and orchards climbing Vesuvius' slopes near Naples exist because of eruptions. GEOTHERMAL ENERGY is volcanic heat put to work: Iceland warms its homes with it, and Kenya's Olkaria plants in the Rift Valley generate a major share of that country's electricity. MINERAL WEALTH concentrates in volcanic rocks — the Bushveld's platinum and chromium, kimberlite diamonds, copper, gold and sulphur elsewhere — while volcanic stone quarries well, and hot springs and spectacular cones bring TOURISTS.

Management succeeds here more than with earthquakes for one gracious reason: volcanoes usually announce themselves. MONITORING reads the announcements — swarms of small earthquakes as magma forces upward, the ground swelling measurably on tiltmeters and satellite radar, gas output climbing, ground temperature rising — and converts them into graded alert levels. HAZARD MAPS mark which valleys lahars will use and which flanks pyroclastic flows threaten; PLANNING keeps permanent settlement out of the worst zones and rehearses evacuation routes and shelters. The proof of the method is Mount Pinatubo in 1991: instruments read the warnings, tens of thousands of people were moved in time, and one of the century's largest eruptions killed a small fraction of those it would otherwise have taken. Education completes the system — communities that know their alert levels and routes leave in hours, not days.

## Subtopic: Why the Pap Pot Spits

Stand at the stove with two pots. In the first, a thin vegetable soup simmers: bubbles rise through it easily and pop softly at the top — blip, blip — nothing dramatic. In the second, a stiff pap thickens: the bubbles cannot slip through that heavy porridge, so steam gathers under the surface until it bursts free with that angry PLOP that spits scalding blobs at your wrist. Every cook knows which pot to fear.

You now understand volcanoes. Underground, molten rock — magma — carries dissolved gas, and the gas must escape. In THIN, runny magma, the gas bubbles out gently and continuously, so the volcano never builds pressure: it oozes. Rivers of glowing lava pour out, run far, and cool into wide gentle slopes. In THICK, stiff magma, the gas is trapped exactly as in the pap pot — worse, because the stiff melt plugs the pipe like a cork in a bottle. Pressure stacks up for years, sometimes centuries, and when the plug fails the mountain does not leak; it detonates, flinging out ash, cinders and rock instead of rivers.

What decides thick or thin? Mostly the amount of silica in the melt — and that traces back to the plate setting: plates pulling apart deliver runny, low-silica basalt; plates diving under each other brew thick, high-silica magma. So the geography chooses the pot.

And keep the naming rule: below ground the melt is called magma; the metre it crosses into daylight, it becomes lava. Same substance, new address.

## Subtopic: Syrup, Lasagne and the Sand Heap

A volcano's outline is its autobiography — the shape tells you exactly what came out of it.

THE SYRUP SPILL. Tip golden syrup onto a cold plate and watch it creep into a wide, low, glistening disc. Runny basalt lava does precisely that at country scale: each flow travels kilometres before hardening, so the volcano grows enormously broad and barely steep — you could drive to the top without noticing the gradient. That is a SHIELD volcano. Hawaii's Kilauea is one, pouring out lava year after year with hardly an explosion.

THE LASAGNE. Now build a dish of lasagne: a layer of loose mince, held down by a sheet of pasta, then more mince, another sheet, again and again. That is the COMPOSITE volcano's recipe. An explosion scatters loose ash on the slopes — the mince — then a quieter spell of lava flows over it and sets hard — the pasta sheet — locking the layer beneath. Hundreds of alternating layers later you have the tall, steep, beautiful cone with its graceful concave sweep. Beautiful, and lethal: Pinatubo and Mount St Helens are lasagne mountains.

THE SAND HEAP. Pour dry building sand from a bag and it piles into a small, steep cone with wide loose flanks. A CINDER CONE is only that: fragments thrown from a vent, landing and heaping around it. Small, steep, big crater on top — and sometimes brand new, like the Mexican cone of Parícutin, which grew out of a flat field within one man's lifetime.

Inside, all of them run the same plumbing: the magma chamber is the tank; the vent is the pipe; the crater is the funnel mouth at the top; and side pipes sometimes pop small extra cones out of the shoulders. One more shape completes the set: empty the tank completely in one colossal eruption and the summit, with nothing beneath it, caves in — leaving a caldera, a hole many kilometres wide where a mountaintop stood. One in Oregon now holds a lake ten kilometres across.

## Subtopic: What the Volcano Leaves Behind

Now the South African chapter, because you have seen these landforms — perhaps without knowing what you were looking at.

Some volcanic rock froze in the open air. Around 180 million years ago, as Gondwana started ripping apart, the crust of southern Africa split along cracks kilometres long, and basalt flooded out — not from a proud cone but from the fissures themselves — sheet after sheet, layer on layer. The surviving stack is the high Drakensberg and the whole roof of Lesotho. That is why the escarpment skyline runs in level ramparts and giant stairs rather than pointed summits: you are reading the edges of stacked lava sheets, each step a flow.

Other magma never saw daylight. It halted underground, cooled slowly into hard dolerite — and then erosion spent millions of years removing the softer cover. Where the magma had spread flat between the Karoo shales, the leftover slab now sits like armour on hilltops: every flat-topped koppie along the road from Beaufort West to Graaff-Reinet is a hill wearing its dolerite lid, standing above plains where the unarmoured shale has washed away. At the Valley of Desolation, just outside Graaff-Reinet, the dolerite has weathered into colossal stacked columns leaning over the plain — the country's most dramatic monument to magma that never erupted. And where magma had squeezed up through a vertical crack instead, it stands today as a long, dark, dead-straight ridge striding across the veld.

Then there are the pipes that came from deepest of all. Kimberlite blasted up from the mantle carrying passengers — diamonds — and the pipes at Cullinan and Venetia are mined for them to this day.

Balance the books before we finish. Debits: lava takes property; ash breaks roofs, chokes crops and grounds aircraft; lahars bury valley towns; and the pyroclastic flow — a glowing hurricane no vehicle outruns — takes lives. Credits: ash-fed soils among the richest on earth, free underground heat that lights Iceland and much of Kenya, and mineral treasure — our platinum, our diamonds — delivered from the mantle by volcanic plumbing.

The saving grace is that volcanoes send warnings: swelling ground, swarming tremors, rising gases, climbing temperatures. So scientists watch the instruments, map the danger valleys, plan the settlement lines and practise the evacuations — watch, map, plan, practise — and in 1991 that exact sequence walked tens of thousands of people away from Pinatubo before it exploded.
