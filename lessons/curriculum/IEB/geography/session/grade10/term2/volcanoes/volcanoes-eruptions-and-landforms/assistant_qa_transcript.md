**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the Drakensberg is made of lava, does that mean South Africa used to have huge volcanoes? Where did the actual mountains of fire go?

**Tutor:** Here is the surprise: there were probably never great cone-shaped volcanoes there at all. The Drakensberg basalts are fissure eruptions — the crust cracked open along fractures kilometres long as Gondwana strained apart, and extremely runny basalt simply flooded out of the cracks, spreading in thin sheets across a landscape the size of a country. Runny lava cannot build a steep cone; it drains away too fast, so each eruption added a flat layer, and over a few million years the layers stacked more than a kilometre thick. What you see today is also not the original extent. Those basalts once reached far beyond the present escarpment, and about 180 million years of erosion has eaten the stack backwards, leaving the dramatic wall of the escarpment as a retreating cliff edge, not a volcano's flank. So when you stand at the Amphitheatre and look up at those level ramparts, you are reading two stories at once: the stacking of lava sheets from fissures long buried, and the slow demolition of most of them since. The fire is gone because the crack system died when the continents finished separating — the same event, incidentally, that fed the dolerite sills and dykes through the Karoo below.

**Thandi:** Next one: you said lava flows can usually be outwalked. Then how did a pyroclastic flow destroy an entire town in minutes? What makes it so unbelievably fast?

**Tutor:** Because a pyroclastic flow is not a liquid creeping downhill — it is a collapsing cloud, and it moves the way an avalanche does, not the way syrup does. Picture the explosive eruption first: a column of ash, gas and shattered rock is blasted kilometres into the sky. As long as the column is hotter and lighter than the surrounding air it climbs; but the moment part of it grows too dense to stay airborne, that whole section falls back onto the mountainside. Now you have hundreds of thousands of tonnes of scalding material dropping from height onto a steep slope — and it does not land and stop. The mixture of gas and fine ash behaves almost without friction, each particle riding on hot expanding gas, so the mass accelerates downslope like water bursting from a dam, except at several hundred degrees and at highway-to-aircraft speeds. St Pierre in 1902 was overrun within about a minute of the flow leaving Mount Pelée; of tens of thousands of residents, only a handful survived. That is why hazard maps treat the flanks of a composite volcano so strictly: against a lava flow you walk away, but against a pyroclastic flow the only working defence is not being there — which is exactly what evacuation before the eruption achieves.

**Thandi:** Something is bothering us about the diamonds: why do they only come up through those kimberlite pipes? Why can we not just find diamonds in ordinary lava?

**Tutor:** Because diamonds are fussy about pressure and about speed, and only kimberlite delivers both. Diamond is carbon crystallised under crushing pressure, which means it forms only very deep — around 150 kilometres and more, in the cold thick root of an ancient continent. Ordinary basaltic magma is born much shallower, so it never visits the diamond storerooms in the first place. Kimberlite magma is the rare exception: it originates below the diamond zone and then travels upward with extraordinary violence — gas-charged and fast, punching a narrow carrot-shaped pipe through the entire lithosphere in what geologists reckon may be hours rather than centuries. The speed matters as much as the depth, and here is the elegant part: diamond is not actually stable at surface pressures — given heat and time it would revert to worthless graphite. A slow magma would cook every diamond it carried back into pencil material on the way up. The kimberlite express is so quick that the diamonds arrive quenched, frozen in their high-pressure form before they can change their minds. That is why the pipes at Cullinan and Venetia are mined as narrow deep funnels rather than broad fields, and why each one is a drill core through the deep earth — mantle samples included, delivered free of charge.

**Thandi:** Last one: with all the satellites and instruments watching, why did anyone still die at volcanoes like Armero? If volcanoes give warnings, why are the warnings sometimes not enough?

**Tutor:** Because the chain that saves lives has four links — instruments, interpretation, decision and response — and a failure of any one breaks it. Armero in 1985 is the case every volcanologist studies precisely because the science mostly worked: the volcano was known to be restless, a hazard map existed showing the mudflow paths, and the eruption itself was actually modest. What failed was downstream of the instruments. Warnings were unclear and disputed, evacuation orders came late or were doubted, and when the lahars swept down the mapped valleys in the night, a town that could have walked to safety in half an hour was still in its beds. Contrast Pinatubo six years later: the mountain gave similar signals, but scientists and authorities communicated one clear message, alert levels were explained in advance, drills had been run, and tens of thousands of people were already outside the danger zones when the climactic blast came. Same physics, opposite outcomes — the difference was entirely human. That is why the management section of this lesson ends with education and rehearsal rather than with instruments: a seismometer cannot carry anyone off a mountain. For your exams, frame it this way: monitoring makes warning possible; only preparedness — maps understood, routes practised, orders trusted — makes warning effective.
