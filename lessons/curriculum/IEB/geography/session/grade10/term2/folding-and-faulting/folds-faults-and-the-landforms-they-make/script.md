# Part 1 — Expert

The previous lesson supplied the force; this one watches the rock respond. Squeezed rock that yields by bending gives folds and fold mountains; stressed rock that yields by breaking gives faults, block mountains and rift valleys. You need both processes by definition, by labelled description and by real example — and the worked example lives on our doorstep, because the Cape Fold Mountains, whose sheltered valleys grow much of South Africa's fruit and wine, display nearly every term in this lesson.

## Subtopic: Compression, Tension and Shearing

Rock is deformed by three tectonic forces, and each one belongs to a plate boundary you met last lesson.

COMPRESSION drives rock together from opposite sides, shortening and thickening the crust; it is the force of convergent boundaries. TENSION hauls rock apart from opposite sides, stretching and thinning the crust; it is the force of divergent boundaries. SHEARING drives adjacent rock in opposite directions along the same line, so one mass drags past the other; it is the force of transform boundaries. Squeeze, stretch, slide — three forces, three boundaries.

Whether a stressed rock bends or breaks is decided by three conditions, and answers that omit them stay mediocre.

DEPTH and TEMPERATURE. Kilometres down, rock is hot and confined by pressure on every side, so it deforms plastically — it takes a permanent new shape without fracturing. Close to the surface it is cold, unconfined and brittle, and it cracks.

RATE. Stress applied gradually over millions of years lets rock creep into a bend. Stress applied abruptly overwhelms the rock's strength and it breaks.

ROCK TYPE. Thinly bedded sedimentary rocks — shales, layered sandstones — flex comparatively easily; a massive body of granite would rather snap than fold.

And there is an order of events worth memorising. Under growing stress, rock first deforms ELASTICALLY — release the stress and it springs back. Past its ELASTIC LIMIT it deforms PLASTICALLY and keeps the new shape: that is folding. Push harder, or push suddenly, and it FRACTURES; and once the rock on either side of the fracture actually moves, a fault exists. Elastic, plastic, fractured, displaced — the identical sequence will explain earthquakes in the next lesson.

## Subtopic: Folds — Anticlines, Synclines and the Fold Family

FOLDING is the buckling of rock strata under compression. Work always from a cross-section — a vertical slice through the crust, as if a giant spade had cut straight down and you were looking at the exposed face. Flat-lying sedimentary layers, stacked like the pages of a closed book lying on a table, are driven together from both ends. Unable to escape sideways, they wrinkle upward and downward into arches and troughs.

Learn the labels. An ANTICLINE is the upfold — the arch — its layers rising to a CREST and dipping away on either side. A SYNCLINE is the downfold — the trough — its layers sagging to a low line and climbing away on both sides. The tilted sides of any fold are its LIMBS, and adjacent folds share them, so a folded landscape in section is one continuous wave. The line along an anticline's highest points is its AXIS, sitting in an imaginary AXIAL PLANE. And keep one diagnostic fact for interpretation questions: once erosion bites into an anticline, the OLDEST strata appear at its core, because folding lifted the deepest layers highest.

Raise the compression and the folds evolve through a fixed series. A SYMMETRICAL or simple fold has limbs dipping equally about a vertical axial plane. An ASYMMETRICAL fold leans, one limb steeper than the other. An OVERFOLD has been pushed past vertical, one limb overhanging. A RECUMBENT fold has been flattened until it lies on its side. Beyond that the rock gives up bending: the fold shears along its axis and the upper limb is driven forward over the lower — an OVERTHRUST fold, or nappe, the exact frontier where folding turns into faulting.

The landform outcome is FOLD MOUNTAINS: long parallel ranges aligned at right angles to the compression, with parallel valleys between. But resist the assumption that every anticline stands as a mountain. The crest of an anticline has been stretched over the arch, so it is riddled with cracks and easy prey for weathering; a syncline's rock has been squeezed dense and tight. Over long spans of time the arch may be excavated into a valley while the neighbouring trough survives as high ground — INVERSION OF RELIEF, a higher-order favourite that turns the landscape upside down relative to its structure.

## Subtopic: Faults and the Landforms They Make

A FAULT is a fracture along which the rock on either side has MOVED. Without displacement a fracture is merely a joint; displacement is what earns the name fault.

The vocabulary comes first, because diagram questions demand it. Movement happens along the FAULT PLANE. When that plane slopes, the block lying on top of it is the HANGING WALL and the block beneath is the FOOTWALL. Vertical displacement is the THROW; horizontal displacement is the HEAVE. The block left standing higher is the upthrow side; its partner is the downthrow side.

Each of the three forces manufactures its own fault type.

TENSION makes a NORMAL fault: the crust stretches and the hanging wall slips DOWN the fault plane relative to the footwall. Its surface signature is a FAULT SCARP — an abnormally straight, steep step in the land, sometimes running unbroken for tens of kilometres.

COMPRESSION makes a REVERSE fault: the crust shortens and the hanging wall is driven UP over the footwall. When the fault plane lies at a shallow angle and rock travels far across the top of other rock, the structure is a THRUST fault.

SHEARING makes a SHEAR fault — also called a strike-slip or tear fault: the movement is horizontal, throw is minimal, and anything crossing the fault line is offset sideways, the San Andreas being the world's most studied case.

Combinations of faults build the landforms. Two roughly parallel normal faults with the central block DROPPED between them make a GRABEN, expressed at the surface as a RIFT VALLEY: broad, flat-floored, walled by facing fault scarps — the architecture of the East African Rift. The opposite arrangement, a central block left STANDING while the flanks drop, or forced up between the faults, is a HORST, expressed as a BLOCK MOUNTAIN: straight steep sides and a level top, utterly unlike a fold mountain's curving profile. The Black Forest in Germany and the Vosges in France are twin horsts flanking the Rhine rift valley — a graben with a block mountain on each shoulder, all in one view. A sequence of parallel faults, each block dropping further than the last, descends in a giant staircase called STEP FAULTING; and a block faulted on only one side tilts, presenting one steep faulted face and one long gentle back slope.

## Subtopic: The Cape Fold Mountains — and Describing Structures in Words

Now the local case: the CAPE FOLD MOUNTAINS, where the whole topic stands in stone.

The raw material is the Cape Supergroup — sands and muds laid down in a shallow sea and cemented into quartzitic sandstone of exceptional hardness. Between roughly 250 and 280 million years ago, during the assembly of Gondwana, compression driving in from the south buckled those strata, and the textbook prediction appeared on the ground: long, narrow, parallel ranges with long, narrow, parallel valleys between. The Western Cape holds two trends. One set of ranges runs roughly north to south — the Olifants River Mountains and the Piketberg among them — while another runs roughly west to east, including the Riviersonderend, the Kammanassie and the Tsitsikamma ranges; the two directions knot together in the crumpled country around Ceres.

The human map still obeys the folds. The sheltered valleys collect deep soils shed from the ridges and are threaded by rivers off the mountains: the Tulbagh basin grows deciduous fruit, the Robertson valley is wine country along the Breede, and the narrow coastal shelf below the Tsitsikamma carries forest and dairy. Because each range is a genuine wall, roads cross where engineers found or forced a gap — Michell's Pass above Ceres, the Tradouw Pass through the Langeberg, Meiringspoort threading a river gorge where folded strata stand bent like ribbons in the cliff faces on either side of the road. And since these mountains stopped growing long ago, erosion has had time to invert parts of the relief: in places the stripped cores of anticlines now lie low while synclinal rock holds up the heights.

Finally, the skill that earns the marks: converting structures into precise sentences, because a question may supply the description and demand the term, or the reverse. Fix one order — the force, the response, the shape, the landform. Rift valley: tension stretched the crust; two parallel normal faults formed; the central block subsided; a flat-floored valley now lies between two steep, straight, facing scarps. Block mountain: faulting dropped the land on both flanks or raised the central block; the result stands with straight steep sides and a level summit, unlike any folded crest. Anticline: compression buckled the strata into an arch; the limbs dip away on both sides; erosion exposes the oldest rock at the core.

## Subtopic: The Dough and the Rusk

Put a fat lump of bread dough on the counter and press it inward from both sides with your palms. It humps up into ridges with dips between them, lying across the direction you pushed. You have just built fold mountains. That inward push is COMPRESSION — the force wherever two plates drive together.

Now take the dough by both ends and pull. It stretches, goes thin in the middle, and finally pulls apart. That is TENSION, the force where plates separate. Slide your left hand away from you and your right hand toward you with the dough between them, and the lump twists and drags against itself — SHEARING, the force where plates scrape past.

Squeeze, stretch, slide. Three forces, three boundaries, and every landform in this lesson comes from one of them.

But why does rock sometimes behave like dough and sometimes like a rusk? Dunk a rusk and it bends a little; snap a dry one and it breaks with a crack. Three things decide which way rock goes. Depth: far underground, rock is hot and squeezed from all sides, so it yields slowly like warm dough; near the surface it is cold and brittle like the dry rusk. Speed: push anything slowly and it sags; hit it and it shatters. And the material itself: layered rocks — shale, thin sandstones — bend along their layers, while one solid mass of granite would rather crack.

Bending is FOLDING. Breaking and then shifting is FAULTING. Same three forces; the conditions choose the outcome.

## Subtopic: Hills, Hollows and the Surprise Valley

Back to the squeezed dough, and now we name what we made.

Every hump is an ANTICLINE — the arch, the shape of a rainbow, the crest of a swell out at sea. Every dip between humps is a SYNCLINE — the trough between two swells, the sag, the bowl. The sloping sides are LIMBS, and each limb is shared between the fold on its left and the fold on its right, so the whole cross-section reads as a single wavy line rolling up and down across the page.

Keep squeezing and the folds pass through five stages — tell it as a story. A gentle squeeze: both limbs slope evenly — symmetrical. Harder from one side: the fold leans — asymmetrical. Harder still: the crest tips past upright and hangs over — an overfold. Keep going: the whole fold lies down flat on its side — recumbent. And past the limit, the rock tears along the fold and the top limb is shoved right over the bottom one — an overthrust, the moment folding hands the job to faulting. Even, leaning, overhanging, lying down, torn.

Now the twist this landscape loves to play. Everyone assumes the arch becomes the mountain and the trough becomes the valley. Often the map ends up the other way around. Roll a thick magazine into a curve and watch its spine: the outside of the bend splays and creases. The crest of an anticline suffers exactly that — stretched over the arch, it is full of fine cracks, and rain, frost and rivers exploit every one of them. The syncline, squashed tight, resists. Give erosion a few million years and the proud arch has been hollowed into a valley while the humble trough stands as the ridge line. That is INVERSION OF RELIEF: the landscape upside down relative to its own structure.

## Subtopic: The Shifting Paving Slabs

Go out to a cracked concrete path or a paved driveway, because every fault landform is lying there in miniature.

Two slabs with a crack between them, and one has settled LOWER than its neighbour — the classic trip hazard. That is a NORMAL fault, the child of TENSION, and scaled up it prints a FAULT SCARP on the landscape: a step so straight and so long that no river could have made it. Straightness that unnatural is a geographer's clue that a fault runs beneath.

Where tree roots have jacked one slab UP so it rides over the edge of the next, you have a REVERSE fault, the child of COMPRESSION — one block forced up and over its neighbour. And where two slabs have crept sideways so the line painted across them no longer joins, that is a SHEAR fault: no step up or down, just an offset — the same signature the San Andreas leaves on fences and streams, drawn at continental size.

Now use three slabs. Let the MIDDLE one sink between its two neighbours and you are looking down into a rift valley: a flat dropped floor with a straight wall on each side. At full scale that is the East African Rift — a floor wide enough for farms and lakes, walled by scarps hundreds of metres high. Let the middle slab stand PROUD while the outer two settle, and you have a block mountain: steep straight sides, flat top, the shape of a table. Germany's Black Forest and France's Vosges are that middle slab twice over, standing shoulder to shoulder on either side of the dropped Rhine valley between them. And a row of slabs each settling a little lower than the last makes the staircase — step faulting.

Last skill: telling folding from faulting at a glance. Folded country rolls — curved ridges in long parallel waves, like the ranges you cross at Michell's Pass or squeeze between in Meiringspoort, where the strata in the gorge walls are visibly bent into ribbons. Faulted country is drawn with a ruler — straight scarps, level-topped blocks, stepped edges. Curves mean the rock bent; straight lines mean it broke.
