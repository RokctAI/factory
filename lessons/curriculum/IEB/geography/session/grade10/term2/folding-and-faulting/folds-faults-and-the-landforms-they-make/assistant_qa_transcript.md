**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if the Cape Fold Mountains stopped growing millions of years ago, why are they still standing? Should erosion not have flattened them completely by now?

**Tutor:** They are still standing because of what they are made of, and the numbers make the point beautifully. The ranges are built of Cape Supergroup quartzitic sandstone — sand grains cemented by silica until the rock is nearly as hard as the quartz itself. Erosion has had on the order of 250 million years to work, and it has removed an astonishing amount: the softer shales and mudstones that once lay between and above the hard layers are largely gone, which is why the valleys are wide and deeply floored with soil. What survives is the resistant skeleton. So the mountains you see today are not the original fold crests frozen in time; they are the durable leftovers of a much larger folded mass, standing in relief precisely because everything weaker around them has been carried to the sea. That is also why inversion of relief shows up there: survival in that landscape is decided by rock strength, not by whether a layer happened to be arched up or sagged down. A useful exam sentence: the Cape Fold Mountains are old, erosional mountains — their height today measures the hardness of their rock, not the freshness of their folding.

***

**Thandi:** Next: hanging wall and footwall confused half the class. Is there a reliable way to remember which is which, and to work out whether a fault is normal or reverse?

**Tutor:** There is, and it comes from the people who spend their working lives inside faults — miners. Picture yourself standing in a tunnel dug along a sloping fault plane. The block of rock above your head, where a lamp would hang, is the HANGING wall. The block under your boots, where your feet stand, is the FOOT wall. That is genuinely where the names come from, and the image survives an exam better than any definition. Then the test for fault type is one question: what did the hanging wall do? If it slid DOWN relative to the footwall, the crust must have been stretched to open that room to move — a normal fault, from tension. If the hanging wall was forced UP over the footwall, the crust must have been shortened — a reverse fault, from compression. Two further checks confirm you: normal faulting leaves scarps and dropped blocks, so it goes with rift landscapes; reverse and thrust faulting stack rock and thicken the crust, so it goes with collision zones. Lamp above, boots below, then ask which way the lamp block moved — that little routine will not fail you.

**Thandi:** Here is one about the diagram section: why do the questions insist we describe folds and faults in words when a sketch would be so much quicker to give?

**Tutor:** Because the words are the evidence that you understand the mechanism, while a sketch can be copied from memory without any understanding at all. Anyone can draw a wiggly line and label it anticline; only someone who has grasped the process can write that compression buckled the strata into an arch, that the limbs dip away on either side, and that erosion exposes the oldest rock at the core — notice how that sentence contains the force, the response, the shape and a testable consequence. There is also a practical reason: many questions run in reverse. You are given a description — a wide, flat-floored valley walled by straight, steep scarps facing each other — and asked to name the structure and the force. If you have practised building descriptions in the fixed order, force, response, shape, landform, you can also take descriptions apart in that order: straight facing scarps means faulting, a dropped floor means a sunken block, a sunken block needs tension, therefore a graben expressed as a rift valley. Describing in words and interpreting descriptions are the same skill run forwards and backwards, and that is exactly why it is examined so heavily.

**Thandi:** Last one: rocks are stone — how can something as brittle as stone bend into folds without shattering? Some of us still cannot picture it.

**Tutor:** Your instinct is right for every rock you have ever handled, and the resolution lies in three things no classroom can show directly: depth, time and confinement. First, depth means heat, and warm materials yield where cold ones snap — the same bar of toffee that shatters from the fridge will sag over your hand once warmed. Rock kilometres down sits at hundreds of degrees. Second, confinement: at depth the rock is squeezed from every direction at once, and material that cannot open a crack — because pressure slams every crack shut as it forms — has no choice but to flow around the stress instead. Third, and most important, time. The Cape folds absorbed compression over tens of millions of years, which works out to movements far slower than the growth of your fingernails. At that rate, mineral grains dissolve at stressed edges, re-grow in sheltered ones, and glide microscopic distances along planes inside their crystals; billions of tiny adjustments add up to a bend that no single moment would reveal. Watch mountains being squeezed for a human lifetime and you would swear nothing was happening. So the honest picture is this: cold, shallow, hurried rock is a rusk and it breaks; hot, deep, patient rock behaves like dough — it folds.
