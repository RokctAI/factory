**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the plates move only a few centimetres a year, how does anyone actually measure that? Nobody can see a continent move.

**Tutor:** Not with the eye, but the measurement is now routine and startlingly precise. The workhorse is satellite positioning — the same constellation your phone uses, but processed to millimetre accuracy. Permanent receiver stations are bolted into bedrock on every continent, including several in South Africa, and each one logs its position continuously for years. Subtract this year's position from last year's and the drift falls straight out of the data: the African and South American stations really are separating by a few centimetres annually, in close agreement with what the magnetic stripes on the Atlantic floor imply as a long-term average. There are older, wonderfully clever methods too. Radio telescopes on different continents watch the same quasar — a radio source billions of light-years away — and the tiny difference in the signal's arrival times fixes the distance between the telescopes to millimetres; repeat the experiment for a decade and the baselines stretch measurably. And laser pulses bounced off reflector satellites do the same job. Three completely independent techniques, one answer. When methods that share no equipment and no assumptions agree, that is when scientists stop arguing — which is precisely what happened to plate tectonics.

***

**Thandi:** Next one: Wegener had all four types of evidence, and people still laughed at him. What was actually missing, and what finally changed everyone's minds?

**Tutor:** What was missing was a believable force, and the lesson in this story is that evidence of movement is not the same as an explanation of movement. Wegener suggested the continents ploughed through the ocean crust the way an icebreaker shoves through pack ice, and physicists could show that no available force was remotely strong enough — tides and the earth's spin fail by orders of magnitude — and that continents attempting it would crumple. So the geology community filed the fossils and the fits away as curiosities. Two mid-century developments broke the deadlock. First, the mantle came to be understood as capable of slow plastic creep, which made convection a respectable engine: the continents do not plough through anything, they are carried, passengers rather than ships. Second, the oceans were finally surveyed — sonar mapped the ridges, magnetometers found the mirrored stripes, and drilling confirmed the sea floor ages outward from the ridge. Sea-floor spreading supplied the conveyor belt Wegener never had, and within about a decade the ridiculed idea became the organising theory of the entire subject. Wegener himself did not live to see it; he died on the Greenland ice cap in 1930.

***

**Thandi:** Something from the boundaries section: you said the East African Rift could flood like the Red Sea did. Does that mean Africa is actually breaking in two? And how long would that take?

**Tutor:** It genuinely does mean that, with the usual geological patience attached. The rift marks a boundary being born: the Somali plate, carrying the Horn of Africa and much of the east coast, is easing away from the rest of the continent at somewhere around half a centimetre to a centimetre per year. You can already read the stages in order along the rift like pages in a manual. In Ethiopia's Afar region the crust is so thin that fissures open, volcanoes are active, and parts of the land surface sit below sea level — the most advanced stage. Southward through Kenya, Tanzania and Malawi the rift is younger: fault scarps, ribbon lakes, volcanoes, but thicker crust. The Red Sea shows the next chapter, a rift that thinned until the ocean broke in, and it is now widening into a young ocean with its own miniature spreading ridge. Run the East African Rift forward some tens of millions of years and the likely outcome is a new sea lane where the valley floor is today, with East Africa sailing off as an island continent. Nothing in your lifetime changes on any map — but the lakes, the scarps and the volcanoes you learned today are the construction site of an ocean.

**Thandi:** Last one: South Africa sits far from every plate boundary, so why must we still learn the Ring of Fire and subduction, and are we truly safe from big earthquakes here?

**Tutor:** Two answers, one practical and one honest. Practically, the boundary processes are not foreign affairs. The Cape Fold Mountains exist because compression once acted on this very crust when Gondwana was assembling — today's Andes are a live demonstration of the process that built the mountains you can drive through near Worcester. The rift system is on our own continent, our trade routes and airline connections cross the Ring of Fire, and a tsunami born at an Indonesian subduction zone crossed the Indian Ocean in 2004 and measurably reached South African harbours. Understanding boundaries is understanding both our own rocks' history and our neighbours' present dangers. Now the honest answer on safety: being mid-plate lowers the risk, it does not delete it. Old faults from ancient events lie healed but not erased inside the plate, and occasionally one moves — the Ceres-Tulbagh earthquake of 1969 remains the country's most damaging natural one. Add the tremors triggered by deep gold mining on the Witwatersrand and around Klerksdorp, which residents there feel regularly. So the syllabus point stands: South Africa is a low-risk country, not a no-risk one, and the difference between those two phrases is exactly why the measurement and preparedness lessons that follow matter here too.
