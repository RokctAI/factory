# Part 1 — Expert

Look at any world map long enough and a suspicion forms: the continents look like pieces of something that broke. That suspicion turns out to be correct. The continents have moved, are moving at this moment, and their collisions and separations manufacture every great mountain chain and set off nearly every serious earthquake on the planet. This session assembles the evidence that the continents drift, identifies the engine that drives them, and works through the three kinds of plate boundary and the landforms each one builds — the scaffolding that folding, faulting, earthquakes and volcanoes will all hang from for the rest of the term.

## Subtopic: Continental Drift and the Evidence for It

The idea has a definite birthday. In 1912 Alfred Wegener, a German meteorologist, argued that today's continents once formed a single supercontinent, PANGAEA — the name means "all the earth" — which started breaking up around 200 million years ago into LAURASIA in the north and GONDWANA in the south. Southern Africa lay near the heart of Gondwana, pressed against South America on one side and Antarctica on the other.

Wegener built his case on four independent categories of evidence, and you are expected to command all four by name.

The FIT of the continents. South America's Atlantic coastline tucks into the western curve of Africa far too neatly for chance — and the match sharpens further when you fit the CONTINENTAL SHELVES, the true drowned edges of the landmasses, rather than the present shorelines.

FOSSIL evidence. Mesosaurus was a small reptile of fresh water; open ocean would have killed it, yet its remains occur in the Karoo rocks of South Africa and in beds of identical age in Brazil. The seed fern Glossopteris produced seeds far too heavy for wind to ferry across an ocean, yet it is dug up in South Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica. Lystrosaurus, a stocky land reptile from our Karoo, appears again in India and Antarctica. Living things that could not cross water are found on both sides of it.

GEOLOGICAL evidence. Rock formations and entire mountain belts break off at one coastline and resume across the ocean. The Cape Fold Mountains have their continuation in the Sierra de la Ventana ranges of Argentina and in the mountains of Antarctica — the same rock types, the same ages, folded in the same event.

CLIMATIC evidence. At the base of the Karoo sequence lies the Dwyka tillite, debris bulldozed and dumped by a continental ice sheet — in a country of sunshine and drought. On the glacial pavements near Barkly West you can still run your hand along scratches that ice gouged into solid rock some 300 million years ago. Deposits of the same age occur in South America, India and Australia, and the directions of the scratches only make sense — radiating from one ice centre — when the southern continents are reassembled. Meanwhile Antarctica, today buried under ice, contains coal, which only forms in warm swamps.

Wegener's fatal gap was the mechanism: he could not explain what force moved continents, and the scientific establishment rejected him for decades. The questions for this section are with you now — the four categories of evidence, one specific example of each, and the reason the theory was initially refused.

## Subtopic: The Engine — Convection Currents and Sea-Floor Spreading

The missing engine was eventually found in the mantle. Radioactive decay inside the earth, plus heat surviving from the planet's violent formation, keeps the interior at thousands of degrees. That heat sets up CONVECTION CURRENTS in the plastic asthenosphere: hotter, less dense rock creeps upward, spreads out sideways beneath the rigid lithosphere, sheds its heat, grows denser and sinks back down — a complete circulation loop turning at only a few centimetres per year, about the pace at which your hair grows.

State this with precision, because sloppy wording costs marks: the mantle is SOLID rock deforming plastically. There is no underground sea of lava, and the currents involved would look motionless to any human observer.

Riding on this circulation, the lithosphere — crust plus rigid upper mantle, roughly 100 kilometres thick — is cracked into about fifteen major TECTONIC PLATES. Two additional forces supply most of the push. RIDGE PUSH: new crust at a spreading ridge stands high, and gravity slides the plate down and away from that elevation. SLAB PULL: once a cold, dense plate edge starts sinking into the mantle, its weight drags the rest of the plate after it, and this pull is now regarded as the stronger of the two.

Confirmation came from the one place nobody had properly examined — the ocean floor. In the early 1960s Harry Hess proposed SEA-FLOOR SPREADING: molten rock wells up along a mid-ocean ridge, hardens into new oceanic crust, and is carried away from the ridge on either side like goods on twin conveyor belts. Two discoveries clinched it. AGE: the ocean floor is brand new at the ridge crest and ages steadily outward toward the continents, and no sea floor anywhere exceeds about 200 million years — against continental rocks of three billion and more. PALAEOMAGNETISM: the earth's magnetic field reverses from time to time, and iron minerals in cooling lava freeze the field's direction into the rock. The ocean floor therefore carries magnetic STRIPES parallel to the ridge, and the pattern on one side MIRRORS the other exactly — a symmetrical tape recording of crust being made and carried apart.

The South Atlantic is currently widening by roughly two to five centimetres each year; give that 130 million years and you have the ocean between Cape Town and Buenos Aires. Take this section's questions now: convection worded correctly, ridge push separated from slab pull, and the two proofs of sea-floor spreading.

## Subtopic: Divergent and Transform Boundaries

Classify every plate boundary by RELATIVE MOVEMENT — apart, together, or sliding past. Start with the two gentler cases.

At a DIVERGENT boundary the plates separate, and because new crust is manufactured in the gap these are also called CONSTRUCTIVE boundaries. Under an ocean the upwelling magma builds a MID-OCEAN RIDGE — an underwater mountain range with a rift running along its crest where the plates part company. The eruptions here are FISSURE eruptions of fluid basaltic lava, building low shield volcanoes; where the ridge grows tall enough to break the surface it produces volcanic islands, of which Iceland is the giant example and Ascension Island, in the Atlantic between Africa and Brazil, a smaller one. Earthquakes are common but SHALLOW and mostly moderate, because the young crust is thin, hot and mechanically weak.

Where divergence happens under a continent, the result is a RIFT VALLEY at continental scale. The EAST AFRICAN RIFT stretches roughly 6 000 kilometres from Ethiopia's Afar region down toward the lower Zambezi. Picture the sequence: tension stretches the crust, two long parallel fault systems develop, and the strip between them subsides like a platform being lowered, leaving a wide flat floor between imposing fault scarps. The floor collects long, narrow, extremely deep lakes — Tanganyika and Malawi — while volcanoes, Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya among them, grow along the fractures. Let the process run further and the sea invades: the Red Sea is a rift that has already flooded.

At a TRANSFORM boundary the plates slide horizontally past one another, and because crust is neither made nor destroyed these are called CONSERVATIVE boundaries. The type example is the San Andreas Fault, where the Pacific plate grinds north-west past the North American plate through California. No volcanoes form — nothing descends, so nothing melts — and no fold mountains either. The signature landform is a long, conspicuously straight fault trace with features displaced sideways across it: stream channels kinked, roads and fence lines offset by metres. The earthquakes, however, are dangerous ones. Friction locks the plates, strain builds for decades, and the eventual release is shallow, sudden and violent, often directly beneath cities.

Constructive and conservative done; destructive is next, and it is the big one. Pause for this section's questions: both boundary types with their alternative names, the landforms of each, and the reason one produces volcanoes while the other cannot.

## Subtopic: Convergent Boundaries and Their Landforms

At a CONVERGENT, or destructive, boundary plates advance toward each other and lithosphere is destroyed. There are three combinations, and each builds its own set of landforms.

OCEANIC AGAINST CONTINENTAL. The oceanic plate, at about 3,0 grams per cubic centimetre against the continent's 2,7, is the denser, so it SUBDUCTS — it bends down and descends beneath the continental edge along an inclined zone traced by earthquake foci that get deeper inland. The landform kit is always the same three items: an offshore OCEAN TRENCH marking the down-bend; FOLD MOUNTAINS crumpled up along the continental margin by the compression; and COMPOSITE VOLCANOES threaded along those mountains, fed by magma created where water sweated out of the descending slab lowers the melting point of the mantle wedge above it. The west coast of South America displays the full kit — the Peru-Chile Trench offshore and the Andes with their volcanoes behind it, all from one subduction system.

OCEANIC AGAINST OCEANIC. The older, colder, denser of the two plates subducts, opening a trench — the Mariana Trench, at about eleven kilometres the deepest gash on earth — and building an ISLAND ARC, a curved line of volcanoes rising from the sea floor. The Aleutian Islands, curving between Alaska and Asia, and the great arc of Indonesia are the pattern made visible.

CONTINENTAL AGAINST CONTINENTAL. Now neither side will sink — both crusts are thick, light and buoyant — so the collision zone can only crumple and thicken upward, stacking the loftiest FOLD MOUNTAINS on the planet. India has been ramming into Eurasia for around 50 million years, and the Himalayas that resulted are still gaining height. Learn the twist this case carries: mighty mountains and severe earthquakes, but effectively NO volcanoes, because no slab goes down to make magma.

Map every earthquake and volcano on earth and they draw the boundaries for you: the RING OF FIRE around the Pacific rim, the belt running from the Mediterranean through Iran into the Himalayas, and the mid-ocean ridge system. South Africa sits far inside the African plate, distant from every boundary — hence its scarcity of natural earthquakes, and hence why most tremors in the goldfields of Gauteng and the North West are triggered by deep mining rather than by plates.

Three traps to sidestep: a plate is lithosphere, never crust alone; the plate that subducts is the DENSER one, not the smaller one; and continent-on-continent collision builds no volcanoes. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — the three convergent cases with their landform sets, and the world pattern explained.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same restless planet through a torn photograph, the skin on a pot of milk, and three trolleys in a supermarket aisle.

## Subtopic: The Torn Photograph

Take an old family photograph and tear it in two. Post the halves to relatives in different towns. Years later the halves surface again — and the question is how anyone proves they belong together. The torn edges matching is suggestive. But the real proof is the PICTURE: if a face continues across the tear, if a doorway lines up, if the pattern on a dress runs perfectly from one half onto the other, the argument is finished.

The continents are the two halves of that photograph. The matching torn edge is the coastline fit — South America nesting into the curve of West Africa. The picture running across the tear is everything else.

The fossils continue across. A little freshwater reptile called Mesosaurus, which could never have survived an ocean crossing, lies in the rocks of the Karoo and lies again in Brazil. A plant called Glossopteris, with seeds too heavy for any wind, grew across South Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica. One picture, torn apart.

The rocks continue across. The Cape Fold Mountains run down our coast, stop at the sea — and rocks of the same type, same age and same folding stand in Argentina and in Antarctica. Mountain ranges do not leap oceans.

The ice continues across. Near Barkly West you can touch bedrock scored by glaciers, and the boulder-clay those glaciers dumped sits at the bottom of the Karoo rocks — evidence of an ice sheet in a hot country. Match the scratch directions in Africa, South America, India and Australia and they all point away from one centre — but only if you push the continents back together first.

Wegener showed all this in 1912 and was ridiculed anyway, for a single missing piece: he could not say what did the pushing. Your questions on the evidence are with you now — the fit, the fossils, the rocks, the ice, and the gap that sank the theory for fifty years.

## Subtopic: The Skin on the Milk

Warm a pot of milk slowly and watch it. Heat enters at the bottom; the warm milk rises up the middle, drifts outward across the top, cools against the air, and slides down the sides to be warmed again. A slow, silent wheel of circulation — a convection current, running in every warm pot in every kitchen.

The mantle turns the same wheel, unimaginably slowed down. Its heat comes from radioactive decay and from the planet's fiery birth. Its "milk" is solid rock that creeps like ancient toffee, circulating at the speed your hair grows.

Now let the milk form a SKIN, as warm milk does. The skin is the lithosphere — the crust with the rigid top of the mantle stuck to it, a hundred kilometres of solid plate. The circulating milk drags the skin around the surface. Where a current rises, the skin is pulled apart and tears; where currents sink, skin is dragged toward the plughole. The skin does not float wherever it likes — the wheel underneath is steering it.

Two extra pushes complete the engine. Fresh crust at a ridge sits up high, so gravity toboggans the plate away downhill from the bump — a shove from behind. And once one edge of a cold heavy plate starts to sink, its weight hauls in the rest, the way a belt half over the edge of a table takes the buckle with it. The push of the ridge, the pull of the slab.

And the sea floor kept the paperwork. Rock at the ridge crest is newborn and gets older with every kilometre you travel away from it — yet nowhere older than about two hundred million years, while the continents hold rocks fifteen times that age. Better still, because the earth's magnetic field flips now and then, and cooling lava locks in the direction of the moment, the sea floor is striped like a barcode — and the barcode on the west of the ridge is the mirror image of the barcode on the east. A conveyor belt, printing matching receipts in both directions.

Questions on the milk pot are coming to you right now — what turns the wheel, the two extra pushes, and how the striped sea floor proves it.

## Subtopic: Three Ways Plates Meet

Everything two plates can do to each other comes down to three moves, and a supermarket aisle full of trolleys will teach you all of them.

MOVE ONE: rolling apart. Two trolleys are pulled away from each other and a gap opens between them. In the earth, molten rock rises into that gap and sets hard, so new ground is genuinely being manufactured — the constructive boundary. Under the ocean this builds a long underwater mountain range with a crack along its spine, tall enough at Iceland to stand on. Across a continent it unzips a rift valley, and Africa owns the world's greatest: from Ethiopia down toward the Zambezi, the ground has split along two parallel lines and the strip between has sunk, leaving a flat floor walled by cliffs, floored in places by deep ribbon lakes like Malawi and Tanganyika, with volcanoes — Kilimanjaro is one — standing along the cracks.

MOVE TWO: ramming head-on. The destructive boundary, because something has to give. When a sea-floor trolley meets a continental trolley, the sea floor is the HEAVIER of the two, so it dips and drives underneath: a deep trench where it bends down, folded mountains piled up along the coast, and volcanoes planted on the mountains — the west of South America in one diagram. When two sea-floor plates meet, the older heavier one dives and a curved chain of volcanic islands boils up, the way Indonesia did. And when two continents meet, neither will duck — both are too light to sink — so the ground simply crumples upward into the biggest mountains on earth, the Himalayas, which are still rising. Keep the gift fact: continent against continent means huge mountains, huge earthquakes and NO volcanoes, because nothing sinks and so nothing melts.

MOVE THREE: scraping past. Two trolleys squeeze by each other in a narrow aisle, sides rubbing. Nothing is created, nothing destroyed — the conservative boundary — and no volcanoes at all. But the sides snag; push builds up; and when they suddenly jerk free, everything rattles. That is the San Andreas Fault in California: a long dead-straight scar across the land, with streams, roads and fences stepped sideways wherever they cross it, and a history of shallow, brutal earthquakes.

Apart, together, past — constructive, destructive, conservative. Own those three and the rest of the term is applications. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — the three moves with their proper names, the landforms each one builds, and the meeting that never grows a volcano.
