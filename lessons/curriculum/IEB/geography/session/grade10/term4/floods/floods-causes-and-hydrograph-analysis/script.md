# Part 1 — Expert

No natural hazard touches South African lives more often than the flood. Every summer, somewhere in this country, a river leaves its channel and takes streets, fields or homes with it. This session builds the full explanation in two halves — the physical machinery that turns rain into flood, and the human decisions that turn flood into disaster — and then trains the skill the topic is really testing: reading a flood hydrograph, described entirely in words, and using it to compare catchments, so that any storm graph put in front of you becomes a story you can tell.

## Subtopic: What Turns Rain into a Flood — Physical Causes

Define the event precisely first. A FLOOD happens when water covers land that is usually dry — most commonly when a river is handed more water than its channel can pass on, so the surplus spreads sideways across the floodplain. Notice what the definition does not say: it does not say heavy rain. Heavy rain is only the beginning of the explanation, because identical storms produce a shrug in one valley and a catastrophe in the next. The rest of the explanation lives in the rain's character and in the catchment that receives it.

The rain's character has two dimensions. The first is INTENSITY — how fast the water arrives. Sixty millimetres delivered in ninety minutes of hail-flecked thunderstorm beats the soil's infiltration speed and runs straight off the surface; the same sixty millimetres drizzled out over a fortnight disappears into the ground without drama. South Africa's interior summer is thunderstorm season, so short violent bursts are our standard delivery. The second dimension is DURATION. The great wide-area floods come from weather systems that refuse to move — above all the CUT-OFF LOW, a pool of cold upper air that pinches off from the westerly wind belt and then sits, rotating slowly, milking moisture out of the atmosphere over the same catchments for days on end. Cut-off lows drowned large parts of Natal in September 1987 and battered the Western Cape repeatedly through 2023. In the far north-east there is a third supplier: tropical cyclones and their remnants spinning in from the Mozambique Channel, as Domoina did in 1984 when it flooded the rivers of northern KwaZulu-Natal, and as Dineo's remains did to Limpopo in 2017.

Now the catchment. Five physical properties decide what a catchment does with a storm. ANTECEDENT MOISTURE — the water already sitting in the soil from earlier rain — sets the available storage: soil that is already full converts nearly every new drop into surface runoff. SLOPE controls speed: on steep mountain gradients water reaches the channels before infiltration gets a chance, which is why the fastest, most violent floods belong to mountain streams. ROCK AND SOIL control absorption: shale, granite domes and thin hard-baked soils turn rain away, while deep sands swallow it. VEGETATION works entirely on the flood's side of the ledger when present: leaves intercept the rain, stems slow its crawl downhill, roots keep the soil open, and the plants themselves pump water back out of the ground between storms. Strip it and every one of those services stops. Finally, CATCHMENT SHAPE: a round, compact catchment is a funnel whose every corner drains to the outlet in roughly equal travel time, so the whole storm arrives together in one stacked surge; a long thin catchment strings the arrivals out and flattens the surge.

Take this section's questions now: intensity against duration, the stalled cut-off low, and the five catchment properties that decide a storm's fate.

## Subtopic: The Human Hand in Flooding

Hold a distinction at the centre of this section: floods are natural events, but flood disasters are largely built. People amplify the hazard, and people place themselves inside it.

The largest amplifier is URBANISATION. A growing city replaces soil with surfaces that cannot drink — tar, paving, concrete, roof sheeting — and then adds gutters and stormwater pipes whose entire engineering purpose is to move rainwater off property and into the nearest stream as quickly as possible. Both changes push in the same direction: more of the storm reaches the river, and it reaches the river sooner. A stream that once gathered its water gently from grassland now receives the whole suburb's rainfall through concrete throats within minutes. The Jukskei, the small river that drains northern Johannesburg, is the living demonstration: its catchment is one of the most heavily sealed in the country, and it flips from a trickle to a torrent within an hour of a Highveld storm.

The second amplifier is the stripping of vegetation through DEFORESTATION AND OVERGRAZING. The immediate effect is faster, larger runoff; the delayed effect is worse. Bare soil erodes, and the eroded sediment travels downstream to settle exactly where it does the most damage — on riverbeds and dam floors, raising channels and eating storage, so every following flood rides higher on a shallower river. Third comes WETLAND DESTRUCTION. A healthy wetland is flood infrastructure that nature provides free: it spreads an arriving surge across reeds and mud, holds it, and pays it back to the river slowly over weeks. Drain that wetland for plots or plant it under a mall, and the surge passes through undelayed. Fourth, NEGLECTED SYSTEMS: stormwater drains blocked by litter, culverts too small for the storms they now face, and dams whose walls and spillways go unmaintained.

The fifth factor is the one that costs lives: SETTLEMENT ON FLOODPLAINS. A floodplain looks like ordinary flat land, but it is the river's own construction — deposited by floods, and revisited by them on a schedule no title deed interrupts. Yet the pressure of housing pushes people onto it from both ends of the income scale: developers because flat riverside land is cheap to service, and families in informal settlements because it is the only land left unclaimed — and it is unclaimed for a reason. In Alexandra, homes stand close enough to the Jukskei to hear it; in Mamelodi east of Pretoria, riverside households watched the water come through their doors in December 2019. The honest geographical sentence is this: in South Africa, exposure to flooding maps onto poverty, because the most dangerous land is the land left over for those with the fewest choices.

Pause for this section's questions: the sealed and piped city, silt and lost wetlands, and why the floodplain fills with the people least able to leave it.

## Subtopic: The Flood Hydrograph, Described in Words

Everything so far becomes measurable on one instrument: the FLOOD HYDROGRAPH, the graph of a river's answer to a single storm. Exams will ask you to label it, define its parts and interpret it — so learn to speak it fluently without seeing it.

Set up the frame. Along the horizontal axis runs TIME, in hours or days. Up the vertical axis runs DISCHARGE — the volume of water passing the gauging station every second, measured in cubic metres per second, a unit spoken as cumecs. Near the origin, the storm itself is usually drawn as a short block of rainfall bars. Against that frame the river draws one wave.

Walk the wave from left to right. Before the rain, the trace runs low and flat: this is BASE FLOW, the river's ordinary discharge, supplied not by recent rain but by groundwater leaking steadily out of the saturated ground into the channel — the reason a healthy river keeps flowing through the dry months. Some time after the rain begins, the trace bends upward into the RISING LIMB. It climbs steeply, because the first water to reach the gauge is the storm's fast water — surface runoff racing over the ground and through any drains. The climb ends at PEAK DISCHARGE, the highest flow of the event and the moment the river is most likely to top its banks. Now measure backwards: the time interval separating the heaviest rainfall from that peak is the LAG TIME, and the definition must be stated exactly that way — heaviest rain to highest flow. Lag time is travel time: every drop must journey from where it landed, over slopes and along tributaries, to the gauge, and the lag is that journey summed across the catchment. A long lag is a merciful river that gives warning; a short lag is a river that arrives before the warning does. Past the peak, the trace descends along the FALLING LIMB, also called the recession limb — and it descends noticeably more gently than it rose, because the late-arriving water is the slow water, seeping sideways through soil and trailing in from the catchment's far edges. Eventually the trace settles back onto base flow, usually a little above its pre-storm level, because the freshly recharged soil now feeds the channel more generously.

Interpretation then rests on three measurements, and only three. How high did the discharge peak? How short was the lag? How steep were the limbs? High, short and steep together describe a FLASHY catchment — one that flings storms at its river. Low, long and gentle describe an absorbent catchment that swallows storms and repays them slowly. Every physical and human factor from the first two sections registers on the hydrograph through those three readings.

Your questions on this section are ready now: axes and units, every named part of the wave in order, lag time defined precisely, and the three readings.

## Subtopic: Reading Hydrographs — Urban Against Rural, Flash Against Regional

The classic comparison question hands you one storm and two catchments, and expects two hydrographs described in disciplined contrast.

Let the first catchment be rural grassland with intact wetlands and deep soil. Its hydrograph tells a patient story: a LONG lag, because interception and infiltration hold the water back; a LOW, broad, rounded peak, because much of the storm entered the ground and is arriving stretched out over time; gently sloping limbs; and, between storms, a healthy persistent base flow, because the groundwater the storm recharged keeps paying into the channel. Let the second catchment be the same valley after urbanisation: rain now lands on roofing and tar, and stormwater pipes deliver it to the channel at design speed. Its hydrograph is the same story told in a shout: a SHORT lag, a HIGH and narrow peak — the identical volume of water compressed into a fraction of the time — limbs rising and falling steeply, and a starved base flow between storms because so little water ever reached the ground. Summarise the transformation in one line: sealing a catchment raises the peak, shortens the lag and steepens the limbs.

The same three readings separate the two great flood types. A FLASH FLOOD is the extreme flashy signature made real: intense rain on a steep, sealed or saturated catchment, a lag measured in minutes, and a violent short peak. On the third of December 2022 a storm broke over the Jukskei's paved catchment upstream of Alexandra; the river rose with almost no warning on people gathered at the water for a river service, and at least fourteen people died in a flood that had passed within hours. A REGIONAL FLOOD is the broad signature at provincial scale: a stalled system raining for days over thousands of square kilometres, rivers climbing steadily and staying up. In September 1987 a cut-off low anchored itself over Natal and delivered several hundred millimetres across days; rivers from the Mgeni northwards spread across their floodplains, bridges and roads failed across the province, and more than three hundred people died — a slower flood than the Jukskei's, but incomparably larger in reach and duration.

Finish with management, because the hydrograph writes the prescription itself: every effective measure either lengthens the lag or lowers the peak. Rewet and protect wetlands, revegetate catchments, choose permeable surfaces over sealed ones, keep drains clear, refuse development on floodplains, and build warning systems fast enough to beat whatever lag remains — with honest planning that stops the poorest being left the river's own land.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the two catchments contrasted reading by reading, flash against regional through the two named events, and the remedies the hydrograph prescribes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same topic again, this time starting at home with a bucket of water, a lawn and a driveway.

## Subtopic: The Lawn and the Driveway

Fill a bucket at the outside tap. Throw it across the lawn. Then fill it again and throw it across the paved driveway. Everything in the flood topic is sitting in the difference between what happens next.

On the lawn: the water darkens the grass and is gone. Ten minutes later the soil is damp and nothing has moved anywhere. On the driveway: the water is already at the gate, running for the street in a thin fast sheet before the bucket is even empty. Same bucket, same amount of water — the only thing that changed is the surface it met.

The lawn is a healthy catchment. Grass and leaves catch the water, roots hold the soil open, the ground drinks, and whatever the ground stores comes back out slowly, days later, as the seep that keeps rivers alive between rains. The driveway is every surface that refuses to drink: bare rock on a mountainside, soil baked hard by drought, soil already full from last week's storms — and, biggest of all, the city itself, which is thousands of hectares of deliberate driveway: tar, paving, rooftops, all draining to gutters designed to hurry the water away.

So ask the flood question the simple way: when does a river flood? When its catchment stops behaving like the lawn and starts behaving like the driveway. Some catchments were born that way — steep and rocky, shedding every storm. Some become that way for a week at a time — soak a lawn thoroughly enough and your next bucket runs off it exactly as if it were paved, which is why the last storm of a wet fortnight is the dangerous one. And some are converted permanently: build a suburb and you have paved your catchment for good.

Two last pieces. How the water arrives matters as much as how much: a garden sprinkler running all afternoon wets the lawn gently, but a pressure washer delivering the same litres in two minutes gouges and floods it — intensity beats total. And people can un-lawn the land directly: cut the trees, let cattle strip the grass, drain the wet marshy patch at the bottom of the valley, and a catchment that once drank its storms starts shedding them.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: lawn against driveway, the three ways a catchment turns into paving, and the pressure washer point.

## Subtopic: A Graph You Can Hear

Stand on a bridge over a river with your eyes closed through one whole storm, and you would hear the entire hydrograph. Read the graph as that soundtrack and it stops being intimidating.

Before the storm, the river runs with a low, steady murmur. That murmur is BASE FLOW — and here is the detail worth marks: it is not rainwater at all, but groundwater, seeping out of the saturated earth into the channel day and night. It is the reason rivers do not fall silent in the dry season.

The rain arrives, loud on your jacket — but the river's sound does not change. Not yet. The water that is landing on distant slopes still has to travel to reach you, and that silent in-between stretch is the LAG TIME: the gap between the heaviest rain and the river's highest flow. Then the pitch begins to climb — quickly. This is the RISING LIMB, and it is steep because the first arrivals are the sprinters: surface runoff that raced downhill, plus anything the stormwater pipes fired straight into the channel. The roar builds to its loudest moment — PEAK DISCHARGE, maximum flow, maximum danger, the moment the river decides whether it stays inside its banks. Afterwards the sound fades, but slowly, far more slowly than it built: the FALLING LIMB is gentle because the late water is the walkers — moisture oozing through soil, arrivals from the far corners of the catchment. Long after the sky has cleared, the river is still louder than it was at the start, because the soaked ground keeps topping it up: base flow has settled at a slightly higher murmur.

To interpret any hydrograph they can print, ask the storm's three questions. How loud did it get — the height of the peak? How soon after the rain — the lag? How fast did it build — the steepness of the climb? Loud, soon and fast is a flashy river, the kind that kills; soft, late and slow is an absorbent catchment taking the storm in its stride. And the favourite comparison becomes obvious the moment you hear it: over a grassy rural valley the storm plays as a long slow swell, while over a paved suburb the identical storm plays as one sharp crack — sooner, higher, steeper — because pipes and paving turned all the walkers into sprinters.

Questions on the storm's soundtrack are up now: base flow, lag time, both limbs and the peak, and the three questions.

## Subtopic: The Jukskei and the Natal Floods — Lessons From Real Floods

Two South African floods, thirty-five years apart, hold every idea in this lesson between them.

The Jukskei, third of December 2022. The Jukskei is not a great river — you can step across it in winter. But it drains northern Johannesburg, which means its catchment is roofs, roads, parking lots and paving from end to end, all plumbed with stormwater pipes pointing at the channel. That Saturday afternoon a thunderstorm burst upstream while congregants stood at the river during a service near Alexandra. The catchment did what paved catchments do: it delivered the storm almost whole, almost immediately. The river rose in minutes — a lag time of next to nothing — and a surge swept through the gathering; at least fourteen people died in a flood that was over the same day. Read the lessons in the topic's language: an intense storm, a fully sealed catchment, a lag too short for any warning, and people standing in the channel's own space — the complete anatomy of a flash flood, in the middle of a city.

Natal, September 1987. The opposite flood in almost every reading. A cut-off low stalled near the coast and rained for days — several hundred millimetres onto hills that were soaked before the worst of it began. This was not one racing surge but a whole province's rivers climbing together and refusing to come down: the Mgeni and its neighbours spread kilometres wide, bridges tore away, roads closed across the region, and more than three hundred people died over days, many in low-lying settlements along the rivers. The lessons: duration stacked on saturation; a regional flood's signature — the broad, sustained hydrograph rather than the spike; and, again, the deaths concentrated where housing stood on the water's land.

Now put the pair side by side and the whole topic recites itself. Causes: intensity on a sealed catchment at the Jukskei; days of rain on saturated ground in Natal — with the cut-off low, our flood-maker-in-chief, behind the second. Human hands: the paved catchment and riverside settlement in Johannesburg; floodplain settlement in Natal. Characteristics: the sharp crack against the long swell — flash against regional. And the remedies are the failures reversed: unseal and revegetate what we can, protect the wetlands that still exist, keep every drain clear, keep homes off the river's land and give people safer ground to hold, and build warnings that move faster than the water. The rain will return on its own schedule; the hydrograph only asks what kind of catchment it will find when it does.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the two floods compared cause by cause and reading by reading, and the remedy each one teaches.
