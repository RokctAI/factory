Hey, it's Thandi — I know the hydrograph looks like something out of a maths paper, but breathe: this entire topic is one backyard experiment, one wave with five names, and three questions. Let me fold it up small for you.

The experiment: throw a bucket of water on the lawn, then on the driveway. The lawn drinks it and lets it go slowly — that is a healthy catchment, all grass, roots, deep soil and wetlands. The driveway sheds it to the gate in seconds — that is rock, baked ground, soil already full, and above all the paved city. Every single cause of flooding is just something that turns lawn into driveway: a violent storm, steep slopes, last week's rain still in the soil, stripped vegetation, drained wetlands, tar and rooftops.

The wave with five names, in order: base flow — the river's everyday groundwater murmur. Rising limb — steep, because the fast surface water sprints in first. Peak discharge — the top, the danger moment. Lag time — define it word-perfectly: the gap between the heaviest rain and the highest flow. Falling limb — gentler than the rise, because the slow soil water strolls in last.

The three questions unlock any hydrograph: how high the peak, how soon the lag, how steep the climb. High, soon, steep — flashy and dangerous, the paved or rocky catchment. Low, late, gentle — the lawn. Urban against rural is just those three questions answered twice.

Case studies, one line each: the Jukskei, December 2022 — a storm on Johannesburg's sealed catchment, minutes of lag, at least fourteen lives at a river service: the flash flood. Natal, September 1987 — a cut-off low raining for days on soaked hills, rivers up across the province, more than three hundred lives: the regional flood.
