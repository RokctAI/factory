# Part 1 — Expert

A country's laws can promise water; only its engineering, its municipalities and its citizens can deliver it. South Africa makes the promise in its constitution and translates it into a monthly allowance of free water for poor households — and then must keep that promise in one of the driest countries on earth. This session examines the free basic water policy and its arithmetic, the very different obstacles delivery meets in rural and in urban South Africa, the heavy engineering that moves and cleans the nation's water, and finally the discipline of sustainable water use — split, as the topic demands, between the state's responsibilities and the citizen's.

## Subtopic: Free Basic Water — The Policy and the Promise

The legal spine comes first. Section 27 of the Constitution declares that everyone has the right of access to sufficient water. The National Water Act of 1998 then rebuilt the country's water law on a principle worth quoting: the nation's water belongs to all its people, held in trust and managed by the state — and before any farm, mine or factory receives an allocation, a RESERVE is set aside, consisting of the water human beings need for basic living plus the water the rivers themselves need to keep functioning as ecosystems.

FREE BASIC WATER, adopted as national policy in 2001, turns the right into a measurable entitlement: 6 000 litres of clean water per poor household per month, at no charge. Learn the arithmetic that generates the figure, because questions probe it: an allowance of 25 litres per person per day, multiplied by an assumed household of eight people, over a month, gives six kilolitres. Policy adds a geography clause — the supply point should stand within 200 metres of the household — and a candour clause is worth adding yourself: 25 litres covers drinking, cooking and basic hygiene, nothing more, while a fully plumbed suburban resident routinely uses around 150 litres a day. The allowance is a floor beneath human dignity, not a standard of comfort.

Free water still has to be paid for — dams, pipelines, purification and pumps all bill somebody — so understand the funding machinery. Municipalities cover the free allocation from national grants and from the design of the water bill itself: the RISING BLOCK TARIFF. Consumption is priced in steps — the first block free or nearly so, each subsequent block more expensive per litre than the last. The intent is redistribution and restraint in a single instrument: a frugal household never climbs out of the cheap blocks, while heavy consumption is charged at rates that both discourage waste and cross-subsidise the basic allowance. The irrigated lawn, in effect, helps pay for the village standpipe.

Progress since 1994 is real — the large majority of households now have access to a basic supply — but the term access deserves suspicion. A shared standpipe hundreds of metres away is access; so is a connection that delivers nothing for weeks because the pump upstream has failed. Between the entitlement on paper and water actually flowing stands everything the next section describes.

## Subtopic: Why Delivery Is Hard — Rural and Urban Challenges

Structure any answer here geographically, because the rural and urban problems are different in kind, not just in size.

Rural South Africa first. The core difficulty is DISPERSAL ACROSS DISTANCE: in the former homeland regions of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo, small settlements scatter across vast and often rugged terrain, so every kilometre of pipe serves a handful of households rather than a street of them — the cost per connection multiplies, in precisely the regions that historic neglect left furthest behind. Add FRAGILE SOURCES: many inland districts depend on a small dam, a spring or a borehole, and a single dry season can fail all three. Add the MAINTENANCE TRAP: schemes get built with capital grants, then break — a pump burns out, a pipeline cracks, diesel money runs dry — and a small municipality without engineers or revenue cannot repair what it could not afford to operate; the village quietly returns to the river. And the failure is carried on particular shoulders: WOMEN AND GIRLS do the fetching, hours and kilometres of it, paid for in schooling, safety and strength — while water shared with livestock brings waterborne diseases, cholera among them, back into South African headlines.

Urban South Africa fails differently. The city's problem is SPEED: people arrive faster than pipes, and new informal settlements form on unserviced land — often the floodplains and steep slopes hardest to plumb — where shared standpipes and queues stand in for taps. The second problem is MONEY: municipal water systems run on revenue, but widespread unemployment leaves many households unable to pay, and the resulting cycle of billing disputes, credit control and disconnections is one of the country's most persistent flashpoints. The third is the SILENT LEAK: much urban infrastructure is decades old, and nationally a third or more of treated municipal water is NON-REVENUE — escaping through burst mains, misfiring meters, or use that is never billed. Hold the implication: a city losing a third of its product must treat and pump three litres to sell two. Add theft and vandalism of pumps, cables and fittings, and the urban system bleeds from many small wounds at once.

Beneath both geographies lies the same institutional illness: municipalities short of money, skills and maintenance discipline. Infrastructure is a promise that must be renewed daily, and where renewal stops, the access statistics keep smiling while the taps go dry — as residents of many South African towns, from the Vaal to the Eastern Cape, can testify.

Pause for this section's questions: the rural cost-and-distance trap and its human carriers, the urban trio of speed, money and leaks, and the municipal weakness beneath both.

## Subtopic: Big Engineering — Transfers, Dams and Purification

The state's structural answers operate at three scales: move the water, store the water, clean the water.

Moving means INTER-BASIN TRANSFERS — taking water from a catchment with surplus and piping it into a catchment with deficit. South Africa leads the world in this art because its geography demands it: Gauteng, the economic engine, occupies a high, riverless watershed. The masterpiece is the LESOTHO HIGHLANDS WATER PROJECT. Its two founding facts: the Lesotho highlands catch the region's most generous precipitation, and they stand far above Gauteng's altitude. So dams in the Maluti mountains — KATSE and MOHALE — harvest the water at its source, and tunnels bored through the ranges let it run northward by GRAVITY into the Vaal river system: no pumping, no fuel bill, all the way to the border and beyond. Lesotho converts its one abundant resource into national income — royalties among the largest items in its budget, plus hydro-electricity generated by the falling water — while Gauteng secures its supply. The scheme is still growing: the POLIHALI dam, its newest phase, is under construction to raise the transfer. Contrast the TUGELA-VAAL scheme, which performs the same service against the grain: it PUMPS Thukela water up the escarpment into Sterkfontein Dam, paying in electricity for every metre of lift — the running cost that explains why Sterkfontein is held as a drought reserve rather than spent daily. A third classic, the ORANGE-FISH TUNNEL, sends Gariep Dam water south to irrigate the Fish and Sundays valleys of the Eastern Cape.

Storing means DAMS — the Gariep and Vanderkloof regulating the Orange, the Vaal Dam anchoring Gauteng, hundreds more besides — smoothing rivers that deliver in summer what the country needs year-round, at the familiar costs: evaporation, silting, drowned valleys and starved downstream ecosystems.

Cleaning means MUNICIPAL PURIFICATION, and its sequence is a standard test item, so command the five steps in order. One, SCREENING: coarse grids strip branches, leaves and litter from the raw intake. Two, COAGULATION AND FLOCCULATION: a dosing chemical, commonly aluminium sulphate, is mixed through the water so that fine suspended dirt binds into heavier clumps called flocs. Three, SEDIMENTATION: in calm tanks the flocs settle out, and the clarified upper water moves on. Four, FILTRATION: beds of graded sand and gravel comb out the fine particles that settling missed. Five, DISINFECTION: chlorine — supplemented in some works by ozone or ultraviolet light — destroys remaining pathogens, and the finished water waits in covered reservoirs for the pipe network. Two national audit programmes keep score: Blue Drop for drinking water works, Green Drop for wastewater works — the second mattering just as much, because every failing sewage plant discharges its problem into the river from which a downstream town must drink.

Your questions on this section are ready now: the gravity logic of the Lesotho scheme, its pumped and tunnelled cousins, and the five purification steps in strict order.

## Subtopic: Sustainable Water Use — Government and Individuals

SUSTAINABILITY in water has a precise meaning: meeting the present's needs without draining the future's — never withdrawing faster than the system renews, and never letting what exists become unusable. The topic's standard structure splits the work between two role-players; keep the lists separate and both full.

The state's half. STOP THE BLEEDING: repair municipal pipes, meter honestly, and manage pressure in the network — unglamorous work that recovers more water than any new dam, given that a third or more currently escapes unbilled. DEFEND QUALITY: enforce the National Water Act against polluters, restore failing sewage works, and contain acid mine drainage before it reaches rivers and aquifers. CLEAR THE INVADERS: the Working for Water programme pays crews to fell the alien wattles, pines and gums that line watercourses and drink measurably more than indigenous cover — a rare policy that creates employment and releases water simultaneously. PRICE FOR RESTRAINT: rising block tariffs year-round, escalating restrictions in drought. DIVERSIFY AND PLAN: maintain the great transfer schemes, protect and recharge aquifers, and build the new sources the coasts allow — desalination — and the source every city already owns: treated wastewater reclaimed for reuse. And TEACH: sustained public campaigns that make careful water use a norm rather than an emergency measure.

The citizen's half is a list of small habits with a large sum. Repair the dripping tap and the trickling toilet — each a silent thief of thousands of litres a year. Shower short instead of bathing deep; close the tap while teeth are brushed and dishes soaped. Put a tank under the roof's downpipe and give the garden rainwater instead of drinking water. Send GREYWATER — the lightly used outflow of basins, baths and washing machines — onto beds and fruit trees rather than down the drain. Plant indigenous and water-wise, water at dusk when evaporation sleeps, sweep paving rather than hosing it, report the street leak instead of stepping over it, and obey restrictions as a matter of citizenship.

Two South African cities have already run the experiment. Cape Town, threatened with Day Zero in 2018, roughly halved its daily consumption through exactly these habits and kept its taps alive. Nelson Mandela Bay, hit by its own multi-year drought into 2022, showed the harder version of the lesson: where savings come late and leaks stay unfixed, the emergency stretches out for years. Sustainability, in other words, is not a slogan but a running total — millions of daily decisions, standing on a foundation of pipes the state keeps whole.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same topic again — through two buckets and a kettle, a river sent through a mountain, and two cities that met the same drought differently.

## Subtopic: The First Six Thousand Litres

Start with the number at the heart of the policy and make it physical. Every poor South African household is entitled to 6 000 litres of clean water a month, free. Where does the number come from? From a daily ration: 25 litres per person per day, counted for a household of eight, added up over a month — six kilolitres, the first six thousand litres on the account, priced at nothing.

Now feel 25 litres. It is two ten-litre buckets and a full kettle — for one person, for one whole day: every sip, every meal cooked, every wash. Stand that against an ordinary plumbed suburban life, which runs through about 150 litres per person per day without noticing — one long shower alone can swallow the ration. So do not mistake the policy for generosity: it is a floor. The Constitution, in section 27, says everyone has the right of access to sufficient water; the 6 000 litres is the state translating that sentence into something a municipal account can deliver.

Who pays for the free part? The bill itself does, through a design called the rising block tariff — think of it as a staircase of prices. The first steps of monthly use cost nothing or nearly nothing. Climb higher — fill a pool, soak a lawn — and each further block of litres costs more than the block before, steeply so at the top. A careful household lives on the cheap bottom steps its whole life; a lavish one climbs into prices designed to sting, and the money collected at the top of the staircase funds the free water at the bottom. One price list, doing two jobs: protecting the poor and punishing waste.

Carry one honest distinction into every essay: access is a paper word. A standpipe 200 metres off, shared by a whole street, is access. A tap connected to a pump that failed in autumn is access. The promise is written in policy documents; whether it is kept is written in buckets, queues and repair schedules.

## Subtopic: Moving a River Over a Mountain

Here is Gauteng's awkward secret: the richest concentration of people and industry in the country was built where the gold was, not where the water is — high on a watershed, with no great river within reach. The Vaal does its best, but the Vaal alone is not enough. So South Africa did something audacious: it arranged for a neighbouring country's river to arrive by tunnel.

Lesotho is a kingdom made of mountains, and those mountains do two things Gauteng cannot: they catch the region's heaviest rain and snow, and they stand far higher than Johannesburg. Put those two facts together and the engineering designs itself. Build dams up among the peaks — Katse and Mohale — to catch the water where the sky delivers it. Drill tunnels clean through the mountain ranges. Then simply let go: the water runs downhill the whole way, under nobody's power but gravity's, out of the highlands and into the streams that feed the Vaal. Gauteng gains a secure supply; Lesotho gains one of the biggest earners in its national budget — royalties for the water, plus electricity spun out of its fall — and the scheme keeps growing, with the new Polihali dam being added to enlarge the flow. That is the Lesotho Highlands Water Project: a river exported, downhill all the way.

Now flip the logic to see why gravity matters so much. The Thukela also has water to spare — but it flows at the FOOT of the escarpment, below the plateau that needs it. Its water must therefore be forced UP the mountain wall, pump station by pump station, into Sterkfontein Dam — and every litre lifted costs electricity. That running bill is the whole reason Sterkfontein works as the system's emergency reserve, kept brimming and left alone until drought demands it. Two schemes, one law: water that falls downhill is cheap; water pushed uphill is precious.

Between the dam and your glass stand five gates, and the water must pass every one. Gate one, screening: steel grids stop the sticks, plastic and leaves. Gate two, coagulation: a dosed chemical makes the invisible fine dirt cling together into soft clumps. Gate three, sedimentation: in still tanks the clumps sink, and only the clear water above moves on. Gate four, filtration: a deep bed of sand and gravel combs out whatever still floats. Gate five, disinfection: a measured dose of chlorine ends every microbe the journey missed. Five gates — screen, clump, settle, filter, chlorinate — and the brown dam water arrives at the tap clean.

## Subtopic: The City That Counted Its Days

Early in 2018, Cape Town did something no major city had done: it named the date its taps would be shut. Day Zero — the morning the dams would be judged too low to supply the suburbs, and four million people would queue at guarded collection points for 25 litres each. Three failed rainy seasons had drained Theewaterskloof, the city's great reservoir, to bleached mud with a channel through it. The question the world asked was simple: can a modern city save itself by behaviour alone?

It can. Cape Town roughly halved its daily water use — from above a thousand million litres a day down toward five hundred million — and Day Zero retreated off the calendar until the rains returned. The halving was not one heroic act but thousands of small ones running in parallel: showers timed to two minutes with a bucket catching the spill, and the bucket water flushing the toilet; washing-machine outflow piped onto gardens; rain tanks bolted under gutters across the suburbs; pools covered, lawns surrendered to brown; restaurants serving water only on request; the city publishing dam levels weekly while neighbours compared consumption like league results; and the tariff staircase climbing sharply with every restriction level. The lesson deserves its one-line form: the fastest, cheapest dam a city can build is the water its people stop wasting.

But the experiment has a control case, and honesty requires it. Nelson Mandela Bay met its own long drought into 2022 with savings that came later and leaks that stayed unfixed — and instead of a sharp crisis conquered, the emergency dragged on for years, suburb by suburb, standpipe by standpipe. Same country, same kind of drought, different discipline — and that contrast is the whole sustainability chapter in miniature.

So close the topic with its two-sided summary. The state's side: fix the pipes that lose a third of the water, keep sewage works alive so rivers stay drinkable, tear out the thirsty alien trees and pay wages for it, price water in rising blocks, maintain the dams and transfer schemes, and develop desalination and reuse. The citizen's side: the mended drip, the short shower, the closed tap, the rain tank, the greywater bucket, the indigenous garden, the evening watering, the reported leak. Neither side succeeds alone — careful citizens cannot outsave rotten pipes, and perfect pipes cannot outrun careless habits. A dry country survives on both.
