**Thandi:** Four questions people always ask on this lesson. First: why do we memorise 465 against 860 and the 9 per cent — what do those numbers actually do in an answer?

**Tutor:** They do the work that adjectives cannot. Saying South Africa is dry earns almost nothing, because dry is an opinion until it is measured. The pair 465 against 860 converts the opinion into a comparison — we receive a little more than half of what an average piece of the planet receives — and the 9 per cent converts rainfall into the number that actually matters for supply, which is river flow. Those two figures also chain together beautifully in an evaluation question: start with the modest rainfall, multiply it by the harsh harvest rate, and you have explained in two steps why a country that is not a desert still behaves, hydrologically, like a poor one. Then use them as the measuring stick for everything else: evaporation matters because it is why the 9 per cent is so low; dams matter because they defend the little that survives; the east-west gradient matters because it says even the 465 is not shared fairly. Numbers first, then the argument hangs off them — that is the habit worth building.

***

**Thandi:** Next: learners mix up periodic and episodic rivers constantly. Is there a clean way to keep the three types apart?

**Tutor:** Tie each type to a calendar and a place, and the confusion disappears. A perennial river answers to no calendar — it flows every day of the year, wet season or dry, because between rains the groundwater keeps seeping into its channel; its home is the wet east and south, and the Thukela is the model. A periodic river follows the seasonal calendar — it flows during the rainy season and for a while after, then dries; its home is the middle of the country, where summer storms are real but the dry winter is long. An episodic river follows no calendar at all, only luck — it flows for a few days after a rare heavy storm and can then lie dry for years; its home is the Kalahari and Karoo margins, and the Molopo is the model. Then add the ordering test that questions love: the sequence you meet travelling west is perennial, periodic, episodic — in that strict order — because river type is simply rainfall translated into flow. If you can state the order and say why it cannot be otherwise, you have shown understanding rather than memory.

***

**Thandi:** Third one: the lesson calls Sterkfontein the fixed deposit. Why keep a huge dam full and refuse to use it, when towns elsewhere run short?

**Tutor:** Because Sterkfontein's value lies precisely in not being spent. Consider its design. It sits high in the Free State, filled not by a generous river of its own but by water pumped up the escarpment from the Thukela — and pumping uphill is brutally expensive, so every litre in that reservoir arrived with a price tag. Spend it on ordinary demand and you are buying everyday water at emergency prices. But there is a second, cleverer reason: Sterkfontein is deep, cool for its surface area, and loses proportionally less to evaporation than the shallow, sprawling reservoirs — so of all places in the Vaal system, it is the best vault in which stored water can wait without shrinking. That combination — costly to fill, efficient to hold — is exactly the profile of a savings account rather than a spending account. So the system runs on the Vaal Dam day to day, and Sterkfontein stands behind it as insurance, opened only when drought pulls the Vaal down. It is a good example of how management, not just rainfall, creates availability — the same water, placed cleverly, protects millions more people.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: if a question asks whether South Africa's water problem is physical or human, what is the strongest way to answer?

**Tutor:** Refuse the either-or, and structure the refusal. Open by stating the physical floor: nothing human caused the 465 millimetres, the ferocious evaporation, the east-west tilt or the 9 per cent runoff — those set limits no policy can repeal. Then show the human multiplication: we built our economic heartland on a dry watershed, so the water must be carried to the people rather than the people living by the water; we let irrigation take roughly 60 per cent while fields still flood-irrigate under a thirsty sun; we lose a third of treated municipal water through leaks; we allow sewage failures and acid mine drainage to subtract usable water from the little there is; and we tolerate alien invasive plants drinking the runoff before anyone measures it. Then land the synthesis with a comparison: the physical scarcity is the hand we were dealt, the human factors decide how well the hand is played — and the recent Eastern Cape emergency showed a drought becoming a crisis exactly where maintenance and management had thinned. Physical facts explain why water is scarce; human choices explain why scarce water is sometimes not there at all. Two lists, one verdict — that structure earns full marks.
