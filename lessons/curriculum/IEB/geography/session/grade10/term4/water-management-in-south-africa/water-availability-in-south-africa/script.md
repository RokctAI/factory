# Part 1 — Expert

Take any question South Africa argues about — where its cities can grow, what its farms can plant, why its taps sometimes fail — and underneath it sits one geographical fact: this is one of the thirty driest countries on earth. This session puts numbers to that dryness, walks the map of the country's rivers, explains why its lakes had to be built rather than found, and then assembles the complete set of factors, physical and human, that determine how much usable water South Africa actually commands. Everything the rest of the term says about managing water rests on what is established here.

## Subtopic: A Dry Country by the Numbers

Two numbers open every strong answer on South African water. The first pair: this country averages about 465 millimetres of rain a year, while the planet as a whole averages about 860. We live on a little more than half the world's ration. The second: of the rain that does fall, only about 9 per cent ever runs into a river where it can be captured and used. Hold both, because together they are the national water budget in miniature.

Averages, though, are the beginning of the analysis, never the end — because South Africa's rain is spectacularly uneven, in space and in time. Across space, the rule is a slide from east to west. The eastern escarpment and the KwaZulu-Natal coast catch more than 1 000 millimetres a year; the central plateau makes do with 400 to 600; and west of that the totals collapse — under 200 millimetres across the Karoo and Namaqualand, and under 50 on the desert coast near the Orange River mouth. Roughly two thirds of the country falls below 500 millimetres a year, which is the conventional threshold for growing crops on rain alone. Across time, the rain is seasonal and moody: the interior and east receive summer thunderstorm rain, the south-western Cape receives winter frontal rain, and only a narrow southern coastal ribbon is watered in all seasons. Year on year the totals lurch between drought and deluge, and the further west you stand, the wilder the percentage swings become.

Then comes the subtraction the sun performs. Evaporation over the South African interior is fierce enough that, across most of the country, the atmosphere could lift MORE water off an open surface in a year than the sky delivers — in parts of the Northern Cape the potential loss passes 2 500 millimetres a year, several times the actual rainfall. Every dam, pan and puddle is a target. That is the deeper reason behind the 9 per cent runoff figure: between the thirsty air, the plants, and the dry soils that drink first, barely a tenth of our modest rainfall survives to become river flow — and most of the river flow that can be affordably captured already has been.

Take this section's questions now: the two master numbers, the east-to-west slide, the three rainfall seasons, and what evaporation does to the budget.

## Subtopic: Rivers of South Africa — Perennial and Not

Rivers are the country's water arteries, and the map of them must be known by name, direction and type.

Begin with the giant: the ORANGE, also known by its older name, the Gariep. It is born in the Lesotho highlands — the wettest ground in the region, where rain and snow feed the headwaters — and then travels roughly 2 200 kilometres westward across the full width of South Africa to enter the Atlantic at Alexander Bay, marking the border with Namibia along its lower reaches. Pause on the paradox: for its final hundreds of kilometres the Orange flows through country that is nearly desert. The land around it could never have generated such a river; what passes Alexander Bay is essentially Lesotho's mountain water in transit. Its great partner is the VAAL, which drains the industrial plateau and carries Gauteng on its back — the hardest-working river in the country, endlessly reinforced because the province it serves has no great river of its own.

The rest of the rivers sort themselves by coastline. Flowing east to the warm Indian Ocean: the THUKELA, dropping off the Drakensberg through KwaZulu-Natal with flow so valuable that some of it is pumped up the escarpment into the Vaal system; the PHONGOLO and KOMATI in the north-east; and the LIMPOPO tracing the northern border toward Mozambique. Along the southern coast run the GOURITZ and the BREEDE; in the winter-rainfall south-west, the BERG and the Western Cape's OLIFANTS. Notice the pattern the escarpment writes: the eastward rivers are short and steep because the high ground stands close to that coast, yet they carry the country's best flows because they drain its wettest slopes.

Now the classification the topic turns on. A PERENNIAL river flows every day of the year, sustained between rains by groundwater; South Africa's perennial rivers belong to the well-watered east and south. A NON-PERENNIAL or periodic river flows only during and shortly after the rainy season. And an EPISODIC river — the far end of the scale — runs only after occasional heavy storms: the Molopo and Auob of the Kalahari margins can stand dry for years and then flow for a few loud days. Travel west across the country and the river types change in exactly the order the rainfall map dictates — perennial, then periodic, then episodic, then dry sand.

Two working habits of our rivers complete the picture. They run brown: eroding soils load them with silt, which settles behind every dam wall and slowly eats the storage. And even the perennial ones breathe with the seasons, high in the rains and low in the dry — which is why South Africa cannot simply use its rivers as they flow, but must store them.

Pause for this section's questions: the Orange traced source to mouth, the Vaal's burden, the coastal groups, and the three river types laid over the rainfall gradient.

## Subtopic: Lakes, Dams and Groundwater

Ask the class to name five natural lakes in South Africa and watch the silence: the country has almost none. The exceptions are a short and famous list — Lake Fundudzi in the Soutpansberg, ponded behind an ancient landslide; the clustered pans around Chrissiesmeer in Mpumalanga; the coastal lakes of northern KwaZulu-Natal such as Sibaya. The scarcity has a logic: natural lakes generally require either glaciers, which our warm history never supplied, or rivers with water to spare, which our climate never permitted. The same dryness that denied us lakes made storing water a matter of survival.

So the state built what nature withheld: more than 500 large DAMS, holding around two thirds of the country's entire average annual river flow — one of the most engineered river systems on earth. Learn the leaders and their jobs. On the Orange stands the GARIEP, the country's largest reservoir, with VANDERKLOOF just downstream; the pair tames the river for irrigation and generates hydro-electric power. The VAAL DAM is Gauteng's storage tank. STERKFONTEIN, high in the Free State, is the strategic reserve — deliberately kept full with water pumped up from the Thukela, and drawn on only when drought threatens the Vaal system. THEEWATERSKLOOF carries Cape Town, and its shrinking to cracked mud in the 2017 to 2018 drought taught the world the phrase Day Zero. In KwaZulu-Natal, MIDMAR on the Mgeni waters the cities of that province, and JOZINI on the Phongolo irrigates the hot north-east. Every one of these assets pays the two South African taxes: the sun lifts a deep layer off each dam's surface every year, and the rivers' silt settles below, so capacity shrinks from both directions.

The third source lies out of sight. GROUNDWATER is rain that soaked downward into AQUIFERS — bodies of porous rock, gravel or sand that store water and yield it to boreholes and springs. It is the quiet hero of the dry two thirds of the country: Karoo and Kalahari towns, and thousands of farms, live on boreholes and windpumps, and the dolomite formations of Gauteng and North West hold some of the strongest aquifers in the land. Groundwater's advantages are exactly what surface water lacks — it hides from evaporation, and it waits underneath places that have no rivers. Its vulnerabilities are invisible until too late: draw an aquifer down faster than rain recharges it and the water table sinks a little further every year; let pollution seep in — the acid water draining from derelict mines is the notorious case — and the contamination is close to permanent.

Your questions on this section are ready now: why the lakes had to be built, the major dams matched to their jobs, and groundwater's advantages against its vulnerabilities.

## Subtopic: The Factors Behind Water Availability

Now assemble the section the whole topic has been building toward: the factors that determine water availability in South Africa, organised — as strong answers organise them — into physical and human.

The physical set. RAINFALL AMOUNT fixes the ceiling: 465 millimetres against the world's 860. RAINFALL DISTRIBUTION decides who stands under which part of the ceiling: generous in the east, meagre in the west. SEASONALITY AND VARIABILITY mean the water arrives concentrated in one season and unreliably between years, so availability depends on the ability to store, not merely on what falls. EVAPORATION, exceeding rainfall over most of the interior, taxes every open surface continuously. RELIEF AND GEOLOGY shape what can be kept: the escarpment builds short, strong eastern rivers and offers good dam sites, while the underground geology grants some regions generous aquifers and others none. And the RUNOFF RATIO — that stubborn 9 per cent — compresses everything above it: modest rain becomes scarce river.

The human set, which grows more decisive every decade. THE MISMATCH OF PEOPLE AND WATER: the country's economic core, Gauteng, sits deliberately on a high watershed — placed by gold, not by geography's permission — so its water must be imported across provinces and an international border. RISING DEMAND: population growth, urbanisation and industry push consumption upward relentlessly, and irrigation agriculture alone takes roughly 60 per cent of the national total. POLLUTION subtracts usable water as effectively as drought adds scarcity: failing sewage works, industrial discharge, fertiliser washing into dams and feeding algal blooms, and acid mine drainage seeping from the old gold workings. ALIEN INVASIVE PLANTS — thirsty stands of wattle, pine and gum crowding along watercourses — transpire far more than the indigenous vegetation they displaced, intercepting a meaningful slice of runoff before any person uses it. LEAKING SYSTEMS: in many municipalities a third or more of treated water never earns revenue, lost through burst pipes, failed meters and unbilled use. And MANAGEMENT itself belongs on the list, because in a dry country water availability is manufactured — by storage, transfers, treatment, maintenance and law — and collapses where those fail: the long Eastern Cape drought that pushed Nelson Mandela Bay's supply dams to critical lows by 2022 was a rainfall failure compounded by every human factor above.

Carry the one-sentence summary: South Africa's water problem is a half-ration of rain, harvested at 9 per cent, taxed by the sun, delivered to the wrong side of the country, and further reduced by pollution, invasive plants and leaks. Every scheme in the next lesson answers an item on that list.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the physical factors, the human factors, and the national problem stated in one sentence.

The same country again — through the tank behind the house, a long drive west, and the lakes we had to build ourselves.

## Subtopic: The Tank Behind the House

Think of the whole country's water supply as one big rain tank standing behind a house, fed by a gutter off the roof. Four honest facts about that tank explain the entire topic.

Fact one: the gutter runs weak. South Africa's roof catches about 465 millimetres of rain a year while the world's average roof catches about 860 — so before anything else happens, our tank fills at barely half the world's rate.

Fact two: the sun works the tank all day. Over most of this country, the sky can lift more water in a year than it delivers — in the Northern Cape it could take back several times the annual rain if the water stood in the open. Leave the lid off the tank and the sun drinks with you.

Fact three: most of the roof's water never reaches the gutter at all. Of every 100 litres of rain that lands on South Africa, only about 9 make it into a river where they can be caught. The rest evaporates on the way, gets drunk by plants, or sinks beyond reach. A weak gutter delivering 9 litres in every 100 — that is the entire national income of water.

Fact four: the gutter hangs on one side of the roof. Stand in the east — the Drakensberg slopes, the KwaZulu-Natal coast — and the rain comes down generously, over 1 000 millimetres a year. Now travel west and watch the sky close: 600, then 400, then 200, until near the Atlantic coast a year brings less than 50 millimetres — a single decent storm's worth. Two thirds of the country gets less than the 500 millimetres that rain-fed crops demand. So the east grows maize under open sky while the west runs sheep between windpumps — and much of the country's population and industry stands, inconveniently, far from where the gutter drips.

That is why water is never off South Africa's agenda: a weak gutter, a hungry sun, a 9 per cent delivery, and the rain falling far from many of the people.

## Subtopic: A Road Trip from Wet to Dry

Load the car in Durban and drive west, all the way to the Atlantic, and the rivers you cross will teach you this whole section in one trip.

The first crossings are the country's strongest rivers. Near the start you cross the Thukela, rolling off the Drakensberg full and brown — short, steep and powerful, because the escarpment stands close to this wet coast, and so valuable that engineers pump part of it up the mountain wall for Gauteng's benefit. Rivers like this are PERENNIAL: they flow every single day of the year, because between rains the saturated ground keeps seeping into them.

Climb the escarpment onto the plateau and the rivers change temperament. Here you cross the Vaal — a working river, drinking-trough of Gauteng, the one river that must carry the country's biggest cluster of people and factories despite the plateau's modest rain. Push further west and the streams beside the road start keeping seasonal hours: full in summer after the thunderstorms, sand and stones by winter. These are PERIODIC rivers — they flow in and after the rainy season, then stop.

Keep driving into the Karoo and the Kalahari edge, and now the riverbeds are just names on signboards — the Molopo, the Auob — dry crossings of pale sand that might not see water this year or next. These are EPISODIC rivers: they run only after rare heavy storms, loudly and briefly, then vanish again for years. You have driven the rainfall map: perennial in the east, periodic in the middle, episodic in the west — the rivers change type in exactly the order the rain thins out.

And then, near the end of the trip, the great exception. In the middle of near-desert you cross a wide, permanent, green-edged river: the Orange, the Gariep. It breaks the pattern because it obeys a different rainfall map — it was filled far away in the mountains of Lesotho, and it spends the journey west delivering that mountain water across country that could never have made a river of its own, all the way to Alexander Bay. One last observation before the coast: every river you crossed ran brown with silt, and even the permanent ones rose and fell with the seasons — which is why the next section is about storage, because a country whose rivers keep such irregular hours has to build the savings accounts itself.

Questions on the road trip are up now: the three river types in driving order, the rule that links them to rainfall, and the exception that carries Lesotho's water through the desert.

## Subtopic: The Country That Built Its Own Lakes

Here is a party trick for geography learners: ask anyone to name three natural lakes in South Africa. Almost nobody can — because almost none exist. The short list is the whole list: Fundudzi, held back by an ancient landslide in the Soutpansberg; the pans around Chrissiesmeer; Sibaya and its coastal neighbours in northern KwaZulu-Natal. Lakes need spare water, and this country has never once had spare water.

So we manufactured our lakes — over 500 large dams, storing about two thirds of everything our rivers carry in an average year. Meet the family and their jobs. The Gariep, on the Orange, is the biggest — it and Vanderkloof below it turn Lesotho's exported rain into irrigation and electricity. The Vaal Dam is Gauteng's tank, the reservoir a quarter of the economy drinks from. Sterkfontein, up in the Free State, is the family's fixed deposit: filled expensively with water pumped up from the Thukela, and left untouched until drought comes knocking. Theewaterskloof is Cape Town's dam — the one whose cracked, empty floor in 2018 put Day Zero into the world's vocabulary. Midmar keeps KwaZulu-Natal's cities running, and Jozini opens the Phongolo's water onto the hot northern irrigation lands.

But every South African dam pays two taxes it cannot dodge. The sun-tax: a deep layer off the surface of every reservoir, every year, lifted straight back into the sky. And the mud-tax: our eroding soils ride the rivers downstream and settle behind every wall, so each dam is slowly, silently filling with silt from the bottom while the sun empties it from the top.

Which is why the dry two thirds of the country banks underground instead. Groundwater — rain that soaked down into aquifers, sponges of porous rock — is invisible savings: the sun cannot touch it, and it waits underneath the very districts that have no rivers. Drive the Karoo and count windpumps: each one is a straw into the underground account, and whole towns live off it. Two rules keep the account solvent. Withdraw no faster than the rain deposits, or the water table drops year after year until the boreholes gasp. And guard the account from poison: pollution that seeps underground — acid water leaking out of old mine workings is the infamous example — can close an aquifer for generations.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — why our lakes are built, the dams and their jobs, the two taxes every dam pays, and the two rules of the underground account.
