Hey, it's Thandi — this lesson looks like a pile of policies and pipelines, but it folds down to one number, one law of gravity, five gates, and two lists. Let me pack it for you.

The number: 6 000 litres of free water per poor household per month. Where from? Twenty-five litres per person per day — two buckets and a kettle — times eight people, times a month. Backed by section 27 of the Constitution and paid for by the rising block tariff: a staircase where the bottom steps are free and the top steps sting, so heavy users fund the basic allowance. And remember the honesty line: access on paper is not water in the hand — pumps break, and rural distance plus urban leaks are why delivery is hard.

The law of gravity: Lesotho's mountains catch the region's best rain and stand higher than Johannesburg, so Katse and Mohale catch the water and tunnels let it run downhill into the Vaal — no pumping, with Lesotho earning royalties and power. The Tugela-Vaal scheme does it the hard way, pumping up the escarpment into Sterkfontein, which is why Sterkfontein is saved for droughts. Downhill is cheap; uphill is precious. That contrast answers almost any transfer question.

The five gates, in order: screen, coagulate, settle, filter, chlorinate. Say them in your sleep.

The two lists: the state fixes pipes, protects rivers, clears alien plants, prices in blocks, builds and diversifies; the citizen mends drips, showers short, tanks the rain, reuses greywater, reports leaks. Proof it works: Cape Town halved its use and beat Day Zero in 2018 — while Nelson Mandela Bay showed what happens when the savings start late.

One number, one law of gravity, five gates, two lists. That is the whole lesson — now go collect your marks from the practice questions. You've got this.
