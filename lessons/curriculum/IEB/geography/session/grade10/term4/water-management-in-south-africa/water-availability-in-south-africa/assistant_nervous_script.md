Hey, it's Thandi — if this lesson feels like a flood of numbers and river names, take a breath: the whole thing folds down to two numbers, one road trip, and two bank accounts.

The two numbers: South Africa gets about 465 millimetres of rain a year while the world averages 860 — half a ration — and only about 9 per cent of what falls ever becomes river flow, because the sun and the soil take the rest first. Say those two and you have the national water problem in one breath.

The road trip: start in Durban and drive west. First the strong eastern rivers that flow every day — perennial, like the Thukela. Then the plateau streams that keep seasonal hours — periodic. Then the Kalahari beds that lie dry for years — episodic. The rivers change type in exactly the order the rain thins out. And one river breaks the rule on purpose: the Orange, carrying Lesotho's mountain water clean across the desert to Alexander Bay.

The two bank accounts: above ground, we built our lakes because nature gave us almost none — Gariep the biggest, Vaal Dam as Gauteng's tank, Sterkfontein the fixed deposit kept for drought, Theewaterskloof the Day Zero warning — but every dam pays the sun-tax off the top and the silt-tax at the bottom. Below ground, aquifers are the hidden savings that keep the Karoo alive through boreholes and windpumps: withdraw no faster than the rain deposits, and never let poison seep in.

Factors question? Split the page: physical — amount, distribution, seasonality, evaporation, relief, the 9 per cent; human — Gauteng in the wrong place, irrigation's 60 per cent, pollution, alien plants, leaks.

Two numbers, one road trip, two accounts. You can carry that into any question they set. Go do the practice questions and prove it. You've got this.
