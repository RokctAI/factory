**Thandi:** Revision questions, rapid fire. First: the layers of the atmosphere — is there a trick for remembering which way temperature goes in each one?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is better than a rhyme because it is the actual physics: ask where each layer's heater is. The troposphere is heated by the ground beneath it, so moving up means moving away from the heater — temperature falls, at about 6.5 degrees per kilometre. The stratosphere carries its own heater in the middle of itself: the ozone layer, absorbing ultraviolet — so climbing toward the ozone means warming. The mesosphere has no heater at all — no ground below it that matters, no ozone of its own — so cooling resumes and it holds the atmosphere's coldest point. And the thermosphere sits in raw sunlight so thin that each molecule absorbs ferocious energy — temperature soars. Falls, rises, falls, rises — but derived, not memorised. Do the same with the two protective jobs: the greenhouse blanket works low, intercepting the longwave heat the GROUND sends up; the ozone sunscreen works high, intercepting the ultraviolet the SUN sends down. If you can name each layer's heater and each gas's direction of interception, every structure question unravels on its own.

**Thandi:** Next: learners can define all three rainfall types but freeze when a question asks which type explains this place's rain. What is the method?

**Tutor:** Interrogate the place with three questions in a fixed order — what season, what surface, what wind. Question one, the season of the rain: winter rain in the south-western corner of the country is frontal almost by definition, because that is when the westerly cold fronts swing far enough north to strike the Cape; summer rain over the interior plateau is convectional by default, because that is when the ground bakes hard enough to launch afternoon thunderstorms. Question two, the surface: if the place sits at the foot of a mountain barrier facing the sea — George under the Outeniquas, the Drakensberg's seaward face — think orographic, and immediately check the far side, because a rain shadow behind the barrier clinches the diagnosis; a question that pairs a wet town and a dry town across one range is an orographic question, always. Question three, the wind: ask where the moist air is coming from and what forces it upward — heat from below, a mountain in the way, or a cold wedge underneath. And write the mechanism, not just the label: state that air was forced to rise, cooled to dew point, condensed and rained. The label earns one mark; the rising-cooling-condensing chain earns the rest.

**Thandi:** Third: folds against faults. Both come from the same squeezing, so how do we keep the landforms and terms from blurring together?

**Tutor:** Anchor everything to one variable: how the rock answered stress — by bending or by breaking. If it bent, you are in fold country and only four terms exist: the up-arch is the anticline, the down-sag is the syncline, stacked bends make fold mountains, and South Africa's specimen is the Cape Fold ranges, crumpled about 300 million years ago. If it broke, you are in fault country with its own four: tension pulling blocks apart gives a normal fault; compression driving one block over another gives a reverse fault; a block dropped between two parallel faults is a graben, the rift valley, with East Africa as the living example; and the proud block left standing between dropped neighbours is a horst, the block mountain. The blur disappears once you sort the eight terms into their two families first and never mix the families in one sentence. Then, for earthquakes, remember that breaking is also the trigger: strain stored along a locked fault releases at the focus underground, the epicentre is the surface address above it, and the two measurements answer different questions — magnitude asks how much energy the snap released, one number; Mercalli asks what people and buildings felt, town by town. Bend or break: one decision, and the whole section files itself.

**Thandi:** Last one: in mapwork, where do most gradient calculations actually lose their marks?

**Tutor:** Almost never in the division — in the two conversions before it and the dress code after it. Walk the routine and flag the traps. Trap one, the heights: read both spot heights or contour values correctly and subtract — 1 220 minus 1 060 is 160 metres; the error here is misreading a contour value by one interval, so check against the 20-metre interval that the number is even possible. Trap two, the distance: what you measure is centimetres on paper, and the gradient needs metres on the ground — on 1:50 000, multiply your centimetres by half to get kilometres, so 4.8 centimetres is 2.4 kilometres, then convert to 2 400 metres. Mixing metres of height with kilometres of distance is the classic wreck: both must be in metres before you divide. Then the division, distance by height: 2 400 over 160 is 15. And finally the dress code — the answer is not 15, not 0.067, but 1 in 15, stated in exactly that form, ideally with an interpretation attached: a climb of one metre for every fifteen forward, hard work on a bicycle. Heights checked, units matched, answer dressed — do those three and the gradient question becomes the most reliable marks you will earn in your final exams.
