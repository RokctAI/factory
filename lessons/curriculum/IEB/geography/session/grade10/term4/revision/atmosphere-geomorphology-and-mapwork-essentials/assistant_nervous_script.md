Hey, it's Thandi — a whole year of physical geography in one revision session sounds heavy, but look closer: it is three stories, and you already know all three.

Story one, the air. The sun heats the ground and the ground heats the air — that is why peaks are cold and why storms build in the afternoon. Warm air carries invisible water; cool it to its dew point and the water reappears — on a cold cooldrink can, on dawn grass, or up high as cloud. And rain only ever needs one thing: rising air. Three lifts, three rain types — hot floor for the interior's thunderstorms, mountain wall for George's green side and Oudtshoorn's dry side, cold wedge for Cape Town's winter. Keep the blanket and the sunscreen apart: greenhouse gases keep heat in; ozone keeps ultraviolet out.

Story two, the ground. The crust is cracked into plates riding slow currents below, and plates can only pull apart, push together or grind past — rifts, mountains and volcanoes, earthquakes. Rock squeezed slowly folds — anticline up, syncline down, Cape Fold ranges. Rock forced too fast snaps — faults, with the dropped graben and the standing horst. Quakes: focus below, epicentre above, magnitude for energy, Mercalli for effects.

Story three, the map. Contours are rings of equal height — crowded means steep, the V points upstream. Gradient is one routine: height difference, distance in the same units, divide, say it as 1 in something — 160 metres over 2 400 gives 1 in 15. And the weather chart is a scorecard per town: cloud in eighths, wind shaft from the wind's origin, temperature and dew point — close together means rain is near.

Three stories: air rises, plates move, maps talk. Walk in with those and pick up your marks one calm question at a time. You've got this.
