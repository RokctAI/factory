# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session walks the physical geography of the Grade 10 year in one continuous line: the atmosphere — what it is made of, how it is layered, how it is heated and how it makes rain; the restless crust — moving plates, folded and faulted rock, earthquakes and volcanoes; and the mapwork toolkit that ties the year together — scale, contours, gradient and the synoptic weather chart, all described in words precise enough to rebuild every diagram from memory. Treat each definition here as one to own exactly, and each South African example as one ready to deploy in your final exams.

## Subtopic: The Atmosphere — Composition, Structure and Heating

Composition first, with the percentages attached. The ATMOSPHERE is the envelope of gases gravity holds against the planet: NITROGEN about 78 per cent, OXYGEN about 21 per cent, argon just under 1 per cent — and then the trace gases whose importance dwarfs their quantity. CARBON DIOXIDE, around 0.04 per cent, and WATER VAPOUR, ranging from nearly zero over the winter Karoo to about 4 per cent over a steamy coastline, both trap the earth's outgoing heat; OZONE, rarer still, absorbs incoming ultraviolet radiation before it reaches skin and crops.

Structure next: four layers, each defined by what temperature does with height. The TROPOSPHERE hugs the surface and contains virtually all weather, moisture and dust; within it temperature FALLS as you climb, at the average lapse rate of about 6.5 degrees Celsius per kilometre, until the TROPOPAUSE calls the boundary. Above it, the STRATOSPHERE reverses the trend — temperature RISES with height, because the OZONE LAYER lives here, absorbing ultraviolet energy and warming its surroundings; this is the planet's sunscreen, the layer the world moved to protect when CFCs were banned. Higher again, the MESOSPHERE resumes the cooling and holds the atmosphere's coldest point — it is where meteors burn. Finally the THERMOSPHERE, where the thinnest air absorbs raw solar energy and temperatures soar.

Heating is governed by one counter-intuitive sentence: the air is warmed from BELOW, by the ground, not from above by direct sunshine. Incoming solar energy — INSOLATION — travels as shortwave radiation, and roughly half survives clouds, scattering and reflection to reach the surface. The heated surface then broadcasts LONGWAVE terrestrial radiation back upward, and it is this outgoing longwave energy that carbon dioxide and water vapour intercept. Because the heater is at the bottom, temperature falls with altitude — the reason Drakensberg resorts sit under frost on mornings when Durban swims. Four processes distribute the heat: RADIATION, energy moving as waves; CONDUCTION, heat passed by direct contact into the air touching the ground; CONVECTION, warmed air rising in vertical currents; ADVECTION, heat shifted horizontally by wind. The GREENHOUSE EFFECT itself is natural and life-giving — an unblanketed earth would average around minus 18 degrees — but the ENHANCED greenhouse effect, thickened by fossil-fuel carbon dioxide and methane, drives the warming problem. Close the section with the local temperature factors: latitude; altitude; distance from the sea; ocean currents — the warm Agulhas hugging the east coast against the cold Benguela chilling the west; and aspect, the direction a slope faces.

Take this section's questions now: the gases with their percentages, the four layers by temperature behaviour, heating from below, and the four transfer processes.

## Subtopic: Moisture, Clouds and the Three Rainfall Types

Rain is the end of a chain, and revision means owning every link. Invisible WATER VAPOUR enters the air by EVAPORATION from sea, dam and soil and by TRANSPIRATION from plants. HUMIDITY measures the load: absolute humidity is the actual mass of vapour per cubic metre, while RELATIVE HUMIDITY — the one tested — expresses the vapour present as a percentage of the maximum that air at that temperature could carry. The governing fact: warm air carries more vapour than cold. Cool a parcel of air and its relative humidity climbs with no water added, until at the DEW POINT the parcel is saturated at 100 per cent. Cool it further and CONDENSATION begins — vapour becoming liquid droplets on microscopic CONDENSATION NUCLEI of dust, salt and smoke. At ground level condensation writes itself as dew, frost, mist and fog; aloft, it writes CLOUD.

The cloud families, named by form and height. CIRRUS: high, icy, feather-thin streaks — fine weather today, often a change tomorrow. CUMULUS: heaped white towers built by rising convection currents; let one keep growing into the anvil-headed CUMULONIMBUS and it delivers thunder, hail and violent rain. STRATUS: featureless grey sheets that blanket the sky and drizzle. For cloud to become rain, droplets must collide and merge until too heavy to float — but the essential physics is one requirement: significant rain demands RISING AIR, because rising air cools, and cooling squeezes out the water. Air can be forced to rise in exactly three ways, and those three ways are the three rainfall types.

CONVECTIONAL RAIN: intense surface heating drives air upward in convection currents that cool, condense and tower — the Free State summer afternoon, where a clear Bloemfontein morning manufactures a bruised, hail-bearing storm by four o'clock, is the type specimen. OROGRAPHIC or RELIEF RAIN: moving moist air strikes a mountain barrier and is forced up the WINDWARD slope, cooling and raining as it climbs; crossing the crest it descends, warms and dries, leaving the LEEWARD side in a RAIN SHADOW — sea air off the Indian Ocean soaks the forests above George on the Outeniqua front, while Oudtshoorn's semi-desert ostrich country waits in the shadow directly behind. FRONTAL or CYCLONIC RAIN: two unlike air masses meet and the warm one is levered up over the cold along a FRONT — the mechanism that steers winter cold fronts from the Southern Ocean onto the Western Cape and gives Cape Town its wet winters, while the rest of the country receives its rain from summer convection. That seasonal division of labour — winter fronts in the south-west, summer thunderstorms over the interior — settles a remarkable number of questions.

Pause here for this section's questions: relative humidity and dew point, the three cloud families, and each rainfall type matched to its South African address.

## Subtopic: The Restless Crust — Plates, Folds, Faults and Quakes

The solid earth, from the inside out. At the centre, the CORE: a solid iron-nickel inner sphere inside a liquid outer shell. Around it the MANTLE, hot rock that creeps like slow toffee over geological time. On top, the thin cool CRUST in two varieties — OCEANIC crust, thinner, denser, basaltic; CONTINENTAL crust, thicker, lighter, granitic. The crust plus the rigid uppermost mantle forms the LITHOSPHERE, broken into PLATES that ride on the softer ASTHENOSPHERE beneath. That the plates move was argued first from evidence any reader can inspect: the jigsaw fit of South America against Africa; identical fossils — Mesosaurus, the fern Glossopteris — stranded on both sides of an ocean; rock layers and coal seams that match across the Atlantic; and glacial scratches in lands now tropical. Alfred Wegener bundled the evidence into CONTINENTAL DRIFT; the engine he could not supply was found later in the mantle's CONVECTION CURRENTS — hot rock rising, spreading, cooling, sinking, rafting the plates along.

Plates interact in three ways, and each interaction manufactures scenery. At DIVERGENT boundaries plates separate; magma wells into the gap, building mid-ocean ridges beneath the sea and rift valleys on land. At CONVERGENT boundaries plates collide: where ocean meets continent the denser oceanic slab SUBDUCTS, diving beneath and melting to feed volcanic chains like the Andes; where ocean meets ocean, subduction stacks up volcanic island arcs like Japan; where continent meets continent nothing can sink, so the crust buckles skyward into fold mountains — the Himalayas. At TRANSFORM boundaries plates rasp past one another, building nothing but banking strain that releases as earthquakes — the San Andreas Fault is the household name. When compression BENDS rock layers slowly, they fold: the arch-up is an ANTICLINE, the sag-down a SYNCLINE, and stacked folds make FOLD MOUNTAINS — South Africa's Cape Fold ranges were crumpled about 300 million years ago, ancient before the Andes were born. When stress SNAPS rock instead, the break is a FAULT: tension pulls blocks apart along a NORMAL fault; compression shoves one block over its neighbour along a REVERSE fault. Let a block drop between two parallel faults and you have a GRABEN — the East African Rift Valley is a continent slowly unzipping — while the high block left standing between dropped neighbours is a HORST, a block mountain.

Earthquakes and volcanoes finish the inventory. An EARTHQUAKE is stored strain released in seconds; the underground point of rupture is the FOCUS, and the surface point directly above it, where shaking usually peaks, is the EPICENTRE. Keep the two measuring systems apart: MAGNITUDE scales measure the energy released — instrument-read, one number per event; the MERCALLI scale measures INTENSITY — observed effects and damage, varying from place to place. And remember that casualties track preparedness more than magnitude: building codes, drills and rescue capacity decide whether equal quakes kill unequally. A VOLCANO is a vent where magma, gas and ash escape: runny low-silica lava spreads into broad, gently-sloped SHIELD volcanoes like Hawaii's; sticky, gas-charged lava alternating with ash builds the steep, explosive COMPOSITE cones of the subduction zones. South Africa has no live volcano — though the Pilanesberg's ring of hills is the eroded plumbing of an extinct one — and volcanic districts stay populated worldwide because ash weathers into fertile soil and the heat below can be tapped for geothermal power.

The questions for this section are ready: earth's layers, Wegener's evidence, the three boundary types with their landforms, folds against faults, horst against graben, and the two earthquake measures.

## Subtopic: Mapwork Essentials — Scale, Contours, Gradient and the Synoptic Chart

Mapwork carries guaranteed marks for the prepared, so drill the toolkit until it runs without thought. The working document is the 1:50 000 TOPOGRAPHIC MAP: one unit of map length equals 50 000 on the ground, which converts to the workhorse fact that 2 centimetres on the map is 1 kilometre in the world. Scale wears three outfits — a word statement, the ratio, and the line scale you can measure against directly. Position is written in degrees, minutes and seconds of latitude and longitude — south and east in this country. Direction introduces the standard trap: maps are drawn on TRUE NORTH but compasses obey MAGNETIC NORTH, and the angle between them, the MAGNETIC DECLINATION, is printed on each sheet with its annual change; a bearing question expects the true bearing first, then the declination — updated to the current year — applied to produce the magnetic bearing.

Now relief, the map's third dimension. A CONTOUR LINE connects points of identical height above sea level; on the 1:50 000 sheet the CONTOUR INTERVAL is 20 metres. Three laws govern them: contours never cross, never fork, and their spacing is a slope-meter — crowded lines mean STEEP ground, generous spacing means gentle ground. From the laws, read the landforms: closed rings shrinking inward mark a summit; a broad flat crown ringed by a tight stack of contours is the flat-topped hill of the Karoo; where contours cross a river valley they bend into a V whose point aims UPSTREAM into the high ground; the same V pointing down the slope marks a spur; evenly spaced contours draw a uniform slope, crowding at the top signals convex, crowding at the bottom signals concave; and a dip of low ground between two summits reads as a saddle or neck.

Two calculations return every year, so rehearse them as procedures. GRADIENT is vertical rise compared with horizontal distance, in matching units. Worked cleanly: two points read 1 220 metres and 1 060 metres, so the rise is 160 metres; their map separation of 4.8 centimetres converts at 2 centimetres per kilometre to 2.4 kilometres, which is 2 400 metres; divide 2 400 by 160 and the answer is 15 — a gradient of 1 in 15, meaning one metre climbed for every 15 travelled. Always present it as 1 in something and add the interpretation: 1 in 15 taxes a cyclist, 1 in 150 is nearly level. The CROSS-SECTION converts a ruler line into a profile: log every contour the line crosses, plot height against distance, then narrate the shape — rising from the river terrace, steepening where the contours bunch, flattening over the crest — and judge whether the proposed road, pipeline or dam wall belongs there. Finally the SYNOPTIC CHART, the mapwork of the sky. Each reporting station wears a STATION MODEL: a circle shaded in eighths for cloud cover; a wind shaft drawn from the direction the wind comes, its feathers counting speed in knots; air temperature at upper left, dew-point temperature at lower left — and when those two numbers converge, saturation and rain stand near. Isobars — lines of equal pressure in hectopascals — ring the highs and lows around the stations, and the saw-toothed line of a COLD FRONT approaching the Cape tells its two-act story: ahead of it, warmth, northerly airflow, sliding pressure; behind it, a sharp chill, wind swung to the south-west, climbing pressure and clearing showers.

The last expert questions are with you now: scale conversions and declination, landforms named from contour descriptions, the 1 in 15 gradient worked through, and the station model decoded element by element.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole year once more — with a tar road, a cooldrink can, a dry mud pan and a dam in a drought.

## Subtopic: The Tar Road at Noon — Air, Heat and Rain Made Simple

Stand barefoot next to a tar road at midday in January and you learn the atmosphere's biggest secret through your feet: the heat is coming off the GROUND. Look along the road and see the air shimmer and wobble upward. The sun's rays mostly pass through air without warming it; they warm surfaces — tar, soil, rooftops — and the surfaces then warm the air lying on them. Heated from below: that one phrase explains why mountain peaks are icy though they stand closer to the sun — they stand further from the heater; why the hottest hour is mid-afternoon and not noon — the ground needs time to bank heat and pass it up; and why air rises in wobbling columns over hot ground — the convection you can see above the tar.

Now for moisture, fetch a cold cooldrink can from the fridge and set it in the sun. Within a minute the outside is wet. No water leaked; the air did it. Air always carries invisible vapour, and warm air carries plenty — but chill a layer of air against that icy metal and it can no longer hold its load. The temperature where the air is full up is the DEW POINT, and cooling past it forces the vapour to condense into droplets — on the can, on cold dawn grass as dew, and high in the sky as cloud. So the recipe for rain is short: lift moist air, because lifted air cools, and cooled air lets go of its water.

And there are only three lifts. Lift one, the hot-floor lift: the Free State bakes all morning, the heated air rockets upward after lunch, and by four o'clock Bloemfontein is under a purple thunderstorm — convectional rain. Lift two, the wall lift: moist sea air runs into the Outeniqua mountains and must climb; it rains on George's green side going up, then rolls down the far slope dry — orographic rain, with Oudtshoorn parked in the rain shadow. Lift three, the wedge lift: a winter cold front slides in from the Southern Ocean like a cold chisel under the Cape's warm air, prising it upward — frontal rain, Cape Town's soaked July. Three lifts, three rainfall types, three postcards.

One last pairing to keep straight: the atmosphere owns a blanket and a sunscreen, and they are different layers doing different jobs. The blanket is greenhouse gas — carbon dioxide and water vapour — catching the heat the ground radiates OUT at night; natural and necessary, but thickening as we burn coal. The sunscreen is the ozone layer, far up in the stratosphere, blocking ultraviolet on the way IN. Blanket stops heat leaving; sunscreen stops burn arriving. Never swap them.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: the tar road's lesson, the sweating can, the three lifts, and blanket against sunscreen.

## Subtopic: The Dry Mud Pan — Plates, Folds and Faults Made Simple

Find a dried-out pan or puddle after a Karoo summer: the mud has hardened and cracked into plates — stiff polygons resting on softer, damper mud beneath. That is the earth in miniature. The crust is broken into rigid plates, and beneath them the mantle's hot rock creeps in slow rolling currents — rising, spreading sideways, sinking — carrying the plates on its back the way a conveyor carries luggage.

Neighbouring plates can only do three things, and each move builds its own furniture. Pull APART, and molten rock rises to plug the widening gap — undersea, that seam is a mid-ocean ridge; on land it is a rift valley, and East Africa's is a continent slowly tearing open. Push TOGETHER, and something must give: if one plate is ocean floor, it loses, diving under and melting into a line of volcanoes; if both edges are continents, neither can dive, so the ground concertinas upward — that crumpled concertina is the Himalayas. Slide PAST, and nothing is built at all — but friction locks the edges, strain accumulates for decades, and when the lock slips the ground jolts: an earthquake.

Shrink the two rock behaviours to desk scale. Push a rug slowly across the floor and it humps into ridges with dips between: squeezed rock does the same, and the up-arches are anticlines, the down-sags synclines — the Cape's folded ranges are a rug pushed about 300 million years ago and never straightened. Now flex a plastic ruler faster and further until it snaps: rock pushed past its patience breaks along a fault. Pull two breaks apart and the middle block slumps — a rift valley floor; leave a block standing proud between two dropped neighbours — a block mountain. And when a locked fault finally slips, name the two points before the shaking stops: the FOCUS is the underground spot where the rock gave way; the EPICENTRE is the ground-level address directly above it, where the cameras gather. Then keep the two measurements in separate pockets: magnitude is the quake's own energy, read by instruments, one number for the whole event; Mercalli intensity is what people and buildings experienced, and it changes from town to town. One quake, one magnitude, many intensities.

This section's questions are up now: the cracked pan, the three moves, the rug against the ruler, and focus, epicentre and the two measurements.

## Subtopic: The Dam in a Drought — Mapwork Rehearsed Out Loud

Watch a farm dam fall through a long drought and it will teach you contours. As the water drops, each old level leaves a pale watermark around the banks — ring below ring below ring. Every ring joins all the points that were once at exactly the same water height: that is precisely what a contour line is, a ring of equal height. Where the dam's bank is steep, the rings sit shoulder to shoulder; where the bank shelves gently, they spread far apart. Crowded rings, steep ground; spaced rings, gentle ground — the first law of map reading. Rings closing into ever smaller circles climb to a summit; and wherever the rings cross an inflowing stream they kink into an arrowhead — and that V points UPSTREAM, into the high ground, one of the most dependable facts on any map sheet.

Now say the two calculations aloud until they are muscle memory. Gradient, as a spoken routine: find the height difference — top point 1 220 metres, bottom point 1 060, difference 160; find the distance — 4.8 centimetres on a 1:50 000 sheet, and since every 2 centimetres is a kilometre, that is 2.4 kilometres, or 2 400 metres; divide distance by height — 2 400 over 160 gives 15; answer, spoken in the required dress: 1 in 15, climb one metre for every fifteen forward, a slope that makes a bicycle stand on its pedals. Cross-section, as a spoken walk: start on the riverbank at 1 060, rise steadily across three evenly spaced contours to 1 120, then the contours crowd — a steep pitch to the 1 220 crest — then open out down a long gentle back slope to the plain. If you can talk the line, you can draw the graph.

Finish with the weather chart, read like a scoreboard. Each town's circle is its scorecard. Shading fills the circle in eighths of cloud. The wind shaft sticks out toward where the wind COMES FROM — a shaft on the north-west side means a north-westerly wind — and its feathers count the speed in knots. Top left of the circle, the air temperature; bottom left, the dew point — and the closer those two scores stand, the closer the air is to saturation and rain. Then track the toothed line advancing on the Cape: the cold front. Before it arrives the scorecard reads warm, wind from the north, pressure easing downward. As it passes, everything flips in an afternoon: wind veers south-west, temperature tumbles, pressure climbs, showers sweep through and clear. Read one scorecard, narrate one front, and the synoptic question is yours.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the drought rings and the upstream V, the 1 in 15 routine spoken in full, and the scorecard decoded.
