Hey, it's Thandi — half a year of human geography in one session sounds like a mountain, but it is actually one story told three times, and you already know the plot.

Scene one, where people are. People arrange themselves like a school at first break: crushed where something is served, absent where nothing is. Gauteng is the tuckshop — a quarter of the country on the smallest province, because gold started serving jobs there in 1886. The Northern Cape is the far field — about two people per square kilometre. Density is just people divided by area — 3 million over 25 000 square kilometres is 120 — and always add that averages hide the crush.

Scene two, who the people are. The pyramid is a choir photo: front row for babies, back rows for elders. Three looks — how full the front, how fast the rows thin, who reaches the back. Wedge means young and growing; block means rich and ageing; countries square off as deaths fall first and births follow. South Africa stands mid-journey, with the HIV notch in the young-adult rows and the treatment recovery after it.

Scene three, people meet water. The country is a small, unpredictable, leaking tank: half the world's rainfall, nine per cent runoff, a third lost in the pipes. Answers: dams and the Lesotho transfer, six thousand free litres a month, farm efficiency first. And when the tank overflows, the flood finds the floodplain settlers — short lag, high peak, the Jukskei in minutes, Natal over days.

The whole thing in one line: people moved for work, settled on the land that was left, and the land that was left belonged to the water. Carry that line in, stay calm, and go take the practice questions apart. You've got this.
