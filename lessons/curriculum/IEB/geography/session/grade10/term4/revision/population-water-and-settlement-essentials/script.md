# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session gathers the human geography of the Grade 10 year into one connected argument: where South Africans and the world's people actually live and why; how populations grow, age and appear on a pyramid; why people migrate and what their arrival does to cities; and how the year's water topic — drought, flood and management — meets the settlement story at its most dangerous point, where the poorest households and the rising rivers occupy the same ground. Every definition is given in wording precise enough to reuse directly, and every example is South African wherever the content allows.

## Subtopic: Where People Are — Distribution, Density and Why

Two terms open the topic, and they must never be swapped. POPULATION DISTRIBUTION is the WHERE — the pattern people make across space, described in words such as clustered, dispersed, coastal, empty. POPULATION DENSITY is the HOW MANY — people per unit of area, calculated as total population divided by total area and expressed in people per square kilometre. Make the calculation reflexive: a metropolitan region of 3 million people spread over 25 000 square kilometres has a density of 3 million divided by 25 000, which is 120 people per square kilometre. Then immediately name the trap: density is an AVERAGE, and averages iron out the truth — Australia's national density is famously tiny, yet almost every Australian lives squeezed onto two coastal fringes, with a near-empty continent between them. Always ask how the people inside an average are actually arranged.

The world's arrangement is dramatically lopsided. The great majority of humanity lives north of the equator, gathered into a few dense zones — East Asia, South Asia, Europe, north-eastern North America — on river plains, fertile lowlands and coasts. The near-empty zones repel for listable reasons: too dry, as in the Sahara and the Kalahari; too cold, as in Siberia and the polar fringe; too high and broken, as in the high Himalayas and the Andes plateaus; too thickly forested and disease-ridden, as in stretches of the Amazon and Congo basins.

Explanations divide into PHYSICAL and HUMAN factors. Physical: water first, always, then climate, soil, relief and mineral resources. Human: work, transport, trade, history and politics. South Africa performs the whole theory on one map. Rain thins from east to west, so people thicken eastward — the wet side of the country carries the crowds. But the single densest knot of humanity ignores the rain entirely: GAUTENG, the smallest province, holds roughly a quarter of the national population on a dry plateau far from any coast, because in 1886 the Witwatersrand's gold began pulling labour, railways and industry uphill — a human factor overruling every physical one. At the other extreme, the NORTHERN CAPE spreads a tiny population across the country's largest province — around 2 people per square kilometre — because aridity gives farming almost nothing to work with. Add the harbour cities — Cape Town, Durban, Gqeberha — grown fat on trade, and the apartheid legacy that packed millions into former homeland districts on land chosen for their exclusion rather than its fertility, and the national map is fully accounted for.

Take this section's questions now: distribution against density, the calculation with its average trap, and the physical and human factors read off the South African map.

## Subtopic: Growth, Structure and the Population Pyramid

Growth is counted through the vital rates, per thousand people per year. The BIRTH RATE counts live births per 1 000; the DEATH RATE counts deaths per 1 000. Their difference is NATURAL INCREASE, usually converted to a percentage: a birth rate of 24 against a death rate of 9 leaves 15 per thousand, which is 1.5 per cent growth per year — and note that natural increase deliberately excludes migration, which only enters when total population change is asked. Three companion indicators complete the kit: the FERTILITY RATE, the average children born per woman, with roughly 2.1 marking replacement level; the INFANT MORTALITY RATE, deaths under one year per 1 000 live births — the most honest single measure of living conditions anywhere; and LIFE EXPECTANCY, the years a newborn can expect.

The DEMOGRAPHIC TRANSITION MODEL strings the rates into a story with stages. Stage one: births and deaths both high, growth negligible — humanity's condition for most of history. Stage two: clean water, vaccination and better food pull the death rate down while births stay high — population explodes; this is where much of the developing world entered the twentieth century. Stage three: as children survive and women gain schooling and contraception, the birth rate follows the death rate down — growth decelerates. Stage four: both rates low, population steady — the developed world's plateau. Stage five, already visible in Japan and parts of Europe: deaths outnumber births and populations shrink and grey. Attach the carefully worded concept: OVERPOPULATION is never a raw headcount but a RELATIONSHIP — more people than an area's resources can support at an acceptable standard of living. A thin scatter of people on barren land can be overpopulated; a dense, productive city need not be.

The POPULATION PYRAMID is the structure made visible: horizontal bars by age group, males on one side, females on the other, infants at the base and the elderly at the summit. Read any pyramid in three moves. The base: wide means high birth rates. The sides: rapid narrowing upward means many deaths young — hard living, high infant mortality. The top: a broad summit means long lives. The two archetypes follow: the TRIANGLE — swollen base, fast-tapering sides, empty top — is the young, poor, fast-growing population, its working adults burdened by heavy YOUTH DEPENDENCY; the COLUMN — modest base, straight sides, wide top — is the wealthy, slow country whose problem is rising AGED DEPENDENCY. The DEPENDENCY RATIO formalises the burden, comparing those under 15 and over 64 with the working ages between. South Africa's pyramid sits between the archetypes: a base narrowed by fertility that has fallen to around 2.3, still youthful overall — and scarred, because the HIV and Aids pandemic bit visibly into the young-adult bars and dragged life expectancy down through the 2000s, before the antiretroviral treatment programme, the largest on earth, turned the curve upward again. Whatever pyramid appears, comment on base, sides and top — births, survival, age — and the marks follow.

Pause for this section's questions: the vital rates with the 1.5 per cent calculation, the transition stages in order, and the pyramid read in three moves.

## Subtopic: People on the Move — Migration, Urbanisation and Settlement

MIGRATION is a lasting change of home, and its vocabulary comes in pairs that must be exact. Crossing a border, one person earns two labels: an EMIGRANT as they leave their country, an IMMIGRANT as they enter the next. INTERNATIONAL migration crosses borders; INTERNAL migration stays inside them, and its overwhelming modern form is RURAL-URBAN migration. Duration gives permanent against temporary — the seasonal fruit picker, the daily COMMUTER — and choice gives VOLUNTARY migration, undertaken for work, study or family, against FORCED migration, where REFUGEES flee war or persecution across borders and the internally displaced flee without ever leaving their country.

The explanatory machine is PUSH AND PULL. Push factors drive people from the origin: joblessness, drought, failing services, landlessness, violence. Pull factors draw them to the destination: wages, schools, clinics, electric light, and the letters and remittances of those who went ahead. South Africa supplies both case studies. The internal flow: young adults leave the former homeland districts of the Eastern Cape and Limpopo for Gauteng and the coastal metros — the origin regions age and hollow as their most energetic people depart, sustained partly by money sent home, while the receiving cities gain labour and energy and groan under housing shortfalls. The international flow: South Africa is southern Africa's magnet, drawing migrants from Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Lesotho and further, along paths first beaten by a century of mine-labour recruitment — and an honest answer includes the tension: competition over jobs and services that periodically ignites as xenophobic violence.

Where the movers land is the settlement story. URBANISATION is the rising PERCENTAGE of a population living in urban places — a share, not a count — and South Africa stands beyond two thirds urban and still climbing. Because arrivals outpace delivery, the housing backlog answers itself in INFORMAL SETTLEMENTS: self-built homes on whatever land stands unclaimed — and the land standing unclaimed in a city is usually unclaimed for a reason: the steep slope, the land beside the factory, the floodplain. Services trail behind: shared standpipes, pit toilets, unlit paths. The defensible response is upgrading rather than removal — bringing water, sanitation, electricity and secure tenure to where people already live — because the settlements sit where they sit for the soundest reason in economics: within reach of work. Keep this section's final image lit for the next one: the cheapest land in the city, and the river that made it cheap.

This section's questions are ready: the migration pairs, push against pull in the two South African flows, and urbanisation with its informal settlement consequence.

## Subtopic: Water in a Dry Country — Floods and Water Management

South Africa ranks among the thirty driest countries: about 465 millimetres of average annual rainfall against a world average near 860, delivered generously in the east, grudgingly in the west, and erratically everywhere, under evaporation fierce enough to exceed rainfall across most of the interior. Two thirds of the country receives less than the 500 millimetres that rain-fed cropping needs, and only about 9 per cent of the rain ever becomes river flow. From those numbers the whole engineering follows: rivers few and seasonal — the Orange carrying Lesotho's water west, the Vaal shouldering Gauteng, the Thukela pumped uphill as a reserve; storage in more than 500 large dams from the Gariep to the Vaal Dam; inter-basin TRANSFER SCHEMES, led by the Lesotho Highlands Water Project running downhill into the Vaal system; and boreholes into GROUNDWATER carrying the arid two thirds.

Yet the dry country also drowns, and revision must hold both truths at once. The physical side of flooding: violent convectional downpours, days of rain from a stalled CUT-OFF LOW, cyclone remnants in the north-east — landing on catchments primed by steep slopes, hard or saturated soil and thin vegetation. The human side: city surfaces sealed under tar and roofing with stormwater pipes hurrying runoff to the channels, wetlands — the natural shock absorbers — drained and built over, and settlement pressed onto floodplains, where informal homes occupy the water's own right of way. The FLOOD HYDROGRAPH prints the diagnosis: time against discharge, and a sealed or stripped catchment draws the short LAG TIME and tall narrow PEAK of a flashy river, while a vegetated catchment draws the long lag and low rounded peak of absorption. The two anchoring cases: the Jukskei flash flood of December 2022, when a storm on Johannesburg's paved catchment rose in minutes and killed at least fourteen people at the river near Alexandra; and the Natal floods of September 1987, when a cut-off low rained for days onto saturated hills and more than three hundred people died as rivers stood in flood across the province.

Management closes the loop. The constitution promises sufficient water; policy delivers the FREE BASIC WATER allowance of 6 000 litres per poor household per month, strained through rural distances, urban backlogs, ageing pipes and unpaid bills. Irrigation takes roughly 60 per cent of the nation's water, so a percentage point of farm efficiency — drip lines, covered canals, scheduled watering — outweighs whole suburbs of shorter showers; but the household list still matters, and one city proved it: facing Day Zero in 2018, Cape Town halved its daily use and kept its taps open. SUSTAINABLE use means drawing no faster than renewal, defending wetlands and catchments as working infrastructure, and planning for a warming climate that will sharpen both the droughts and the storms.

The last expert questions are with you now: the dryness numbers with their engineering answers, floods explained physically and humanly with the hydrograph's two signatures, and the management chain from free basic water to sustainability.

## Subtopic: The Tuckshop at First Break

The bell rings for first break and within two minutes the whole school has arranged itself: a crush at the tuckshop, comfortable groups in the quad, and nobody at all on the far field. Nobody planned it; everyone simply moved toward what they wanted. That self-arranged pattern is population DISTRIBUTION — the where. Counting turns it into DENSITY — the how many per space: forty learners packed into the few square metres at the tuckshop counter is a huge density; the same forty spread across the field would be a tiny one. Density is always people DIVIDED BY area, and it only means crowded relative to the space that holds them.

Now scale the schoolyard up to the country. South Africa's tuckshop is GAUTENG: the smallest province holding roughly a quarter of the nation, because that is where the thing everyone needs — work — has been served since gold was struck on the Witwatersrand in 1886. The quad is the coastal cities — Cape Town, Durban, Gqeberha — pleasant, provisioned, busy with trade. And the far field is the NORTHERN CAPE: the biggest space with the fewest people, around 2 per square kilometre, because almost no rain means almost no farming and almost no jobs. The whole map runs on schoolyard logic: people cluster where life serves something, and avoid where it serves nothing — too dry, too steep, too far.

One warning before the questions: density is an average, and averages hide the crush. Announce that the school has three learners per classroom of space, and you have said nothing about the forty at the tuckshop counter. Australia plays this trick on the world map — a density so low it sounds empty, while nearly every Australian queues on two coastal strips. When a question hands you a density figure, always ask how the people inside it are actually spread.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: tuckshop logic, Gauteng and the far field, and the average that hides the crush.

## Subtopic: The Choir on the Steps

Line a whole country up like a choir on the school-hall steps — youngest in the front row, oldest at the back, one side for males, the other for females — and photograph it. That photograph is the population pyramid, and reading it takes three looks. Look one, the front row: packed? Babies are arriving fast — a high birth rate. Look two, how quickly the rows thin as your eye moves back: fast thinning means many lives end young — high infant mortality, hard conditions. Look three, the back rows: well filled? People are living long.

Two choir shapes to memorise. The wedge — an overflowing front row, rows shrinking fast, a nearly empty back — sings for a young, poor, fast-growing country: swarms of children, few elders, and the middle rows carrying everyone else on their backs; that carrying load is youth dependency. The block — a modest front row, even rows all the way back — sings for a rich, slow country: few babies, long lives, and the opposite worry, too few workers supporting too many pensioners. Countries migrate from wedge toward block as they develop, and the journey has stages: first the death rate falls — the explosion, because the front rows keep filling while the back rows stop emptying — then the birth rate follows as children survive and women gain choices, and the shape squares off.

South Africa's choir stands mid-journey: still young at the front, but the front row no longer overflowing — families now average near two children — and with a scar you must be able to point to: through the 2000s the HIV pandemic thinned the young-adult rows visibly and dragged life expectancy down, until the treatment programme — the largest of its kind in the world — refilled the ranks and turned the numbers upward. And keep the guard-rail concept beside the photograph: overpopulation is not how many stand on the steps, but whether the hall behind them can feed and house that many — always a relationship between people and resources, never a bare count.

This section's questions are up now: the three looks, wedge against block, and South Africa's mid-journey choir.

## Subtopic: The Leaking Jojo Tank — South Africa's Water Story

Give the whole country one green Jojo tank and its water story tells itself in three faults. The tank is small: South Africa catches about half the world's average rainfall, most of it on the east side. The tank fills unpredictably: generous summers, then drought years, under a sun that steals from the open surface. And the tank leaks: in many towns a third of the piped water escapes through cracked mains and dripping taps before anyone drinks it.

Every response in the syllabus answers one of the three faults. Against smallness: engineering — more than 500 large dams for storage, water fetched over the border from Lesotho's high, wet mountains and run downhill into the Vaal for Gauteng, and boreholes drinking from the underground reserves that keep the Karoo alive. Against unfairness: the constitution's promise, cashed out as 6 000 free litres per poor household per month — easy to legislate, hard to pump to a scattered village or a fast-growing settlement. Against waste: behaviour and maintenance — and remember the proportions: farming takes roughly 60 per cent of the water, so drip irrigation on the farms outsaves every shower timer in town, though the town still matters, as Cape Town proved by halving its use to beat Day Zero.

Then comes the day the tank overflows — and the overflow lands exactly where the settlement story left its poorest people. The migrants who reached the city and found no housing built where land was free, and land in a city is free because something is wrong with it — often, because a river built it and means to return. Seal the suburbs upstream under tar, pipe the stormwater straight to the channel, drain the wetlands that used to absorb the blow, and the storm arrives at those doorsteps sooner and higher — the short lag and high peak of the sealed catchment's hydrograph. The Jukskei showed the fast version in December 2022, rising in minutes through the gatherings and homes along its banks; Natal in September 1987 showed the slow vast version, days of rain and a province's rivers standing in flood. Fold the year into one sentence and carry it to your final exams: people moved for work, settled on the land that was left, and the land that was left belonged to the water — which is why population, settlement and water are one story wearing three names.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the three faults of the tank and their three answers, and the chain that runs from migration through settlement to the flood.
