# Part 1 — Expert

Rain looks like an event; it is actually a production line. Water vapour enters the air, the air is pushed upward, rising air cools, cooled air reaches the temperature at which it can carry no more, the surplus condenses onto floating particles, and what the air can no longer hold falls back out. This session installs that production line piece by piece: humidity and dew point first, then condensation in all its forms and the height at which cloud must begin, then the cloud families and the weather each one advertises, and finally the three mechanisms that do the pushing over South Africa.

## Subtopic: Humidity, Saturation and Dew Point

Moisture boards the atmosphere by EVAPORATION — off the oceans, off farm dams, off wet soil — and by TRANSPIRATION through the leaves of plants; the combined flow is EVAPOTRANSPIRATION. Airborne water VAPOUR is a true gas and completely invisible. The white you can see in a cloud has already stopped being vapour: it is liquid droplets.

HUMIDITY measures the vapour content of air, and it comes in two versions. ABSOLUTE humidity is the raw mass of vapour in a cubic metre of air, in grams. RELATIVE humidity is the working tool: the vapour actually present, written as a percentage of the most that air at that temperature could carry.

Everything turns on one law: warm air can carry far more water vapour than cold air, and the capacity climbs steeply with temperature — not in a straight line but in an accelerating curve.

Put numbers on it. Air at 25 degrees Celsius can carry a maximum of about 23 grams of vapour per cubic metre. Suppose it holds 11,5 grams. Relative humidity: 11,5 over 23, times 100 — exactly 50 per cent. Now chill that identical parcel, adding not one drop. Around 13 degrees its ceiling has fallen to roughly 11,5 grams, so 11,5 over 11,5 gives 100 per cent: SATURATION. The moisture never changed; the capacity collapsed around it.

The temperature at which a cooling parcel hits saturation is its DEW POINT — a moisture reading wearing a temperature's clothing. Its value is always comparative. Air temperature sitting just above the dew point means near-saturation: sticky air, condensation on the doorstep. A wide spread between the two means dry air and a rainless sky. Whenever both numbers are available, read the GAP, never either number alone.

Take the questions on this section now: absolute against relative humidity, the fifty-per-cent calculation carried to saturation, and dew point defined as the temperature at which saturation arrives.

## Subtopic: Condensation — From Dew to the Condensation Level

CONDENSATION is the change from vapour to liquid. It requires two things: air cooled to its dew point, and CONDENSATION NUCLEI — floating specks of dust, smoke, pollen and sea salt for the water to gather on. Clean air resists condensing even below its dew point; the specks make it possible.

What form the condensation takes depends on where the cooling happens. On a cloudless, windless night the ground radiates its heat to space, grows cold, and chills the layer of air resting on it. If that air's dew point sits above freezing, moisture beads onto grass, roofs and windscreens as DEW. If the dew point lies below zero, the vapour skips the liquid stage and fixes directly onto surfaces as ice crystals: FROST — the reason a Karoo sheep farmer fears a still, starry July night far more than a merely cold one. Cool an entire layer of surface air below its dew point and the droplets hang suspended instead: MIST, or FOG once visibility drops under one kilometre. RADIATION fog pools in valley floors on calm winter dawns — the Hex River valley vineyards often start the day inside it. ADVECTION fog is made by movement: moist ocean air sliding across the cold Benguela water turns to a grey wall that drifts over Saldanha and the West Coast fishing villages.

Then the vertical story, the one that builds every cloud. Push a parcel of air upward and the pressure around it slackens, so the parcel expands — and expansion itself cools it. This is ADIABATIC cooling: temperature falling with no heat taken away. While the parcel stays unsaturated it cools at the DRY ADIABATIC LAPSE RATE of 1 degree per 100 metres. The moment saturation arrives, condensation begins releasing latent heat inside the parcel, braking the cooling to the SATURATED ADIABATIC LAPSE RATE of about 0,5 degrees per 100 metres.

The altitude where the rising parcel first reaches its dew point is the CONDENSATION LEVEL — the floor of the cloud. Calculate one. Surface temperature 28 degrees, dew point 16: the parcel must shed 12 degrees, and at 1 degree per 100 metres that costs 1 200 metres of climbing. Cloud base: 1 200 metres up. This single number explains a familiar sight — a fleet of fair-weather clouds whose flat bases all float at exactly the same height, because every rising parcel in that air mass saturates at the same level.

Your questions on this section are with you now: dew, frost, mist and fog separated by their causes, adiabatic cooling explained from expansion, and a condensation level worked from the numbers.

## Subtopic: Clouds — Naming Them and Reading Their Weather

Cloud names intimidate until you notice they are built from just four Latin bricks. CIRRO: high and wispy. ALTO: middle. STRATO: flattened into layers. CUMULO: heaped and piled. Add NIMBUS, the wet brick: it flags a cloud that is actually raining.

HIGH clouds, above about 6 000 metres, are frozen — ice crystals, not droplets. CIRRUS paints thin white brushstrokes across a blue sky: fine weather now, but frequently the outriders of a front still a day distant. CIRROSTRATUS spreads into a pale milky veil, and its trademark is the HALO — a luminous ring around sun or moon that seasoned farmers read as rain within a day or two. CIRROCUMULUS ripples into rows of small white flakes, the pattern nicknamed a mackerel sky.

MIDDLE clouds, from about 2 000 to 6 000 metres, wear the prefix alto. ALTOSTRATUS is a grey wash through which the sun glows dimly, as if behind bathroom glass — it usually thickens as steady rain approaches. ALTOCUMULUS is a quilt of soft grey-white rolls and patches.

LOW clouds sit below about 2 000 metres. STRATUS is the featureless grey ceiling of a dull, drizzly day — and fog is nothing but stratus that has settled onto the ground. STRATOCUMULUS lumps that ceiling into rounded masses. NIMBOSTRATUS is the heavyweight: thick, dark, shapeless, and the source of long, steady soaking rain rather than brief showers.

The VERTICAL family provides the drama. CUMULUS is the flat-bottomed, cauliflower-headed cloud of a sunny morning, riding on its condensation-level shelf. Keep feeding it uplift through a hot afternoon and it mutates into CUMULONIMBUS — a tower climbing from low altitude to the tropopause, spreading sideways into an ANVIL where the stable stratosphere refuses it entry, and unloading downpours, lightning, thunder and the hailstones that strip orchards and dent bakkies across the summer-rainfall interior.

The skill under examination is forecasting from the name, and the questions on this section are in front of you now: give the family, give the height band, and state the weather each cloud is advertising.

## Subtopic: The Three Rainfall Types

All rain is manufactured by the same four steps — lift, adiabatic cooling, saturation at the dew point, condensation and fall. The three rainfall types are not three recipes; they are one recipe with three different LIFTING mechanisms. Identify the lift and the rest of the answer assembles itself.

RELIEF or OROGRAPHIC rainfall: the lift is a landform. Moist maritime air driven inland collides with a mountain rampart and is forced up its face. Climbing air cools, saturates, and unloads on the WINDWARD slope. Beyond the crest the air descends, is compressed, warms, and dries out — the LEEWARD side sits in a RAIN SHADOW. South Africa supplies textbook cases on both coasts: moist Indian Ocean air rising against the Drakensberg escarpment feeds the lush eastern slopes while the plateau behind receives far less; and along the southern coast, sea air lifting over the Outeniqua range keeps George and Knysna famously green while the Klein Karoo on the far side is dry enough for ostrich ranching.

CONVECTIONAL rainfall: the lift is heat itself. A summer sun cooks the interior plateau, the scorched ground superheats the air upon it, and powerful thermals hoist that air kilometres upward. With enough moisture and enough lift, cumulus fattens into cumulonimbus. The result has an unmistakable daily rhythm: cloudless morning, building towers past noon, and a violent, localised cloudburst in the late afternoon — thunder, lightning, often hail, finished within the hour. That is the summer rainfall regime of the Highveld interior, from the Free State grain districts through Gauteng into Mpumalanga, and it is why interior rain arrives as artillery rather than drizzle.

FRONTAL or CYCLONIC rainfall: the lift is a collision. Where a cold air mass meets a warm moist one, the denser cold air knifes underneath and levers the warm air off the surface, so it rises along the sloping frontal boundary. A COLD front drives that wedge aggressively: steep uplift, a narrow line of cumulonimbus, hard showers, and a sharp temperature drop as it passes. A WARM front lifts gently: the warm air glides up over retreating cold air, spreading broad layered cloud and long, quiet rain. Our national example is seasonal and precise: the Western Cape's winter rain, delivered by successions of cold fronts wheeling in from the SOUTH-WEST off the Atlantic between about May and August, one after the next.

The classic error, which the closing questions of this part will now test: classifying rainfall by its violence instead of its lift. Name the LIFTING MECHANISM first, then attach the South African region and its season — those are separate pieces of a complete answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same production line again — this time run with a kitchen sponge, a family of surnames, and three different machines for lifting air.

## Subtopic: Squeezing the Sponge

Think of air as a sponge for water vapour, with one strange property: warmth makes the sponge bigger. WARM air is a big sponge that can soak up plenty; COLD air is a small sponge that fills up fast.

Now do the experiment nature does every night. Take a warm sponge that is half full and cool it down — without adding any water at all. Cooling shrinks the sponge around the water it already carries. Keep going and the moment arrives when the shrinking sponge is exactly full: the air is SATURATED. The temperature where that happened is the DEW POINT. Nothing was poured in. The container got smaller; the contents stayed the same.

You have watched this happen your whole life. Walk out of an air-conditioned mall into a muggy Durban afternoon and your glasses instantly mist over — the cold lenses chill the air touching them, that air's sponge shrinks below its load, and the surplus lands on the glass. Same story with your breath on a frosty morning: warm, moist lungs-air meets winter air, cools in an instant, and its extra moisture flashes into a small visible cloud. No mystery, just a shrinking sponge.

Numbers, briefly, because they earn quick marks. Air at 25 degrees can hold about 23 grams of water per cubic metre. If it carries 11,5 grams, it is half full — relative humidity 50 per cent. Cool it to about 13 degrees, where the limit is 11,5, and it stands at 100 per cent, full to the brim, with rain or dew now one small nudge away.

And the habit to carry into every weather question: never read the dew point alone; read the DISTANCE between air temperature and dew point. Small distance — sponge nearly full, weather brewing. Wide distance — dry sponge, dry day. The questions on the sponge are with you now: big sponge, small sponge, what saturation means, and why the gap is the number that matters.

## Subtopic: Clouds Have Surnames

Cloud names sound like a Latin exam, but they work like family surnames — four name-parts, endlessly recombined. Learn four words and the whole sky introduces itself.

CIRRO is the high family, wispy and thin. ALTO is the middle family. STRATO means flattened out, a sheet or blanket. CUMULO means piled up in heaps. And NIMBUS is the surname that matters most: it announces that the cloud is raining right now.

Meet the families. Highest of all, far above airline cruising height, live the ice-crystal clouds. CIRRUS: white feathers combed across blue — fair for the moment, but often the advance guard of a front arriving tomorrow. When those feathers merge into a milky film and a pale ring appears around the sun, you are looking at CIRROSTRATUS, and the front has moved closer; farm families have read that halo as a rain warning for generations.

The middle floor belongs to the altos. ALTOSTRATUS turns the sun into a smudge behind frosted glass and usually thickens as steady rain approaches; ALTOCUMULUS scatters the sky with soft grey-white patches, like a duvet pulled apart.

Down low sit the greys. STRATUS is the flat blanket of a gloomy drizzly day — and when that blanket lies down on the ground itself, we stop calling it cloud and call it fog. NIMBOSTRATUS is the dark, thick, shapeless one that sets in and rains steadily through the whole afternoon.

Then the show-offs, built upward instead of outward. CUMULUS is the white cauliflower of a fine morning, and notice its haircut: a crisp flat base, with every cumulus in the sky floating on the same invisible floor — the height where rising air hit its dew point. Give it a long hot afternoon of uplift and it grows into CUMULONIMBUS, the thundercloud: a tower to the roof of the weather-layer, flattening into an anvil against that ceiling, then hurling down rain, lightning and hail.

Four name-parts, one sky. Your questions on the surnames are up now: the four bricks, the three height floors, and which family member brings which weather.

## Subtopic: The Ramp, the Fire and the Wedge

Every raindrop in history was made the same way: air went up, cooled, filled to the brim, and let go. The only question that separates the three rainfall types is — what sent the air up? Three lifting machines. Learn them as the ramp, the fire and the wedge.

THE RAMP is a mountain range. Wet sea air blows inland, hits the rock wall, and has exactly one exit: upward. On the climb it cools and fills; at the top it tips its water down the side facing the wind. Rolling down the far slope it compresses, rewarms, and arrives as dry air — so one side of the ramp drips while the other side rations water. That is RELIEF rainfall: the Outeniqua slopes above George soaked green, the Klein Karoo behind them dry enough for ostriches; the Drakensberg's seaward face lush, the plateau beyond it thirsty.

THE FIRE is the summer ground itself. On a January afternoon in the Free State the earth bakes until the air above it lifts off like heat over a braai — invisible columns punching kilometres upward. Cloud builds through the day, stacking into a dark tower, and somewhere around four o'clock the tower breaks: twenty minutes of furious rain, lightning, maybe hail, sometimes drowning one farm while the neighbouring one stays bone dry. That is CONVECTIONAL rainfall — the Highveld summer in a single afternoon.

THE WEDGE is cold air on the move. A cold air mass drives in from the south-west, and because cold air is heavier, it slides underneath the warm moist air like a splitting wedge under a log, heaving it off the ground. The lifted air cools and rains along the whole moving boundary. That is FRONTAL rainfall — and it is the Western Cape's entire winter: wedge after wedge spinning off the Atlantic from May to August, watering Cape Town in exactly the months the summer-rainfall interior goes quiet.

Ramp, fire, wedge. Three machines, one recipe. And dodge the classic trap: never classify rain by how hard it falls — classify it by the machine that lifted the air, then add the region and the season as their own separate facts.
