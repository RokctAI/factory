# Part 1 — Expert

Everything in weather and climate for the next three years rests on the work of this single session. The air above your head is not a vague emptiness — it is a precisely proportioned mixture of gases, stacked into four layers that behave in completely different ways, and carrying inside it a thin chemical shield that decides how much ultraviolet radiation lands on Bloemfontein, on the Garden Route, on your own skin. Four blocks of content today: what the air is made of, how it is layered, the ozone story, and why the whole envelope matters.

## Subtopic: The Composition of the Atmosphere

Begin with the figures, and quote them for DRY air by volume, because that is the standard convention. NITROGEN takes 78 per cent. OXYGEN takes 21 per cent. ARGON takes 0,93 per cent. CARBON DIOXIDE sits at roughly 0,04 per cent — four molecules in every ten thousand. Traces of neon, helium, methane and hydrogen fill the last sliver. Those four figures — 78, 21, 0,93 and 0,04 — must be automatic before you leave this section.

Next comes the classification you will be asked to make: PERMANENT gases against VARIABLE components. Nitrogen, oxygen and argon are permanent — sample the lower atmosphere above Musina or above the middle of the Atlantic and their proportions barely shift. The variable components are a different story. WATER VAPOUR can be almost absent in the winter air over Sutherland and can approach four per cent in the muggy summer air over Richards Bay. DUST and other aerosols — veld-fire smoke, sea spray salt, pollen, mine dust, volcanic ash — rise and fall with place and season. And OZONE, the third variable, gathers far overhead in the stratosphere.

Now attach a function to each component, because naming a gas earns little and explaining its work earns much. Nitrogen does two jobs: it DILUTES the oxygen, so that burning proceeds at a manageable pace rather than a roar, and it is the raw stock that soil bacteria fix into nitrates, the nitrogen compounds every plant needs to build protein. Oxygen powers respiration in every animal and drives every flame. Carbon dioxide, tiny as its share is, feeds photosynthesis and is a principal greenhouse gas. Water vapour is the parent of every cloud, every frost and every drop of rain, and it ferries latent heat across the globe; it is also the most abundant greenhouse gas of all. The aerosols earn their place because every speck is a CONDENSATION NUCLEUS — a landing platform onto which cooling water vapour condenses. Strip the air of dust and cloud droplets struggle to be born.

The classic slip is to quote the standard percentages as though they described the humid air of a coastal morning. They describe dry air, and saying so is part of a complete answer. The questions for this section are with you now: the four figures, the permanent-versus-variable division, and one clearly stated function per component.

## Subtopic: The Four Layers — Structure by Temperature

The atmosphere is sliced into layers using a single test: does temperature rise or fall as altitude increases? Learn the four layers from the ground upward, and for each one carry four facts — its height range, the direction of temperature change, the REASON for that direction, and one distinguishing feature.

The TROPOSPHERE reaches from the surface up to roughly 18 kilometres above the equator but only about 8 kilometres above the poles, averaging near 12. It contains about three-quarters of the atmosphere's entire mass and nearly all of its water vapour. Temperature FALLS as you climb, at an average lapse rate of 6,5 degrees Celsius for every 1 000 metres — which is why hikers topping out on Thabana Ntlenyana can stand in sleet while the Umgeni valley below sits in shirtsleeve warmth. The reason is the heart of the matter: this layer is warmed from BELOW, because the ground absorbs sunlight and re-radiates long-wave energy upward, so air grows colder the further it sits from its heat source. Every cloud, every storm, every weather event you will ever study lives here. The layer ends at the TROPOPAUSE, at around minus 55 degrees, where the fall in temperature stops.

The STRATOSPHERE stretches from the tropopause to about 50 kilometres, and its temperature RISES with height — a standing inversion. The cause is ozone, concentrated between roughly 20 and 30 kilometres, absorbing ultraviolet radiation and releasing the energy as heat. With warm air resting on top of cooler air, convection is shut down: no rising currents, almost no water vapour, no cloud, no weather. That flat calm is why long-haul aircraft out of O.R. Tambo climb into the bottom of this layer and stay there. It closes at the STRATOPAUSE, near 0 degrees.

The MESOSPHERE, from 50 to about 80 kilometres, holds no ozone to warm it, so temperature FALLS once more, bottoming near minus 90 degrees at the MESOPAUSE — the coldest point anywhere in the atmosphere. Its claim to fame is friction: incoming meteoroids burn up against its thin air, and we call the streaks shooting stars.

The THERMOSPHERE begins around 80 kilometres, and temperature RISES sharply into the hundreds and thousands of degrees, because the few molecules left up there absorb the fiercest short-wave solar radiation. The air is so close to vacuum that the reading is almost meaningless for warmth — nothing is present to deliver the heat. Inside it sits the ionosphere, the electrically charged region that bounces radio signals around the curve of the earth, and the auroras glow here.

Hold the graph in your mind, because you will be given one to read: height on the vertical axis, temperature on the horizontal. The plotted line slants LEFT while climbing the troposphere, bends RIGHT through the stratosphere, slants LEFT again across the mesosphere, and bends RIGHT once more in the thermosphere — a zigzag whose three elbows are the three pauses. Your questions on this section are ready now: the order of the layers, their height ranges, and the reason behind every reversal.

## Subtopic: The Ozone Layer — Shield, Damage and Repair

Ozone is oxygen in a three-atom arrangement instead of the usual two. It is manufactured in the stratosphere itself: ultraviolet radiation splits ordinary oxygen molecules, and the loose atoms bond onto other oxygen molecules to make ozone. The whole shield, gathered mainly between 20 and 30 kilometres, is astonishingly slight — squeezed down to surface pressure it would form a sheet about three millimetres deep, thinner than a matchstick. Its single, essential job: it soaks up nearly all incoming ultraviolet-B and ultraviolet-C before those wavelengths reach living tissue.

The destroyers are CHLOROFLUOROCARBONS — CFCs — gases that industry loved precisely because they seemed harmless: non-toxic, non-flammable, and utterly stable. They filled spray cans, fridge and air-conditioner circuits, foam packaging and cleaning solvents for half a century. Their stability is the trap. Rain cannot dissolve them and surface chemistry cannot break them, so they ride the winds upward for years until stratospheric ultraviolet finally cracks them open and sets CHLORINE free. Chlorine attacks ozone CATALYTICALLY: the atom emerges from each reaction unchanged and simply attacks again, so one chlorine atom can wreck tens of thousands of ozone molecules before it is removed.

Each southern spring the depletion concentrates over Antarctica into the seasonal thinning called the ozone hole, because the winter polar vortex isolates that air and ice-crystal clouds inside it provide the surfaces on which the chlorine chemistry runs fastest. This matters directly to us: South Africa lies close to that thinning, already receives some of the strongest sunshine of any inhabited country, and records skin-cancer rates among the highest in the world.

Keep the consequences as a tidy list. On people: more skin cancer, faster skin ageing, more cataracts and eye damage, weakened immune responses. On ecosystems: extra ultraviolet damages phytoplankton, the microscopic base of the ocean food web that ultimately feeds the snoek and sardine fisheries off our Atlantic coast, and it cuts yields in sensitive crops such as beans and maize seedlings. On materials: plastics, rubber and paint break down faster. Then the repair: the Montreal Protocol, signed in 1987, phased out CFC manufacture worldwide, and measurements now show the layer slowly healing — the clearest success story in international environmental law. At household level, old fridges and air-conditioners should have their coolant professionally recovered, never left to leak on a dump, and ozone-safe products deserve the shelf space.

One boundary must stay sharp in your mind, and the questions now with you will probe it: ozone depletion is about extra radiation getting IN through the stratosphere, while the greenhouse effect is about heat being trapped in the troposphere. Different gases, different layers, entirely different mechanisms.

## Subtopic: Why the Atmosphere Matters — and the Exam Method

Finish where the topic itself finishes: with the atmosphere's importance taken as a whole. It supplies oxygen for respiration and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis. Acting as an insulating blanket, its natural greenhouse effect holds the planet's average surface temperature near 15 degrees Celsius; strip the blanket away and the figure falls to about minus 18. It filters ultraviolet through the ozone shield and incinerates meteoroids before they land. It stores and moves the water of the hydrological cycle, and it exports surplus tropical heat toward the poles, moderating both. It spares us the savage day-night swings of an airless world — the Moon's surface soars far above one hundred degrees in daylight and plunges below minus one hundred and fifty in darkness. And it scatters blue light across the sky, carries every sound you hear, keeps aircraft aloft and radio signals travelling.

Now the working method, three habits that hold for every question this topic can produce. FIRST: describe structure from the ground upward, and give each layer its full set of four — height range, temperature direction, reason, distinguishing feature. A layer named without its reason is a half-answer. SECOND: when you are handed a temperature-height graph, go straight to the TURNING POINTS. Every elbow in the line is a pause — tropopause, stratopause, mesopause — and once the pauses are labelled, every layer falls into place without a single memorised height. THIRD: for any ozone question, build the answer in three separate sentences — where the layer is, what causes the damage, what the consequence is — because that is how complete answers are structured and rewarded.

And guard the vocabulary. The ozone LAYER is natural, high and protective. Ozone at street level, cooked out of exhaust fumes and industrial emissions by sunlight, is a pollutant and a lung irritant. One molecule, two opposite report cards, and altitude is the only difference. The closing questions of this part are in front of you now: the functions of the atmosphere, the four-part layer description, and street-level ozone kept firmly apart from the shield.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same air, same four layers, same thin shield — this time told with a choir, a mountain climb, and a farmer's shade net.

## Subtopic: The Air Recipe

Put a choir of exactly one hundred singers on a stage, and let that choir be a lungful of dry air. Seventy-eight of them are basses: nitrogen. Twenty-one are tenors: oxygen. The last singer, standing alone at the end, is everything else at once — almost all argon, with carbon dioxide as barely a breath of that one voice. To hear carbon dioxide honestly you need a stadium of ten thousand singers, and then only four of them are carbon dioxide. Four voices in ten thousand — and yet those four feed every green plant in the country and help hold the planet's warmth in at night.

Do not write nitrogen off as filler. Think of strong cement: use it neat and everything sets too hard, too fast. Nitrogen is the sand mixed in — it thins the oxygen out so that fire burns at a walking pace. In pure oxygen a single spark in a workshop would not make a small flame; it would make a fireball. Nitrogen is why a candle burns politely.

Then there is the ingredient that never gets printed in the recipe because it will not hold still: WATER VAPOUR. On a sticky January afternoon in St Lucia the air is drenched with it; on a July morning in Sutherland there is next to none. Because it swings by the hour, every textbook table quietly says "dry air" at the top — and noticing those two words is the mark of a careful geographer.

Finally, the specks you see drifting through a bar of sunlight: dust, smoke, salt, pollen. Every speck is a tiny perch, a place for cooling water vapour to settle and become a droplet. No perches, no droplets, no rain. The same specks scatter sunlight, which is why midday skies are blue and why the sun sinks blood-orange through the smoke of a winter veld fire. Before we start climbing, your questions on the recipe are with you now — the four numbers, the restless ingredient, and the job the specks do.

## Subtopic: Four Camps on One Impossible Mountain

Now climb a mountain that does not exist, four camps high, where the fires burn in all the wrong places.

BASE CAMP is the troposphere, and its fire is the GROUND you stand on: the earth soaks up sunshine all day and breathes the warmth back out, so every step you climb takes you further from the fire and deeper into cold. That is the whole reason Matroosberg carries snow on a weekend when Paarl, just down the valley, is picnic weather. Base camp holds about three-quarters of all the air on the mountain, and every cloud, storm and rainbow belongs to it.

CAMP TWO is the stratosphere, and here someone has hung the fire ABOVE the camp: the ozone up near the tent roof catches ultraviolet and glows warm, so the higher you climb, the warmer it gets. Warm air floating on cold air is a lid — nothing stirs, nothing rises, no cloud ever forms. Perfectly still. That stillness is exactly why airliners cruise in the floor of this camp, gliding above every storm on the mountain.

CAMP THREE is the mesosphere, and it has no fire at all. The cold returns and deepens, and the roof of this camp is the coldest spot on the whole mountain — around minus ninety degrees. Space gravel slams into the thin air here and burns to streaks of light: shooting stars are camp-three weather.

THE SUMMIT is the thermosphere: a furious fire and almost nothing to warm. The thermometer claims thousands of degrees, yet a climber would freeze, because there are hardly any molecules left to carry the heat across to a body. This is where radio signals bounce back down to earth and where auroras shimmer.

Chant the climb once — troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere — and feel the temperature go DOWN, UP, DOWN, UP as you rise. Your questions on the mountain are up now: the four camps in order, where each fire sits, and the down-up-down-up rhythm.

## Subtopic: The Shade Net and the Moth

Every farmer knows what shade netting does: it hangs over the orchard and takes the sting out of the sun so the fruit below does not scorch. The ozone layer is the country's shade net, rigged about twenty-five kilometres overhead — and gathered up, the whole net would squash into a sheet three millimetres thick. Thinner than the cardboard of a cereal box, and it stands between your skin and the harshest ultraviolet the sun throws.

CFCs are the moths in the net. They escaped for fifty years from old fridges, aerosol cans, air-conditioners and foam packaging. Their trick is patience: they are so chemically lazy that rain cannot wash them down, so they drift upward for years until fierce sunlight finally cracks them open and lets the chlorine out. And chlorine is a moth that never gets full. One chlorine atom chews through an ozone molecule, comes out the other side untouched, and moves straight to the next — one atom, tens of thousands of holes, like a hole-punch that never blunts.

Every southern spring the biggest tear opens above Antarctica, because the winter winds down there seal the air into a closed room where the chewing chemistry runs wild. We live almost next door to that tear, under some of the fiercest sunshine on earth, and our skin-cancer numbers rank among the world's worst — which is why the slip-on hat between ten and three is not a nagging rule but plain sense.

Now the ending, and for once it is a good one: this is the environmental disaster humanity actually repaired. The world signed the Montreal Protocol, the factories stopped making CFCs, and year by year the net is re-weaving itself. Your part is small and real — when an old fridge dies, have its gas drained by a technician instead of letting it leak on a scrapheap.

Keep one pair of ideas from ever blurring, because it is the favourite trap of the topic: the ozone problem is about radiation leaking IN through camp two; the greenhouse problem is about heat locked DOWN at base camp. Two different problems on two different levels of the mountain. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the net, the moth, the treaty that worked, and those two problems kept wide apart.
