Hey, it's Thandi — I know how this topic looks from the outside: four layer names that all end in -sphere, a spray of percentages, and a chemistry subplot about chlorine. It feels like a pile of facts. It is actually one idea wearing four costumes, and once you spot the idea the pile collapses into something you can carry.

The idea is: find the fire. Troposphere — the fire is the ground, so climbing means cooling. Stratosphere — the fire hangs overhead in the ozone, so climbing means warming. Mesosphere — no fire, cold returns, coldest roof in the sky. Thermosphere — a raging fire with almost no air to warm. Whisper "ground, overhead, none, raging" to yourself and you can redraw the whole zigzag graph in an exam with zero heights memorised.

Composition is four numbers and two words. Seventy-eight, twenty-one, nought comma nine three, and four-in-ten-thousand for carbon dioxide. The two words are "dry air" — water vapour changes by the hour, so it gets left out of the recipe, and explaining that is an easy mark waiting for you.

Here is the habit that separates full answers from half answers: never name a layer without its reason. Add "because it is heated from below" or "because ozone absorbs ultraviolet there" and a one-mark sentence becomes a two-mark sentence. For ozone questions, write three short sentences — where, what causes it, what follows — instead of one tangled long one.

And hold the big two apart: ozone thinning lets radiation IN up in the stratosphere; the greenhouse effect keeps heat DOWN in the troposphere. Different gases, different layers.

One idea, four costumes, four numbers. Now go prove it to yourself on the practice questions — you're readier than you feel!
