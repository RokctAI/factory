# Part 1 — Expert

A topographic map is flat paper describing ground that is anything but flat, and contour lines are the code it uses to do it. Master that code and every mapwork task for the rest of your school career becomes a reading exercise instead of a guessing game. This session works on a 1:50 000 map extract with a 20 metre contour interval — the standard for South African topographic sheets. We build from the definition of a contour and the rules it must obey, through the principle that turns line spacing into slope, to the V-shaped fingerprint of a river valley and its dangerous double, the spur, and we finish by reading an entire landscape and defending every claim with evidence from the map itself.

## Subtopic: What a Contour Line Actually Is

A contour line joins every point on the map that stands at the same height above sea level. If you could walk the line itself out in the landscape, your altimeter would never move — no climbing, no descending, just a level path curving around the hillside.

On our extract the contour interval is 20 metres, so neighbouring lines differ in height by exactly 20 metres: 240, 260, 280, 300, and so on. That interval holds across the entire sheet, and it is printed in the map key — always confirm it before doing anything else, because other maps use 10 or 50 metre intervals and every height reading depends on knowing which one you hold.

Three rules govern contours, and each one is a free error-check. One: contour lines never cross, because no point on earth can stand at two heights simultaneously. Two: a contour never dies in the middle of the sheet — it either closes back on itself as a loop or exits at the map edge. Three: every fourth or fifth contour is printed thicker and carries its height value; these index contours are the anchors you count from.

For a point lying between two contours, the lines bracket its height. A farmhouse between the 280 and 300 lines stands somewhere between 280 and 300 metres, nearer in height to whichever line it sits closer to. And where contours ring a summit, look for a dot with a number: the spot height. On our extract the dot reads 347, so that hilltop is exactly 347 metres above sea level — sharper information than the highest closed contour, which can only report "higher than 340".

The questions for this section have just reached you. Work with the three rules in hand, and be exact about what the spot height 347 tells you that the 340 ring never could.

## Subtopic: Spacing Is Gradient — Steep and Gentle Slopes

Here is the most valuable sentence in all of mapwork: closely spaced contour lines mean a steep slope; widely spaced contour lines mean a gentle one.

Do not memorise that — derive it, so it can never desert you. The climb between two neighbouring contours is locked at 20 metres by the interval. The only thing left to vary is the horizontal distance covered while gaining those 20 metres. Lines a full kilometre apart mean a kilometre of easy walking for 20 metres of height — barely a slope at all. Lines pressed within 80 metres of each other deliver the same 20 metres of climb in a fraction of the distance — hard work, steep ground. Gradient is simply rise measured against run, and since the rise never changes, the spacing of the lines is a direct picture of the run.

To locate the steepest ground on our extract, hunt for the tightest bunching — the zone where the 260, 280, 300 and 320 lines squeeze into the narrowest band. That compressed ladder is your steepest slope, and the justification to write down is exact: the contour lines are closest together at this point, so the land gains its fixed 20 metres of height over the shortest horizontal distance on the map.

Learn the three classic spacing patterns as well. Contours at even spacing top to bottom: a uniform slope, one steady gradient. Contours bunched near the SUMMIT and opening out towards the foot: a CONCAVE slope — brutal at the top, relaxing lower down, and because the ground drops away and then flattens, an observer on the crest sees the entire slope to its base. Contours open near the summit and bunched at the FOOT: a CONVEX slope — deceptively gentle at first, then steepening below, so the swell of the upper slope hides the lower ground and the crest has a blind spot. Being able to name these from spacing alone, and to state the line-of-sight consequence, is a skill worth real marks.

Take this section's questions now. Every one of them rewards the reasoning over the slogan — fixed rise, varying run — and two hinge on reading concave against convex straight from the spacing.

## Subtopic: Finding the River Valley — the V Rule

Rivers carve valleys, and valleys write a signature into the contours that you can spot from across the room: successive contour lines kink into V shapes, and every V aims uphill — at the higher ground the stream descends from.

Understand why and you will never draw the arrow backwards. A contour approaching a valley is trapped by its own definition: it must hold its height, yet the valley floor ahead is lower than it is allowed to go. Its only option is to swing up along the valley side, cross the stream at the exact point where the ground regains its height, and swing back down the far side. From above, that in-and-out excursion is a V — and the sharp tip marks the deepest the line had to push upstream, which is always towards the high ground.

On our extract, then: find the drainage line, printed blue or not, by looking for a chain of V-kinks repeating through consecutive contours. The tips of the Vs point upstream to the source; the water leaves through the open mouths of the Vs, downhill. Then audit your reading with the numbers: trace the channel and the contours it crosses must fall in the direction of flow — 320, then 300, then 280. A river reading 280 up to 300 is a river you have traced backwards.

Now the double agent: the spur. A spur is a tongue of high ground poking out between two valleys, and its contours bend as well — but the bulge aims downhill, away from the summit, and no stream ever runs along it, because water drains off a spur's flanks rather than down its spine. Valley: V uphill, water likely. Spur: bulge downhill, water never. Mixing them up is the most common contour mistake at this level, and the antidote never changes — read the heights on both sides of the bend and establish which way is up.

Our extract has become a sentence you can read aloud: a river threading north-west out of the high ground, the 347 summit above it, and the steep eastern face where the contours crowd.

This section's questions are with you now. Judge every bend twice — once by shape, once by the numbers — and let the numbers have the final say, because shapes can deceive and values cannot.

## Subtopic: Reading the Whole Landscape — and the Exam Method

Time to assemble the complete toolkit, feature by feature, the way a full mapwork answer demands.

A round hill: closed, roughly circular contours climbing in value towards the centre, often crowned by a spot height. A ridge: high ground stretched long and narrow, its closed contours drawn out like an elongated racetrack. A saddle: the low neck between two summits, where each hill's contours face the other across the gap. A cliff: contours converging until they nearly merge — height gained with almost no horizontal ground. A plain: a near-empty sheet, contours few and far between, land lying close to flat.

Then work every contour question through the same five steps. Step one: take the contour interval from the key — 20 metres here. Step two: locate the index contours and settle the direction of rise. Step three: read the features from their patterns — uphill Vs for valleys, downhill bulges for spurs, closed rings for hills, crowding for steep ground. Step four: test each identification against the printed heights; any claim the numbers contradict is wrong. Step five: write the answer as evidence — spacing quoted, heights quoted, feature named.

Here is what that sounds like at full strength, on our extract. The river valley runs from the south-east towards the north-west: the contour Vs point south-east into the higher ground, so the flow is north-west. The steepest ground lies on the eastern flank of the hill crowned by spot height 347, where the 260 to 320 contours crowd into their narrowest band — sixty metres of rise over the shortest horizontal distance on the sheet. The spot height fixes the summit at exactly 347 metres, a precision the 340 ring alone could never supply.

Notice the rhythm of those sentences: name the feature, point at the evidence, read the numbers. That is contour literacy, and the next lesson sharpens it into arithmetic when we calculate gradient as an exact ratio. Between now and then, any 1:50 000 sheet — your own district, if you can find it — is worth ten minutes of staring: the flat paper will start bulging into hills under your eyes.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They sweep across hills, ridges, saddles, cliffs and plains, and each rewards the same discipline: feature, spacing, heights.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same map, same 347 hilltop, same river — but this time we put boots on the ground and walk it, because every contour idea is easier on foot.

## Subtopic: Walking the Hill — What the Lines Really Are

Out in the Eastern Cape you can see contour lines without a map: look at any big grassy hill where cattle graze. Cows hate climbing, so they tread level paths around the slope, and after years the hillside carries neat horizontal stripes. Each cattle path is a contour line in the flesh — a route around the hill on which you never go up and never go down.

Now give the hill a full set of those paths, spaced so each one is exactly 20 metres higher than the path below. Seen from directly above — which is all a map is, a view from straight up — the paths become nested rings. The 20 metre gap in height is the contour interval, one number that holds for the whole sheet, so every ring quietly announces its own altitude.

Once the lines are level paths in your head, the rules stop needing memory. Two paths cannot cross, because the crossing point would have to be two different heights at the same instant, and no patch of grass can manage that. A path cannot simply stop either: walk level around a hill for long enough and you must arrive back at your own bootprints — or step off the edge of the map. The rules are not rules; they are just what level walking means.

One last mark to greet like a friend: a small dot with a number, 347 on our hill. That is the spot height — the exact altitude of that single spot, almost always the summit. The rings can only shrug and say the top is somewhere above 340; the dot names it precisely, like the candle on top of a layered cake telling you exactly how tall the cake is.

The questions for this piece are with you now. Keep the cattle paths in view while you answer — nearly every contour puzzle is secretly a question about level walking.

## Subtopic: Squeezed Lines, Sore Legs

Now the sentence that earns more mapwork marks than any other, rebuilt so you own it instead of renting it.

Every move from one contour to its neighbour costs the same entrance fee: 20 metres of climb, fixed by the map. The only variable is how much sideways walking you do while paying it. Think of two ways up to the top tier of a stadium: the long wheelchair ramp that circles the outside, and the concrete stairs behind the stand. Both lift you the same height. The ramp stretches the climb over so much distance you hardly feel it; the stairs collect the whole fee in a few metres, and your calves file a complaint.

Contour spacing is exactly that choice, printed. Wide gaps are the ramp — 20 metres of height spread over a long stroll, gentle ground. Tight gaps are the stairs — the same 20 metres extracted almost at once, steep ground. Asked for the steepest slope, hunt the tightest packing; asked to justify it, give the reason in full: the lines sit closest together there, so the fixed rise happens over the shortest sideways distance.

Push the squeeze to its limit and the map draws a cliff: ground so nearly vertical that 20 metres of height costs almost no sideways distance at all, pressing the lines together until they seem to fuse into one. Learners meeting this for the first time often suspect a smudge or a printing fault. It is neither — it is the map raising its voice.

Two slope shapes deserve names, because your legs would tell them apart instantly. A concave slope hits you with its worst immediately below the crest and then relents towards the bottom — lines tight up top, opening downslope — and from the crest you can watch the whole slope run out to its foot, because the ground falls away and then lays itself flat in front of you. A convex slope lulls you first and punishes you later — lines open up top, tightening below — and the gentle upper swell blocks your view, the way the crust of a round loaf hides the plate it sits on. That hidden ground matters to more than hikers: a farmer planning a fence line and a rescue team searching a mountainside both need to know what the crest cannot see.

Take the questions on this section now, and answer every steepness question twice — once with the ramp and the stairs, then once in map language.

## Subtopic: Finding the River Without Looking for Blue

Final skill, and the one that rescues you when the printing is faint: finding the river with no blue line to lean on.

Go back to the cattle. Picture one particularly stubborn cow who grazes only along her own level, at exactly 300 metres, never a step up or down. She ambles along the hillside until the ground ahead dips into a valley with a stream at the bottom. Straight across is forbidden — the middle of the dip is below her level. So she turns INTO the valley, follows her height along its side, steps across the stream at the one point where the far bank returns to 300 metres, and ambles back out along the opposite side.

Sketch her route from above: it is a V. The tip of the V is the deepest she went up the valley — always towards the higher ground the stream is arriving from. So the V points upstream, and the water slides out through the V's open mouth, downhill. Every contour crossing that valley repeats the same V, one after another, like arrowheads aimed at the source.

Now the impostor. A spur is the reverse landform — a nose of high ground shoved out between two valleys. Our cow rounding a spur must walk around the OUTSIDE of the nose, so her path bows outwards, downhill, away from the summit. Bending lines again, but bulging the opposite way — and never a stream on it, because water runs off a spur's sides instead of along its back.

If you would rather trust arithmetic than animals, the heights will always referee. Read the values beside the bend: the side with the bigger numbers is uphill. Bend aimed at the bigger numbers — valley. Bend aimed away — spur. And a stream crossing contours that read 300, then 280, then 260 is being followed downstream, guaranteed, because water has never once flowed uphill.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now. Answer them with the stubborn cow walking in your head and a finger on the contour numbers, and from today onward flat maps will hand you their hills, valleys and rivers on request.
