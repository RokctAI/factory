**Thandi:** Class questions before we finish! First one: who decides that the contour interval is 20 metres? Would a map ever use a different interval, and why?

**Tutor:** The mapmaker decides, and the choice is a compromise between detail and readability. South Africa's standard 1:50 000 topographic series uses 20 metres because, across most of our terrain, that interval shows the shape of the land without drowning the sheet in ink. But imagine mapping two extremes. On the plains around Bloemhof the land might rise only 30 metres across the entire sheet; with a 20 metre interval you would draw one lonely line and the map would look flat as glass, so a mapmaker wanting detail there would choose 10 metres or even 5. Now map the Drakensberg escarpment with a 5 metre interval: thousands of metres of relief would demand hundreds of lines, packed so densely the sheet turns solid brown and becomes unreadable — so mountainous sheets ask for larger intervals, 50 metres or more. The lesson for you is a habit, not a number: the interval is a decision printed in the key, and reading it is always your first move, because every height, every gradient and every steepness comparison you make afterwards stands on it. Assume 20 and you will usually be right in this country — but check, because usually is not always.

***

**Thandi:** Next: closed rings of contours mean a hill. But a dam or a big sinkhole is also a closed shape. How does the map stop you reading a hole as a hilltop?

**Tutor:** Beautiful question, because the shapes really are identical — nested closed rings — and only the heights tell the truth. On a hill the values climb towards the centre: 300 on the outer ring, 320 inside it, 340 at the heart, with perhaps a spot height crowning it. In a depression — a sinkhole in dolomite country, a pan, the inside of an old quarry — the values sink towards the centre: 300 outside, 280 within, 260 at the middle. So the reflex is always to read two adjacent ring values before announcing a summit. Mapmakers do help you: many maps mark depression contours with small tick marks, little teeth drawn on the downhill side of the line, pointing into the hollow. If you see toothed rings, you are looking into a hole, not up at a peak. But ticks are a courtesy, not a guarantee, and a faded print can lose them — the values cannot fade into their opposites. Numbers first, decoration second: that ordering settles hills against hollows, just as it settles valleys against spurs.

***

**Thandi:** Third one: a friend says her house sits between the 280 and the 300 lines, and she wrote its height as 290. Is that actually correct, or just a safe guess?

**Tutor:** It is a reasonable estimate dressed up as a certainty, and the difference matters. What the map genuinely tells you is a bracket: the house stands above 280 metres and below 300. Writing 290 asserts the exact middle, which is only true if the house sits exactly halfway between the lines AND the slope between them is perfectly even — two conditions she has not checked. The honest technique is interpolation: judge the house's position along the gap between the two contours. If it sits about a quarter of the way from the 280 line, estimate a quarter of the 20 metre interval — 285. Three-quarters of the way across, 295. That is an estimate too, but it is an estimate with reasoning behind it, and stating it properly earns credit: the house lies between the 280 and 300 metre contours, roughly a quarter of the way from the lower line, so its height is approximately 285 metres. And remember the estimate carries an invisible health warning — on a convex or concave slope the ground between two contours is not evenly graded, so interpolation gives an approximation, never a measurement. Only a spot height or a benchmark gives exactness.

**Thandi:** Last one: on a hike, how would GPS on a phone compare with reading contours? Do we still need this skill when the phone just tells us our altitude?

**Tutor:** The phone tells you a number; the contours tell you the future. Your GPS altitude says you stand at 412 metres — useful, but it says nothing about whether the next kilometre of trail climbs a cliff, ambles up a gentle spur or drops into a gorge. A contour map answers all of that before you take a step: crowded lines across your route warn you of the climb, Vs show you where streams cross the path and which way water will be flowing when you need to refill, and a convex slope on the map warns you that the descent you cannot see from the crest will steepen under your feet. Mountain rescue teams still plan on paper contours for exactly this reason — a battery dies, a signal drops, but the printed shape of the land does not. There is also a deeper point for your studies: the phone reports your position in the landscape, but contour literacy lets you reconstruct the landscape itself, anywhere on the sheet, including places you will never walk. One is a reading; the other is understanding. Take both on the mountain — but only one of them into the exam room, where the map and your reasoning are all you have.
