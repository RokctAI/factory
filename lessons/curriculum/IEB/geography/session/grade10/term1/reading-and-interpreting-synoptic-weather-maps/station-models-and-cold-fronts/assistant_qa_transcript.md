**Thandi:** Questions from the class! First: wind speed came in knots — 15 knots, 28 kilometres per hour. Why does weather use sailors' units instead of normal ones?

**Tutor:** Because weather and the sea grew up together. A knot is one nautical mile per hour, and a nautical mile is not an arbitrary distance — it is one minute of latitude, one-sixtieth of a degree, measured along the earth's surface. That definition made it the natural unit for anyone navigating by chart and compass: sail sixty nautical miles due south and you have moved exactly one degree of latitude. Ships kept the world's earliest systematic weather logs, ships' officers became the first marine forecasters, and when meteorology formalised itself it inherited their units whole — knots for wind, and much of the chart language besides. The habit stuck because it remains genuinely convenient: aviation and shipping, the two industries that live or die by wind, still work in knots everywhere on earth, so weather services report in the unit their most critical users need. For your own reading, keep the conversion loose and quick: one knot is close to two kilometres per hour — a bit less, about 1,85 — so 15 knots is around 28 in road units, and 30 knots is a hard blow near 55. Quote knots on the chart, convert roughly when you want the feel of it on your skin.

**Thandi:** Next: the lesson said isobars kink where they cross a front. Why would smooth lines suddenly bend — what is the front actually doing to the pressure?

**Tutor:** The kink is the honest signature of a boundary between two different bodies of air. Remember what an isobar is: a line of equal pressure, and pressure at the surface reflects the weight and temperature of the whole column of air standing above that point. Cold air is dense and heavy; warm air is lighter. A front is the surface where those two columns meet, so as you step across it, the character of the overlying atmosphere changes abruptly — and the pressure pattern cannot stay smooth across an abrupt change. The isobar, obliged to follow its single pressure value, has to change direction where the air mass changes, and it does so in a sharp elbow rather than a gentle curve, with the kink pointing along the front. There is a practical bonus hiding in this: fronts are usually attached to a low, and the kinked isobars form a trough — a V of low pressure extending out from the depression's centre. Forecasters hunt fronts by hunting troughs, even before any triangles are drawn. So when a question asks you to justify a front's position, the kinked isobars are admissible evidence alongside the station reports — the chart bends where the atmosphere breaks.

**Thandi:** Third one: old farmhouses always have a barometer in the passage, and somehow it told people rain was coming before the sky said anything. How does a needle know the future?

**Tutor:** It does not know the future; it weighs the present very precisely — and the present leans. A barometer measures the weight of the air column above the house. Weather systems announce themselves through that weight before they announce themselves in the sky, because pressure changes travel ahead of the cloud and rain. A depression approaching from the south-west is a region of ascending, lighter air: as it draws near, the column above the farmhouse gets progressively lighter, and the needle eases downward — often six, twelve, even twenty-four hours before the first cloud line shows on the horizon. Falling steadily: unsettled weather approaching. Falling fast: something vigorous, arriving soon. Rising: the high building in, fine settled days ahead. That is why the old instruction on the dial says change, rain, stormy on one side and fair, very dry on the other. Your synoptic chart is really a barometer for the whole country at once — the isobars are everyone's needles plotted together. And the exam version of gran's passage barometer is the pressure tendency question: falling means the front is approaching, rising means it has passed. The needle was never magic; it was the atmosphere confessing early.

**Thandi:** Last one: why do the Cape's cold fronts come in winter? Durban gets its rain in summer, so why does one corner of the country run on the opposite timetable?

**Tutor:** Because the whole machinery of the southern hemisphere shifts with the seasons, and the Cape sits right at the edge of its reach. Cold fronts are born in the belt of westerly winds and travelling depressions that circles the globe over the Southern Ocean, far south of Africa. In summer, that belt sits well poleward, and its fronts pass south of the continent — the Cape sits under the subtropical high instead: sinking air, clear skies, the long dry summer that empties its dams. In winter the entire belt migrates north by several degrees of latitude, following the sun's retreat, and suddenly the northern edge of the storm track sweeps across the Cape — front after front, roughly weekly, from May to August. The south-western Cape is the only part of the country that pokes far enough south to be caught by that migration, which is why it alone has winter rain. Durban runs on the opposite engine: its summer rain comes from moist tropical air and convection, systems that strengthen in the hot months and fade in winter, when the interior high and cool dry air take over. One country, two rain calendars — not a quirk, but two different rain machines, each with its own working season. Say which machine and which season, and that comparison question is fully answered.
