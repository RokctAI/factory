Hey, it's Thandi — a synoptic chart looks like somebody spilled circles, feathers and toothy lines all over a map of the country, and you are somehow supposed to find "the weather" in it. Breathe. This topic is friendlier than it looks, because it is not really memory work at all — it is reading, and you already know how to read.

Start with the only physics in the whole chart: sinking air clears, rising air rains. The H is air easing down — squeezed, warmed, clouds dissolved, blue sky. The L is air climbing — expanding, cooling, moisture let go, rain. Two words each: sink, clear; rise, rain. Half the questions on any chart are those four words wearing different costumes.

The little station circles are just reports folded small, and you unfold them in the same order every time: sky first — how many eighths shaded; temperature top left; then the gap — how close the dew point sits to the temperature, because a small gap means the air is nearly full and weather is close; then the wind — the shaft flies in from where the wind was born, and the feathers count tens and fives of knots.

The cold front is a story in three acts you can actually feel: warm sticky north-westerly before, a dark burst of rain during, and cold south-westerly with a climbing barometer after. If you ever get one lonely station and the question "has the front passed?", ask the three instruments — wind vane, thermometer, barometer — and they will always agree on the answer.

Read, don't recall: quote the numbers, name the directions, state whether pressure is rising or falling. That habit alone puts you ahead of most of the room in your final exams.

Sink-clear, rise-rain, unfold the button, three acts. Now go read some weather on the practice questions — you've got this!
