# Part 1 — Expert

Behind every forecast you have ever heard stands one document: the synoptic chart. Grade 10 is the year you stop glancing at it and start reading it for yourself. This session works one complete interpretation from start to finish: a station model reporting 21 degrees Celsius, a dew point of 18 degrees, seven-eighths cloud cover and a north-westerly wind of 15 knots, plotted on a chart carrying a cold front. We build upward from isobars and pressure cells, decode that station symbol by symbol, identify the front and prove the identification, and then describe — in measured observations, never impressions — the weather it drags in behind it.

## Subtopic: What a Synoptic Chart Shows

The word synoptic means seen together, and that is the whole idea: hundreds of weather stations across the region all take their readings at the same instant, and everything is plotted onto one map so the atmosphere's full pattern appears at a glance. A synoptic chart is a photograph of the weather at one moment, drawn in symbols.

Its skeleton is the isobars: smooth curves connecting all points where atmospheric pressure is equal, measured in hectopascals — hPa. If you can read contour lines, you can already read isobars; they are contours of pressure instead of height. Where they close around the letter H, you have a high-pressure cell: values climbing toward the centre. Where they wrap the letter L, a low-pressure cell or depression: values sinking toward the centre.

Pressure earns its place at the centre of the chart because it choreographs the vertical movement of air. Inside a high, air subsides — and subsiding air is compressed, warms, holds tighter to its moisture, and delivers still, clear, settled conditions. Inside a low, air ascends — and ascending air expands, cools, sheds its moisture as cloud, and delivers unsettled, wet conditions. Sinking means clear; rising means rain. Keep that pairing loaded; it answers more synoptic questions than any other fact.

The spacing of the isobars speaks too, exactly as contour spacing does. Isobars crowded together mark a steep pressure gradient, and air pours across a steep gradient fast: strong wind. Isobars far apart, a lazy gradient, light air.

Finally, sprinkled across the chart are small circles with lines and numbers attached — one for every reporting station. Each is a station model: an entire local weather report folded into a symbol the size of a fingernail. Unfolding one is the next skill.

The questions for this section are with you now. They lean on the two core pairings — subsiding air with clear settled weather, ascending air with cloud and rain — and on crowded isobars meaning strong wind.

## Subtopic: Decoding the Station Model

A station model wraps at least five observations around one small circle. Unpack our example piece by piece.

The circle reports cloud cover in OKTAS — eighths of the sky. Shade the circle in proportion to the cloud: our station is shaded seven-eighths, so all but one slice of the sky is clouded over — a heavy, grey ceiling with a single tear of blue. A fully blackened circle is overcast at eight oktas; an empty circle is a cloudless sky.

At the upper left of the circle stands the air temperature: 21 degrees Celsius here — warm for the season.

Beneath it, the dew point: 18 degrees. The dew point is the temperature to which this air must cool before it saturates and moisture starts appearing. Alone it means little; against the air temperature it means everything. Our gap is three degrees — this air is three degrees of cooling away from making cloud and drizzle. That is very humid air. Compare a desert station reporting 28 degrees with a dew point of 6: twenty-two degrees of cooling required before anything condenses — parched air under clear skies. Narrow gap, moist; wide gap, dry. Put that comparison, with its numbers, into every answer you write.

The wind is drawn as a shaft with feathers, and the shaft flies INTO the station circle from the direction the wind BLOWS FROM. Ours arrives from the north-west: a north-westerly wind — winds are always named for their origin, never their destination. The feathers count speed: each full feather is 10 knots, each half feather 5. Our shaft carries one full feather and one half: 15 knots, roughly 28 kilometres per hour — enough to stream a flag.

Now read the whole station aloud in one sentence: warm at 21 degrees, humidity almost at saturation with the dew point at 18, sky seven-eighths clouded, and a north-westerly at 15 knots feeding more moist air in. Every reading points the same direction — this station is standing in the path of arriving weather. The next section finds out what is coming.

This section's questions are in front of you. Name every element with precision — the okta shading, the temperature, the size of the dew point gap, the wind's origin and its feather-count speed — because the precision is where the marks live.

## Subtopic: Finding the Cold Front

On South African charts the recurring intruder — above all in winter — is the cold front: the forward edge of a mass of cold polar air driving in from the south-west, almost always slung beneath a low-pressure depression passing to the south of the continent.

Its chart symbol is a bold line set with solid triangles, like teeth. The triangles are mounted on the side the front is advancing TOWARD — for us, pointing north-east across the country, the direction of attack. Its counterpart the warm front wears semicircles instead of triangles; it seldom crosses South Africa itself, but the symbol must be known.

Never settle for recognising the symbol — prove the line is a front with the evidence around it. Ahead of the line, on the north-eastern side, warm moist air is streaming in and being lifted: precisely the picture our station painted. A north-westerly wind, 21 degrees, a dew point crowding the temperature, cloud thickening toward overcast — that combination is the standard pre-frontal signature, warm air running ahead of the advancing cold.

On the line itself the collision happens. The invading cold air, denser than what it meets, rams underneath the warm moist air and pitches it violently upward. That forced ascent manufactures a wall of cumulonimbus along the front — the dark line of thunderheads that carries the front's hardest rain, its gusts and its lightning.

Check one more fingerprint: where isobars cross a front they kink sharply, bending at the line rather than curving smoothly through it. Symbol, pre-frontal stations, cumulonimbus line, kinked isobars — four confirmations for one identification. And on the far side of the line, everything is different: that transformation is the next section's business.

Take this section's questions now. Each asks you to justify the identification, so answer with evidence from both sides of the line — never with the triangles alone.

## Subtopic: The Weather Behind the Front — and the Exam Method

Describe the weather BEHIND the cold front: the task every synoptic exercise eventually sets. Behind means south-west of the line — ground the front has already crossed, now flooded with the cold air that did the pushing. Report it the way a station would.

Temperature: sharply down. The warm air has been levered off the surface and replaced wholesale; a station reading 21 degrees ahead of the front may read 10 or 11 behind it.

Wind: swung with the front. The pre-frontal north-westerly gives way to a south-westerly off the cold ocean. In a table of hourly observations, that NW-to-SW swing is usually the single clearest marker that the front has gone through.

Pressure: climbing. The front travels with a low; once the line passes, the depression slides away to the east and the next high-pressure cell builds in behind it, so the barograph trace turns upward.

Cloud and precipitation: showery first, clearing later. Cold air crossing a still-warm land surface stays unstable, so the immediate wake is cumulus and scattered showers; as the high settles in, the sky opens into cold, polished blue. Colder, showery, clearing — the post-frontal trio.

A full answer therefore reads: behind the front the temperature has fallen sharply, the wind has swung from north-westerly to south-westerly, pressure is rising as the high advances, and scattered showers are giving way to clearing skies in cold, stable air.

Now fix the general method, the checklist for any synoptic task. One: find and label the pressure cells, and recall sinking-clear, rising-wet. Two: read isobar spacing for wind strength. Three: decode each station model — temperature, dew point gap, oktas, wind origin and speed. Four: locate any front by its symbol, then confirm it with evidence: warm moist north-westerly flow ahead, a cumulonimbus line on the front, cold south-westerly showers behind, kinked isobars throughout. Five: write observations, not impressions — quote values, name directions, state pressure tendencies.

This chart never leaves you. It returns next year carrying Africa's weather systems, and in your final year carrying the great mid-latitude and tropical storms — and it will speak the same symbol language you now read. Between sessions, pull up today's real chart from the weather service, pick one coastal station and one inland, and narrate tomorrow to yourself.

The final questions of this part are with you now. Answer them as a set of observations — temperature with its value, wind with its direction, pressure with its tendency, cloud with its fraction — because that is the shape of a complete answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chart again — same 21 degrees, same north-westerly, same front on the doorstep — but read from the stoep, as weather you can feel coming.

## Subtopic: Highs, Lows and Which Way the Air Is Going

Set the numbers aside for a minute. Air has weight, and pressure is nothing more than how hard the column of air above you is pressing down. Under an H the press is firm; under an L the press has eased.

But the pressing is not what makes the weather — the vertical traffic is. In a high, air is easing slowly downward, and air that descends gets squeezed. You have felt what squeezing does to a gas: pump up a bicycle tyre and the pump barrel grows warm in your hand — compression heats. Warmed air grips its moisture tighter, cloud dissolves, and you get those glassy blue winter days with icy nights. In a low, air is climbing, and climbing air expands. You have felt that too: hold down a deodorant spray and the can turns cold as the gas inside expands — expansion chills. Chilled air cannot keep its water, the water lets go, and you get cloud, then rain. Pump warm, spray cold; sink clear, rise rain.

Those curling lines around the H and the L are isobars — they connect all the places where the air presses equally hard. Read them exactly like contour lines: they draw a landscape of pressure, and wind is air running downhill across that landscape, out of the high country of the H toward the hollow of the L. Squeezed lines mean a steep pressure slope and air sprinting down it — a hard wind. Wide-set lines, a gentle slope, a stroll of a breeze.

Good — the questions for this piece are with you now. For any weather question, start by asking which way the air is moving, up or down, and most answers will already be half-written.

## Subtopic: The Little Circle That Holds a Whole Weather Report

Now for the small circles freckling the chart. Each one is a weather station, and each is a full report folded to the size of a shirt button. Open ours up.

The circle is the sky itself. Slice the sky over the station into eight pieces like a milk tart, and count the cloudy slices. That count gets shaded into the circle. Ours shows seven slices of eight shaded — nearly the whole tart, a low grey lid with one strip of blue left. Eight from eight is overcast; an empty circle is a spotless sky.

The upper-left number is the plain temperature: 21 degrees, a warm day.

The number under it, 18, is the dew point — and this figure is a moisture gauge wearing a disguise. Think of a steel farm gate at sunrise, strung with beads of dew: overnight the metal cooled the air touching it until that air could no longer keep hold of its water. The temperature where letting-go begins is the dew point. So never ask what the dew point is; ask how FAR it sits from the temperature. Ours sit at 21 and 18 — three degrees apart. Three small degrees of cooling and this air starts turning to droplets: thick, damp air with weather loading up. If the pair read 28 and 6 instead, the air would need twenty-two degrees of cooling before anything happened — a bone-dry afternoon with nothing to give.

Last, the little feathered shaft flying into the circle. It flies in from the direction the wind comes from — ours from the north-west, so we call it a north-westerly. Winds take the name of their birthplace, the way we call someone a Karoo farmer because of where he is from, and for the same reason: origin tells you character. A north-westerly over the winter Cape has been over warm, wet ocean — it arrives loaded. The feathers give speed: full feather 10 knots, half feather 5. One full plus one half on our shaft: 15 knots, around 28 kilometres per hour — washing-line weather.

Well done — take this section's questions now, and read every station in the same order: sky first, temperature, then the gap, then the wind and its birthplace.

## Subtopic: Before, During and After the Cold Front

Put the pieces together and our station has been telling one story the whole lesson: warm and sticky, sky closing over, a loaded north-westerly pushing in. Something is coming — and on a winter chart that something is nearly always the cold front.

Picture a long blade of cold air sweeping up from the ocean south-west of the country, moving north-east across the map. Cold air is heavier than warm, so it refuses to blend — it drives in underneath, the way a spatula slides under a pancake, and flips the warm moist air clean off the ground. Air thrown upward like that cools hard and fast, dumps everything it was carrying, and raises a marching wall of thundercloud along the line of the front. On paper that line wears its row of triangles, and the triangles aim exactly where the front is headed.

The day then plays out in three acts you can feel from the stoep. Act one, before: warm for a winter's day, air thick enough to taste, a north-westerly stiffening, cloud shutting out the last blue — our station, precisely. Act two, at the line: the light drops, gusts slam the shutters, and the rain lands all at once, a downpour with attitude rather than a drizzle. Act three, behind: the temperature falls off a cliff, 21 down to about 11; the wind whips around to a raw south-westerly off the cold sea; the barometer bottoms out and starts its climb as the next high moves in; and the solid rain crumbles into passing showers before the sky rinses clean and cold. A day or so later, that same blade of air arrives over the interior plateau as the cold snap that has the whole Free State reaching for jerseys.

And here is your portable test, usable on one lonely station with no front drawn anywhere. Interrogate three instruments. The wind vane: north-westerly says the front is still on its way, south-westerly says it has been through. The thermometer: warm and humid says ahead, suddenly cold says behind. The barometer: falling says approaching, rising says passed. Our station answers north-westerly, warm, and falling — three instruments, one verdict: brace for the front.

Those are the last questions of the lesson, and they are with you now. Answer them with real observations — degrees with their values, winds with their directions, pressure with its tendency — because measured facts are what a full answer is built from.
