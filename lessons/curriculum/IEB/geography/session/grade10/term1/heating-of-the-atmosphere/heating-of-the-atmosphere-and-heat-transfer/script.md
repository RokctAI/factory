# Part 1 — Expert

Every wind that bends the grass, every cloud that builds over the escarpment, every cold snap that empties the beaches — all of it runs on one engine: the earth is heated unevenly. This session opens that engine. We follow the sun's energy in, watch what the atmosphere and the surface do with it, define the four processes that move heat through air, build the greenhouse mechanism in strict order, and close with the five factors that decide why one South African town swelters while another shivers.

## Subtopic: Insolation and the Earth's Heat Budget

INSOLATION is INcoming SOLar radiATION — the short-wave energy streaming from the sun. To master the budget, follow one hundred units of it from the top of the atmosphere downward.

Around 30 of those units are turned away before they do any work: REFLECTED back into space by the bright tops of clouds, by dust and gas molecules scattering the beam, and by pale surfaces below — snowfields, sea ice, dry pans, light Kalahari sand. The fraction a surface reflects is its ALBEDO. A fresh white salt pan turns back most of what hits it; a dark plantation or deep ocean swallows nearly everything.

Roughly 20 units are ABSORBED during the descent — ozone taking ultraviolet high in the stratosphere, water vapour, dust and cloud droplets taking their cut lower down. Notice how modest that figure is: the atmosphere is a poor absorber of SHORT-WAVE radiation, and that weakness is the key to the whole topic.

The remaining 50 units arrive at the surface and are absorbed there, warming soil, rock and water. The warmed surface then returns energy skyward — but changed. Because the earth is vastly cooler than the sun, it radiates in LONG waves, the infrared band called TERRESTRIAL radiation. And toward long-wave energy the atmosphere reverses its character completely: what it waved through on the way in, it seizes on the way out.

Finally, the balance. Taken over a full year and the full globe, energy received equals energy lost — the planet neither roasts nor freezes. But the balance is a planetary total, not a local truth. Between roughly 40 degrees north and 40 degrees south, income permanently exceeds spending: a SURPLUS. Poleward of those latitudes, spending exceeds income: a DEFICIT. Radiation cannot fix that gap; MOVEMENT fixes it. Winds sweep surplus heat toward the poles, and ocean currents — the warm Agulhas sliding down our east coast among them — do the same work in water. The entire global circulation is the planet balancing its books.

The questions for this section are with you now: the thirty-twenty-fifty division explained, albedo defined with a high and a low example, short-wave in against long-wave out, and circulation as the answer to surplus and deficit.

## Subtopic: How Heat Moves — Radiation, Conduction, Convection, Advection

Four processes. Learn each as a definition plus a located example, because that pairing is what a full answer requires.

RADIATION is energy travelling as electromagnetic waves and needing no substance to travel through — the only process that can cross the emptiness between sun and earth. Two streams concern us: short-wave solar radiation inbound, long-wave terrestrial radiation outbound.

CONDUCTION passes heat by direct contact, molecule bumping molecule. Air conducts poorly, so conduction matters only in the thin skin of atmosphere actually touching the ground: midday tar heating the air just above it, or frosty soil on a windless night chilling the air that lies on it.

CONVECTION moves heat VERTICALLY by moving the air itself. Surface-warmed air expands, loses density and rises; cooler, denser air slides underneath to replace it, and the loop becomes a CONVECTION CURRENT. Convection is the lift that carries heat up through the troposphere — and the reason a blazing Free State morning so often ends in a hammering afternoon thunderstorm.

ADVECTION moves heat HORIZONTALLY on moving air. The afternoon sea breeze that rescues Gqeberha from a hot morning is advection of cool marine air inland; the winter berg wind that drops off the plateau and pushes the KwaZulu-Natal south coast into the thirties is advection of hot, dry interior air toward the sea.

Now bank the consequence that unlocks half the course: the atmosphere is heated FROM BELOW. Insolation slips mostly through the air, pays its energy to the ground, and the ground then heats the air — by conduction in the lowest layer, by convection above it, by long-wave radiation throughout. That is why temperature falls as altitude rises in the troposphere: climbing means leaving the heater behind. Questions that look like they are about mountains, lapse rates or snow are very often this one fact in disguise.

Take this section's questions now: four definitions, four placed examples, and the heated-from-below principle stated as the reason temperature falls with height.

## Subtopic: The Greenhouse Effect and Enhanced Warming

The mechanism must be told in sequence — five steps, no shortcuts. One: short-wave solar radiation crosses the atmosphere with little absorption. Two: the earth's surface absorbs it and warms. Three: the warm surface re-emits the energy as LONG-WAVE terrestrial radiation. Four: greenhouse gases absorb that long-wave radiation. Five: they re-radiate it in every direction, and the share directed back down — COUNTER-RADIATION — warms the lower atmosphere above what it would otherwise be.

Name the gases: water vapour first, the most abundant; then carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone and the CFCs. And be clear that this mechanism is NATURAL and indispensable. Strip it away and the global average surface temperature drops from about 15 degrees Celsius to about minus 18 — thirty-three degrees standing between us and a frozen planet.

The trouble begins where humanity accelerates it: the ENHANCED greenhouse effect, gases added faster than forests, soils and oceans can absorb them. Know the sources as a list you can deploy: coal, oil and gas burned for electricity, factories and transport; deforestation, which both destroys a carbon sink and releases the carbon the trees were holding; agriculture, with cattle herds and flooded paddies leaking methane; rotting landfill waste; and industrial chemistry such as cement kilns. South Africa's electricity system, built overwhelmingly on coal, makes our own emissions unusually carbon-heavy for the size of our economy.

Then the consequences for Africa, continent-specific and concrete. Average temperatures climbing faster here than the global mean. Rainfall turning erratic — deeper droughts across the south and west, of the kind that recently pushed Nelson Mandela Bay's reservoirs toward empty. Falling yields of maize and other staples, striking hardest where households grow their own food. Rising seas leaning on ports, estuaries and low-built coastal settlements. Malaria-carrying mosquitoes advancing into highland areas that were once too cool for them. And running beneath all of it, thin public budgets that make adaptation — new dams, resistant seed, sea walls — hardest to afford exactly where it is needed most.

The distinction that carries the marks, and the questions now with you test it directly: the natural greenhouse effect keeps us alive; the enhanced greenhouse effect is the human-made intensification of it. Same physics, different verdict.

## Subtopic: The Five Factors That Set Temperature

Why does Musina simmer in the Limpopo valley while Molteno logs the coldest readings in the country? Five factors, each with a mechanism you must be able to state.

LATITUDE. Near the equator the sun stands close to overhead, so a beam of energy lands concentrated on a small patch and crosses a thin slice of atmosphere. Move poleward and the same beam arrives at a slant: it smears across a wider area and threads a longer path through absorbing, scattering air. Weaker concentration plus longer path equals cooler surface — the master gradient from equator to poles.

ALTITUDE. Air cools with height at about 6,5 degrees Celsius per 1 000 metres, because the heat source is the ground below and because thin, dry high-altitude air holds little to trap warmth. Compare Bloemfontein, near 1 400 metres on the plateau, with Durban at the shoreline on almost the same latitude: the arithmetic gives 1 400 metres at 6,5 degrees per 1 000, roughly a 9 degree penalty from altitude alone, and the two cities' winters feel it.

DISTANCE FROM THE SEA — CONTINENTALITY. Water warms and cools slowly: radiation penetrates deep into it, waves and currents stir the warmth downward, evaporation bleeds heat away, and each degree of change simply costs more energy than it does on land. Coastal air is therefore cushioned. Gqeberha rides through the year on a small ANNUAL TEMPERATURE RANGE, while far-inland De Aar swings from baking summers to frost-white winter mornings. Annual range is the phrase to attach to this factor — it is precisely what nearness to the sea controls.

OCEAN CURRENTS. Two rivers of seawater bracket the country. The warm AGULHAS runs south along the east coast, heating and moistening the air above it — East London's mild, humid climate and the summer downpours of the eastern seaboard ride on it. The cold BENGUELA creeps north along the west coast, chilling the air into stability — fog banks drifting over Lambert's Bay, meagre rain, and, carried to its conclusion further north, the Namib: a desert lying beside an ocean. One country, two currents, two opposite coasts.

CLOUD COVER AND ASPECT close the list. Cloud works both ends of the day: it blunts the daytime maximum by reflecting insolation and props up the night-time minimum by intercepting outgoing long-wave, so overcast regions live in a narrow daily range while the clear skies of the Karoo deliver scorched afternoons and frozen dawns. Aspect is the southern-hemisphere mirror rule: NORTH-facing slopes catch the direct sun, so they run warmer and drier than south-facing slopes — the reason vineyard rows, orchard blocks and even mountain vegetation change with the direction a slope faces.

The final questions of this part are with you now: five factors, five mechanisms, and the Agulhas and Benguela each locked to its own coast.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same engine, told again with a water delivery, a day at the beach, and two towns at the far ends of the thermometer.

## Subtopic: One Hundred Buckets of Sunlight

Imagine the sun sends the earth a daily delivery of one hundred buckets of energy. Walk the delivery route.

Thirty buckets never arrive. They are tipped straight back at the gate — bounced off the shining tops of clouds, off dust hanging in the air, off bright ground like snow, sea ice and pale pans. That bounce-back fraction has a name: ALBEDO. A whitewashed farmhouse roof throws sunlight back and stays cooler; a black car bonnet keeps nearly every bucket and burns your hand by ten in the morning — high albedo, low albedo.

Twenty buckets leak along the way — sipped by ozone high up, and by water vapour, dust and cloud lower down. Only a fifth. Sunshine mostly ignores the air it passes through.

Fifty buckets land on the ground. The ground drinks them, and the ground warms. That is the delivery: thirty bounced, twenty leaked, fifty delivered.

Now the twist that makes sense of everything afterwards. The ground does not hoard its fifty buckets; it pours them back toward the sky — but in a different form. Energy arrived as short-wave sunshine. It leaves the cool earth as LONG-WAVE heat. And the atmosphere, so careless with short-wave on the way in, catches long-wave hungrily on the way out. In one door untouched, stopped at the other.

Last stop on the route: over the whole planet in a whole year, deliveries in equal losses out — the books balance. But the tropics always receive more than they lose, and the poles always lose more than they receive. Something must cart the extra from the fat account to the thin one, and that something is winds and ocean currents. Every gale is a delivery truck balancing the planet's accounts. Your questions on the buckets are up right now — the thirty, the twenty, the fifty, and why the energy changes form on the way out.

## Subtopic: Hot Sand, Cool Breeze

Go stand on a beach at one o'clock in the afternoon, barefoot. The whole physics of atmospheric heating is under your feet.

First lesson: the sand is scalding, but the air around your shoulders is pleasant. If the sun heated air directly, the air would burn and the sand would not. It is the other way around because sunshine passes through air and lands on surfaces — the SAND is the heater, and air only warms by touching it and rising off it. The atmosphere is heated from below. Every mountain-top snowfall and every cold summit on earth is that single beach fact, scaled up.

Now collect all four heat movers without leaving your towel. The warmth of the sun on your face is RADIATION — arriving across a hundred and fifty million kilometres of nothing, no carrier required. The burn on your bare soles is CONDUCTION — heat crossing by direct touch, sand to skin, and it is exactly how the ground heats the first thin layer of air lying on it. Look down the beach and watch the air shimmer and wobble above the hot sand — that is CONVECTION, warmed air rising in wriggling columns while cooler air slides in underneath; the same columns, grown huge over the interior, stack up the afternoon thunderclouds. And at about three o'clock the famous relief arrives: the sea breeze, cool marine air sliding sideways onto the land. Heat moved horizontally by wind is ADVECTION — the same process that, reversed in winter, sends a berg wind pouring hot plateau air down onto this very coast.

Four beach sensations: sun on your face, burnt soles, the shimmer, the breeze. Radiation, conduction, convection, advection. And the questions on the beach are with you now — the four movers matched to their sensations, plus the big one: the heater is the sand below you, never the sky above.

## Subtopic: Why Musina Swelters and Molteno Freezes

Musina, on the Limpopo, wears summer like a furnace; Molteno, on the Stormberg plateau, has recorded the coldest official temperature in South Africa. Same country. Five reasons, none of them chance.

ONE — the angle of attack. Hold a hosepipe straight down over one spot and you soak a small circle hard; tilt it and the same water scatters thin across the yard. Sunlight works identically. Musina, closer to the tropics, takes its sunshine nearly straight down, concentrated. Molteno, further poleward, takes it on the slant, spread and weakened. That is latitude.

TWO — height. Every 1 000 metres of climb costs about six and a half degrees, because the ground below is the heater and you are walking away from it. Molteno sits high on its plateau, above 1 700 metres, so it starts every winter night with a huge head start toward frost. Bloemfontein against Durban tells the same story with numbers: near 1 400 metres of altitude, nearly the same latitude, about nine degrees surrendered to height alone.

THREE — the sea. Water is the slowest thing in the landscape to change temperature: it warms late, cools late, and drags the coast along with its calm. Gqeberha's thermometer barely strays through the year. De Aar, marooned in the dry middle of the country with no ocean to steady it, leaps to the high thirties in January and drops below freezing on July nights. The further from the sea, the wilder the annual swing.

FOUR — the two currents. Down the east coast flows the warm Agulhas, a heater in the water: East London gets mild air, humidity and proper summer rain from it. Up the west coast crawls the cold Benguela, a chiller: Lambert's Bay gets cool foggy mornings and very little rain, and follow that cold water north and the land beside it turns into the Namib. Warm current, generous coast; cold current, thirsty one.

FIVE — the sky and the slope. Cloud is a two-way shield: by day it blocks incoming sun and keeps the maximum down, by night it traps outgoing heat and keeps the minimum up. Molteno's winter misery needs the opposite: cloudless Karoo-edge skies that let every scrap of daytime heat escape after dark. And on any hill in this hemisphere, the NORTH-facing slope is the sunny one — warmer, drier, first choice for the orchard — while the south face stays cool and shaded.

Angle, height, sea, currents, sky. Five dials, and every town's thermometer is just where its dials are set. The final questions of the lesson are coming to you now — the five factors, and the Agulhas and the Benguela each kept firmly on its own side of the country.
