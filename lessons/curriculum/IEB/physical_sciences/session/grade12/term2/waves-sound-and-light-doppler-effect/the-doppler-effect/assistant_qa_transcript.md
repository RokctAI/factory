**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First: the equation treats a moving source and a moving listener differently — one changes the denominator, the other the numerator. If all that matters is relative motion, why are they not interchangeable?

**Tutor:** Because sound has a home — the air — and the two motions do different things inside that home. A moving SOURCE physically re-spaces the wavefronts in the medium: each crest is emitted from a new position, so the wavelength in the air ahead of it genuinely shrinks. The waves themselves are different once launched, and that geometric squeezing produces the denominator. A moving LISTENER changes nothing in the air at all — the crests keep their spacing — but the listener sweeps through them at a different rate, harvesting more or fewer per second, and that harvesting rate is the numerator. Two mechanisms, two homes in the formula. And the asymmetry is measurable, not cosmetic: a source approaching at 30 metres per second and a listener approaching at 30 metres per second give slightly DIFFERENT detected frequencies, because 340 over 310 is not the same number as 370 over 340. For light in a vacuum there is no medium, so that asymmetry disappears and only relative motion counts — but for sound, the air is a genuine referee. For your calculations, the practical takeaway is just discipline: ask WHO is moving relative to the air, put the listener's speed upstairs and the source's speed downstairs, and never let them swap houses.

***

**Mandy:** Next: as the fire engine drives straight past me, does the pitch slide down smoothly the whole time, or does it jump from the high note to the low note at the instant it passes?

**Tutor:** In the idealised drive-straight-through-you version, it is a jump: approaching gives one steady high note, receding gives one steady low note, and the switch happens at the moment of passing. Notice something learners find surprising — while the engine approaches at constant speed, the pitch does not keep rising; it holds constant at its shifted value, because the shift depends on the SPEED of approach, not on the distance. Constant speed, constant shift. But in the real world the engine never passes exactly through you; it passes a few metres to the side. What matters in the equation is the component of the velocity along the line between source and listener. Far up the road, the engine comes almost directly at you: full component, full shift. As it draws level, its motion is momentarily sideways-on to you: the along-the-line component shrinks to zero, and for that instant you hear the siren's TRUE frequency. Then the component grows again pointing away, and the note settles onto its low receding value. So the realistic experience is a high plateau, a rapid slide through the true note as it sweeps past, and a low plateau — the closer it misses you, the faster the slide. The calculations you meet keep things fully toward or fully away, but writing one sentence about the velocity component when a question asks why the pitch changes smoothly shows real understanding.

**Mandy:** On the reverse problems: you insisted we write "the train is approaching" before doing any algebra. If the algebra gives the speed anyway, why does that sentence matter so much?

**Tutor:** Because the sentence is the algebra's steering wheel — skip it and the same equation happily drives you to a wrong answer with full confidence. Here is the mechanism. The formula carries a plus-or-minus in the denominator, and nothing inside the mathematics chooses between them; the choice comes from the physics, and the physics comes from comparing the two frequencies. Hearing 800 when the source emits 750 means the detected value is higher, so the train approaches, so the denominator must shrink: 340 minus v s. Choose the plus sign instead and the equation still solves beautifully — it just describes a train that does not exist, and worse, it returns a NEGATIVE speed whose meaning you would have to untangle under time pressure. The comparison sentence eliminates that fork before it opens. It also arms your final check: with 340 minus v s equal to 318,75, the speed is 21,25 metres per second — positive and reasonable for a train, and consistent with the approach you declared. Direction stated, signs chosen, answer audited: three links of one chain. There is also a marking reality — reverse problems reward the reasoning, not just the number, and the direction statement IS the reasoning made visible. One written sentence converts a coin-flip into a certainty, and in this topic that sentence is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

**Mandy:** Last one: with red shift, how do astronomers know the galaxy's light was not simply redder to begin with? Maybe it just contains different elements from our lab samples.

**Tutor:** That is exactly the objection a good scientist should raise, and the answer is the most elegant part of the whole story: astronomers do not measure colour, they measure PATTERN. Each element's spectral lines form a set of frequencies with fixed internal spacing ratios — hydrogen's lines relate to each other in proportions set by the physics of the atom itself, the same here as anywhere in the universe. A fingerprint is not identified by where it sits on the page but by the pattern of its ridges. When a galaxy's spectrum is examined, the astronomer finds a family of lines whose spacing ratios match hydrogen's exactly — unmistakably hydrogen — but with EVERY line in the family sitting at a lower frequency than hydrogen produces in the laboratory. A genuinely different element would show a different pattern, not the same pattern shifted; and a galaxy that merely glowed redder would brighten some frequencies without moving the absorption lines at all. A uniform slide of an intact, recognisable pattern has only one explanation: the source is receding, and the fractional size of the slide gives its speed. Then the clincher that turns one measurement into cosmology: the same intact hydrogen pattern appears in thousands of galaxies, always slid toward the red, and slid FARTHER for fainter, more distant galaxies. Same fingerprint, bigger shift, greater distance — that systematic trend is what forces the conclusion of an expanding universe, rather than any assumption about what colour a galaxy ought to be.
