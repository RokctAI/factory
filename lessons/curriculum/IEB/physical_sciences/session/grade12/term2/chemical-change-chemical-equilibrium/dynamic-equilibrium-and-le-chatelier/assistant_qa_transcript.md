**Mandy:** Class questions before we close! First: if the forward and reverse rates are equal at equilibrium, why don't the concentrations of reactants and products end up equal too?

**Tutor:** Because rates and amounts answer two different questions, and equilibrium only promises to match the rates. Picture the shop again with one twist: suppose it is a tiny shop that people leave quickly. The crowd inside might hold steady at just fifteen — but as long as thirty enter and thirty leave every minute, that fifteen is a perfect equilibrium. A cavernous shop next door might hold two thousand with the same thirty-in, thirty-out traffic. Same balanced rates, wildly different steady crowds. Chemically, where the balance point sits depends on the reaction's own character — how eager the forward conversion is compared with the reverse. A reaction whose forward step is intrinsically fast and whose reverse is sluggish piles up products before the reverse rate can climb high enough to match; the rates finally equalise with the vessel already dominated by product, and K c comes out large. The opposite temperament leaves the vessel mostly reactants with a small K c. The mathematics agrees: the reverse rate depends on the product concentrations, so a lazy reverse reaction needs a LOT of product around before it can run as fast as the forward one. So never argue from equal rates to equal amounts — the balance of traffic says nothing about the size of the crowd. Equal rates is the definition; the concentrations settle wherever the chemistry, summarised by K c, dictates.

**Mandy:** Next: Le Chatelier says the system opposes a disturbance. Why does it only PARTLY undo the disturbance instead of pushing all the way back to the original concentrations?

**Tutor:** Because the machinery doing the opposing is self-weakening — every step of the correction blunts the correction. Follow the oxygen injection closely. The moment the extra oxygen lands, the forward rate leaps, because forward collisions now happen more often. The system starts consuming the excess. But watch what each conversion does: it removes the very reactant surplus that boosted the forward rate, so the forward rate starts sliding back down; and it manufactures product, which feeds the reverse rate, so the reverse rate climbs. The gap between the two rates — the engine of the correction — narrows with every passing second, and the correction stops the instant the rates meet again. They meet BEFORE the oxygen concentration has fallen to its old value, because the system now carries more product than before, and that extra product keeps the reverse rate propped up. Full restoration is actually impossible on the system's own steam: to return every concentration to its original value, the mixture would have to satisfy the K c ratio with MORE total matter in the vessel than before, and the arithmetic simply does not allow the same ratio with the extra moles still in there. What the system can and does restore exactly is the RATIO — the value of K c. So the honest summary is: concentrations are partly corrected, the equilibrium constant is fully restored. The see-saw settles tilted, but the referee's score is untouched.

**Mandy:** On the graphs: you said halving the volume makes every concentration jump instantly, before any shift happens. Why does the jump come first and the chemistry only afterwards?

**Tutor:** Because concentration is a fraction, and the two things in the fraction change on different clocks. Concentration is moles divided by volume. When the piston slams the volume down to half, the moles have had no time to react — molecule counts are untouched in that instant — but the denominator under every one of them has halved. Same moles, half the volume: every gaseous concentration doubles in the same tick of the clock. That is pure arithmetic, no chemistry involved, which is why EVERY line on the graph jumps together — the fingerprint that distinguishes a volume change from a concentration change, where only the injected species jumps. Then the chemistry wakes up, on its slower clock. The doubled concentrations no longer satisfy K c — with unequal gas moles on the two sides, doubling everything inflates one side of the ratio more than the other, precisely because the coefficients sit as exponents. Three doublings on the left of our sulfur trioxide reaction against two on the right leaves the ratio out of balance, so a net shift begins toward the side with fewer gas moles, and the lines curve away from their jumped values toward new shelves. So read such a graph as two acts: act one, an instantaneous, purely arithmetical jump in every gas line; act two, the gradual, curved Le Chatelier response that restores the ratio. Jump is physics of the container; curve is chemistry of the contents.

**Mandy:** Last one: industry runs the sulfur trioxide converter at a moderate temperature. If cooling increases the yield, why not run it as cold as possible?

**Tutor:** Because yield and speed pull in opposite directions on the temperature axis, and a factory needs both. Le Chatelier is on the cold team: the forward reaction is exothermic, so cooling favours it, and the EVENTUAL equilibrium at a chilly temperature holds a beautiful fraction of sulfur trioxide. But rate theory is on the hot team: cold particles rarely clear the activation energy, so at a low temperature that beautiful equilibrium is approached at a crawl — the plant would make an excellent yield at a rate no business could survive. Percentages do not pay salaries; tonnes per day do. So engineers hunt the compromise: a temperature warm enough for the reaction to run briskly, cool enough that the equilibrium position is still respectable — and that optimum sits in the middle, at a few hundred degrees, not at either extreme. Then they claw back what the compromise surrendered, using the other levers. The vanadium pentoxide catalyst attacks the speed problem without touching the yield, letting the plant run cooler than it otherwise could for the same pace. Raised pressure pushes the three-moles-to-two equilibrium toward product. And continuously drawing off the sulfur trioxide keeps the equilibrium permanently frustrated, dragging conversion far beyond what a sealed vessel would settle for. Every industrial condition is a negotiated treaty between Le Chatelier and collision theory — and explaining that treaty, lever by lever, is exactly what an extended question on this topic wants from you.
