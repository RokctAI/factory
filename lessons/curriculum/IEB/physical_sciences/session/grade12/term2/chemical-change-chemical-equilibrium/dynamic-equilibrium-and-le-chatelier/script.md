# Part 1 — Expert

Some reactions never finish. Seal sulfur dioxide and oxygen together to make sulfur trioxide and the mixture settles into a permanent stand-off: sulfur trioxide forming and decomposing at exactly equal rates, indefinitely. Chemical equilibrium is the chemistry of that stand-off — how it establishes itself, how Le Chatelier's principle predicts its response to any disturbance, and how the equilibrium constant K c puts a number on it. This topic rewards precise definitions above everything else, so we build them first and build them exactly.

## Subtopic: Open, Closed and Reversible

Three definitions lay the foundation, and each must be word-perfect.

An OPEN system continuously exchanges both matter and energy with its surroundings — a fizzing beaker on the bench, bubbling its gas away into the room. A CLOSED system exchanges energy but NOT matter — a sealed flask: heat crosses the glass freely, but nothing material gets in or out. Equilibrium can only establish itself in a closed system, because if a product escapes, the reverse reaction is starved of its ingredients and the balance can never form.

A REVERSIBLE reaction is one in which the products can be converted back into the reactants — written with double arrows pointing both ways. Sulfur dioxide and oxygen combine to form sulfur trioxide, while sulfur trioxide simultaneously decomposes back into sulfur dioxide and oxygen.

Now the centrepiece. CHEMICAL EQUILIBRIUM is the state of a reversible reaction, in a closed system, in which the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, so that the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant. The equilibrium is called DYNAMIC because both reactions keep running at full speed — molecules convert in both directions every instant — while the totals stand perfectly still.

Two precision points that separate strong answers from weak ones. First: constant concentrations do NOT mean equal concentrations. An equilibrium mixture may hold far more reactant than product, or the reverse; the requirement is only that nothing is changing. Second: from outside, the system looks utterly inert — colour, pressure, concentration all frozen — yet at particle level the two-way traffic never pauses. Writing "the reaction has stopped" is precisely the error this topic is designed to expose.

How does the stand-off arise? At the moment of mixing, reactant concentrations are at their peak, so the forward rate is at its peak; no product exists yet, so the reverse rate is zero. As product accumulates, the reverse rate climbs while the forward rate sinks — until the two rates meet. From that instant, every forward conversion is matched by a reverse one.

This section's questions are with you now: three definitions word for word, and the word dynamic defended with the particle picture.

## Subtopic: Le Chatelier's Principle

The principle: when the equilibrium in a closed system is disturbed, the system re-establishes a new equilibrium by favouring the reaction that opposes the disturbance. Learn that statement, then march it through the standard disturbances using the industrial reaction for sulfuric acid production: two sulfur dioxide plus one oxygen in equilibrium with two sulfur trioxide, forward reaction exothermic.

CONCENTRATION. Inject extra oxygen and the system opposes the addition by consuming oxygen: the forward reaction is favoured, and the equilibrium shifts toward sulfur trioxide. Draw sulfur trioxide off as it forms, and the system opposes the removal by producing more: forward again. This is why industrial plants continuously remove product — an equilibrium kept permanently frustrated keeps on producing.

TEMPERATURE. The forward reaction is exothermic, so treat the equation as carrying "plus heat" on the product side. Raise the temperature and the system opposes the added heat by favouring the direction that ABSORBS heat — the endothermic reverse reaction — so sulfur trioxide decomposes and the yield falls. Cool the vessel and the exothermic forward direction is favoured: more product. Temperature is the one disturbance that also changes the value of K c itself.

PRESSURE, for gases only. Count the gas moles on each side: three on the left — two sulfur dioxide plus one oxygen — and two on the right. Increase the pressure by shrinking the volume, and the system opposes the crowding by favouring the side with FEWER gas moles: forward, toward sulfur trioxide. Drop the pressure and the many-mole side is favoured instead. When both sides carry equal gas moles, pressure changes shift nothing at all.

The CATALYST, deliberately last: a catalyst accelerates the forward and reverse reactions EQUALLY, so it shifts no equilibrium and improves no yield — it only brings the system to equilibrium sooner. Industrially that is still treasure: the sulfur trioxide converter runs over a vanadium pentoxide catalyst, at a moderate temperature chosen as a compromise between yield and speed, and at raised pressure — every one of those choices is a Le Chatelier argument, and explaining them is a classic extended question.

Questions on the principle are in front of you now — name the disturbance, name the opposing direction, state the shift.

## Subtopic: Reading Equilibrium Graphs

Equilibrium questions live on concentration-time graphs, and the graphs are read with three eyes: flats, jumps, and curves.

FLAT sections mean equilibrium: every species holding a constant concentration. The lines can run level for long stretches at very different heights — level, not equal, is the signature.

A vertical JUMP in a single line is an imposed disturbance at that instant. Someone injected extra oxygen: its line leaps upward instantly, while every other line still sits at its old value in that first moment. A sudden removal appears as an instant drop. Only the disturbed species jumps.

Then the CURVES record the response. After the oxygen injection, the system sets about consuming it: oxygen's line curves back downward — never all the way to its old level — sulfur dioxide's line falls alongside as it is consumed too, and sulfur trioxide's line climbs. All three settle onto new flat shelves: a NEW equilibrium at new concentrations. That is Le Chatelier drawn in ink — the disturbance partially opposed, never completely undone.

Volume and pressure disturbances sign their names differently: change the volume and EVERY gaseous concentration jumps in the same instant — halving the volume doubles them all at once — and then the curves relax as the shift plays out. A temperature change leaves no instant jump at all, only curves bending smoothly away from their old levels toward new ones, because heating changes no concentration directly.

Rate-time graphs tell the identical stories in a second dialect: at equilibrium the forward and reverse rate lines lie on top of one another. Add a reactant and the forward rate spikes then decays while the reverse rate climbs, until the two meet again at a new shared level. A catalyst sends both rate lines jumping upward together, meeting sooner, with the final concentrations untouched.

The standard identification question — what happened at time t — is answered by the jump pattern alone: one line jumping is a concentration change; all gas lines jumping together is volume or pressure; no jump but bending curves is temperature; both rate lines leaping as one is a catalyst.

This section's questions arrive now — jumps for disturbances, curves for responses, flats for equilibrium.

## Subtopic: The Equilibrium Constant K c

Equilibrium carries a number. For a reaction written a A plus b B in equilibrium with c C plus d D, the equilibrium constant K c equals the concentration of C raised to the power c, times the concentration of D raised to the power d, divided by the concentration of A raised to the power a, times the concentration of B raised to the power b. Products over reactants, every concentration in moles per cubic decimetre, every one raised to its balancing coefficient. Two standing exclusions: pure solids and pure liquids never enter the expression — their concentrations cannot change — so only gases and dissolved species are written.

Work the standard calculation. Hydrogen plus iodine in equilibrium with two hydrogen iodide, in a sealed 4 cubic decimetre vessel. Initially 2,0 moles of hydrogen and 2,0 moles of iodine are injected; at equilibrium the vessel contains 3,0 moles of hydrogen iodide. Build the table. Forming 3,0 moles of hydrogen iodide consumed 1,5 moles of hydrogen and 1,5 moles of iodine — the two-from-one-plus-one ratio read straight off the equation. Remaining: 0,5 moles of each reactant. Now convert to concentrations by dividing every amount by the 4 cubic decimetres: hydrogen 0,125, iodine 0,125, hydrogen iodide 0,75 moles per cubic decimetre. The expression: K c equals the hydrogen iodide concentration squared, divided by the product of the hydrogen and iodine concentrations. Substitute: 0,75 squared over 0,125 times 0,125 — 0,5625 over 0,015625. K c equals 36.

Interpretation earns its own credit. A LARGE K c — far above one — says the equilibrium sits well toward the products: the forward reaction has run nearly to completion. A SMALL K c — far below one — says the vessel still holds mostly reactants. Our 36 declares a mixture strongly favouring hydrogen iodide.

The closing fact, tested relentlessly: K c is constant at a given TEMPERATURE, and temperature is the ONLY thing that changes it. Add reactant, squeeze the vessel, stir in catalyst — concentrations rearrange, the ratio recovers, and K c stands unmoved. Raise the temperature of an exothermic reaction and K c genuinely falls; of an endothermic one, and it genuinely rises.

The final expert questions are with you now — the moles table, concentrations not moles, coefficients as exponents, and temperature alone moves K c.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chemistry now inside a shopping centre: a busy shop, a stubborn see-saw, and a referee holding a scoreboard.

## Subtopic: The Shop Where the Crowd Never Changes

Picture the most popular shop in the centre on a Saturday morning. Shoppers pour in at the entrance; shoppers pour out. Suppose that, every minute, exactly thirty walk in and exactly thirty walk out. Count the crowd inside: one hundred and fifty. Count again a quarter of an hour later: one hundred and fifty. Has the shop gone quiet? The opposite — the doorway is churning non-stop. The COUNT is constant while the TRAFFIC never rests.

That is dynamic equilibrium, in one scene. The forward reaction is the door in; the reverse reaction is the door out; and when those two rates match, the concentrations — the crowd inside — hold rock-steady even though individual molecules are converting in both directions every second. Anyone announcing "the reaction has stopped" is standing outside counting heads through the glass, deceived by a steady total.

Two refinements complete the picture. First, a steady crowd need not be a big crowd — the shop can hold constant at fifteen shoppers or at fifteen hundred; likewise an equilibrium may sit far on the reactant side or far on the product side. Constant never means equal. Second, the shop needs working doors — but the building needs walls. If shoppers could drift out through a missing back wall and never return, no in-out balance could ever form. That missing wall is an open system; equilibrium insists on a closed one, with nothing material leaking away.

And how does the balance first appear? At opening time the empty shop fills furiously — everyone streaming in, nobody inside yet to leave. As the crowd grows, more shoppers finish and exit. In-rate easing, out-rate rising — until they draw level. From then on the crowd is frozen while the doors churn.

Your first questions are with you now — steady count, busy doors, closed walls.

## Subtopic: The Stubborn See-Saw

Le Chatelier's principle has a grand name, but it describes something familiar: push a stubborn thing, and it leans back against your push.

The equilibrium is a balanced see-saw. Every disturbance is a passenger landing on one end — and the system responds by working to lift that end again. It never fully wins; the see-saw settles at a NEW balance point, still somewhat tilted, the push partly absorbed. Partly undone, never completely undone — that is the signature of every equilibrium response.

Run the pushes. Pile extra reactant onto the left seat: the system leans right, consuming the addition and making more product. This is why factories keep drawing product off the right seat — each removal tips the see-saw rightward again, and the permanently frustrated equilibrium keeps delivering.

Heat rides the see-saw too, and one trick simplifies every temperature question: write heat into the equation as though it were a chemical. Forward reaction exothermic? Then heat sits on the PRODUCT side. Warm the vessel and you have dropped a passenger onto the product end — the system leans back toward the reactants, and the yield falls. Chill it, lifting heat off the product side, and the system leans forward, making more product, heat included. One mental move, and no case list to memorise.

Pressure squeezes gases. Crowd the vessel and the system escapes toward the side with FEWER gas molecules — less crowding, squeeze partly relieved. Count the moles printed on each side of the arrows; the smaller count is where a squeeze always sends you.

And the catalyst never climbs onto the see-saw at all. It oils the pivot: both directions accelerate equally, balance arrives sooner, and the final tilt is exactly what it would have been anyway. Sooner to equilibrium, never more product — a distinction worth rehearsing until it is reflex.

Questions on the see-saw are coming to you now — find the push, lean against it, settle part-way back.

## Subtopic: The Referee with the Scoreboard

After all the pushing and leaning, chemistry appoints a referee with a single number on the scoreboard: K c. The number answers one question only — when everything settles, who is ahead, products or reactants?

The score is a fraction: products on top, reactants underneath, each concentration raised to the power of its number in the balanced equation. A HUGE score — in the thousands — means the top towers over the bottom: the vessel is nearly all product; the forward reaction all but finished the job. A TINY score — a small decimal — means the reaction barely left the start: mostly reactants remain. Our hydrogen iodide example scored 36: solidly product-side.

The calculation is bookkeeping in four lines, and the layout earns as much as the answer. Line one: what went IN — initial moles. Line two: the CHANGE — how much reacted, locked to the ratio of the balanced equation; if 3,0 moles of hydrogen iodide formed, then 1,5 moles of each reactant were spent, because the equation makes two from one-plus-one. Line three: what REMAINS at equilibrium. Line four: divide every amount by the vessel's volume, because the referee accepts only concentrations — moles per cubic decimetre — never raw moles. Skipping that final division is the most expensive shortcut in the topic.

Then the referee's single loyalty: temperature. Add reactants, squeeze the vessel, sprinkle in catalyst — the concentrations scatter and regroup, but they always regroup to the SAME score. Only a temperature change rewrites the number itself: heat an exothermic reaction and its score genuinely drops — products lose ground at the new temperature; heat an endothermic one and the score climbs.

The whole topic in one breath: the shop's crowd holds steady while its doors churn, the see-saw leans against every push and settles part-way, and the referee keeps one score — loyal to temperature alone.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — in, change, remain, divide — then read the score for who is winning.
