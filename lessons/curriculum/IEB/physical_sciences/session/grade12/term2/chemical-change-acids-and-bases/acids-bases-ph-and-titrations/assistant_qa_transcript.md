**Mandy:** Class questions before we wrap up! First: if strong acids ionise completely, does that mean a strong acid is always more dangerous than a weak one? Vinegar seems harmless but I have seen warning labels on concentrated ethanoic acid.

**Tutor:** Your warning label is the whole lesson in one sticker, because hazard rides on BOTH axes at once — strength and concentration — plus what the substance itself can do. Take the two extremes. A very dilute solution of hydrochloric acid, a strong acid, can be so weakly concentrated that your own stomach outdoes it daily — strength complete, amount trivial, hazard low. Concentrated ethanoic acid, a weak acid, is the reverse: only a fraction of its molecules are ionised at any instant, but there are so MANY molecules per litre that it burns skin and its vapour stings the eyes — the label is earned. And there is a subtlety that catches even good chemists: a weak acid is a reservoir. As the few ionised protons react and are consumed, Le Chatelier tilts the ionisation equilibrium forward, and the intact molecules release more protons — the acid keeps restocking the very ions you removed. So a concentrated weak acid can deliver, over the course of a reaction, nearly as many protons as its strong cousin, just not all upfront. The measured pH tells you the hydronium on duty right now; the concentration tells you the size of the army in reserve. Judge hazard by asking three questions — how willing, how much, and what else can this molecule do — and never by the word weak alone.

***

**Mandy:** Next: you called water an ampholyte, giving or taking protons depending on its partner. How does water decide which role to play?

**Tutor:** Water does not decide — the partner does, and the deciding rule is a beautiful one: in any encounter, the stronger acid present gets to be the acid, and everyone else must accept. Think of proton donation as a contest of eagerness. Nitric acid is a far more eager proton donor than water, so when the two meet, water's only available role is acceptor: it takes the proton and becomes hydronium. Ammonia is a more eager proton ACCEPTOR than water, so in that beaker the roles flip: water is the best donor in the room, hands over a proton, and becomes hydroxide. Same molecule, opposite jobs, chosen entirely by the company. This is also why the ampholyte ions make such good short questions. The hydrogen carbonate ion carries a donatable hydrogen AND has room to take another proton, so against an acid it accepts and against a base it donates. If you ever need to predict the role, line the species up by their K a values — the strength table does the thinking: bigger K a donates, and the partner accepts. One caution to finish: do not confuse an ampholyte with a neutral substance. Pure water plays both roles simultaneously with itself — one molecule donating to another in the auto-ionisation — which is exactly where K w and the pH 7 of purity come from. Neutrality is not the absence of acid-base activity; it is a perfectly balanced two-way trade.

**Mandy:** On the calculations: for sulfuric acid you doubled the concentration before taking the log, and the pH changed from 2,3 to 2. That seems a small difference for so much fuss. Why does it matter?

**Tutor:** Because the log scale is a compressor, and small differences in pH hide large differences in chemistry. A pH gap of 0,3 looks cosmetic, but undo the logarithm and it is a factor of two in hydronium concentration — the solution genuinely carries TWICE the active acid, reacts roughly twice as fast with a metal in the early stages, and needs twice the base to neutralise. Report 2,3 for a solution that is truly pH 2 and every one of those downstream predictions is wrong by a hundred percent. The habit to build is this: always translate hydronium arithmetic BEFORE the logarithm, never after. Doubling concentration is a subtraction of about 0,3 on the pH ladder — the logarithm turns multiplication into subtraction — and you cannot apply that correction as an afterthought reliably. The diprotic step itself is pure Lowry-Brønsted bookkeeping: one mole of sulfuric acid stocks the solution with two moles of donatable protons, so the hydronium concentration is twice the acid concentration, and THEN the log converts it. The same discipline runs in reverse for bases through K w: get the hydroxide concentration right first, divide ten to the minus fourteen by it, and only then take the negative log. In every pH question the logarithm should be the final act — one keystroke at the end of clean mole arithmetic. Let the log compress your correct answer, never your mistake.

**Mandy:** Last one: in the titration, why must the indicator change colour at the salt's pH rather than just at 7? Neutralisation means neutral, surely?

**Tutor:** That instinct — neutralisation means pH 7 — is precisely the trap the topic sets, and hydrolysis is the spring. Follow the flask to the end. At equivalence, all the original acid and base have converted into salt and water; what is left in solution is really just the salt's ions swimming in water. If both parents were strong — sodium chloride from hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide — those ions are spectators, too feeble to trade protons with water, and the flask truly sits at 7. But let one parent be weak and its child ion inherits the family's appetite. Sodium ethanoate's ethanoate ion is the conjugate base of a weak acid, which makes it a respectable proton taker: it lifts protons off water molecules, leaving hydroxide behind, and the equivalence mixture settles slightly BASIC — above 7 before a single extra drop of base is added. Ammonium chloride works the mirror image: ammonium is the conjugate acid of a weak base, donates protons to water, and equivalence sits acidic. Now the indicator's job description: it must shout at the moment the mole ratio is satisfied — the equivalence point — wherever that pH happens to be. Choose an indicator that flips at 7 for a weak-acid titration and it will stay silent at true equivalence, flipping only after you have overshot with excess base; your measured volume, and every number computed from it, comes out wrong. So the rule is not "flip at neutral" but "flip at equivalence": read the salt, let the stronger parent name the pH, and station the dye exactly there.
