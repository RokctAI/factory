# Part 1 — Expert

Acids and bases close this term's chemistry and open its most calculation-rich territory: pH, the ionisation constants K a and K b, and the titration — the one experiment every final-year chemist must be able to run on paper and at the bench. This session lays the definitions, separates strong from concentrated with real precision, builds the pH toolkit step by step, and finishes with titration arithmetic, indicator choice and the hydrolysis of salts.

## Subtopic: Definitions and Conjugate Pairs

Two generations of definition, both needed. ARRHENIUS: an acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions — strictly, hydronium ions — when it dissolves in water, and a base is a substance that produces hydroxide ions in water. Useful, but chained to water. The broader definition to prefer is LOWRY-BRØNSTED: an acid is a PROTON DONOR, and a base is a PROTON ACCEPTOR — a proton here meaning a hydrogen ion.

The genius of Lowry-Brønsted is that it reduces every acid-base reaction to the hand-over of one proton, and that hand-over creates pairs. When nitric acid donates a proton to water, the water acts as a base and becomes the hydronium ion; the nitrate ion left behind is the CONJUGATE BASE of the acid. A conjugate acid-base pair is two species differing by exactly one proton — nitric acid and nitrate; ammonium and ammonia; hydronium and water. In any reaction, find the two pairs by interrogating each species: did it lose a proton here, or gain one?

Water earns a sentence of its own: it is an AMPHOLYTE — a substance able to act as either an acid or a base — donating a proton to ammonia in one flask and accepting one from nitric acid in the next. The hydrogen carbonate ion and the hydrogen sulfate ion are ampholytes as well, and naming them is a favourite short question.

Acids also differ in how many protons they carry to market: monoprotic acids such as hydrochloric and nitric acid donate one proton per molecule; a diprotic acid such as sulfuric acid can donate two. That factor of two will ambush careless calculations for the rest of the topic.

This section's questions are with you now: both definitions, pairs separated by exactly one proton, and the ampholytes named.

## Subtopic: Strong, Weak, Concentrated, Dilute — and K a

Now the distinction that marks out the strongest candidates: STRENGTH is not CONCENTRATION.

A STRONG acid ionises COMPLETELY in water: every last molecule of nitric acid hands over its proton; none survives intact. The strong acids to know: hydrochloric acid, nitric acid, sulfuric acid. A WEAK acid ionises only PARTIALLY: in a bottle of ethanoic acid — the acid in vinegar — only a small fraction of the molecules have donated protons at any moment, the rest sitting whole, in equilibrium with their own ions. The weak acids to know: ethanoic acid, oxalic acid, phosphoric acid and carbonic acid. Among the bases: sodium hydroxide and potassium hydroxide are strong, ionising completely; ammonia is weak, seizing protons in only a fraction of its meetings with water.

CONCENTRATED and DILUTE measure something entirely separate: how much acid or base is dissolved per volume of solution — moles per cubic decimetre — with no reference to what fraction ionises. All four combinations therefore exist: a concentrated weak acid, like the ethanoic acid in double-strength vinegar; a dilute strong acid, like the trace of hydrochloric acid in a pool-testing kit; and the remaining two besides.

Because a weak acid's ionisation is an equilibrium, it owns an equilibrium constant: K a, the acid ionisation constant — with K b for bases. A LARGE K a says the equilibrium sits far toward ionisation: strong. A SMALL K a — ethanoic acid's is near ten to the minus five — says barely ionised: weak. K a is strength written as a number, and comparing two K a values ranks two acids instantly.

How would you TELL strong from weak at equal concentration, at the bench? Three measurable symptoms, all downstream of the ion count. The strong acid shows the LOWER pH — more hydronium in solution. It conducts electricity BETTER — more charge carriers per litre. And it reacts FASTER with magnesium or a carbonate — more effective collisions involving hydronium. Same label concentration, three different behaviours in the glass.

Questions on this section are in front of you now — strength is the fraction ionised, concentration is the amount per volume, and K a is strength quantified.

## Subtopic: pH, K w and the Calculations

The pH scale folds an enormous range of hydronium concentrations into one small number line: pH equals the negative logarithm of the hydronium ion concentration. Every pH step is a factor of TEN in concentration: pH 2 carries ten times the hydronium of pH 3. Low pH, acidic; 7, neutral at 25 degrees Celsius; high, alkaline.

Beneath the scale runs water's own equilibrium: water auto-ionises, a minute fraction of its molecules trading protons to form hydronium and hydroxide. The ionic product K w equals the hydronium concentration multiplied by the hydroxide concentration, and it holds at 1,0 times ten to the minus fourteen at 25 degrees. Pure water carries each ion at ten to the minus seven — hence pH 7. K w works like a see-saw: push the hydronium up and the hydroxide must sink so the product stays fixed.

Calculation one: a strong monoprotic acid. Nitric acid at 0,001 moles per cubic decimetre ionises completely, so the hydronium concentration is 0,001 — ten to the minus three. pH: negative log of ten to the minus three, which is 3.

Calculation two: the diprotic trap. Sulfuric acid at 0,005 moles per cubic decimetre delivers TWO protons per molecule, so the hydronium concentration is 2 times 0,005, which is 0,01 — and the pH is 2, not 2,3. That factor of two, applied BEFORE the logarithm, is the difference between the full answer and a lost step.

Calculation three: a strong base, routed through K w. Potassium hydroxide at 0,01 moles per cubic decimetre gives a hydroxide concentration of ten to the minus two. Hydronium: K w divided by that — ten to the minus fourteen over ten to the minus two, which is ten to the minus twelve. pH: 12. Firmly alkaline, as expected.

And the weak-acid caveat, conceptual but frequently tested: ethanoic acid at 0,001 moles per cubic decimetre does NOT have pH 3. Only a fraction of its molecules ionise, so the hydronium concentration falls well short of 0,001 and the pH lands noticeably higher — around 3,9 — closer to neutral than a strong acid of the identical concentration. Concentration alone never fixes pH; strength always stands between them.

This section's questions arrive now — count the protons, respect the powers of ten, and send every base through K w.

## Subtopic: Titrations, Indicators and the pH of Salts

A TITRATION finds the unknown concentration of an acid or base by reacting it exactly against a standard solution — one whose concentration is precisely known. The equivalence point is the moment acid and base have reacted in the exact mole ratio of the balanced equation, and the calculation runs four lines.

Worked example: 30 cubic centimetres of potassium hydroxide solution is exactly neutralised by 15 cubic centimetres of nitric acid of concentration 0,2 moles per cubic decimetre. The equation: one mole of acid per mole of base. Moles of acid used: concentration times volume in cubic decimetres — 0,2 times 0,015, which is 0,003 moles. The one-to-one ratio makes the base 0,003 moles as well. Its concentration: 0,003 divided by 0,030 cubic decimetres, which is 0,1 moles per cubic decimetre. Formula, substitution, ratio, answer — and the volumes converted to cubic decimetres before anything else touches a calculator. Had the acid been sulfuric, the ratio line would carry the factor two: one mole of that acid neutralises two moles of base.

The INDICATOR announces the endpoint with a colour change, and choosing the right one is a genuine skill resting on hydrolysis — because the salt left behind at equivalence sets the pH there. A salt of a strong acid and a strong base — potassium nitrate, sodium chloride — leaves the solution NEUTRAL at equivalence: bromothymol blue, changing near 7, is the natural fit. A salt of a WEAK acid and a strong base — sodium ethanoate — hydrolyses BASIC: the ethanoate ion accepts protons from water, freeing hydroxide, so equivalence sits above 7 and the indicator must flip there: phenolphthalein, colourless to pink across exactly that range. A salt of a strong acid and a WEAK base — ammonium chloride — hydrolyses ACIDIC: the ammonium ion donates protons to water, equivalence lands below 7, and methyl orange, changing in that region, is the choice. The whole rule folds into one sentence: the STRONG parent decides the salt's pH, and the indicator must change colour at the equivalence pH.

Auto-ionisation, hydrolysis, ionisation constants — notice that this entire topic is the equilibrium chemistry of the last fortnight wearing acid-base clothing, and the hardest questions are built exactly on that seam.

The final expert questions are with you now — four-line titrations, volumes in cubic decimetres, and the strong parent naming the salt's pH.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chemistry again with a parcel, a bottle of cordial, and a ladder of tens.

## Subtopic: Givers and Takers

Strip the vocabulary away and acid-base chemistry is pass-the-parcel with one tiny parcel: the proton — a hydrogen atom stripped of its electron, the smallest package chemistry posts.

An acid is a GIVER: a molecule holding a proton it is prepared to hand over. A base is a TAKER: a molecule with room to receive one. Every acid-base reaction, without exception, is a single hand-off — giver passes proton to taker, transaction complete. Nitric acid meets water; the acid gives, the water takes, and the after-picture shows the nitrate ion — the acid minus its parcel — and the hydronium ion — water clutching an extra one.

That after-picture also tames the word CONJUGATE, which intimidates far more than it should. Once a giver has handed over its proton, the leftover species could, in principle, take one back — a giver minus its parcel is a taker in waiting. So every acid trails a shadow partner, its conjugate base: the same species, one proton lighter. Ammonium and ammonia. Hydronium and water. To spot a pair, count hydrogens: a difference of exactly one, everything else identical, and the pair is confirmed.

And some players cover both positions. Water takes a proton from nitric acid, yet gives one to ammonia. A substance that gives or takes depending on the company it keeps is an ampholyte — chemistry's utility player — and water is the most celebrated name on that team sheet.

Your first questions are with you now — find the giver, find the taker, and match every player to its one-proton-lighter shadow.

## Subtopic: Strong Is Not Concentrated

Here is the sentence that upgrades your chemistry all year: strong is about WILLINGNESS, concentrated is about AMOUNT. Everyday speech treats them as twins; chemistry runs them on separate axes, and questions probe the difference constantly.

Think cordial. How much syrup you pour into the glass is CONCENTRATION — half the glass, concentrated; a splash, dilute. Now imagine two different syrups: one dissolves totally and instantly the moment it meets water; the other barely mixes, most of it sulking at the bottom however hard you stir. That eagerness to break up is STRENGTH. A strong acid is the instant syrup: in water, EVERY molecule splits and hands its proton over. A weak acid is the sulker: a small fraction splits; the rest floats intact, holding on.

All four combinations exist, and you can point to each. Concentrated strong: plenty of acid, all of it split — battery acid. Dilute strong: a trace, but fully split — pool-test drops. Concentrated weak: plenty, barely split — double-strength kitchen vinegar. Dilute weak: a splash, barely split — vinegar shaken into salad dressing.

Chemists write the willingness as a number: K a. Large K a, eager splitter, strong. Minute K a, reluctant, weak. And the bench betrays the strong acid even at equal concentration through three symptoms: a lower pH, brighter conductivity — more ions on charge-carrying duty — and faster fizzing against magnesium. The same amount on the label; entirely different behaviour in the glass.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — one axis for amount, one for willingness, and never let them blur.

## Subtopic: The Ladder of Tens and the Exact Balance

The pH scale is a ladder whose every rung is a factor of TEN. Step down one rung — pH 5 to pH 4 — and the solution holds ten times the hydronium. Three rungs, a thousand times. That is why pH changes that sound trivial are chemically violent, and why aquatic life collapses when a river slips two rungs: its water turned a hundred times more acidic.

The middle rung, 7, is where pure water stands — not because water is empty of ions, but because it holds two exactly equal, exactly tiny crowds: hydronium and hydroxide, each at one part in ten million. Acids swell the hydronium crowd and drop the pH; bases do the opposite. And the two crowds ride a see-saw: their product is a fixed number, so whenever one crowd grows, the other is forced to shrink.

Now the exact balance: the titration, the most careful pouring in chemistry. Its question: how concentrated is this mystery bottle? Its method: add the known solution drop by drop until givers and takers have matched exactly — every proton delivered to a receiver, none left over. That perfect-match instant is the equivalence point, and from the two volumes and the single known concentration, the unknown emerges in four lines of arithmetic. The only visible sign of the match is a flash of colour: the indicator, a dye that flips shade at a chosen pH, stationed to shout at precisely the moment of balance.

One elegant twist closes the lesson: the point of balance is not always at pH 7. The salt created by the neutralisation can itself lean acidic or basic — the child takes after the STRONGER parent. Vinegar neutralised by caustic soda leaves a faintly basic solution, so the indicator must flip above 7; ammonia neutralised by hydrochloric acid leaves faintly acidic, flipping below. Match the indicator to the salt's lean, and the shout arrives at the true moment of balance.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — ten times per rung, four lines per titration, and the salt takes after its stronger parent.
