Hi, Mandy here — acids and bases can feel like a topic with too many words and too many logs, so let me shrink it to three moves you already know how to make.

Move one: find the parcel. Every acid-base reaction is one proton changing hands — acid gives, base takes. Conjugate pairs are just the before-and-after photos: same species, one proton apart. Count the hydrogens and the pair identifies itself. Water plays both positions, so expect a question about that.

Move two: keep the two axes apart. Strong is willingness — the fraction that ionises, written as K a. Concentrated is amount — moles per cubic decimetre. Vinegar can be concentrated and weak; pool-test acid is dilute and strong. If a question compares two acids, first ask which axis it is testing.

Move three: pH is clean arithmetic with the log saved for last. Strong monoprotic acid: concentration is the hydronium, take the negative log. Diprotic sulfuric acid: DOUBLE the concentration first, then log. Any base: find the hydroxide, divide ten to the minus fourteen by it, then log. And a weak acid always lands closer to 7 than its concentration suggests, because only a fraction ionises.

Titrations are four lines — moles of the known, apply the ratio, divide by the unknown's volume in cubic decimetres, answer. Convert the volumes before anything else. Then let the salt's stronger parent choose the indicator: basic salt, phenolphthalein; acidic salt, methyl orange.

Parcel, axes, log-last, four lines. That is the entire topic. The practice questions are waiting — go show them who is strong AND concentrated. You've got this!
