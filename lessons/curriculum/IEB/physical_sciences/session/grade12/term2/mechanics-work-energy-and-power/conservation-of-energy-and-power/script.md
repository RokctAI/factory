# Part 1 — Expert

The work-energy theorem watched a single account — kinetic energy. This session opens the whole ledger. Mechanical energy, the sum of kinetic and gravitational potential energy, stays constant in a frictionless world; in the real world, friction and other non-conservative forces move energy across the boundary, and the equation W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p measures the traffic exactly. That equation, together with power — the rate of doing work — anchors some of the most dependable question types in the whole mechanics course.

## Subtopic: Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces

A CONSERVATIVE force is a force for which the work done in moving an object between two points is independent of the path taken. The gravitational force is the standard example: hoist a toolbox straight up two floors, or carry it up the zigzag staircase to the same landing, and gravity's work is identical — negative m g h either way, fixed by the height difference alone. Every joule gravity charges on the way up, it refunds in full on the way down; nothing ever leaks.

A NON-CONSERVATIVE force is one whose work DOES depend on the path. Friction is the defining case: slide a box across the workshop and back again, and friction bills you for every metre of the round trip — the longer the route, the bigger the charge, and none of it is ever refunded. Applied forces, air resistance and tension are all treated as non-conservative as well.

The distinction earns its keep because conservative forces allow a potential energy to be defined. Gravitational potential energy, E p equals m g h, is the stored account — the work gravity stands ready to repay if the object comes down. Mechanical energy is the two accounts added together: E mech equals E k plus E p.

Now the PRINCIPLE OF CONSERVATION OF MECHANICAL ENERGY: in the absence of air resistance or any external force, the total mechanical energy of an object remains constant. When only conservative forces act — no friction, no air resistance — the sum of kinetic and potential energy at one point equals the sum at every other point.

One application shows its muscle. A stone is dropped from a balcony 4,9 metres up, air resistance ignored. At the top: all potential, no kinetic. At the ground: all kinetic, no potential. Equate them: m g h equals a half m v squared. The mass cancels — every mass falls alike — and v squared equals 2 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 4,9, which is 96,04. The stone arrives at 9,8 metres per second, and not one equation of motion was needed.

This section's questions are with you now: both definitions word for word, the principle with its conditions attached, and the mass cancelled wherever it may be.

## Subtopic: The Equation W n c Equals Delta E k Plus Delta E p

Once friction or any other non-conservative force enters, mechanical energy stops being constant — but it changes by a precisely known amount. The bookkeeping identity: the work done by non-conservative forces equals the change in kinetic energy plus the change in potential energy. In symbols, W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p. When W n c is zero the equation folds back into conservation of mechanical energy; when it is not, W n c IS the leak, stated in joules.

The method is four steps, always the same. One: fix a reference level for height, normally the lowest point in the problem. Two: write down the kinetic and potential energy at the start and at the end — four numbers. Three: form the two changes, each one final minus initial. Four: add them; the sum is the non-conservative work, and its sign tells the story — negative means energy drained away, almost always through friction; positive means energy was pumped in by an engine or a person.

Work the standard scene. A 40 kilogram child starts from rest at the top of a slide 2,5 metres high and reaches the bottom at 5 metres per second. Reference level: the bottom. Initial kinetic energy: zero. Initial potential energy: 40 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 2,5, which is 980 joules. Final kinetic energy: a half multiplied by 40 multiplied by 25, which is 500 joules. Final potential energy: zero. Delta E k: 500 minus zero — positive 500. Delta E p: zero minus 980 — negative 980. The sum: negative 480 joules. That is W n c: friction collected 480 joules from the ride and delivered them as heat to the slide's surface — the reason a busy slide is warm to the touch by mid-afternoon.

Run the cross-check that earns full confidence: frictionless, the child would arrive at v squared equals 2 g h — the square root of 49, exactly 7 metres per second. Arriving at only 5 confirms that energy left the system, and a slower-than-frictionless arrival must pair with a negative W n c. The sign and the story have to agree.

Questions on this section are in front of you now — four numbers, two changes, one signed sum, and a sentence saying where the energy went.

## Subtopic: Working the Equation on Slopes and Rough Ground

The same identity solves for any single unknown — an arrival speed, a launch height, or the friction force itself. One example, taken all the way to the force.

A 3 kilogram box is released from rest at the top of a rough ramp 5 metres long, whose top stands 2 metres above the ground. It reaches the bottom at 4 metres per second. Reference level: the ground. Initial energies: kinetic zero; potential 3 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 2, which is 58,8 joules. Final energies: kinetic a half multiplied by 3 multiplied by 16, which is 24 joules; potential zero. Delta E k: positive 24. Delta E p: negative 58,8. W n c: 24 minus 58,8, which is negative 34,8 joules.

Now turn the work into a force. Friction acted along the whole 5 metres of surface, opposing the motion: W friction equals negative f multiplied by 5, so negative 34,8 equals negative 5 f, and f equals 6,96 newtons. Watch which distance does the dividing: the 5 metres ALONG the ramp, because friction charges by path length — never the 2 metres of height, which belongs to gravity alone.

The same machinery climbs. If a winch now hauls that box back up the ramp at constant speed, delta E k is zero, delta E p is positive 58,8, and friction still takes its 34,8 joules along the surface — so the winch must deliver W n c equals 58,8 plus 34,8, which is 93,6 joules of positive non-conservative work. Going down, friction fought gravity; going up, everything fights the winch.

One more setting with no ramp at all — a projectile in real air. A cricket ball thrown straight up returns to the hand moving SLOWER than it left. Air resistance is non-conservative: its work is negative on the way up AND on the way down, and the kinetic energy missing at the catch equals the total work done against the air. The identical equation runs the audit, no surface required.

This section's questions arrive now — path length for friction, height for gravity, and always check the sign against the story.

## Subtopic: Power — the Rate of Doing Work

Two winches lift identical water tanks onto the same roof. The work is the same — but the winch that finishes in half the time is the more powerful machine. POWER is the rate at which work is done, equivalently the rate at which energy is transferred: P equals W divided by delta t. The unit is the watt — one joule per second. A 3 000 watt kettle element pushes 3 000 joules into the water every second it runs.

First calculation, one you can do on any staircase with a stopwatch: a 55 kilogram learner runs up stairs 3,2 metres high in 4 seconds. Work against gravity: 55 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 3,2, which is 1 724,8 joules. Power: 1 724,8 divided by 4 — 431,2 watts. Climb the same stairs in 2 seconds and the work is untouched but the power doubles. Power measures speed of delivery, never size of the job.

Second, the pump. A borehole pump raises 150 kilograms of water per minute from a depth of 25 metres at steady speed. Work per minute: 150 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 25, which is 36 750 joules. Minimum power: 36 750 divided by 60 seconds — 612,5 watts. The word "minimum" is doing real work in that sentence: a genuine pump also spends energy on pipe friction and on the kinetic energy of the moving water, so the true motor must exceed 612,5 watts, never merely equal it.

Third, the constant-velocity form. For an object holding a steady speed against a resisting force, the average power is P equals F multiplied by v — force times speed. A bus holding 20 metres per second on a level road against total resistive forces of 2 500 newtons is delivering 2 500 multiplied by 20, which is 50 000 watts — 50 kilowatts spent entirely on standing still against friction and air. The formula is just the definitions stacked: work is force times distance, and distance per second is speed.

The final expert questions are with you now — joules first, then divide by seconds, and F times v for anything cruising.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole energy story once more, told in money: an honest banker, a toll collector with a receipt book, and the difference between rich and fast.

## Subtopic: The Energy Toll Road

Picture a girl on a playground swing, pulled far back and released. At the highest point she is rich in height-money — potential energy. Sweeping through the bottom, the height-money has become speed-money — kinetic energy. Up the far side, speed buys height again. In a perfect world with no air and no rubbing at the pivot, she would swing forever, the two accounts trading one fixed total back and forth, cent for cent. That fixed total is mechanical energy, and the perfect swing is exactly what the principle of conservation of mechanical energy describes.

Why does gravity permit the perfect trade? Because gravity is an honest banker. Whatever it charges you to climb, it repays to the last cent when you descend — and it never asks which route you took, ladder or ramp or staircase. Same height, same charge, same refund. Forces with the honest-banker property are the conservative ones.

Friction operates a different business model. Friction is a toll collector who charges per metre of road, pockets the payment, and burns it on the spot — literally, as heat. Take the long way around and pay more; return along the same road and pay AGAIN. Refunds: never. That is the meaning of non-conservative.

So the real girl on the real swing rises a little less each pass. Her mechanical energy shrinks swing by swing, and the missing amount is precisely the toll collector's takings at the pivot and in the air. The universe lost nothing — the bearing is a shade warmer — but her two accounts will never see that money again.

One habit before any numbers: interrogate every force in the problem with a single question — banker or toll collector? Gravity: banker. Friction, air resistance, engines, muscles: toll collectors, or donors when they pay in.

Your first questions are with you now — perfect swing first, then open the gate for the toll collector.

## Subtopic: Friction's Receipt

Here is the satisfying part: even when energy leaks, the books still balance — you just have to read the receipt. The receipt is W n c equals delta E k plus delta E p, and using it feels like checking your wallet after a trip to the shops.

The child on the slide: she set off 2,5 metres up with 980 joules of height-money and no speed-money. At the bottom she holds 500 joules of speed-money and no height. Count the wallet: 980 went in, 500 came out. The receipt reads minus 480 joules — friction's toll, now warming the metal of the slide. That number is not an error and not a mystery; it is a measurement of exactly what the toll collector took.

Read the sign the way you read a bank statement. Negative W n c: money left your accounts — friction or air resistance was on duty. Positive W n c: somebody paid money IN — an engine, a winch, a person pushing. Zero: the perfect swing, two accounts merely trading places.

The receipt can even convict without a site inspection. If a frictionless slide would deliver the child at 7 metres per second and she actually arrives at 5, energy left the system — the two speeds alone prove the toll collector was working. And when a ball thrown upward returns to the hand slower than it left, that lost speed is the air's receipt: negative work charged on the climb AND on the fall, both legs of the journey billed.

One warning worth underlining twice: friction charges along the PATH — every metre of ramp surface — while gravity charges only for HEIGHT. Converting friction's toll into a force means dividing by the slope length; computing height-money means using the vertical rise. Swapping those two distances is this topic's most common error, and after today it will not be yours.

Questions on the receipt are coming to you now — wallet in, wallet out, and the sign tells the story.

## Subtopic: Power — Rich Against Fast

Two learners carry identical boxes of books up the same staircase. Same boxes, same height — exactly the same work, joule for joule. But Lerato takes the stairs at a run in 4 seconds while Karabo ambles up in 40. Who did more work? Neither: the bills are identical. Who produced more POWER? Lerato — ten times more — because power is not how much you do, it is how FAST you do it. Work is the size of the bill; power is how quickly you settle it.

The unit makes this concrete. One watt is one joule delivered per second. A phone charger trickles along at roughly 20 watts. A kettle gulps around 2 000 to 3 000 — which is why switching the kettle on while the geyser is heating makes the meter spin: together they demand thousands of joules every single second. When the national grid reports megawatts, it is counting joules per second for the whole country at once.

Two formulas, and the question chooses between them. Given work and time: power is work divided by time. The staircase test is exactly this — your weight, times the height of the stairs, divided by your stopwatch time — and it rates you as a machine in watts. Most learners come out at a few hundred: call it a fraction of a kettle.

Given a constant speed against a resisting force: power is force times speed. A bus cruising steadily on the freeway is not accelerating, so every watt it produces goes to fighting friction and air — force times speed, minute after minute. That is also why cruising faster costs disproportionately more fuel: the resisting force AND the speed climb together, and their product climbs faster than either.

And the pump's "minimum power" now reads itself: the motor must at least pay gravity's bill on schedule — mass per second, times 9,8, times the depth. Real pumps pay a surcharge for pipe friction. Minimum means gravity's share alone, delivered on time.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — same bill, different speed of settlement, and a watt is just a joule in a hurry.
