**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we wrap! First: you called tension and applied forces non-conservative, but a rope does not burn energy as heat the way friction does. Why do they end up in the same box?

**Tutor:** Because the box is defined by bookkeeping, not by villainy. The test for a conservative force is strict: the work it does between two points must depend only on the endpoints, never on the route, so that a potential energy can be defined for it. Gravity passes — height in, height out, full refund. An applied force fails immediately: the work a person or a rope does depends entirely on how far, along which path, and how hard they happened to pull, and there is no formula of position that could store it as a potential energy. The rope may not turn energy into heat the way friction does, but its work is still path-dependent and still comes from outside the kinetic-plus-potential ledger, so it must be entered on the W n c side of the equation. Think of the identity as a customs post: E k and E p are the domestic accounts, and W n c records EVERYTHING crossing the border — friction smuggling energy out as heat, engines and ropes importing it from muscles and fuel. Some border traffic is loss and some is investment, but all of it is traffic. That is why a winch's positive work and friction's negative work sit in the same term with opposite signs. Non-conservative does not mean wasteful; it means outside the two accounts the principle protects.

**Mandy:** Next: you always chose the lowest point as the reference level for height. Would my answer for the friction work change if I put the reference somewhere else, like the top of the ramp?

**Tutor:** Your answer would not change by a single joule, and knowing why gives you real freedom in the exam. Potential energy itself is not measurable in an absolute sense — only DIFFERENCES in potential energy carry physics. Watch the ramp with your unusual choice. Reference at the top: the box starts at height zero, so initial E p is zero, and it ends 2 metres BELOW the reference, at height negative 2, giving final E p of 3 times 9,8 times negative 2 — negative 58,8 joules. Delta E p is negative 58,8 minus zero: negative 58,8 joules, exactly what the ground-level choice produced, because the box still dropped the same 2 metres. The reference shifts both entries in the ledger by the same constant, and the subtraction wipes the constant out. So W n c, the friction force, the arrival speed — every physical answer survives any choice of zero. What changes is only convenience: choosing the lowest point keeps every height positive and every E p non-negative, which means fewer minus signs to drop under pressure. So the strategy is: choose the zero that minimises your sign traffic, state it clearly in your first line, and then trust it completely. A reference level is like the ground floor button in a lift — the building does not care which floor you call zero, as long as you stop changing your mind between floors.

**Mandy:** The ball that comes back slower puzzles me. Gravity's work cancels over the round trip, up against down. Air resistance acts up and down too — why does its work not cancel the same way?

**Tutor:** Because the two forces choose their directions by different rules, and that one difference decides everything. Gravity points down always — a fixed direction, blind to the motion. On the way up it opposes the motion and does negative work; on the way down it assists and does positive work of exactly the same size, because the distance is the same and its direction never budged. Round trip: zero, refund complete, which is precisely the conservative property. Air resistance obeys the opposite rule: it points against the VELOCITY, whichever way the velocity points. Going up, the ball moves up, so the drag points down — negative work. Coming down, the ball moves down, so the drag flips to point up — negative work AGAIN. It is like a headwind that swings around to stay in your face whichever direction you cycle. Two negative legs cannot cancel; they accumulate, and the total is the kinetic energy missing when the ball lands in your hand. That is also why the ball spends less time rising than falling in real air, and why its landing speed is genuinely lower than its launch speed. The compact way to store it: conservative forces are fixed by POSITION, so round trips refund; friction-like forces are set by MOTION, so round trips double-charge. One sentence, and the whole puzzle unlocks.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the borehole pump you kept saying minimum power. In a calculation, how would I know whether to give the minimum answer or something bigger?

**Tutor:** Read what the question hands you, because the word minimum is a confession about missing information. The calculation we can always do is gravity's bill: mass raised per second, times 9,8, times the depth — for our pump, 150 kilograms per minute from 25 metres gives 36 750 joules each minute, which is 612,5 watts. But a real pump spends more: pipe friction charges per metre of pipe, the water arrives at the top still moving so it keeps some kinetic energy, and the motor itself wastes energy as heat in its windings. If the question gives you none of those numbers, you cannot compute them — so the honest answer is the gravitational share alone, labelled as a minimum, and the labelling is part of a full answer: real power must exceed this because friction and kinetic energy are unpaid. If the question DOES quantify the extras — a friction loss in the pipe, an efficiency percentage for the motor — then it wants the full audit. An eighty percent efficient motor delivering 612,5 useful watts must draw 612,5 divided by 0,8, about 765,6 watts from the mains. So the decision rule is simple: minimum when the question is silent about losses, full audit when it prices them. And notice the same logic in reverse for the learner on the staircase: the watts you compute there are a minimum rating of the learner too — muscles waste heat just like motors, which is why the climb leaves you warmer than the arithmetic says it should.
