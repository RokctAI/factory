# Part 1 — Expert

Work, energy and power ask you to change the way you watch motion. Newton's laws follow forces moment by moment; energy methods stand back and audit a whole journey at once — how many joules flowed into a moving object, how many flowed out, and what the balance did to its speed. This session builds the definition of work carefully, sorts every force into positive, negative or zero work, assembles net work from a free-body diagram, and then establishes the result that makes the whole topic run: the work-energy theorem, tested on level ground and on an incline.

## Subtopic: Work Done by a Force

Start with the definition, word for word: the work done on an object by a force is the product of the magnitude of the force, the magnitude of the displacement, and the cosine of the angle between the force and the displacement. In symbols, W equals F delta x cosine theta. Work is a SCALAR — it has size but no direction — and it is measured in joules. One joule is the work done when a force of one newton pushes an object one metre along the force's own line.

Everything interesting lives in that cosine, because it divides all forces into exactly three families. A force along the displacement has theta equal to zero, cosine equal to one, and does positive work — it pumps energy into the object. A force opposing the displacement has theta equal to 180 degrees, cosine equal to negative one, and does negative work — it siphons energy out. A force perpendicular to the displacement has theta equal to 90 degrees, cosine equal to zero, and does no work whatsoever, no matter how enormous it is.

Put all three families to work on one object. A gardener drags a 15 kilogram bag of compost 8 metres across level ground with a horizontal force of 60 newtons, while friction of 25 newtons resists. The applied force: W equals 60 multiplied by 8 multiplied by cosine zero, which is 480 joules — positive. Friction points backward: 25 multiplied by 8 multiplied by cosine 180, which is negative 200 joules. The weight pulls straight down and the normal force pushes straight up — both stand at 90 degrees to the horizontal displacement, so each contributes exactly zero joules. Notice that the normal force supports the entire weight of the bag for the whole journey and still does no work at all.

The classic trap follows immediately: a traveller wheeling a heavy suitcase across a flat airport concourse at constant height does zero work on the suitcase with the upward supporting force, because that force is perpendicular to the horizontal motion. Tiredness is not evidence of work. Only force along displacement counts.

The questions for this section are with you now — state the definition precisely, evaluate the cosine, and place each force in its family: positive, negative or zero.

## Subtopic: Net Work from the Free-Body Diagram

Net work is the total work done on an object by ALL the forces acting on it, and there are two routes that must agree. Route one: compute the work done by each force separately, signs and all, then add. Route two: build the net force first, then apply W net equals F net delta x cosine theta in a single line.

Run both routes on the compost bag. Route one: applied force, positive 480 joules; friction, negative 200 joules; normal force and weight, zero each. Net work: 480 minus 200, which is 280 joules. Route two: the net force is 60 minus 25, which is 35 newtons forward; net work equals 35 multiplied by 8, which is 280 joules. Identical, as the mathematics guarantees.

The free-body diagram is the inventory that keeps route one honest. Draw the dot, draw every force arrow, and then insist that every arrow pays a work term — even when that term is zero. The forces that go missing in learners' solutions are nearly always friction, or the component of gravity along a slope, and a missing arrow is a missing term.

The signs now carry physics, not just bookkeeping. Positive net work means the forces are collectively feeding the object's motion, and it speeds up. Negative net work means the motion is being drained, and it slows down. Zero net work — a bag dragged at constant velocity — means the deposits and the withdrawals cancel exactly over the journey.

One reading skill completes the section: work is done BY a named force ON a named object. The work done by friction on the bag and the work done by the gardener on the bag are different quantities with different values. Half of getting these questions right is underlining which force and which object the sentence is asking about.

Questions on this section are in front of you now — inventory first, one work term per arrow, then the signed total.

## Subtopic: The Work-Energy Theorem

Now the payoff. The work-energy theorem says: the net work done on an object equals the change in the object's kinetic energy. In symbols, W net equals delta E k, which is a half m v final squared minus a half m v initial squared. No exceptions — level ground, slopes, curved paths, any mix of forces.

Send the compost bag through it. Net work was 280 joules and the bag started from rest, so 280 equals a half multiplied by 15 multiplied by v final squared. That gives v final squared equal to 37,33, and v final equal to 6,11 metres per second. No acceleration was ever calculated and no equation of motion was touched — the energy audit went straight from forces to final speed.

The theorem runs equally well in reverse. A 1 200 kilogram bakkie travelling at 25 metres per second brakes to rest in 62,5 metres. The change in kinetic energy is zero minus a half multiplied by 1 200 multiplied by 625, which is negative 375 000 joules. Friction alone produced that change, acting backward over 62,5 metres: negative F multiplied by 62,5 equals negative 375 000, so F equals 6 000 newtons. A question about a force was answered entirely in the language of energy — and whenever a question hands you a distance, the energy language is almost always the faster translation.

Know the theorem's one blind spot: TIME. Work multiplies force by displacement and says nothing about how long the journey took. Questions built around seconds of contact or stopping time belong to the impulse-momentum theorem from earlier in the year — force multiplied by time. Distance given: work-energy. Time given: impulse. Diagnosing which one a question is offering is the topic's sharpest skill.

This section's questions arrive now — state the theorem in words and in symbols, then let it ferry you between forces, distances and speeds.

## Subtopic: Work on an Inclined Plane

The incline is where this topic and Newton's laws shake hands, and one worked example carries the entire method.

A 25 kilogram crate is pulled 6 metres up a ramp inclined at 30 degrees, at CONSTANT velocity, by a force parallel to the ramp, against friction of 35 newtons. Build the free-body diagram: the applied force up the slope; friction down the slope, opposing the motion; the normal force perpendicular to the surface; and the weight straight down, which must be resolved into two components — m g sine theta along the slope, dragging the crate back down the ramp, and m g cosine theta perpendicular to it, pressing into the surface.

The along-slope component: 25 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by sine 30 degrees, which is 25 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 0,5 — 122,5 newtons down the ramp. Constant velocity means the net force is zero, so the applied force must match both opponents together: 122,5 plus 35, which is 157,5 newtons.

Now audit the work over the 6 metres. Applied force: 157,5 multiplied by 6, positive 945 joules. Gravity: its along-slope component fights the motion, so negative 122,5 multiplied by 6, which is negative 735 joules. Friction: negative 35 multiplied by 6, negative 210 joules. Normal force: perpendicular, zero. Net work: 945 minus 735 minus 210 — zero joules. The theorem confirms it independently: constant velocity means the kinetic energy never changed, so the net work HAD to be zero. Running delta E k against your net-work total is a free cross-check, and strong candidates run it on every incline question.

Follow gravity's negative 735 joules to see where they went. The crate rose 6 multiplied by sine 30, which is 3 metres, and 25 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by 3 is exactly 735 joules of gravitational potential energy gained. Gravity's work depends only on the vertical height, never on the length of the path — and that observation is the doorway into the next lesson on conservation of energy.

The final expert questions are with you now — resolve the weight, charge every force over the same displacement, and let delta E k audit your total.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same physics again, with the cosines translated into pictures: a crate of tiles, a bank account, and a plank leaning against a trailer.

## Subtopic: Work Is Not Effort

Here is the sentence that sounds like an insult but is pure physics: you can strain until your arms shake and still do zero work.

Hold a 25 kilogram crate of floor tiles against your chest, standing dead still, for five minutes. Your arms burn, your shoulders complain — and physics calmly records zero work done on that crate. Why? Work only counts when a force moves something ALONG the force's own direction. Your push is upward; the crate went nowhere. No displacement, no work.

Now carry the crate down a long corridor, keeping it at the same height. Still zero work on the crate. Your force points up, the motion runs sideways, and a force at right angles to the motion contributes nothing — the same way leaning your weight straight down on a trolley handle does not push the trolley forward.

Physics only opens its wallet when you lift the crate up onto the trailer: force up, movement up, positive work, energy delivered. And when you lower the crate gently to the ground, something odd appears in the ledger — the crate moves down while your hands push up, force against motion, so your work on the crate is NEGATIVE. You are removing energy from it, which is exactly why it settles softly instead of slamming down.

The whole subtopic in one rule: along the motion, positive work — you feed the object. Against the motion, negative work — you drain it. Sideways to the motion, zero — you are only holding on.

Your first questions are with you now — before touching a formula, ask one thing: did my force point along the movement?

## Subtopic: The Energy Bank Account

Every moving object carries a bank account, and its balance is the kinetic energy — energy of motion, a half m v squared. The work-energy theorem is nothing more than the bank statement: net work equals the change in the balance. Positive work is a deposit; negative work is a withdrawal.

Watch a minibus pull off from a stop. The engine, acting through the wheels, makes deposit after deposit of positive work, and the balance climbs — the minibus speeds up. But the balance grows with the SQUARE of the speed, and that square is the most important number in this subtopic: doubling the speed does not double the account, it quadruples it. At 120 kilometres per hour the minibus holds four times the kinetic energy it held at 60.

Braking is friction making withdrawals: a backward force over the stopping distance, pulling the balance down to zero. Because doubling the speed quadruples the account, the withdrawal must also quadruple — same brakes, same force, four times the stopping distance. That is not a slogan from a driving school poster; it is the work-energy theorem doing plain arithmetic, and it is the physics behind keeping a proper following distance on the N2.

The bag dragged at constant speed fits the same picture: the person deposits, friction withdraws, the two cancel, and the balance never moves. Zero net work never means nothing happened — it means the account closed where it opened.

Carry one sorting habit into every calculation: a question that gives you a DISTANCE is inviting the bank account — work and energy. A question that gives you a TIME belongs to impulse and momentum from earlier in the year. Metres talk to joules; seconds talk to momentum.

Questions on the account are coming to you now — every force is a teller, and the balance is a half m v squared.

## Subtopic: Why Ramps Feel Easier

Nobody lifts a two-hundred-litre drum straight up onto a trailer. You lean a plank against the edge, roll the drum up, and everyone swears the plank made the job easier. Here is the honest physics: the ramp does NOT reduce the work. It reduces the FORCE, and it bills you extra distance in exchange.

Lifting the drum straight up one metre costs its full weight in force, over one metre. Rolling it up a gentle six-metre plank to that same one-metre height needs roughly a sixth of the force — but you push through six metres instead of one. Force divided by six, distance multiplied by six: the work, force times distance, lands on the same total. A ramp is a payment plan, not a discount — same bill, smaller instalments.

Steepness only decides how the instalments are arranged. On a gentle plank, gravity's pull along the slope is small and each push is light; on a steep one, you wrestle most of the weight directly. But reaching the same height always costs the same energy against gravity — weight multiplied by vertical height gained — and it is that vertical height, never the length of the plank, that fixes gravity's share of the bill.

Friction, however, charges differently. Friction bills per metre TRAVELLED along the surface, not per metre of height gained — so the long gentle plank, with six metres of rubbing, collects a bigger friction toll than a short steep one. Choosing a real ramp is a bargain between a force your body can manage and a friction bill you are willing to pay.

And the constant-speed check still stands at the top of the plank: rolled up steadily, the drum's kinetic energy never changes, so every joule you supplied went into height gained and heat from friction — nothing into speed. Deposits into lifting, withdrawals by friction, balance unchanged.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — smaller force, longer path, same work against gravity, and friction billed by the metre.
