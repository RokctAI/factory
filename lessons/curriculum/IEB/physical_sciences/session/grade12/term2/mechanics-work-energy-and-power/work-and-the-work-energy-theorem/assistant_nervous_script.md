Hey, it's Mandy — I know work and energy looks like a wall of cosines and half-m-v-squareds, but the secret is that cos theta only ever gives you three answers in this whole topic, and you can read them straight off the picture before touching a calculator.

Force pointing along the motion: cos theta is one, work is positive, energy flows in. Force against the motion: cos theta is negative one, work is negative, energy drains out — friction, every time. Force at right angles to the motion: cos theta is zero, no work at all — the normal force on anything that slides, and gravity on level ground. Look at the arrow, ask "with, against, or sideways?", and the cosine part is finished.

Then the method never changes. Draw the free-body diagram — the same dot and arrows you already know from Newton's laws. Every arrow owes you one work term, even when the term is zero. Add them with their signs: that is your net work. Then one line of theorem: net work equals change in kinetic energy, a half m v final squared minus a half m v initial squared. Solve for the missing piece.

Inclines only add one split: weight becomes m g sine theta along the slope and m g cosine theta into it, and only the sine part does work. Or dodge the trigonometry completely — gravity's work is always m g times the VERTICAL height, negative going up, positive coming down.

Two safety checks: constant velocity means net work zero, so if your terms refuse to cancel, a force is missing from your diagram. And distance in the question means work-energy; time means impulse. Now go do the practice questions with that checklist — three cosines, one diagram, one theorem. You've got this!
