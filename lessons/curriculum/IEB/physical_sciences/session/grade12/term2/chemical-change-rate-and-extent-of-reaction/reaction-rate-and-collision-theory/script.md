# Part 1 — Expert

Strike a match and the head flares in a fraction of a second; leave a steel gate in the rain and it takes years to streak with rust — yet both are reactions with oxygen. Rate of reaction is the chemistry of HOW FAST, one of the most graph-rich and experiment-rich sections of the whole course. This session defines rate and calculates it from laboratory data, explains every rate factor through collision theory, tours the standard measurement techniques, and closes with the molecular energy distribution curves that carry the topic's most demanding explanations.

## Subtopic: What Reaction Rate Means

The definition: reaction rate is the change in the concentration or the amount of reactants or products per unit time. In symbols, rate equals delta concentration divided by delta time, or delta amount in moles divided by delta time. The units follow directly: moles per second, or, when concentration is tracked, moles per cubic decimetre per second. Rate is reported as a positive quantity whether you watch a reactant vanish or a product accumulate.

Compute one from bench data. Dilute hydrochloric acid is poured over calcium carbonate chips in an open flask standing on a balance; the carbon dioxide produced escapes, so the flask loses mass. In the first 60 seconds the reading falls by 3,3 grams. Moles of carbon dioxide: 3,3 divided by the molar mass of 44 grams per mole, which is 0,075 moles. Average rate over that minute: 0,075 divided by 60, which is 0,00125 moles per second — one and a quarter thousandths of a mole of gas each second, in that interval.

Sit on the word AVERAGE. Reactions run fastest at the very start, when reactant concentrations peak, and slow continuously as reactants are consumed — so the rate over the first minute beats the rate over the second minute, and one reaction owns many average rates depending on the interval you choose. The instantaneous rate at a single moment is the gradient of the amount-time graph at that point: steep gradient, sprinting reaction; zero gradient, reaction over.

And keep rate separated from EXTENT. Extent asks how much product exists when everything stops; rate asks how quickly it appeared along the way. Two runs with identical amounts of reactants reach identical extents — the same final mass of product — even if one runs fifty times faster than the other. Hold those two questions apart and half of this topic's trick questions defuse themselves.

This section's questions are with you now: the definition, a moles-per-time calculation, and average kept strictly apart from instantaneous.

## Subtopic: Collision Theory and the Factors That Change Rate

Collision theory is the engine room of this whole topic — every explanation must run through it. Particles react only when they COLLIDE, and only when the collision is EFFECTIVE, which demands two things at once: the particles meet with the correct orientation, and they meet with kinetic energy equal to or greater than the ACTIVATION ENERGY — the minimum energy required for reaction to occur. Rate is therefore fixed by the number of effective collisions per second, and every factor that alters the rate alters either how often particles collide or what fraction of those collisions succeed.

Now the factors, one at a time. NATURE OF THE REACTANTS: some substances are intrinsically more reactive — calcium tears into water while magnesium barely stirs it — and reactions between free ions in solution generally outpace reactions that must first break strong covalent bonds.

SURFACE AREA: only particles sitting at a surface can be struck. Shred a solid into powder and enormously more particles stand exposed; collisions per second multiply and the rate climbs. It is the reason a pad of fine steel wool ignites in a flame while a solid nail of the same steel only glows: the wool is nearly all surface.

CONCENTRATION — and for gases, PRESSURE: cram more particles into the same volume and collisions come more often, so effective collisions come more often too. Double the concentration of acid on the same chips and the fizz audibly quickens.

TEMPERATURE, the heavyweight: heating raises the particles' average kinetic energy, so a far LARGER FRACTION of collisions now arrives carrying at least the activation energy. Collisions also become somewhat more frequent, but the fraction argument is the essential one — temperature works chiefly by upgrading each collision's chance of success, not merely by adding more collisions.

CATALYST: a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed by the reaction — the full mechanism is the next section's business.

Questions on the factors are in front of you now — every explanation must land on effective collisions per second, or it is not yet an explanation.

## Subtopic: Measuring Rate in the Laboratory

Every rate experiment shares one blueprint: find a measurable quantity that changes while the reaction runs, and log it against time.

Technique one: GAS VOLUME. Lead the gas into a syringe or collect it over water, reading volume against time. The volume-time curve leaps up early, bends as reactants thin out, and runs horizontal when the reaction is done — the plateau height is the extent, the gradient at any moment is the rate.

Technique two: MASS LOSS. Run the reaction in an open flask on a balance while a gas escapes; the reading drops steeply, then levels off. The calcium carbonate calculation earlier came from exactly this arrangement.

Technique three: TURBIDITY — the vanishing cross. Mix sodium thiosulfate with hydrochloric acid and the solution slowly clouds with a fine precipitate of sulfur. Stand the flask on a paper cross and time how long until the cross disappears from view. A shorter time means a faster reaction — the measurement behaves as one over time, so never let a short time masquerade as a slow rate. This is the classic bench investigation for concentration or temperature effects.

Technique four: COLOUR CHANGE, followed by eye against a standard or measured by an instrument, for any reaction with a coloured reactant or product.

Now the graph-reading skill this topic prizes. Equal masses of powdered magnesium and magnesium ribbon react with the same excess acid: two volume-time curves that START differently but END identically. The powder's curve rockets upward — more exposed surface, faster rate; the ribbon's curve rises at a stroll. Yet equal masses of magnesium must release equal moles of hydrogen, so both curves flatten at the SAME final volume. Steeper start, same finish: the rate changed, the extent did not. If one curve instead levels off LOWER, the chemistry differed — less reactant was supplied, or a different reagent ran out first.

This section's questions arrive now — pair each technique with its graph, and read rate from gradients, extent from plateaus.

## Subtopic: Energy Distribution Curves and Catalysts

The topic's deepest explanations ride on one graph: the molecular energy distribution — the Maxwell-Boltzmann curve. Plot the number of particles against kinetic energy at a fixed temperature: a few particles crawl, a few race, and most cluster in a hump around the average. Now draw the activation energy as a vertical line on the energy axis: only particles to the RIGHT of that line — the shaded tail — are capable of effective collisions. The size of that tail, relative to the whole area, sets the fraction of collisions that succeed.

Redraw the graph for each factor. TEMPERATURE RAISED: the entire curve slides toward higher energies and flattens — the total area is untouched, because the particle count is untouched — but the tail beyond the activation line swells dramatically. A modest warming can multiply the reacting fraction several-fold, which is precisely why temperature is the strongest lever on rate.

CONCENTRATION RAISED: the curve keeps its shape and its position but stands taller — more particles at every energy, same average energy. The tail grows in absolute number of particles, not in proportion. More collisions per second; the same success fraction per collision.

CATALYST ADDED: the curve does not move a millimetre. The activation energy LINE moves left instead. A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with a lower activation energy, so the unchanged population of particles suddenly finds far more of itself beyond the new, lower threshold. The particles never changed; the barrier did. And since the catalyst is not consumed, a trace of it serves the entire reaction — the coated honeycomb in a car's exhaust converter keeps working for years.

On the potential-energy diagram from earlier grades, a catalyst lowers the hump between reactants and products while leaving the start level, the end level, and therefore delta H, completely untouched. A catalyst redesigns the journey and never moves the destination.

The final expert questions are with you now — always state which feature of the graph moved: the curve, its height, or the line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chemistry now at a funfair: how fast against how far, and why only the hard bumps count.

## Subtopic: How Fast Is Not How Far

Every journey invites two different questions: how far are you going, and how fast are you moving? Chemistry asks exactly those two about every reaction, and blurring them is where marks quietly leak away.

Take two identical effervescent tablets. Crush one to powder and drop it into warm water; drop the other in whole, into cold water. The powder in warm water roars up in a storm of bubbles and is gone in seconds; the whole tablet in cold water fizzes lazily for minutes. But wait for both glasses to finish, and each has released EXACTLY the same total amount of gas — same tablet, same total fizz. The speed differed wildly; the destination was identical. In chemistry language: different RATE, identical EXTENT.

That one idea decodes the classic graph pair. Two curves of gas volume against time: one leaps up steeply, one climbs gently, and both flatten at the same height. Translation: the same amounts of chemicals — the same destination — with one trip simply driven faster: powder instead of lumps, warmer solution, stronger acid. But if one curve levels off at a LOWER height, that run started with less reactant: a shorter journey, no matter how it was driven.

One more speed habit to own: reactions sprint at the start and limp at the finish. Early on, reactant particles crowd everywhere and encounters are constant; as they are consumed, meetings become rare and the reaction wheezes toward its plateau. That is why every curve in this topic is steep first and flat later — and why "the rate" usually means "the rate right now", read as the steepness at that moment.

Your first questions are with you now — steepness is speed, plateau height is distance, and neither may ever impersonate the other.

## Subtopic: Bumper Cars That Only Count When They Hit Hard

Picture reactant particles as bumper cars at a funfair. Bumps happen constantly — but the game has a strict rule: a bump only COUNTS when the cars hit hard enough, and square-on. Gentle taps and glancing scrapes score nothing; the cars shrug and drift apart unchanged. Chemists call the scoring bumps effective collisions, and the hard-enough threshold carries the name activation energy.

See the game once, and every rate factor explains itself.

Send extra cars onto the same floor — that is concentration. More cars mean more bumps every minute, and among them more of the hard, square-on kind. The reaction quickens.

Break the one giant car into a fleet of small ones — that is surface area. A lump of marble hides nearly all its particles in its interior, where no acid can ever reach them. Grind it down and suddenly every particle rides on an outside edge, available for bumping. The same stone, but now the whole floor is in the game.

Feed every motor more power — that is temperature. Faster cars certainly bump more often, but the true magic is that far MORE of their bumps now clear the hard-enough bar. That is why heat is chemistry's strongest lever: it promotes ordinary taps into scoring hits, across the entire floor at once.

And the catalyst? The catalyst never touches a single car. It lowers the BAR — it opens a side gate through which a softer, easier bump still scores. Same cars, same speeds, a friendlier target; and the catalyst is never used up, so it holds that gate open all evening for every car that comes.

Questions on the bumper cars are coming to you now — every explanation finishes the same way: more counting bumps per second.

## Subtopic: Reading the Story off the Graph

Every rate experiment ends its life as a graph, and the graph is a story you can read aloud once you know its three words: steepness, bend, plateau.

STEEPNESS is speed. Where the curve climbs hard, gas is pouring off, mass is tumbling, the reaction is at full sprint. Compare two curves at the same instant and the steeper one is the faster experiment — the powder, the warmer bath, the stronger acid.

The BEND is the tiring. As reactants are eaten, encounters grow scarce and the curve leans over. The shape of every honest rate story is the same: sprint, tire, stop. No curve in this topic accelerates later by itself — if a graph shows a sudden new burst, something was added midway through the race.

The PLATEAU is the finish line, and its HEIGHT records how much product was ever made — the extent. Here lives the topic's favourite trap, so walk through it slowly: powdered marble and chunky marble, equal masses, same acid. The powder sprints — visibly steeper — yet both plateaus settle at exactly the same height, because equal marble can only ever release equal gas. Faster never means more. Change the AMOUNT of reactant, however, and the plateau itself relocates: half the marble, half the final gas, a lower shelf.

One final reading skill: the vanishing-cross experiment hands you a TIME, not a curve — the seconds until the cross disappears. Short time, fast reaction. When comparing runs, think "one over time": twenty-five seconds is quick, two hundred and fifty is sluggish. Treating a big time as a big rate is the trap; inverting it is the skill.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — steepness for speed, plateau for amount, and let every story run sprint, tire, stop.
