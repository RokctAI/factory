**Mandy:** Time for the class questions before we wrap up! The one that filled my inbox first: learners drew the galvanic cell with a negative anode last term, and today the anode is positive. Half of them think they mislabelled something somewhere. Who is right?

**Tutor:** Everyone is right, and the confusion dissolves the moment you notice that anode was never defined by its sign. Anode means the electrode where oxidation happens — that is the whole definition, and it holds in every electrochemical device on the planet. The sign is a consequence, and it depends on what is driving the electrons. In a galvanic cell the chemistry drives: the spontaneous reaction pushes electrons out of the oxidation electrode, they crowd there before leaving through the wire, and a crowd of electrons reads as negative. In an electrolytic cell the power supply drives: its positive terminal is bolted to one electrode and drags electrons off whatever species touches it — dragging electrons off a species IS oxidation, so oxidation now happens at the positive electrode. Same definition, different driver, opposite sign. So build every answer in this order: first scan the diagram for a power source — if one is present, the cell is electrolytic. Second, find the electrode where a species is losing electrons and stamp it ANODE, before you look at any plus or minus. Third, only then read the polarity off the wiring as a final detail. A learner who starts from the signs is trusting memory under pressure; a learner who starts from the oxidation is reading a definition, and definitions do not wobble in an exam. The ions need no memory work at all — negative ions drift to the positive electrode, positive ions to the negative one, plain electrostatics, and the terms even alliterate for you: anions to the anode, cations to the cathode.

**Mandy:** Next one, and I love this question: in the brine cell, sodium ions are sitting right at the cathode, charged and ready — and the electrode makes hydrogen from water instead. A learner wrote, "it feels like the shop served the person at the back of the queue." Why does water jump the queue?

**Tutor:** Because the electrode is not running a queue — it is running an auction, and electrons go to the strongest bidder, not the closest ion. At the cathode, every species that could conceivably be reduced places a bid, and the bid is its reduction potential. In brine there are two bidders: the sodium ion and water. Sodium's bid is famously feeble — its reduction potential sits near minus two comma seven volts, right at the bottom of the table, which is the table's way of saying that sodium metal would rather be an ion than a metal by an enormous margin. Water bids far higher. So water takes the electrons: two water molecules plus two electrons give hydrogen gas plus two hydroxide ions, and the sodium ions stay in solution untouched. There is also a safety-net argument worth appreciating: even if you could somehow force sodium metal to form in that beaker, it would explode into reaction with the surrounding water instantly and re-ionise — the products would erase themselves. Now carry the auction to the anode, because there the story has a twist. On potentials alone, water should beat chloride at the anode too, and in dilute solution it genuinely does — oxygen comes off. But concentration is allowed to sway a close auction, and the anode contest IS close: in concentrated brine, the sheer number of chloride ions swings the result, and chlorine wins. So the full principle has two layers: reduction potentials set the default winner at each electrode, and a large concentration difference can overturn a narrow verdict — but never a landslide, which is why no strength of brine ever produces sodium metal in water.

**Mandy:** Third: the refinery. Learners accept that copper leaves the dirty slab and lands on the clean sheet, but the two kinds of impurity trip them up — gold falls to the floor while zinc floats around in the solution. What decides who falls and who floats?

**Tutor:** One property decides everything: reactivity relative to copper, tested at two gates. Gate one is the anode, and its test is oxidation. The refinery tunes the voltage so that copper only just dissolves — a deliberately gentle setting. Gold, silver and platinum are nobler than copper; they grip their electrons more tightly, so the gentle voltage cannot oxidise them at all. They never become ions. Instead, as the copper matrix holding them is dissolved away atom by atom, they lose their anchorage and drop — solid, unreacted grains sinking to the tank floor as anode sludge. Chemically nothing happened to them; gravity did all the work. Gate two is the cathode, and its test is reduction. Zinc and iron are more reactive than copper, so they sail through gate one — they oxidise into solution even more willingly than copper does. But to rejoin the metal world they must win reduction at the cathode, and there they are outbid: copper ions are markedly easier to reduce, so the cathode deposits copper and only copper, and the zinc and iron ions simply circulate in the electrolyte as permanent tenants until the plant drains and treats the solution. Three fates from one dial: nobler metals fall as treasure, baser metals dissolve and stay dissolved, and copper alone makes the complete journey — slab, solution, sheet — arriving ninety-nine comma nine nine percent pure. When you write this answer, name the voltage tuning; it is the detail that shows you understand the cell rather than just its outcome.

**Mandy:** Last one — the comparison trap. A learner found the same half-reaction, copper two plus gaining two electrons to give copper, written in a galvanic cell question one week and an electrolytic refining question the next, and wants to know what changes between the two appearances. Anything?

**Tutor:** The half-reaction itself changes nothing — not a coefficient, not an electron. What changes is the context around it, and being able to articulate that context precisely is what separates a complete answer from a lucky one. In a galvanic cell, say the Daniell cell, copper two plus gains two electrons at the cathode because the overall reaction is spontaneous — copper's reduction potential beats zinc's, chemical energy converts to electrical energy, and the cell could light a small globe while the copper electrode thickens for free. In the refining tank, the identical reduction happens at the cathode because the power supply insists: the overall change is non-spontaneous, electrical energy converts to chemical energy, and every gram of copper landing on the sheet is paid for through the electricity meter. So structure any comparison as three constants and three reversals. Constant: the half-reaction equations, the definitions — oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode — and the migration rule, anions to the anode, cations to the cathode. Reversed: the energy conversion direction, the spontaneity, and the electrode polarities — the galvanic cathode is the positive electrode while the electrolytic cathode is the negative one. Write that three-and-three table once on your revision page and the whole of electrochemistry collapses into a single view; it is the highest-value half page you can prepare for this topic.
