Hey, it's Mandy — electrodynamics has a scary name and a tiny heart: one machine, one argument, one clever average. Let me hand you all three.

The machine: a coil spinning between magnet poles. Spin it and electricity comes out — that is the generator, running on changing flux. Feed it current instead and it spins — that is the motor, running on the force a field puts on a current-carrying wire. Same coil, same magnets, opposite energy directions. The only hardware decision is the rings: two complete slip rings hand you AC, and one split ring — swapping connections every half turn, right at the vertical — hands you DC from a generator or continuous spin in a motor. If you can label that diagram and say what each part does, a big slice of the topic is already yours.

The argument: why AC? Two welded points, always together. Transformers change AC voltage easily, and losses in a cable go as current SQUARED — so the grid sends power at huge voltage and small current, then steps down to 220 at your wall. Say both halves; half the argument is half the marks.

The average: rms. The mains wave peaks at 311 volts, but heating follows current squared, which is positive on both strokes — so the honest, DC-equivalent number is the peak divided by root two: 220. See a maximum in a question? Divide by root two first. See a label value? It is already rms — use it straight in P equals V times I.

That is the whole topic: label the machine, weld the argument, divide by root two. The practice questions are waiting, and you are readier than you think — go get them!
