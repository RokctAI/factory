# Part 1 — Expert

No topic in the final chemistry year rewards systematic thinking like organic chemistry, and the whole system rests on two foundation skills: reading a structure to identify its family, and translating between structures and names without error. This session establishes the definitions that anchor the topic, works through the nine homologous series of the syllabus, turns IUPAC naming into a four-step routine you can run in your sleep, and closes with the three types of structural isomer. Because we teach by voice, every molecule will be assembled in words, carbon by carbon — and building structures aloud is the same discipline that keeps them honest on paper.

## Subtopic: The Vocabulary of Organic Chemistry

Organic molecules are molecules containing carbon atoms. Why does carbon get a branch of chemistry to itself? Because every carbon atom forms exactly four bonds, and carbon bonds happily to other carbons, so it strings itself into chains, branches and rings without limit. The four-bond rule has no exceptions in this topic, and counting to four on every carbon is the quickest self-check you will ever own.

Now the definitions, stated so you can quote them. A HYDROCARBON is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen atoms. A FUNCTIONAL GROUP is a bond or an atom or a group of atoms that determines the physical and chemical properties of a group of organic compounds — think of the carbon-carbon double bond in an alkene, or the O H group in an alcohol, or a bromine atom sitting on a chain. A HOMOLOGOUS SERIES is a series of organic compounds that can be described by the same general formula, in which each member differs from the previous one by a C H two unit — one carbon and two hydrogens more than its neighbour.

SATURATED compounds contain only single bonds between their carbon atoms. UNSATURATED compounds contain at least one double or triple carbon-to-carbon bond, which leaves them room to take on more atoms.

Finally, the three levels of writing a molecule down. The MOLECULAR formula is a census: pentane is C five H twelve, and nothing about the arrangement is recorded. The STRUCTURAL formula shows every atom and every bond drawn out in full, so nothing is ambiguous. The CONDENSED formula walks the chain one carbon at a time, each carbon written with its own hydrogens: pentane reads C H three, C H two, C H two, C H two, C H three — five carbons in a line, three hydrogens on each end carbon, two on each inner carbon. Audit it: every carbon holds exactly four bonds. And a warning that saves real credit: when a question demands a structural formula, every single bond must be drawn — a condensed formula offered in its place does not count.

This section's questions are with you now: five definitions quoted precisely, and the four-bond audit run on everything.

## Subtopic: Nine Families and Their Functional Groups

The syllabus organises organic chemistry into nine homologous series, each recognised by its functional group and announced by its suffix.

Start with the three hydrocarbon families. The ALKANES contain only single carbon-carbon bonds, follow the general formula C n H two n plus two, and end in A N E. Their roots are the counting spine of the entire topic: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex, hept, oct — one carbon through eight — and that sequence must be automatic. The ALKENES contain a carbon-to-carbon double bond, follow C n H two n, and end in E N E. The ALKYNES contain a carbon-to-carbon triple bond, follow C n H two n minus two, and end in Y N E. Only the alkanes are saturated; the other two are unsaturated by definition.

Two families are a chain wearing a single decoration. The ALCOHOLS carry a hydroxyl group — an oxygen bonded to a hydrogen — on one of the chain carbons, and their names end in A N O L. The HALOALKANES are alkanes with one or more hydrogens swapped for halogen atoms, announced with the prefixes fluoro, chloro, bromo and iodo.

The last four families all contain the carbonyl group — a carbon double-bonded to an oxygen — and they differ only in where that carbon sits and what else it holds. ALDEHYDES put the carbonyl carbon at the END of the chain with a hydrogen on it; suffix A L, as in butanal, and that carbon is automatically carbon one. KETONES bury the carbonyl carbon INSIDE the chain, carbon on either side of it; suffix O N E, as in butanone. CARBOXYLIC ACIDS give the end carbon both the double-bonded oxygen and a hydroxyl — together the carboxyl group, C O O H; suffix A N O I C acid, as in methanoic acid, the acid an ant injects when it bites. ESTERS link the carbonyl carbon through a second oxygen to another carbon chain — chain, C O O, chain — and take their names from two parents, as in methyl propanoate.

Whatever the question, your opening move never changes: find the functional group. That single observation names the family, fixes the suffix, and — as the next lesson will show — predicts most of the physical behaviour.

Questions on the families are in front of you now — drill functional group, general formula and suffix as one linked package.

## Subtopic: IUPAC Naming, Step by Step

Treat IUPAC naming as an algorithm with four steps, because that is exactly what it is.

Step one: identify the LONGEST continuous carbon chain that contains the functional group; its length gives the root name. Step two: number that chain from the end that hands the FUNCTIONAL GROUP the lowest possible number; when there is no functional group, number so the substituents get the lowest numbers instead. Step three: name every substituent — methyl for a one-carbon branch, ethyl for two carbons, or a halogen prefix — each with its position number attached. Step four: assemble the name with prefixes in alphabetical order in front of the root, using di and tri for repeats, commas between numbers, and hyphens between numbers and words.

Run it on a plain alkane. A chain of five carbons carries a methyl group on its second carbon; every other bond is filled by hydrogen. Longest chain: five, so the root is pentane. Numbering from the near end puts the methyl on carbon two; from the far end it would sit on carbon four, which is worse. Name: 2-methylpentane.

Now an alcohol. A four-carbon chain carries a hydroxyl group on its second carbon. Root: butan. Count from the end nearest the group: the O H lands on carbon two. Name: butan-2-ol. Move that same hydroxyl to the end carbon and you have butan-1-ol — same molecular formula, different compound, and only the number tells them apart.

Next an alkene, where priority is tested. A five-carbon chain has a double bond between carbons two and three, and a methyl group on carbon four. The double bond outranks every substituent, so numbering starts from the end that reaches the double bond first, and the double bond's number is the FIRST carbon of the pair. Name: 4-methylpent-2-ene. Numbering from the wrong end would call it pent-3-ene, and that is a lost answer.

Haloalkanes can carry more than one halogen. A three-carbon chain with a bromine on carbon one and another on carbon two is 1,2-dibromopropane — two position numbers, di for the pair.

And always practise the reverse direction, because questions run both ways. Asked to draw 2-chloropentane: pentane demands five carbons in a row; chloro on carbon two; hydrogens fill every remaining bond; and then the audit — four bonds on every carbon, no more, no fewer.

This section's questions come to you now — four steps forward, four steps backward, audit at the end.

## Subtopic: Isomers — Same Formula, Different Molecule

Structural isomers are molecules with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. The syllabus distinguishes three kinds, and classification questions expect the right label with a reason.

CHAIN isomers rearrange the carbon skeleton itself. C five H twelve can be pentane — five carbons in an unbranched line — or 2-methylbutane — a four-carbon chain with a branch — or 2,2-dimethylpropane — a central carbon holding four others. Three skeletons, one formula, three different names and three different boiling points.

POSITIONAL isomers keep the skeleton and the functional group but park the group on a different carbon. Pentan-1-ol and pentan-2-ol share a five-carbon chain and a hydroxyl group; only the house number changed, and yet they are distinct compounds. Pent-1-ene and pent-2-ene repeat the pattern with a double bond.

FUNCTIONAL isomers share a molecular formula while sitting in DIFFERENT homologous series. The classic pair is an aldehyde against a ketone with the same carbon count: butanal — four carbons, carbonyl at the end — and butanone — four carbons, carbonyl inside — are both C four H eight O, yet they carry different suffixes and different chemistry. Carboxylic acids pair off with esters the same way: butanoic acid and methyl propanoate are both C four H eight O two.

Now the trap. Draw six carbons in a straight row; now draw six carbons in a row that bends halfway across the page. Different molecules? No — identical. A drawing that bends is still the same chain, because isomerism lives in WHICH atom bonds to which, never in how the picture happens to curl. To manufacture a genuine isomer you must actually rewire something: shift a carbon into a branch, move the group's position number, or reorganise the atoms into a new family. And whatever you produce must still balance the original molecular formula — same atoms in, same atoms out.

The final expert questions are with you now: label each pair chain, positional or functional, and justify the label in one sentence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same material told the easy way: every organic name is a delivery address, and you already know how to read those.

## Subtopic: A Surname, a First Name and a House Number

Read any organic name from the back, and it unpacks itself into three pieces of an address.

The ending is the family surname. Hear A N E and you are visiting the alkanes, the plain single-bonded family. Hear A N O L and it is the alcohols, the family with the O H group on the gate. Hear A N O I C acid and you are with the carboxylic acids — the family whose smallest member is the sting in an ant bite. The surname alone tells you which functional group will open the door.

The middle of the name is the street length — how many carbons long the main road runs. The counting rhyme goes meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex, hept, oct: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. So pentanol is a five-carbon street in the alcohol family before you have drawn a single atom.

The numbers are house numbers. Pentan-2-ol: five-carbon street, the O H living at number two. 2-chloropentane: same street, a chlorine at number two instead. And numbering has one golden rule — the most important resident, the functional group, always gets the LOWEST house number on offer, so you count from whichever end of the street makes that happen. If one direction gives the group number two and the other gives it number four, the name uses two, no debate.

The front of the name lists the lodgers: methyl, a one-carbon lodger; ethyl, a two-carbon lodger; chloro and bromo, halogen lodgers — each announced with its house number, and di or tri when identical lodgers arrive in pairs or threes.

Your questions are with you now — surname first, street length second, house numbers last, always from the back of the name.

## Subtopic: Carbon Has Four Hands

Turning a name into a structure needs one unbreakable fact and one reliable routine.

The fact: carbon has exactly four hands, and every hand must be holding something — a carbon, a hydrogen, an oxygen, a halogen. Four, never three, never five. For the helpers: hydrogen brings one hand, oxygen two, any halogen one.

The routine has three passes, always in this order. Pass one: lay the carbon spine, as long as the name says. Pass two: attach the important pieces — the functional group at its house number, then every lodger at its number. Pass three, and only now: fill each carbon's empty hands with hydrogens.

Build 2-methylpentane in the air. Pass one: five carbons in a row, holding hands. Pass two: a methyl group — one carbon with its three hydrogens — onto carbon two. Pass three: count hands and fill. Carbon one holds one carbon, so it takes three hydrogens. Carbon two holds three carbons — its two chain neighbours plus the methyl — so it takes one hydrogen. Carbons three and four each hold two carbons and take two hydrogens each. Carbon five takes three. The methyl carbon takes three. Add it all up: six carbons, fourteen hydrogens — C six H fourteen — and every carbon shows exactly four bonds. The structure marks itself.

Now build butan-2-ol. Four carbons in a row; on carbon two, an oxygen holding a hydrogen — that O H uses one of carbon two's hands. Fill up: three hydrogens on each end carbon, one on carbon two, two on carbon three. Done, and audited. Every structure question you will ever meet is these same three passes, and the four-hands count at the end catches a wrong drawing before anyone else sees it — a five-handed carbon is not a style choice, it is an alarm bell.

Questions on building are coming to you now — spine, attachments, hydrogens, count to four.

## Subtopic: Spot the Twins

Take five carbons and twelve hydrogens. Build them as a straight five-chain and you have pentane. Pull one carbon off the end and hang it on carbon two of what is now a four-chain, and you have 2-methylbutane. Pull off another and pile both onto the middle carbon, and you have 2,2-dimethylpropane. One box of atoms, three genuinely different molecules — different names, different boiling points. Chemists call them structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structure.

Twins come in three varieties. Rebuild the SKELETON — straight versus branched — and you have chain isomers, like the pentane trio. Keep the skeleton but move the functional group to a new house number — pentan-1-ol against pentan-2-ol — and you have positional isomers. Or reshuffle the atoms until the molecule joins a different FAMILY altogether — the same C four H eight O building either butanal or butanone — and you have functional isomers: one formula, two surnames.

Here is the trap that catches nearly every learner exactly once. Draw five carbons in a straight line. Now draw five carbons in a line with an elbow bend in the middle of the drawing. It looks like two molecules; it is one. A chain is defined by who holds whose hand, not by how the queue snakes across the page — a bent photograph of the same five friends is still the same five friends. No bond changed, so no new molecule exists. To make a real twin, a bond must actually move: a carbon rehoused as a branch, a group shifted to a new number, a family changed.

So when a question says draw an isomer, make one genuine change, run the four-hands audit, and confirm the atom count still matches the original formula exactly. Same bricks, different building — and never trust a photograph taken at an angle.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — three kinds of twins, one bent-drawing trap, and the formula must always balance.
