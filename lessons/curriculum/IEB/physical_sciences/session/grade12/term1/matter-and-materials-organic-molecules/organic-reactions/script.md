# Part 1 — Expert

Knowing a molecule's name is grammar; predicting what it does next is fluency. The organic reactions section asks the same question in a hundred costumes: here is a molecule, here are the conditions — what forms, and why? This session works through the full syllabus set: combustion and esterification first, then the three reaction types that decide most of the questions — addition across a double bond, elimination to manufacture one, and substitution to trade one group for another. Conditions are the referee of this whole topic, and by the end you will let them call every reaction for you.

## Subtopic: Combustion and Esterification

Begin with combustion, the reaction that pays the alkanes' way in the world. In excess oxygen, every alkane burns completely to carbon dioxide and water, releasing a large amount of heat — and that heat is why alkanes fuel our lives: the gas bottle under the stove, diesel in a taxi, paraffin in a lamp. The smallest case in words: methane plus two oxygen molecules gives one carbon dioxide plus two water molecules. Balancing follows a fixed order — carbons first, hydrogens next, oxygen counted last, because oxygen arrives as a pure element and adjusts freely. Try pentane: five carbons demand five carbon dioxides; twelve hydrogens demand six waters; that requires sixteen oxygen atoms, so eight oxygen molecules. Pentane plus eight oxygen gives five carbon dioxide plus six water. And respect the phrase EXCESS OXYGEN: the syllabus keeps combustion complete, and complete combustion produces nothing but carbon dioxide and water.

Esterification is combustion's opposite in spirit — it builds rather than destroys. A carboxylic acid and an alcohol, warmed gently with concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst, join to form an ESTER plus water. Track the atoms carefully: the acid surrenders the hydroxyl from its carboxyl group, the alcohol surrenders only the hydrogen from its hydroxyl, and those discarded pieces leave together as the water molecule.

Work a fresh example in words: propanoic acid plus methanol, warmed with concentrated sulfuric acid, gives methyl propanoate plus water. The naming convention never varies: the alcohol supplies the front of the name, ending in Y L; the acid supplies the back, ending in O A T E. Ethanol with methanoic acid gives ethyl methanoate. And read names backwards too: butyl ethanoate must have come from butan-1-ol and ethanoic acid. Esters are the perfumers of chemistry — pineapple, pear drop, banana — but the questions about them are bookkeeping: two parents, one catalyst, one ester, and never forget the water.

This section's questions are with you now — carbons, hydrogens, oxygen last for burning; acid plus alcohol gives ester plus water for perfume.

## Subtopic: Addition: Opening the Double Bond

Addition is the alkenes' signature reaction, because addition needs a place to add — and the double bond is that place. One bond of the double pair unclips, each of the two carbons offers a fresh bonding site, and a small molecule is absorbed whole. Unsaturated becomes saturated, two reactants become one product, and no atoms are discarded. The syllabus runs four versions, separated only by what gets added.

HYDROGENATION adds hydrogen gas across the double bond, with a platinum, palladium or nickel catalyst: propene plus hydrogen gives propane. Industry uses exactly this reaction to harden liquid plant oils into margarine. HALOGENATION adds a halogen with no catalyst at all: propene plus bromine gives 1,2-dibromopropane, one bromine settling on each of the doubled carbons. The speed of that reaction is the standard laboratory test for unsaturation — an alkene strips the red-brown colour out of bromine water almost instantly, while an alkane leaves it standing.

HYDROHALOGENATION adds a hydrogen halide, and now position becomes the question. Take but-1-ene — four carbons, double bond between carbon one and carbon two — and add hydrogen chloride. The hydrogen bonds to one doubled carbon and the chlorine to the other, so two products are conceivable: 1-chlorobutane or 2-chlorobutane. The rule for the MAJOR product: the incoming hydrogen attaches to the double-bond carbon that ALREADY carries more hydrogens. Carbon one of but-1-ene holds two hydrogens; carbon two holds only one. The hydrogen therefore joins carbon one, the chlorine takes carbon two, and 2-chlorobutane dominates. The rich get richer — let that phrase do the remembering.

HYDRATION adds water across the bond, with a dilute acid catalyst, and produces an alcohol under the same richness rule: but-1-ene hydrates mainly to butan-2-ol, the hydroxyl claiming the inner carbon. Recognise addition at a glance: a double bond in the reactant, two molecules in and one out, and mild conditions throughout.

Questions on this section reach you now — one bond opens, two carbons gain partners, and the new hydrogen always joins the carbon that already has more.

## Subtopic: Elimination: Creating the Double Bond

Elimination runs the addition film backwards: one molecule EXPELS a small fragment and knits a double bond across the gap. Two neighbouring carbons each give something up — one surrenders a halogen or a hydroxyl, its neighbour surrenders a hydrogen — and the vacated bonding capacity snaps together as a carbon-carbon double bond. One reactant in, two products out: an alkene plus the ejected small molecule. Three versions carry the syllabus.

DEHYDROHALOGENATION strips a hydrogen and a halogen from a haloalkane, and the conditions must be quoted in full: the haloalkane is heated with CONCENTRATED sodium hydroxide dissolved in ETHANOL — hot, concentrated, ethanolic, all three words. Take 2-bromopentane: five carbons, bromine on carbon two. The bromine departs from carbon two, and a hydrogen must depart from a NEIGHBOURING carbon — carbon one or carbon three — giving pent-1-ene or pent-2-ene. The major-product rule mirrors addition's rule in reverse: the hydrogen is removed from the neighbouring carbon that carries FEWER hydrogens. Carbon three, inside the chain, holds two hydrogens; carbon one, at the end, holds three. Fewer wins, so the double bond forms between carbons two and three: pent-2-ene is the major product, the more substituted and more stable alkene, with pent-1-ene as the minor.

DEHYDRATION expels water from an alcohol: the hydroxyl leaves one carbon while a hydrogen leaves the neighbour. Conditions: heating with an excess of concentrated sulfuric acid, acting as both catalyst and dehydrating agent. Propan-1-ol dehydrates to propene plus water — a standard laboratory route from alcohols to alkenes.

CRACKING is elimination at industrial scale: long alkanes are broken at high temperature and pressure, with or without a catalyst, into a shorter alkane plus an alkene — decane cracking to octane plus ethene, for instance. It exists because crude oil supplies long chains while the economy wants short ones: fuels on one hand, alkenes for plastics on the other. Elimination's fingerprints, for classification: ONE reactant, a double bond APPEARING, and harsh conditions — heat, concentration, strength.

This section's questions arrive now — neighbours give up their pieces, the double bond fills the gap, and the hydrogen leaves the carbon with fewer to spare.

## Subtopic: Substitution: the Straight Swap

Substitution is the tidy reaction: one atom or group vacates a position and another takes its exact place. No double bond forms, none disappears, and saturation is untouched. The syllabus tests two versions and one decisive contrast.

HALOGENATION of alkanes: an alkane and a halogen, in the presence of LIGHT or heat, trade one hydrogen for one halogen. Ethane plus chlorine, under light, gives chloroethane plus hydrogen chloride. Count both products every time: the displaced hydrogen does not evaporate from the equation — it leaves bonded to the other halogen atom as a hydrogen halide. And light is the condition worth underlining: alkanes are saturated and unreactive, and without light or heat the halogen simply waits. That is why an alkane fades bromine only slowly and only in sunlight, while an alkene fades it immediately even in the dark — same reagent, different speed, different mechanism, and a favourite comparison question.

HYDROLYSIS of haloalkanes swaps a halogen for a hydroxyl: the haloalkane reacts with water, or more briskly with DILUTE aqueous sodium hydroxide under gentle warming, and the product is an alcohol. 2-chlorobutane gives butan-2-ol, the hydroxyl landing exactly where the chlorine sat — a swap in place, position preserved.

Now the contrast that separates understanding from rote learning, because the SAME haloalkane with the SAME reagent gives two different products on conditions alone. Take 2-bromopentane and sodium hydroxide. Dilute sodium hydroxide, dissolved in water, gently warmed: SUBSTITUTION, and pentan-2-ol forms. Concentrated sodium hydroxide, dissolved in ethanol, strongly heated: ELIMINATION, and pent-2-ene forms. Dilute, aqueous and mild favours the swap; concentrated, ethanolic and hot favours the ejection. Treat those condition sets as passwords, because classification questions print the conditions precisely so that the conditions can print the answer.

The final expert questions are with you now — a swap preserves saturation, both products get counted, and the conditions deliver the verdict.

## Subtopic: Burning Money and Bottling Fruit

Two reactions frame everything organic molecules are for: one takes them apart for energy, the other assembles them for fragrance. Combustion is the demolisher. Every alkane — the gas in the cylinder, petrol in the tank, wax in a candle — burns in plenty of oxygen down to the same two leftovers: carbon dioxide and water. The carbon skeleton you so carefully learned to name is dismantled entirely, and what you collect in exchange is the energy that held it together. That is the alkane family's entire job description: to be valuable when destroyed. Balancing the equation is a counting routine in a fixed order — carbons, then hydrogens, then oxygen, always oxygen last, because oxygen stands alone on its side and can be adjusted freely without disturbing anything else.

Esterification is the assembler, and it behaves like a wedding. A carboxylic acid — the sharp, vinegar-scented family — meets an alcohol. Add a drop of concentrated sulfuric acid as the officiant and a little warmth, and the two join at their oxygen ends, releasing a single small water molecule like confetti. The product is an ester, and esters are the reason sweets smell of pineapple and pears and bananas: the fragrance industry builds them in exactly this way.

The ester's name is its family tree read in a fixed order: alcohol parent first, with a Y L ending, acid parent second, with an O A T E ending. Methanol plus propanoic acid: methyl propanoate. The questions are bookkeeping, not romance: name the two parents, name the catalyst, name the ester, and always write the water — the equation is unbalanced without it.

Your first questions are with you now — dismantle the skeleton for energy, or marry acid to alcohol and bottle the fragrance.

## Subtopic: The Molecule with Open Hands

Why does an alkene react eagerly while an alkane sits inert in the same flask? Picture their hands. In an alkane every carbon has all four hands gripped tight — four single bonds, everyone fully occupied, nothing on offer. An alkene holds one bond of its double pair loosely, like two friends clasping with a spare hand each, ready to let go and grab something new. Addition is the world accepting that offer.

Offer hydrogen with a metal catalyst, and each doubled carbon grips a hydrogen: alkene becomes alkane. That is margarine manufacturing in one sentence — runny plant oil, nickel catalyst, hydrogen gas, hands filled, spreadable result. Offer red-brown bromine and watch the colour drain in seconds: the open hands snatch the bromine instantly, which is why the bromine test is chemistry's quickest verdict — instant fading means an alkene, slow fading in sunlight means an alkane. Offer water with an acid helper, and one carbon accepts the hydroxyl while the other accepts the leftover hydrogen: the alkene becomes an alcohol.

The interesting case is an offer of two DIFFERENT pieces — hydrogen chloride, say: one hydrogen, one chlorine. The molecule must decide which carbon receives which, and the rule sounds like township gossip but marks like science: the incoming hydrogen goes to the doubled carbon that already owns more hydrogens. The rich get richer. On but-1-ene, the end carbon of the double bond holds two hydrogens and the inner carbon holds one, so the new hydrogen joins the end, the chlorine takes the inner seat, and 2-chlorobutane fills most of the product bottle.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — full hands sit still, open hands grab, and the extra hydrogen always finds the richer carbon.

## Subtopic: The Dance Floor Rules

Here is the master key to classification questions: molecules do not choose their own reactions — the CONDITIONS choose for them. Same two partners, different lighting, entirely different dance. Questions print the conditions because the conditions are the answer in disguise.

Watch one molecule live two lives. Take 2-bromopentane and sodium hydroxide — one pairing, two possible futures. Serve the sodium hydroxide DILUTE, in water, with gentle warmth, and you get the courteous exchange: the hydroxide taps the bromine on the shoulder and slides into its exact seat on the chain. Substitution — calm conditions, polite swap, and pentan-2-ol walks away. Now serve the SAME sodium hydroxide CONCENTRATED, dissolved in ethanol, at a rolling boil, and the temper changes: rather than swapping in, the reagent rips a hydrogen off the carbon next door while the bromine storms out the far side, and a double bond snaps shut across the gap. Elimination — harsh conditions, dramatic exit, and pent-2-ene takes the floor. Two passwords cover it all: dilute, watery, warm — the swap; concentrated, ethanolic, hot — the ejection.

Lighting rules the alkane floor too. An alkane and bromine ignore each other completely in a dark cupboard — full hands, zero interest. Switch on ultraviolet light and the trade begins: one hydrogen out, one bromine in, and hydrogen bromide carries the leftovers away. No light, no reaction: the condition is the permission slip.

So read every organic reaction question in this order: conditions first, reactant second, and the product type all but announces itself. Concentrated acid and strong heat on an alcohol: dehydration, a double bond is coming. Light on an alkane: substitution. Hydrogen and a catalyst: addition, hands being filled. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — the lighting chooses the dance, and the question always prints the lighting.
