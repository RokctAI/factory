# Part 1 — Expert

Why does nail polish remover vanish from an open bottle while cooking oil just sits there? Why is propane a gas under your braai grid while an alcohol of similar size pours as a liquid? Every physical-property question in the organic section is a single question wearing disguises: how strongly do the MOLECULES attract one another? This session revises the three intermolecular forces, then works the three structural levers the syllabus tests — chain length, branching, and functional group — and ends with the four-step explanation scheme that answers every boiling-point and vapour-pressure question completely.

## Subtopic: The Forces Between Molecules

Begin with the distinction the whole topic stands on. The bonds INSIDE a molecule — the covalent bonds joining carbon to carbon and carbon to hydrogen — are interatomic forces, and they are strong. The attractions BETWEEN separate molecules are intermolecular forces, and they are far weaker. When a substance melts or boils, only the forces between molecules are overcome; each molecule leaves the liquid whole. Write that boiling "breaks the bonds" and the answer is already wrong.

Three intermolecular forces cover everything, ranked from weakest to strongest. LONDON forces — dispersion forces — operate between ALL molecules, polar or nonpolar, arising from momentary unevenness in electron clouds. For nonpolar molecules they are the only attraction available, so the alkanes, alkenes and alkynes depend on them entirely. London forces strengthen with molecular size: more electrons and more surface area mean a stronger grip.

DIPOLE-DIPOLE forces operate between polar molecules — molecules with a permanently positive end and a permanently negative end. The carbonyl group polarises aldehydes and ketones, so those families attract each other more strongly than alkanes of matching size.

HYDROGEN BONDS are the strongest of the three: an especially strong dipole attraction that arises when hydrogen is bonded directly to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. Every alcohol qualifies through its hydroxyl group — a hydrogen sitting on an oxygen. Carboxylic acids qualify doubly: the carboxyl group offers an oxygen-bound hydrogen AND a second oxygen to receive a neighbour's hydrogen, so acid molecules clamp together in pairs held by two hydrogen bonds at once.

That gives the family ladder for equal chain length: alkanes at the bottom, aldehydes and ketones next, alcohols above them, carboxylic acids on top. Memorise the ladder — the rest of the lesson climbs it.

This section's questions are with you now — name each family's forces, and keep interatomic and intermolecular in separate boxes forever.

## Subtopic: Chain Length and Branching

Lever one: chain length. March along the alkanes and watch the boiling points rise: ethane boils at minus 88,6 degrees Celsius, pentane at 36,1, hexane at 68,7, heptane at 98,4, and decane all the way up at 174. The trend never wavers, and the explanation is one clean sentence: longer chains have larger surfaces and more electrons, so the London forces between the molecules are stronger, and separating the molecules costs more energy. That is why the shortest alkanes are gases, the middle lengths are liquid fuels, and the longest are waxes.

Lever two: branching, which pulls the other way. Compare three isomers of C six H fourteen. Hexane, the straight chain, boils at 68,7 degrees Celsius. 2-methylpentane, carrying one branch, boils at 60,3. And 2,2-dimethylbutane, with two branches crowded onto one carbon, boils at just 49,7. Identical molecular formula, identical molar mass, and a slide of about nineteen degrees from straightest to stubbiest.

The reason is geometry. London forces act across surfaces in contact: two straight chains can lie against each other and attract along their entire length, like two rulers pressed face to face. Branching packs the molecule into a compact, nearly spherical lump, and lumps touch neighbours only in small patches. Less contact means weaker dispersion forces, means less energy to pull the molecules apart, means a lower boiling point. Say it the way the mark scheme wants it: MORE branching, LOWER boiling point, because the smaller contact surface weakens the London forces.

Melting points and vapour pressure will follow the same master switch, and vapour pressure gets its formal turn shortly: for now, hold the pairing — whatever strengthens the intermolecular forces raises the boiling point and lowers the vapour pressure, always together, always opposite.

Questions on the two levers are in front of you now — longer means higher, more branched means lower, and the reason is contact.

## Subtopic: Functional Groups and Boiling Points

Lever three, the heavyweight: the functional group. To see it cleanly, compare compounds of nearly equal molar mass, so London forces start on level terms and the functional group's contribution stands alone.

Line up three compounds near seventy grams per mole. Pentane — an alkane, molar mass 72 — boils at 36,1 degrees Celsius. Butanal — an aldehyde, also 72 — boils at 74,8. Butan-1-ol — an alcohol, molar mass 74 — boils at 117,7. Nearly identical masses; boiling points climbing by roughly forty degrees per rung, and the ladder of forces explains each step. Pentane is nonpolar: London forces only. Butanal's carbonyl group gives it a permanent dipole: London forces PLUS dipole-dipole attraction, and the boiling point jumps. Butan-1-ol hydrogen-bonds through its hydroxyl group: London forces plus the strongest intermolecular attraction in the topic, and the boiling point jumps again.

Now the top of the ladder. Butanoic acid, molar mass 88, boils at 163,5 degrees Celsius — far beyond the alcohol. Its carboxyl group hydrogen-bonds at two positions, and pairs of acid molecules grip each other in a double hydrogen bond, so escaping the liquid means tearing out of a dimer. Strongest forces, highest boiling point — the ladder keeps its promise.

The same reasoning settles solubility, which the syllabus touches briefly: water is itself a hydrogen-bonding liquid, so molecules able to hydrogen-bond with water — short alcohols, carboxylic acids — mix into it readily, while alkanes, offering only London forces, refuse. That is why petrol beads on a wet road instead of dissolving into the puddle.

And a discipline for comparison answers: state what you are holding constant. Comparing butan-1-ol with decane says nothing about functional groups, because the chain length moved too. Fair test in the laboratory, fair comparison on paper — one lever at a time.

This section's questions arrive now — rank families at equal mass, and name the exact force doing the lifting.

## Subtopic: Vapour Pressure and the Four-Step Answer

Vapour pressure is the pressure exerted by the vapour of a liquid in equilibrium with that liquid, in a closed container. It measures eagerness to escape: surface molecules with enough energy break free into the space above, and the easier the escape, the higher the vapour pressure. A liquid boils at the temperature where its vapour pressure equals the surrounding atmospheric pressure.

The link to everything before is a strict see-saw: STRONG intermolecular forces trap molecules in the liquid, so few escape — LOW vapour pressure, HIGH boiling point. WEAK intermolecular forces let molecules pour out — HIGH vapour pressure, LOW boiling point, and such liquids are called volatile. One ladder of forces, two dials pointing opposite ways.

Test it on our compounds. Hexane: London forces only, high vapour pressure, boiling point 68,7 — spill it and the bench is dry in a minute. Butan-1-ol: hydrogen-bonded, much lower vapour pressure, boiling at 117,7. Esters, holding dipole-dipole forces but unable to hydrogen-bond among themselves, sit in the middle — volatile enough that their fruity molecules drift to your nose, which is exactly why the flavour industry loves them.

Now the answer scheme, because these explanations are marked on structure. Every "explain the difference in boiling point or vapour pressure" answer walks four steps. Step one: identify the structural difference — chain length, branching, or functional group. Step two: name the intermolecular forces in each compound — London, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding. Step three: compare the strengths explicitly and give the reason — more surface, stronger dipole, hydrogen bond present. Step four: close with energy — stronger forces require more energy to overcome, hence the higher boiling point or the lower vapour pressure. Four sentences, in that order, and the word "energy" must appear in the final one.

The final expert questions are with you now — define vapour pressure exactly, keep the see-saw straight, and answer in four steps.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole topic again, this time with household equipment: velcro, fridge magnets and a bottle that empties itself.

## Subtopic: Molecules Are Wearing Velcro

Every molecule in this topic wears a strip of velcro, and boiling points are just the story of how much velcro touches.

The basic velcro is the London force, the kind every molecule owns. Each individual hook is feeble, but hooks add up with size: a long molecule is a long strip. Press two long strips together and they grip along their full length; ripping them apart takes real effort. That is the whole chain-length story: ethane, a stub of velcro, boils at minus 88,6 degrees, while decane, a long strip, holds on until 174. Boiling IS the ripping — heat the liquid until the molecules shake free of each other. The molecules themselves never tear; only their grip on each other lets go.

Branching crumples the strip into a ball. Same amount of velcro, but two crumpled balls meet only where they happen to bump — a patch, not a length. Watch the three molecules built from six carbons and fourteen hydrogens: the straight one boils at 68,7 degrees, the once-branched one at 60,3, and the double-branched ball at 49,7. Not one atom different — only the shape, and the shape decides the contact.

So two free rules. Longer chain: more velcro, higher boiling point. More branching: crumpled velcro, lower boiling point. One word underneath both: contact.

Your first questions are with you now — measure the strip, then ask how much of it can actually touch.

## Subtopic: Some Molecules Also Carry Magnets

Velcro is standard issue. Some families carry upgrades.

A molecule with a lopsided charge — slightly positive at one end, slightly negative at the other — behaves like a weak fridge magnet, the positive end of one clicking onto the negative end of the next. The families with a carbon-oxygen double bond — aldehydes and ketones — carry exactly this magnet. At equal size they out-grip any alkane: pentane, velcro only, boils at 36,1 degrees, while butanal, velcro plus magnets and practically the same mass, needs 74,8.

Then the premium equipment. Bond a hydrogen straight onto an oxygen — every alcohol's O H group does — and it reaches for the oxygen of the next molecule in a hydrogen bond, the strongest grip in the syllabus. That handshake lifts butan-1-ol, barely heavier than pentane, all the way to 117,7 degrees. And the carboxylic acids shake with BOTH hands: their molecules pair up, each clasping the other with two hydrogen bonds, which is why butanoic acid crowns the chart at 163,5.

The handshake also explains the swimming rules. Water is a hydrogen-bonding liquid, a whole crowd holding hands. An alcohol can join the handshake circle, so it dissolves. An alkane arrives offering nothing but velcro, and the circle does not open — petrol floats on a puddle, unmixed and unwelcome.

Carry one ladder into every exam, for molecules of similar size: alkane, then aldehyde or ketone, then alcohol, then carboxylic acid — each rung gripping harder and boiling higher than the rung below.

Questions on the upgrades are coming to you now — velcro, magnets, handshakes, in rising order.

## Subtopic: Why Nail Polish Remover Disappears

Leave the cap off nail polish remover and the bottle drains itself in days. Leave the cap off cooking oil and it waits for you, unmoved, for months. Both are liquids; the difference is how desperately their molecules want out — and vapour pressure is the measurement of that desperation.

Picture the liquid's surface as the exit gate at a stadium after the final whistle. In a weakly gripping liquid — velcro only, no upgrades — molecules constantly pick up enough jostle from heat to hop the gate and leave as gas. A stream of escapees means high vapour pressure, and we call the liquid volatile: nail polish remover, hexane drying off a lab bench, petrol flashing off a forecourt spill. In a strongly gripping liquid — hydrogen-bonded like an alcohol, or thick glycerine holding hands three ways — almost nobody clears the gate. Low vapour pressure, slow evaporation, high boiling point.

So vapour pressure and boiling point are one fact read from two ends: strong grip means high boiling point AND low vapour pressure; weak grip means low boiling point AND high vapour pressure. If your answer ever raises both dials together, one of them is lying.

You can literally smell this physics. Esters — the fruity family — are volatile enough that their molecules leap the gate and sail to your nose, which is why sweets and perfumes lean on them. A substance you can smell across a room has a healthy vapour pressure by definition. Cooking oil, gripping itself firmly, has almost no smell until the pan heats up — heat is the bribe that lifts molecules over the gate.

And whenever a question asks you to explain any of it, walk the same four steps: point at the structural difference, name each liquid's forces, say whose grip is stronger and why, and end with energy — stronger forces need more energy to overcome. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — weak grip leaves the bottle, strong grip stays for supper.
