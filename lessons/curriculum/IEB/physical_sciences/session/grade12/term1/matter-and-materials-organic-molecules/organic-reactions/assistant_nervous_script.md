Hey, it's Mandy — organic reactions can look like a spider's web of arrows, but I promise the whole section runs on one skill: reading conditions. Every question prints its conditions, and the conditions quietly print the answer.

Start by sorting with a count. Two molecules becoming one, with a double bond vanishing: addition. One molecule becoming two, with a double bond appearing: elimination. Two in, two out, saturation unchanged: substitution. Count in, count out, compare double bonds — that is the entire classification game.

Then confirm with the passwords. Catalyst and mild conditions: addition — hydrogen with platinum or nickel, bromine all on its own, water with an acid helper. Hot, concentrated, ethanolic sodium hydroxide: elimination, an alkene is coming. Dilute, aqueous, gently warmed: substitution, an alcohol is coming. Light shining on an alkane: substitution, and remember the hydrogen halide walks away as the second product. Concentrated sulfuric acid with strong heat on an alcohol: dehydration to the alkene.

Two placement rules, one idea behind both: in addition the incoming hydrogen joins the double-bond carbon that already has MORE hydrogens; in elimination the hydrogen leaves the neighbouring carbon with FEWER. Both choices build the deeper, more stable double bond — the rich get richer, both ways.

Esters are bookkeeping, nothing more: alcohol half first ending in Y L, acid half second ending in O A T E, concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst, and water as the second product everyone forgets. Say all four pieces, every time.

Combustion balances carbons, then hydrogens, then oxygen — in that order, always. Underline the conditions in every question before you write anything, and this section turns from a spider's web into a checklist. You've got this!
