Hey, it's Mandy — organic chemistry is the biggest chemistry topic of the year, and I remember staring at my first page of structures feeling like everyone else had been handed a dictionary I never got. Here is the honest truth: it IS a language, but it is the most regular language ever designed. No exceptions to memorise, no irregular verbs — one system that works on every molecule you will ever be shown.

Read every name from the back, in three pieces. The suffix is the surname: A N E means alkane, A N O L means alcohol, A N O I C acid means carboxylic acid. The root is the street length: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex, hept, oct — one through eight carbons. The numbers are house numbers. Pentan-2-ol: five-carbon street, alcohol family, O H at number two. Every name in every exercise falls apart into exactly those three pieces.

Building a structure is three passes, in the same order every time: lay the carbon spine, attach the functional group and branches at their numbers, then fill every spare bond with hydrogen. Never start with the hydrogens.

Then my favourite free checking trick — use it on every structure you ever draw: count to four on every carbon. Four bonds, exactly. A carbon holding five bonds is the most common error there is, and the count finds it in seconds.

Numbering has one rule that settles every dispute: the functional group takes the lowest number, and branches only decide when there is a tie or no group at all. Whisper "group first, branches after" while you count.

And for isomers, ask a single question: did a bond actually move? A bent drawing is the same molecule wearing a different pose. A carbon moved into a branch, a group at a new number, a changed family — those are the real isomers.

Backwards reading, three passes, count to four, group first. Do five molecules tonight and the language starts translating itself. You've got this!
