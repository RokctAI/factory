Hey, it's Mandy — physical properties questions look like they demand a head full of boiling points, and I promise they do not. Nobody expects you to know that hexane boils at 68,7 degrees. The data comes printed in the question; your entire job is to explain the pattern, and there is exactly one pattern to explain.

Everything — boiling point, melting point, vapour pressure, evaporation speed — hangs on a single question: how strongly do the molecules grip each other? Answer that, and every property falls out. Strong grip: high boiling point, low vapour pressure. Weak grip: low boiling point, high vapour pressure, gone from the bottle by Friday. The two dials never rise together — they are opposites, always.

Three levers control the grip, and each question pulls exactly one. Longer chain: bigger surface, stronger London forces, higher boiling point. More branching: crumpled ball, less contact, LOWER boiling point — that one feels backwards, so drill it until it feels normal. Stronger functional group: climb the ladder — alkane, then aldehyde or ketone, then alcohol, then carboxylic acid on top with its double handshake.

Then write the answer in four steps, same order every time. One: name the structural difference. Two: name the forces in each compound — London, dipole-dipole, or hydrogen bonding. Three: say which grip is stronger and why. Four: end with the word energy — stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is higher. That last sentence is the one everyone forgets, and it is the easiest of the four.

And the golden vocabulary rule: molecules never break when a substance boils. Only the forces BETWEEN molecules are overcome. Write "intermolecular forces" — never just "bonds".

One question, three levers, four steps, one forbidden word. That is the whole topic, and the data table does the memorising on your behalf. You've got this!
