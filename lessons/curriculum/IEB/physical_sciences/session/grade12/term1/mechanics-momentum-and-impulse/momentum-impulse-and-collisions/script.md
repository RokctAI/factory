# Part 1 — Expert

Momentum is where the final physics year begins, and it rewards precision from the very first line. This session states the definitions of momentum and impulse in the exact wording your written answers should use, develops Newton's second law in its momentum form, and then builds the two calculations that run the entire topic: the impulse-momentum theorem for a single object, and conservation of momentum for two objects interacting in a straight line. Every quantity here is a vector, so every solution begins by declaring a positive direction — and then obeying it to the final line.

## Subtopic: Momentum and Newton's Second Law in Momentum Form

The definition, word for word: linear momentum is the product of an object's mass and its velocity. In symbols, p equals m v. The unit is kilogram metre per second, and momentum is a VECTOR pointing in the direction of the velocity. Both factors in the product carry weight: a loaded minibus crawling through a rank and a dart leaving a blowgun can hold comparable momentum — one through sheer mass, the other through sheer speed.

First calculation. A 1 500 kilogram minibus travels east at 25 metres per second. Formula: p equals m v. Substitution: 1 500 multiplied by 25. Answer: 37 500 kilogram metres per second, east. Direction is part of the answer — omit it and the answer is incomplete.

Change in momentum is where vectors earn their keep. Delta p equals m v final minus m v initial, and the signs carry the physics. A 0,4 kilogram ball strikes a wall at 12 metres per second and rebounds at 9 metres per second. Declare the direction toward the wall positive. Initial momentum: 0,4 multiplied by positive 12, which is 4,8 kilogram metres per second. Final momentum: 0,4 multiplied by negative 9, which is negative 3,6. The change: negative 3,6 minus 4,8, which is negative 8,4 — a change of 8,4 kilogram metres per second directed away from the wall. Subtract 9 from 12 and answer 1,2 and you have treated vectors as plain numbers — the single most common error in this topic.

Now the law itself. Newton's second law in terms of momentum: the net force acting on an object equals the rate of change of the object's momentum. In symbols, F net equals delta p divided by delta t. This is the deeper form of the second law; the familiar F net equals m a drops out of it whenever the mass is constant.

This section's questions are with you now: definition quoted exactly, positive direction declared, and every subtraction done with signs.

## Subtopic: Impulse and the Impulse-Momentum Theorem

Impulse is the product of the net force and the time for which it acts: impulse equals F net multiplied by delta t. Its unit, the newton second, is identical to the kilogram metre per second — the unit of momentum — and that is the whole point, because the impulse-momentum theorem states that F net delta t equals m delta v: the impulse delivered to an object equals its change in momentum.

Work it on the tennis court. A serve arrives at a returner's racquet at 25 metres per second; the 0,06 kilogram ball leaves the strings at 35 metres per second, straight back. Declare the return direction positive, so the ball arrives at negative 25 and departs at positive 35. Formula: impulse equals m v final minus m v initial. Substitution: 0,06 multiplied by 35, minus 0,06 multiplied by negative 25 — that is 2,1 plus 1,5. Answer: 3,6 newton seconds in the return direction. If ball and strings touch for 0,005 seconds, the average net force is 3,6 divided by 0,005 — 720 newtons, roughly the weight of a fridge, delivered in five thousandths of a second.

The theorem's power lies in the trade between force and time. In any stop or crash, the change in momentum is fixed by the masses and velocities involved; nothing negotiates it downward. What CAN be engineered is the duration of the change, and since F net equals delta p over delta t, doubling the stopping time halves the average force. That one sentence is the physics behind every safety device the syllabus mentions. An airbag stretches the stopping time of a head from milliseconds to tenths of a second. A crumple zone lets the car fold so the stop takes longer. A gravel run-off bed beside a mountain pass brings a runaway truck to rest slowly. A cricketer rides the catch backwards; a long jumper lands in sand rather than on pavement — all the same trade.

When asked to explain, give the chain in order: delta p is fixed, delta t is increased, therefore F net decreases. The phrase "the airbag is soft" explains nothing and earns nothing.

Questions on impulse are in front of you now — honest signs, theorem quoted before substitution.

## Subtopic: Conservation of Linear Momentum

The principle, stated in full: the total linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant in magnitude and direction. An ISOLATED system — also called a closed system — is one on which no net external force acts. During any collision or explosion, the objects push on EACH OTHER with forces that are equal and opposite by Newton's third law; those internal forces shuffle momentum between the objects but can never change the system's total. Only an external force — an outside push, significant friction — can do that, and the standard instruction "ignore the effects of friction" is your licence to treat the system as isolated.

The method never varies: define the system, declare the positive direction, write total momentum before equals total momentum after, substitute with signs.

Work a recoil case. A 6 kilogram launcher, at rest, fires a 0,03 kilogram dart at 200 metres per second. Before the shot the total momentum is zero, so afterwards it must still be zero. Declare the dart's direction positive. Zero equals 0,03 multiplied by 200, plus 6 multiplied by the launcher's velocity. The dart carries 6 kilogram metres per second forward, so the launcher must carry 6 backward: its velocity is negative 1 metre per second — a recoil of 1 metre per second opposite to the dart. Note the asymmetry: momenta equal and opposite, velocities different by a factor of 200, exactly matching the mass ratio.

The same single equation governs every explosion, every pair of trolleys springing apart, every coupling of vehicles. Conservation asks nothing about the forces or times inside the contact — which is precisely what makes it so powerful.

This section's questions arrive now: define the system, quote the principle in full, and let the zero totals carry the algebra.

## Subtopic: Collisions in One Dimension — Elastic or Inelastic

Two objects meet along a straight line: momentum conservation finds the unknown velocity, and a kinetic energy audit classifies the collision.

The standard case. A 3 kilogram trolley moving at 4 metres per second runs into a stationary 2 kilogram trolley and the two lock together. Declare the initial motion positive. Momentum before: 3 multiplied by 4, plus 2 multiplied by zero — 12 kilogram metres per second. Momentum after: the combined 5 kilograms multiplied by v. So v equals 12 divided by 5: 2,4 metres per second, in the original direction.

Now the classification. An ELASTIC collision is one in which total kinetic energy is conserved; an INELASTIC collision is one in which it is not. Momentum is conserved in BOTH — that is the fact learners drop — so the only valid test is a kinetic energy calculation, before and after, separately. Before: a half multiplied by 3 multiplied by 4 squared — 24 joules. After: a half multiplied by 5 multiplied by 2,4 squared — a half multiplied by 5 multiplied by 5,76, which is 14,4 joules. Kinetic energy fell from 24 to 14,4 joules: the collision is inelastic, and the missing 9,6 joules left as heat, sound and deformation. Objects that couple together always test inelastic.

Once more at road scale. A 1 200 kilogram car travelling at 20 metres per second strikes a stationary 1 800 kilogram delivery truck and the vehicles lock. Momentum before: 24 000 kilogram metres per second. Combined mass: 3 000 kilograms. Velocity after: 8 metres per second. Kinetic energy before: a half multiplied by 1 200 multiplied by 400 — 240 000 joules. After: a half multiplied by 3 000 multiplied by 64 — 96 000 joules. The vanished 144 000 joules crumpled steel and shook the air: inelastic, proven by arithmetic rather than assumed from the story.

The final expert questions are with you now: momentum equation first, energy audit second, verdict last — every value signed, every answer in its unit.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same physics again, from the netball court and the kitchen, where your hands already know most of it.

## Subtopic: Moving Oomph — Why Mass and Speed Both Count

Imagine catching a netball lobbed across the court. Routine. Now imagine catching a shot-put arriving at the same speed. Your hands refuse before your brain finishes the thought — because the shot-put carries far more of something, and that something is momentum. Moving oomph: mass multiplied by speed, with a direction stapled on.

Both ingredients matter equally. A loaded cement truck rolling at walking pace carries enormous momentum through mass alone. A pellet from an air rifle carries plenty through speed alone. Double either ingredient and the oomph doubles. That is the entire formula: p equals m v.

Direction is not decoration. A ball that arrives at a wall at 12 and bounces back at 9 has not changed its momentum by 3 — it has changed it hugely, because it first surrendered every scrap of forward oomph and then acquired a new supply pointing the other way. Running from positive 12 to negative 9 is a longer journey than running from 12 to zero. That is why physics nails one direction down as positive and never lets go mid-problem: the signs are bookkeeping for which way the oomph points, and the bookkeeping is the physics.

Underneath it all sits one deep rule: only a net force from OUTSIDE can change an object's momentum — and the quicker the change is demanded, the harder that force must push.

Your first questions are with you now — oomph is mass times speed with a direction, and the signs never take a day off.

## Subtopic: The Egg and the Duvet

Here is an experiment best done in the imagination. Hurl an egg at a brick wall: instant scrambled. Hurl the same egg, just as fast, at a thick duvet pegged to the washing line: it sinks in, slows, and survives. Same egg, same speed, same change in momentum. What saved it?

Time — nothing else. Against the brick, the egg's momentum is destroyed in a millisecond, and forcing that change into a sliver of time demands a savage force. The duvet yields, and the identical change unfolds across half a second instead. Same total change, hundreds of times more time, hundreds of times less force — and eggshell can survive a small force.

That trade — same change, more time, less force — deserves a permanent place in your head, because it unlocks every safety question ever set. An airbag is a duvet for your face. A crumple zone is a car agreeing to be wrecked slowly so its passengers are not. The gravel run-off bed on a mountain pass is a duvet the length of a rugby field. A cricketer riding a fast catch backwards, a gymnast folding at the knees on landing — every one of them is purchasing time to shrink the force.

When the question says explain, deliver all three links: the change in momentum is fixed; the device increases the time over which the change happens; therefore the force is smaller. Three short sentences, and the explanation is complete.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — in every scenario, find the duvet.

## Subtopic: Trolleys That Crash and Stick

Roll a loaded supermarket trolley gently into a stationary empty one so they latch and move off together. Something remarkable hides in that ordinary event: the total oomph the instant before they touch equals the total oomph the instant after. The crash creates nothing and destroys nothing — it only redistributes.

Put numbers on it. Your loaded trolley: 3 units of mass at 4 of speed, so 12 units of oomph. It latches onto a stationary trolley of 2 mass units, and now 5 units of mass share the same 12 units of oomph — and 12 shared across 5 gives a speed of 2,4. The pair rolls away slower than the original trolley, yet nothing leaked: less speed, more mass, identical total momentum.

Why is the total untouchable? Because during the crash the trolleys push only on EACH OTHER — equal and opposite, Newton's third law — so every bit of momentum one loses, the other collects, like money moving between two pockets of the same jacket. Only an outside hand, or serious friction, can change what the jacket holds in total.

Energy answers to a different authority. Audit the movement energy before and after the latch-up and some has gone missing — spent on the clunk, the jolt, the bent trolley mesh. Collisions that keep their kinetic energy are called elastic; collisions that leak it are inelastic — and anything that sticks, couples or crumples is inelastic without exception. So keep the verdicts separate: momentum, always conserved in the crash; kinetic energy, only sometimes — and only the calculation can say which.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — share out the oomph first, then audit the energy.
