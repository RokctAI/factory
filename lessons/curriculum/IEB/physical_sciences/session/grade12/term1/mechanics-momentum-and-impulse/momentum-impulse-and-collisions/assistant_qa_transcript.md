**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up, the classic: momentum and kinetic energy are both built from mass and velocity, so why does physics keep both on the payroll? What do they actually do differently?

**Tutor:** They answer different questions and obey different laws, and a collision is where the difference stops being philosophical. Momentum is mass multiplied by velocity — a vector, direction included — and it is conserved in every collision inside an isolated system, elastic or not. Kinetic energy is a half m v squared — a scalar with no direction — and it survives only the special, rare case of a perfectly elastic collision. The square is what separates them. Because velocity gets squared, kinetic energy can never cancel: picture two identical minibuses driving toward each other at the same speed. Their total momentum is zero — two equal vectors pointing opposite ways — but their total kinetic energy is enormous, because both squared terms are positive. Let them collide head-on and stop dead: momentum is conserved perfectly, zero before and zero after, while nearly all that kinetic energy converts to heat, sound and twisted metal. So the division of labour is clean: use momentum conservation to calculate velocities after any collision, because momentum always survives; use kinetic energy as the auditor that classifies the collision as elastic or inelastic afterwards. Bookkeeping and health check — most collision questions quietly demand both.

***

**Mandy:** Next: rebound problems. The ball hits the wall at 12 and comes back at 9, and half the class writes a change in momentum of 1,2 instead of 8,4. What habit ends that mistake permanently?

**Tutor:** Make the sign convention the literal first line of the solution — write toward the wall is positive before touching any formula — and then refuse to write a single velocity without its sign. The error comes from computing big speed minus small speed, which subtracts magnitudes as if velocity were a plain number. It is not: the rebounding ball's final velocity is negative 9, not 9. Delta p is m v final minus m v initial: 0,4 multiplied by negative 9 gives negative 3,6; subtract 0,4 multiplied by positive 12, another 4,8 gone; total, negative 8,4 kilogram metres per second, reported as 8,4 kilogram metres per second away from the wall. Notice the rhythm — substitute with signs, compute, and only translate the sign into a direction in the closing sentence. There is also a physical sanity check worth installing: a ball that bounces back has undergone a bigger change of motion than a ball that merely stops, so a rebound answer must EXCEED m times the arrival speed. If yours came out smaller, a sign fell off somewhere. The same discipline flows straight into conservation problems, where one object typically ends up with a negative velocity, and into impulse, where the sign of your final answer hands you the direction of the force at no extra cost.

**Mandy:** Here is the safety-device one: if the change in momentum in a crash cannot be reduced, what does a crumple zone actually change — and why do the explanations insist on time rather than distance?

**Tutor:** The crumple zone changes exactly one quantity the theorem cares about: how long the stop takes. The car and its passengers arrive carrying momentum fixed by mass and speed, and that momentum must reach zero — no design can negotiate that away. Newton's second law in momentum form says the net force equals the change in momentum divided by the time over which it changes, so with the numerator locked, the denominator is the only lever. A rigid car stops in a few hundredths of a second; a car whose nose folds progressively keeps decelerating across several tenths. Stretch the time tenfold and the average force falls tenfold — and force is what injures people. Your explanation must run through time because the theorem itself is written in time — F net delta t equals m delta v — and the expected chain of reasoning is exactly: delta p is fixed, delta t increases, therefore F net decreases. Mentioning distance is not false physics — stopping over a longer distance is how the longer time gets bought — but distance belongs to the work-energy picture of the same crash, which is a later chapter's tool. In this topic, time is the currency. And add the quiet design bonus: the deliberate crumpling also converts kinetic energy into deformation on purpose, which is why engineers design the collision to be as inelastic as possible.

**Mandy:** Last one: the principle demands an isolated system, but nothing on Earth is truly isolated — there is always friction somewhere. So why are we allowed to use conservation for real collisions?

**Tutor:** Because of the timescale, and saying so earns respect in any answer. Conservation requires the net EXTERNAL force to be negligible over the interval you apply it to — and a collision lasts a few hundredths of a second. Within that sliver, the forces the colliding objects exert on each other are colossal — thousands of newtons and beyond — while road friction is trivial by comparison. Impulse is force multiplied by time, so a modest external force acting for a tiny time contributes almost no impulse, and the total momentum immediately before contact matches the total immediately after to an excellent approximation. That is why the standard method compares the instant before impact with the instant after impact and no later. Once the collision ends and the wreckage slides away, friction gets all the time it wants, delivers genuine impulse, and drains the momentum — which is why conservation may never be stretched from the moment of impact to the final resting place of the vehicles. Read the phrase ignore friction as permission to treat those few hundredths of a second as isolated, and keep your before and after snapshots pressed tight against the moment of contact. Choosing the snapshots well is most of the skill in this topic.
