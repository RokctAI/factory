Hey, it's Mandy — projectile motion looks like a storm of falling objects and flying minus signs, and I know the signs are the part that makes your stomach flip. Here is the secret nobody says plainly enough: this is the most predictable topic in the whole subject. The same three equations and the same three graphs, every single time.

Open every solution the same way, whatever the question: write your sign convention. "Upward positive" or "downward positive" — one short line. The acceleration is then settled for good: 9,8 with whatever sign your convention gives it, and it NEVER changes during the flight — not even at the top. That single line guards everything that follows.

Then mine the wording for data. "Dropped" means initial velocity zero. "Maximum height" means final velocity zero. "Returns to the hand" means displacement zero. "Thrown down at 6" means initial velocity 6 with the downward sign. Half the numbers in the question hide inside ordinary words, and underlining them is exactly what the strongest students do.

Choose the one equation holding your three knowns and one unknown, quote it, substitute with signs, solve. Time involved: the first two equations. Time missing: the velocity-squared equation. That is the entire decision tree.

For graphs, memorise one chain: position curves, velocity is a straight sloping line, acceleration is flat at 9,8. Gradient walks you along the chain; area under the velocity graph hands you displacement. And the bouncing ball is just the falling graph photocopied smaller and smaller — parallel teeth, plunges at the floor, zero crossings at each top.

The one trap everyone falls into exactly once: at the top, velocity is zero but the acceleration is still 9,8 downward. Say it out loud tonight. Say it twice.

Sign convention, word-mining, one equation, one graph chain. Four moves, practised until they bore you — and bored is precisely how you want to feel in an exam. You've got this!
