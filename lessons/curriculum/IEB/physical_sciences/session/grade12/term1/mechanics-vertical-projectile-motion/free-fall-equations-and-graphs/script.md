# Part 1 — Expert

A hammer dropped from a roof, a cricket ball hoisted straight up, a tennis ball bouncing down a driveway — vertical projectile motion is the most learnable calculation topic in the mechanics course, because a single force runs the show and three equations describe everything. This session locks in the sign conventions, works the standard calculations for dropped and thrown objects, and then moves to the graphs of position, velocity and acceleration — where the deepest understanding, and the trickiest questions, both live.

## Subtopic: Free Fall and the Equations of Motion

The definition first: a projectile is an object on which the only force acting is the gravitational force. No motors, no hands, and — by the syllabus's standing simplification — no air resistance. From the instant an object leaves your grip, whether climbing, falling or hanging momentarily at the top, it is in free fall, and its acceleration is 9,8 metres per second squared, directed downward, at every single instant.

Three equations of motion carry the entire topic. First: final velocity equals initial velocity plus acceleration multiplied by time. Second: displacement equals initial velocity multiplied by time, plus a half multiplied by the acceleration multiplied by the time squared. Third: final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared plus two multiplied by the acceleration multiplied by the displacement. Every symbol except time is a vector, so line one of every solution declares the sign convention — downward positive or upward positive, stated once and never switched mid-question.

The simplest case. A hammer slips from a roof 44,1 metres above the ground. Declare downward positive. Initial velocity: zero. Formula: displacement equals initial velocity times time plus a half a t squared. Substitution: 44,1 equals zero plus a half multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by t squared, so t squared equals 44,1 divided by 4,9, which is 9. Answer: the hammer falls for 3 seconds. Landing speed: final velocity equals zero plus 9,8 multiplied by 3 — 29,4 metres per second, downward.

Notice what the word "slips" handed over free of charge: initial velocity zero. In this topic the question's vocabulary IS data — "dropped" means initial velocity zero, "thrown downward at 6" means initial velocity 6 in the downward sense, "at its maximum height" means final velocity zero. Underline those phrases before reaching for a formula.

This section's questions are with you now: convention declared, equation quoted, substitution shown, answer with unit and direction.

## Subtopic: Thrown Upward — Symmetry and the Top of the Flight

Launch a ball straight up at 29,4 metres per second. Declare upward positive, so the acceleration is negative 9,8 metres per second squared — gravity pulls down even while the ball climbs.

At the very peak of the flight the velocity is zero, yet the acceleration remains 9,8 metres per second squared downward. Hold that sentence tight, because it decides a question nearly every cycle: the ball is momentarily at rest but still accelerating, because gravity never takes a break.

Time to the top. Formula: final velocity equals initial velocity plus a t. Substitution: zero equals 29,4 plus negative 9,8 multiplied by t. Answer: t equals 3 seconds. Maximum height: final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared plus 2 a delta y, so zero equals 29,4 squared minus 2 multiplied by 9,8 multiplied by delta y, giving delta y equals 864,36 divided by 19,6 — 44,1 metres.

Now collect the symmetry, the topic's free gift. The climb and the descent are mirror twins: time up equals time down, so the full flight back to the launch height lasts 6 seconds; and the ball returns through your hand's height at 29,4 metres per second, the exact speed it left with, now pointing down. At every height along the way, the upward speed equals the downward speed. The mirror works whenever the projectile lands at the height it started from — and shatters the moment it does not, as when a stone is thrown upward from a bridge and falls past its starting point into the gorge. There, trust the equations instead of the shortcut: one equation spanning the whole flight, with the full signed displacement, handles it cleanly.

One more distinction, cheap to learn and expensive to confuse: a ball that climbs 44,1 metres and returns has travelled a DISTANCE of 88,2 metres, but its DISPLACEMENT is zero. Read carefully which one the question wants.

The questions for this section are in front of you now — state the top-of-flight facts exactly, and let the mirror audit your arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The Three Graphs — Dropped, Thrown Up and Bouncing

Any projectile question can arrive wearing a graph, and three graphs tell the entire story: position against time, velocity against time, acceleration against time.

Easiest first: the acceleration-time graph is a horizontal line at 9,8 downward for the whole flight, no matter what the ball is doing. Climbing, dropping, pausing at the peak — the line does not flinch. Only contact with the ground interrupts it, because a ball touching the floor is no longer a projectile.

The velocity-time graph is a straight line with a constant gradient of magnitude 9,8. With upward positive and a launch at 29,4: the line starts at positive 29,4, descends steadily, cuts through zero at 3 seconds — the moment of maximum height — and reaches negative 29,4 at 6 seconds. The zero crossing marks the peak; the unchanging gradient IS gravity, drawn as a slope.

The position-time graph is a parabola: steep early, flattening to a horizontal tangent at the peak, steepening again on the descent. It is NOT a photograph of the ball's path — the ball travels straight up and straight down; the curve is height recorded against clock time, and mistaking it for a trajectory is the topic's classic misreading.

Then the bouncing ball, the topic's showpiece. Drop a ball and let it bounce, with downward declared positive. On the velocity-time graph: the line climbs from zero to the impact velocity, then the bounce appears as a near-vertical plunge to a negative value — the ball now travels upward — with a SMALLER magnitude than the arrival, because every real bounce spends energy. Then another straight segment with the identical gradient of 9,8, crossing zero at the top of the bounce, and the pattern repeats: a sawtooth of parallel lines, every tooth shorter than the one before. Between floor contacts the acceleration graph holds at 9,8 downward; the instants of contact are excluded from it.

Sketch questions reward shape, intercepts and parallel gradients. Questions on this section are with you now — rule straight lines wherever the physics is straight.

## Subtopic: Reading Numbers off the Graphs

These graphs are measuring instruments, and three read-out rules extract every number a question can request.

Rule one: the gradient of a position-time graph is the velocity. A horizontal tangent means momentarily at rest — for a projectile, the top of the flight. Steeper curve, faster motion.

Rule two: the gradient of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration. For any projectile segment that gradient is 9,8 in the downward sense — and reading it off is how a graph proves an object is in free fall.

Rule three: the area between a velocity-time graph and the time axis is the displacement. Area above the axis counts positive, area below counts negative; summed with signs it gives displacement, summed as magnitudes it gives distance.

Apply rule three to the ball launched up at 29,4, upward positive. From zero to 3 seconds the graph encloses a triangle above the axis: area equals a half multiplied by 3 multiplied by 29,4 — 44,1, the maximum height, lifted straight off the graph without touching an equation of motion. From 3 to 6 seconds an identical triangle hangs below the axis: negative 44,1. Displacement for the whole flight: zero, exactly right for a ball back at its launch height. Distance: 88,2 metres.

Finally, describing motion in words, which is asked in words and assessed in words. Cover four things: the direction of motion, whether speed is growing or shrinking, the value of the acceleration, and any special instants — the peak where velocity is zero, or a bounce where velocity reverses abruptly. For a segment running from positive velocity down toward zero: the ball moves upward, slowing at 9,8 metres per second squared, reaching maximum height at the instant its velocity is zero.

The final expert questions are with you now: gradient, gradient, area — and complete sentences for the descriptions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same motion once more, with no equations in sight — just a ball, a mental stopwatch, and gravity's single stubborn habit.

## Subtopic: Gravity's Tax — 9,8 Every Second

Gravity operates the most predictable tax office on Earth. Send a ball straight up, and every second of flight, gravity deducts 9,8 metres per second from your upward speed. No appeals, no holidays, no exemptions.

Launch a ball upward at 29,4 metres per second. One second later it is doing 19,6. Another second: 9,8. A third second: zero — the ball hangs weightless-looking at its peak for one instant. But the tax office does not close: one second later the ball is doing 9,8 downward, then 19,6 downward, then 29,4 downward — arriving back at your hand at precisely its launch speed. Three seconds up, three seconds down, the same speeds visited in reverse, like the upward trip replayed backwards.

That mirror is a free gift with every purchase. Time up equals time down. Speed at any height on the way up equals speed at that same height on the way down. Know half the story and the other half costs nothing — provided the ball lands at the height it started from.

And the trap that snares thousands: at the very peak, the speed is zero, so learners conclude the acceleration must be zero too. It is not. The tax collector does not resign the moment your balance reads zero. The acceleration holds at 9,8 metres per second squared downward for the entire flight — going up, at the top, coming down. Speed can be zero while the RATE OF CHANGE of speed runs at full strength.

Your first questions are with you now — count the seconds, deduct 9,8 each time, and never let the tax office close early.

## Subtopic: The Ball's Diary — Three Graphs, One Story

The three graphs of this topic are three diaries the ball writes simultaneously — one recording where it is, one recording how fast it moves, one recording gravity itself.

The height diary is an arch: rising steeply, levelling at the summit, falling steeply. Careful, though — that arch resembles a flight path, and it is not one. The ball travels straight up and straight down; the arch is height plotted against time, the way a bank balance graphed over a month is not a map of where you walked.

The speed diary is a dead-straight line sloping downward. It opens at the launch speed, slices through zero exactly at the peak, and continues into negative territory — negative meaning nothing more sinister than "now moving the other way". The line is straight because gravity's deduction is steady: the same 9,8 off, every second, no surprises. Wherever the speed diary reads zero, the height diary stands at its summit. The two diaries never disagree.

The gravity diary is the dullest document ever filed: a flat line at 9,8 downward from the moment of release to the moment of landing. Nothing happens on it — and that nothing IS the answer whenever a question asks what the acceleration was doing.

Extracting numbers: steepness of the height diary gives the speed. Steepness of the speed diary gives the acceleration. And the area trapped between the speed diary and the time axis gives how far the ball travelled — triangles, half base times height, geometry you have owned since primary school.

Questions on the diaries are coming to you now — match the summit of one graph to the zero of the next.

## Subtopic: The Bouncing Ball Without Tears

The bouncing-ball graph intimidates everyone on first sight, so assemble it slowly, one bounce at a time.

Release a tennis ball from shoulder height, and declare downward the positive direction. The speed diary starts at zero and climbs in a straight line — the falling ball gathers speed — until it smacks the paving. At that instant the diary does something violent: the line plunges almost vertically from a large positive value to a smaller negative one. Positive to negative, because the ball's direction flipped from down to up inside a few milliseconds. Smaller, because every genuine bounce pays a toll — energy leaves as sound and a squashed thud, so the ball departs slower than it arrived.

Then the story loops. Another straight segment with exactly the same slope — the tax rate never changes — crossing zero at the top of the bounce, growing positive as the ball drops again, another plunge at the floor, smaller again. The finished graph is a sawtooth: parallel straight teeth, each tooth shorter than the last, until the ball finally lies still.

Two landmarks let you read it like a professional. Every crossing of the zero line is a top of flight — the ball suspended for one instant in mid-air. Every near-vertical plunge is a floor strike. Between those landmarks every segment carries the identical slope, because between touches the ball is a pure projectile with gravity as its only companion.

Deliver that final sentence with confidence whenever a question asks: between bounces the acceleration is constant at 9,8 metres per second squared downward — and the parallel teeth of the graph are the proof, drawn for you.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — find the tops, find the strikes, and check every tooth is parallel.
