# Part 1 — Expert

This session runs a full chemistry practice set written specially for this class: seven questions covering the whole year, from organic naming through stoichiometry, rates, equilibrium and acids to the electrochemical cells. Each question is stated in full, attacked with a plan before any writing, worked to the final answer, and priced against this session's own marking guideline — every line tagged with the practice mark it earns. The aim is examination pace: read, plan, execute, audit, move on.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Naming and Isomers

The question. Consider a compound with a four-carbon chain, a hydroxyl group on carbon two, and a methyl branch on carbon three. First, write down its IUPAC name. Second, name the homologous series it belongs to. Third, give the name of a chain isomer of pentane. Fourth, define the term positional isomer.

The plan: run the naming algorithm in strict order — longest chain containing the functional group, lowest number to that group, substituents listed with their positions — and keep every definition word-perfect, because definitions are the cheapest marks in this set.

The name: the parent chain has four carbons carrying an alcohol — butanol. The hydroxyl claims the lowest possible number, carbon two; the methyl branch then sits on carbon three. The name assembles as 3-methylbutan-2-ol. In our marking guideline that is three marks: one for the parent and suffix, one for the hydroxyl position, one for the branch with its position. The audit: number from the other end and the hydroxyl would land on carbon three — higher than two — so the chosen numbering stands.

The series: alcohols — one mark, and keep the recognition sentence rehearsed: a homologous series is a family described by one general formula, each member differing from the next by C H two.

The chain isomer of pentane: same molecular formula, C five H twelve, different carbon skeleton — 2-methylbutane, a four-carbon chain wearing a methyl branch. One mark for the name or a correct structure. The definition mark: positional isomers have the same molecular formula and the same functional group, but the group sits at a different position on the chain — but-1-ene against but-2-ene is the standard pair. Two marks, and both halves must appear: same formula and group, different position. The classic forfeit is offering a compound with a different molecular formula, which is not an isomer of anything; count the atoms before answering, always.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Reaction Scheme

The question. A flow diagram shows but-1-ene reacting with hydrogen bromide to form compound X, the major product. X then reacts with dilute aqueous sodium hydroxide to form compound Y. Y is finally heated with concentrated sulfuric acid to form compound Z, the major product. First, name the type of reaction in each of the three steps. Second, identify X, with a reason. Third, identify Y and Z.

The plan: bonds first. Step one consumes a double bond — addition, specifically hydrohalogenation. Step two swaps one group for another on a saturated molecule — substitution, a hydrolysis. Step three removes water and rebuilds a double bond — elimination, specifically dehydration. Three marks, one per type, and the specific names earn wherever they are asked for.

Compound X, with the reason the marking guideline demands: when hydrogen bromide adds across the unsymmetrical double bond, the hydrogen atom bonds preferentially to the carbon that already holds more hydrogen atoms — for but-1-ene, the end carbon — so the bromine lands on carbon two. X is 2-bromobutane, the major product; 1-bromobutane is the minor. Identification, one mark; the rule stated as the reason, two marks. Naming the right compound without the rule leaves the reason marks on the table.

Compound Y: the dilute aqueous base swaps the bromine for a hydroxyl group — butan-2-ol. One mark. Compound Z: dehydration of butan-2-ol removes water and rebuilds a double bond, and the major product carries that double bond toward the middle of the chain — but-2-ene, with but-1-ene as the minor. One to two marks.

The conditions line is worth rehearsing as a chant, because conditions choose the product: substitution wants dilute aqueous base; elimination from an alcohol wants concentrated acid with heat; and had we used concentrated strong base in hot ethanol on X, the same molecule would have eliminated instead of substituting. Same reagents, different conditions, different product — that contrast is where careless marks go to die.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Stoichiometry and Percentage Purity

The question. A 4 gram sample of impure magnesium carbonate reacts with excess hydrochloric acid. The carbon dioxide released occupies 0,896 cubic decimetres at STP. Calculate the percentage purity of the sample. The equation: magnesium carbonate plus two hydrochloric acid gives magnesium chloride plus water plus carbon dioxide.

The plan: gas volume to moles through the molar volume, moles of gas to moles of carbonate through the balanced equation, moles to mass through the molar mass, and mass to percentage. Four conversions, one bridge each — write the chain as signposts before computing, because the chain is the marks.

Moles of carbon dioxide: volume over molar volume, 0,896 divided by 22,4 — 0,04 moles. Formula, one mark; answer, one. The balanced equation says one mole of carbonate releases one mole of carbon dioxide, so moles of pure magnesium carbonate: 0,04 moles. That ratio line is its own mark, and quoting it explicitly — one to one, read from the equation — is what secures it.

Mass of pure carbonate: moles times molar mass. Magnesium 24, carbon 12, three oxygens 48 — molar mass 84 grams per mole. Mass equals 0,04 times 84 — 3,36 grams. Two marks: molar mass assembled, mass computed.

Percentage purity: pure mass over sample mass, times 100 — 3,36 over 4, times 100, which is 84 percent. Final answer with the percent sign, one mark. The audit: purity must land between nought and 100, and an answer above 100 means the chain broke — usually the molar volume used at the wrong conditions, or a ratio inverted. Eighty-four percent of a mineral sample being carbonate is chemically sensible; the number passes.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Rates and the Collision Story

The question. A learner reacts 3 grams of magnesium ribbon with excess dilute acid and measures the gas collected. The reaction produces 0,12 moles of hydrogen in the first 40 seconds. First, calculate the average rate of hydrogen production over this interval. Second, the experiment is repeated with magnesium powder of the same mass; state and explain, in terms of the collision theory, how the rate changes. Third, sketch what a catalyst does to the potential energy diagram.

The plan: rate is change over time in whatever currency the question offers — here, moles per second — then collision theory for the explain marks, told with its two levers: the frequency of effective collisions and the fraction of particles above the activation energy.

The rate: 0,12 moles over 40 seconds — 0,003 moles per second. Formula, substitution, answer with unit — three marks in this set, and the unit moles per second is where the third one hides.

The powder: same mass, smaller pieces, larger total surface area. More acid particles can strike magnesium per second, so the frequency of effective collisions rises, and the rate INCREASES. Our marking guideline pays this as a chain — surface area up, collision frequency up, rate up — one mark per link, and the phrase effective collisions, meaning collisions with at least the activation energy and the correct orientation, is the vocabulary a marker scans for. Note what does not change: the total hydrogen eventually produced stays the same, because the same moles of magnesium react — faster, not more, and that exact distinction makes a reliable follow-up question.

The catalyst sketch: the potential energy diagram keeps its reactant level, its product level and its heat of reaction untouched; only the hump between them drops. A catalyst provides an alternative path of lower activation energy — more particles clear the lower bar, the rate rises, and the catalyst emerges unchanged. Marks: lower hump drawn on the same axes, one; both activation energies labelled, one; delta H unchanged, one. The audit is the untouched delta H — a catalyst that appears to change the heat of reaction is a drawing error, every time.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Equilibrium Calculation

The question. Nitrogen and hydrogen react in a closed 2 cubic decimetre vessel: N two plus three H two in equilibrium with two N H three. Initially 0,6 moles of nitrogen and 0,8 moles of hydrogen are sealed in; at equilibrium the vessel holds 0,4 moles of ammonia. Calculate the equilibrium constant.

The plan: the RICE table in moles, ratios from the balanced equation, then — the step every marker watches — divide by the volume for concentrations before touching the constant.

The table. Initial: 0,6 and 0,8 and nought. Change, anchored on the product: forming 0,4 moles of ammonia consumes 0,2 moles of nitrogen — the one-to-two ratio — and 0,6 moles of hydrogen, the three-to-two ratio. Equilibrium moles: nitrogen 0,6 minus 0,2 — 0,4; hydrogen 0,8 minus 0,6 — 0,2; ammonia 0,4. The change row and the equilibrium row carry two marks in this set, and the ratio reasoning must be visible.

Concentrations: each entry over 2 cubic decimetres — nitrogen 0,2; hydrogen 0,1; ammonia 0,2 moles per cubic decimetre. One mark, and it is the most forfeited line in the whole calculation: moles poured straight into the constant score nothing past this point.

The expression, from the balanced equation: K c equals ammonia concentration squared, over, nitrogen concentration times hydrogen concentration cubed. Writing it earns its own mark. Substituting: 0,2 squared over, 0,2 times 0,1 cubed — which is 0,04 over 0,0002 — 200. No units on K c, and the answer mark closes the question. The audit: a K c of 200 means an equilibrium leaning firmly toward products, consistent with a healthy amount of ammonia in the vessel — the number and the story agree.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — Titration and pH

The question. A learner titrates 25 cubic centimetres of a sodium hydroxide solution with 0,1 molar sulfuric acid from a burette. The endpoint arrives after 20 cubic centimetres of acid. The equation: sulfuric acid plus two sodium hydroxide gives sodium sulfate plus two water. First, calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide. Second, calculate the pH of a 0,01 molar nitric acid solution. Third, explain why a solution of ammonium chloride has a pH below 7.

The plan: titration is stoichiometry in glassware — moles of the known, ratio from the equation, concentration of the unknown — then the pH definition, then hydrolysis.

Moles of sulfuric acid: concentration times volume in cubic decimetres, 0,1 times 0,020 — 0,002 moles. One to two marks, and the volume conversion to cubic decimetres is the quiet trap. The equation demands two moles of base per mole of acid, so moles of sodium hydroxide: 0,004. The ratio line, one mark. Concentration: moles over volume, 0,004 over 0,025 — 0,16 moles per cubic decimetre. Formula, substitution, answer with unit — the standard trio.

The pH: nitric acid is strong, fully ionised, so the hydronium concentration equals 0,01, which is 10 to the minus 2. The definition does the rest: pH equals minus the log of the hydronium concentration — minus log of 10 to the minus 2 — pH 2. Two to three marks, and the definition line must be written, not just the answer.

The hydrolysis explanation: ammonium chloride is the salt of a STRONG acid and a WEAK base. The ammonium ion reacts with water, donating protons and forming hydronium ions, so the solution turns acidic — pH below 7. Salt classified, one mark; the hydrolysis reaction described, one; the pH conclusion, one. The audit runs on the pattern: strong-strong salts sit near 7, weak-acid-strong-base above 7, strong-acid-weak-base below — the answer must match the pattern, and here it does.

## Subtopic: Question Walk — The Galvanic Cell

The question. A standard galvanic cell couples an aluminium half-cell with a silver half-cell. Standard reduction potentials: silver plus 0,80 volts, aluminium minus 1,66 volts. First, identify the anode, with a reason. Second, write the half-reaction at each electrode. Third, calculate the standard cell emf. Fourth, write the overall cell reaction. Fifth, state the function of the salt bridge.

The plan: the table decides everything — the half-cell higher on the reduction list runs as reduction and is the cathode; the other reverses into oxidation at the anode. Then the emf from E cathode minus E anode, and the overall reaction from electron bookkeeping.

The anode: aluminium, because aluminium has the lower reduction potential — it is the stronger reducing agent, so aluminium is oxidised, and oxidation defines the anode. Identification with reason, two marks; naming aluminium without the reasoning earns one at most.

The half-reactions. At the anode: aluminium gives aluminium three plus, plus three electrons. At the cathode: silver ion plus one electron gives silver. One mark each, arrows and electrons on the correct sides.

The emf: E cell equals E cathode minus E anode — 0,80 minus, minus 1,66 — 2,46 volts. Formula, substitution, answer: three marks, and the double negative is the single most common slip in all of electrochemistry; rehearse the line aloud — nought comma eight nought minus, minus one comma six six.

The overall reaction balances the electrons: aluminium releases three, silver takes one, so the silver half-reaction triples. Aluminium plus three silver ions gives aluminium three plus, plus three silver. Two marks — species and balance. The audit: electrons must cancel exactly, three for three, and the positive emf confirms a spontaneous reaction, which is what galvanic means.

The salt bridge: it completes the circuit by allowing ions to migrate between the half-cells, maintaining electrical neutrality in both solutions. One to two marks. Close the run with the machine's fixed labels, good for both cells in any examination: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode — the energy direction changes between galvanic and electrolytic, the definitions never do.
