**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the one that breaks everyone's intuition: surely adding a component to a circuit should add opposition. Why does a resistor wired in parallel make the total resistance SMALLER?

**Tutor:** Because a parallel resistor is not another obstacle on the road — it is another road. For series wiring the intuition is exactly right: each added resistor stands in the one and only path, and every charge must fight its way through it, so the total grows. But a parallel resistor joins the same two junctions by a fresh route. The original branch is completely undisturbed — same resistance, same voltage, same current as before — yet the battery now pushes additional current through the new branch as well. Total current between the junctions rises, and since resistance is voltage divided by total current, the resistance falls. Carry the guard-rail with you: a parallel combination always comes out below its smallest branch. Six ohms alongside twelve gives four — below six. If your parallel answer ever lands above the smallest branch, you stopped at one over R and never flipped the fraction at the final step. And whenever the idea wobbles, go back to the intersections: three streets between the same two corners move more traffic than one street, however good that one street is. More streets, less overall opposition — without exception.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the mixed circuit, a learner took the full 24 volts and divided it by the 6 ohm branch to get 4 amperes, and every later line inherited the error. Which rule was skipped?

**Tutor:** The rule that the battery's voltage is a budget for the WHOLE loop, not the private property of one resistor. Before the current ever reaches the parallel pair it must cross the series 4 ohm resistor, and that crossing costs volts — current times resistance, 3 amperes times 4 ohms, which is 12 volts already spent. Only the remainder, 24 minus 12, so 12 volts, arrives at the parallel section, and it is that 12 volts, not 24, sitting across the 6 ohm branch and driving 2 amperes, not 4. The discipline that prevents the slip is to walk the loop in physical order and never use a voltage you have not earned: start at the battery, subtract each series drop as you meet it, and write the surviving voltage on the diagram before touching the branches. Behind it stands the loop rule: the potential differences around the circuit must add up to the battery's emf — 12 across the series resistor plus 12 across the pair makes 24. Run that addition at the end of every circuit question. When your labelled voltages refuse to total the battery, a resistor was skipped somewhere, and the audit finds it while there is still time to fix it.

**Bianca:** Here is the graph question: a test shows current against voltage for a resistor and for a bulb and asks which conductor is ohmic. Plenty of learners can recite the law yet still misread the two curves. What is the reading skill?

**Tutor:** Link the shape of each graph to whether the ratio stays constant. Ohm's law promises direct proportion between current and voltage AT CONSTANT TEMPERATURE, and direct proportion draws exactly one picture: a straight line through the origin. The resistor's plot — straight, passing through zero — is the ohmic one, its unchanging gradient the sign of an unchanging resistance. The bulb's plot bends, and the direction of the bend tells you the mechanism: as the voltage climbs, each extra volt wins less extra current, so the curve flattens. The reason is thermal — the filament runs blazing hot, resistance grows with temperature, and every new volt meets a stiffer filament than the last. The bulb never disproves the law; it breaks the law's own condition, the constant temperature, which is precisely what makes it such a well-loved test question. A complete answer usually needs three sentences: identify the straight line through the origin as the ohmic conductor; state that the filament's temperature rises with current, raising its resistance; conclude that the ratio of voltage to current does not stay constant, so the filament is non-ohmic. And before writing any of it, read the axis labels — a graph of voltage against current bends the opposite way from current against voltage, so check which one you were given, every time.

**Bianca:** Last one: a cost question gave a 1 500 watt appliance running for 20 minutes, and half the class produced answers in the hundreds or thousands of rand. Where does it go wrong, and what is the clean recipe?

**Tutor:** Two unit conversions get skipped, usually both at once. The kilowatt-hour recipe demands kilowatts and hours, and the question deliberately supplies watts and minutes. A learner who multiplies 1 500 by 20 is computing watt-minutes — a unit no electricity supplier has ever billed — and then treats the result as kilowatt-hours, inflating the cost absurdly. The clean recipe is two conversions, then two multiplications. Convert the power: 1 500 watts divided by 1 000 is 1,5 kilowatts. Convert the time: 20 minutes divided by 60 is a third of an hour. Multiply for the energy: 1,5 times a third is 0,5 kilowatt-hours. Multiply by the tariff: at 3 rand per kilowatt-hour, 0,5 times 3 is 1 rand 50. A sensible answer for twenty minutes of one appliance — and that sense check is your last line of defence. Everyday appliances cost cents to a few rand per use; a geyser costs tens of rand per day; so a heater that prices out at 600 rand for twenty minutes is announcing a skipped conversion, not an expensive heater. Keep one definition polished alongside the recipe: the kilowatt-hour measures ENERGY, the total amount used, while the kilowatt measures POWER, the rate of use. The account charges for how much, never for how fast.
