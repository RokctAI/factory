Hey, it's Bianca — electromagnetism can feel like two separate subjects sewn together: hand grips and field circles on one side, flux and emf calculations on the other. Let me fold it down for you, because the whole topic is two sentences and one formula.

Sentence one: a current makes a magnetic field. That is Oersted, and your right hand carries the entire theory — thumb along the current, fingers curling the way the field circles the wire. Coil the wire and you have built a magnet; for a solenoid, curl your fingers with the current in the windings and your thumb points to the north end. Every direction question in the first half of the topic is one of those two grips. Rehearse them with a pencil as the wire until your hand answers before your brain does.

Sentence two: a CHANGING magnetic field makes a current. That is Faraday, and the word doing all the work is changing. A magnet parked inside a coil, however powerful, generates nothing. So whenever a question holds something stationary and asks about the current, the answer is zero, because the flux is not changing. Movement, growth, collapse, rotation — those are what generate.

The formula is emf equals N times delta phi over delta t, and the safe route through it never changes. Compute the flux, B times A times cosine theta, remembering theta is measured from the NORMAL — the perpendicular mast on the coil, not the surface. Compute it twice, before and after. Subtract for delta phi. Multiply by the turns, divide by the time. And convert every length to metres before any area exists, because squared centimetres are where good answers go to die.

The minus sign in the formula simply says the induced current is contrary — it fights the change. Push a north pole at a coil and the coil turns north to push back. Say that in words when direction is asked; calculate with plain magnitudes everywhere else.

Two sentences, two grips, one formula in strict order. You've got this!
