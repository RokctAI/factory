# Part 1 — Expert

Two short sentences carry every generator on Earth: a current produces a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field produces a current. Oersted found the first, Faraday the second, and this session is the working machinery of both. We build the Right Hand Rule for straight wires, loops and solenoids, pin down magnetic flux as a precise quantity, state Faraday's law in wording that earns full credit, and close with the calculation this topic always comes back to: the emf induced in a multi-turn coil when the field through it changes.

## Subtopic: The Magnetic Field of a Current and the Right Hand Rule

Oersted's finding, compressed to one line: a current-carrying conductor creates a magnetic field around itself. Set a small compass beside a wire and the needle swings aside the instant current flows, then relaxes when the current stops. Electricity and magnetism are one subject wearing two coats.

Around a long STRAIGHT wire the field lines form concentric circles centred on the wire, packed tighter near the wire where the field is strongest. The Right Hand Rule reads off the direction: grip the wire with your right hand, THUMB along the conventional current, and your curled FINGERS trace the way the circles run. Reverse the current and every circle reverses with it.

Bend the wire into a single LOOP and apply the same grip to each short piece: all the circular fields thread the centre of the loop the same way, so the field passes straight through the middle, making one face of the loop behave as a north pole and the opposite face as a south pole.

Wind many loops side by side and you have a SOLENOID. The individual loop fields reinforce: inside, the field is strong, nearly uniform and directed along the axis; outside, it curves around in the familiar pattern of a bar magnet. For solenoids there is a second grip: curl the right-hand fingers with the current in the windings, and the thumb indicates the north pole end. Slide in an iron core and the field multiplies — and unlike any permanent magnet, this one has an off switch. Scrapyard cranes lift and release whole vehicles with switchable electromagnets, and relays and electric bells click on the same principle.

The syllabus also asks for a balanced word on high-voltage transmission lines, which carry large alternating currents and so wrap themselves in fields. The genuine issues are the wide servitudes of cleared land they require, their effect on bird flight paths, and public concern about living near them — while research has not established harm from ordinary everyday exposure levels. Two calm sentences covering both sides is the answer.

Attempt this section's questions now — right hand only, thumb for the current, fingers for the field.

## Subtopic: Magnetic Flux — Counting Field Through a Surface

Faraday's law cannot even be stated without a precise measure of how much field passes through a loop. That measure is magnetic flux, symbol phi. Definition: the product of the magnetic flux density and the area of the surface, times the cosine of the angle between the field and the NORMAL to the surface. In symbols, phi equals B A cosine theta. B is in tesla, A in square metres, and theta is always measured from the normal — the line standing perpendicular to the surface, never the surface itself. Flux is measured in weber; one weber is one tesla times one square metre.

The cosine is where marks are won and lost. Field passing STRAIGHT THROUGH the surface means the field runs along the normal: theta is zero, cosine is one, flux is at its maximum. Field lying IN the plane of the surface, skimming across it, sits at ninety degrees to the normal: cosine is zero, and the flux is zero — nothing threads the loop. Think of a window and the sun: face the sun squarely and the room floods with light; turn the window edge-on and nothing enters.

Now a calculation. A coil encloses an area of 0,02 square metres in a field of 0,6 tesla. Field along the normal: phi equals 0,6 multiplied by 0,02 multiplied by cosine of zero, which is 0,012 weber. Tilt the coil until its normal makes 60 degrees with the field: phi equals 0,6 times 0,02 times cosine of 60 degrees, which is 0,6 times 0,02 times 0,5, giving 0,006 weber. Identical coil, identical field — half the flux, purely from orientation.

Hold on to that sensitivity to angle. A generator is nothing but a coil rotating in a steady field, its flux swinging continuously between maximum and zero — and changing flux, as the next section proves, is what electricity is made from.

This section's questions are with you now — measure every angle from the normal, and convert areas to square metres first.

## Subtopic: Faraday's Law and the Direction of the Induced Current

Faraday's experiment is simple enough to repeat in class. Connect a solenoid to a galvanometer. Push a bar magnet in: the needle kicks. Hold the magnet perfectly still, even deep inside the coil: the needle drops to zero. Withdraw the magnet: the needle kicks the OPPOSITE way. It is motion that generates, never presence.

The law, in words worth memorising: the magnitude of the induced emf across a conductor is directly proportional to the rate of change of the magnetic flux through the conductor. Quicker change, larger emf. A magnet shoved in fast beats the same magnet eased in slowly; a stronger magnet or a coil with more turns raises the kick further.

Direction follows a wonderfully obstinate principle: the induced current always flows so as to OPPOSE the change producing it. Bring a north pole toward the face of a solenoid and the induced current circulates to make that face a NORTH pole, pushing the visitor away — resisting its approach. Draw the magnet back and the current reverses, turning the face into a SOUTH pole that tugs at the retreating magnet — resisting its departure. Pair this with the solenoid grip — thumb to the pole you need, fingers reveal the winding current — and every direction question in the topic is answerable in two steps.

The opposition is not a decoration; it is energy conservation in electrical form. If the induced current assisted the change, the approaching magnet would be pulled in harder, inducing still more current, and energy would multiply from nothing. Instead nature makes you work against the opposition, and the work you put in is precisely the electrical energy you draw out.

Work through this section's questions now — first name the change, then choose the pole that fights it.

## Subtopic: Calculating the Induced EMF

The quantitative form of Faraday's law: the induced emf equals the negative of the number of turns multiplied by the change in flux, divided by the time taken. In symbols, emf equals negative N delta phi over delta t. N counts the turns of the coil; delta phi is the final flux minus the initial flux through ONE turn; delta t is the duration of the change. The minus sign is the opposition principle written into algebra — in numerical work, calculate magnitudes and give directions in words.

Here is the standard calculation, worked completely. A coil of 150 turns encloses 0,04 square metres, with the plane of the coil perpendicular to the field so that the field lies along the normal and cosine theta is one. The field strengthens from 0,1 tesla to 0,5 tesla in 0,4 seconds. Find the induced emf.

Flux through one turn, before and after. Initial: 0,1 multiplied by 0,04, which is 0,004 weber. Final: 0,5 multiplied by 0,04, which is 0,02 weber. Delta phi: 0,02 minus 0,004, which is 0,016 weber.

Now the law, in magnitude. Formula: emf equals N delta phi over delta t. Substitution: 150 multiplied by 0,016, divided by 0,4. The numerator is 2,4; divided by 0,4 gives an emf of 6 volts.

If the coil belongs to a closed circuit of total resistance 2,4 ohms, Ohm's law completes the question: current equals emf over resistance, 6 divided by 2,4, which is 2,5 amperes.

The traps, ranked by harvest. Dropping N — a single loop is the same law with N equal to one, but a 150-turn coil multiplies the emf 150-fold, and forgetting it collapses the answer. Substituting B where delta phi belongs — the law runs on the CHANGE in flux, so compute both fluxes and subtract. Ignoring cosine theta when the field is off the normal. And the unit trap: areas must reach the formula in square metres, so centimetre dimensions must be converted before any multiplication.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — flux twice, subtract, multiply by turns, divide by time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same physics again, now as the story it actually was: a lucky accident, and the eleven stubborn years it took to reverse it.

## Subtopic: The Compass That Flinched

Copenhagen, 1820. A lecturer named Oersted is running a demonstration with electric currents when he catches a movement at the edge of the bench: each time the current switches on, a nearby compass needle FLINCHES. Current off — the needle settles. On again — another flinch. A wire is pushing a magnet around without touching it.

That accidental twitch is now rule one of the whole topic: every current dresses itself in a magnetic field. Around a straight wire the field circles like water spiralling around a plughole, and your right hand is the map — thumb pointing with the current, fingers curling the way the field turns.

To make the field earn its keep, coil the wire. One loop funnels all the circles through its centre. Hundreds of loops stacked into a solenoid build a genuine magnet with a north end and a south end — a bar magnet conjured out of electricity. Push an iron core inside and the field grows enormously. And here is the feature no natural magnet can offer: a SWITCH. A scrapyard crane grips a wrecked car, swings it across the yard, and releases it by simply cutting the current. A permanent magnet would never let go.

You have been hearing this rule your entire life without naming it: the buzz of the gate motor, the clack of a relay behind a wall, the low hum beneath a big transmission line — all of it is current wearing its magnetic coat.

Your questions on this section are with you now — thumb for current, fingers for field, iron for muscle, switch for the magic.

## Subtopic: How Much Field Fits Through the Window?

The second discovery needs one preparatory idea with an intimidating name: flux. Strip the name away and it is a counting exercise.

Picture the magnetic field as steady rain, and a loop of wire as the rim of a bucket. Flux asks a single question: how much rain passes through the opening? Three things control the answer. How heavily it rains — the field strength. How wide the bucket's mouth is — the area. And how the mouth is TILTED — the angle. Mouth facing the sky, you catch everything. Tilt it and you catch less. Lay the bucket fully on its side, rim edge-on to the rain, and you catch not a drop, no matter how hard it pours.

That is the entire formula: field, times area, times a tilt factor running from one when you face the rain squarely down to zero when you turn edge-on. In numbers from the first half: a coil of 0,02 square metres in a 0,6 tesla field catches 0,012 flux units facing squarely, 0,006 when tilted to sixty degrees, and nothing side-on.

Why bother counting? Because of the strangest rule in electricity, coming in the next section: nature pays you only when the count CHANGES. A gigantic flux that sits still generates absolutely nothing. Every generator on the planet is a machine for changing this count, over and over — so learn to count before you learn to change.

The questions for this section are coming to you now — rain, bucket, tilt, count.

## Subtopic: Change Makes Electricity

Once Oersted showed electricity making magnetism, every physicist in Europe wanted the reverse: magnetism making electricity. The obvious experiment was tried everywhere — lay a powerful magnet beside a coil and watch the meter. The meter read zero. It read zero for ELEVEN YEARS.

Faraday finally spotted the missing ingredient, and it was not power — it was CHANGE. Move the magnet toward the coil and current flows. Freeze, and the current dies that instant, even with the magnet resting inside the coil. Pull back, and current flows again, the other way. A stationary magnet, however mighty, makes nothing; a moving magnet makes electricity every single time, and the faster it moves, the more you get. Wind more turns and each loop catches the change for itself — a coil of a hundred and fifty turns pushes a hundred and fifty times harder than one loop.

There is also a personality to the current: it is contrary. It always flows the way that FIGHTS whatever you are doing. Push a north pole at the coil and the coil's face becomes north, pushing back at you. Retreat, and the face flips to south, clinging as you go. That contrariness is the price tag on electricity — the effort you spend overcoming it is exactly the energy that comes out of the wires.

Now zoom out to the national grid. A power station is this lesson at industrial scale: steam or falling water spins coils inside strong magnetic fields, the flux through each coil sweeping from full to empty and back many times each second — the bucket tilting into the rain and away again, endlessly. The changing count drives the current that crosses the country to your kettle. A bicycle dynamo lighting a lamp from a spinning wheel is the identical machine, scaled to fit your palm. Tonight, when your phone charges, the energy will have arrived by way of a compass that flinched and one word that took eleven years to find.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — no change, no current; fast change, big current; and the current always pushes back.
