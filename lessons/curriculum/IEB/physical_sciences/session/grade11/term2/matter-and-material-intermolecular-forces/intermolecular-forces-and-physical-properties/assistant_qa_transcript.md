**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First, the wording disaster: a learner wrote "the bonds between water molecules break when water boils" and lost the mark. Isn't that exactly what the lesson taught?

**Tutor:** The idea was right; the vocabulary sold it out — and in this topic, vocabulary carries the marks. The bare word "bond" means a chemical bond: the covalent weld inside a molecule. Write "bonds break when water boils" and the sentence must be read as hydrogen-to-oxygen bonds snapping, which would mean water decomposing into hydrogen and oxygen gas — false, and a serious error of chemistry. What actually gives way during boiling is the grip BETWEEN whole molecules. The sentence that earns everything is: the intermolecular forces between water molecules are overcome, while the covalent bonds within each molecule remain intact. Note the verb too — forces are OVERCOME rather than broken, a phrasing most marking guidelines prefer for exactly this reason. There is one honest wrinkle: the strongest intermolecular force is unhelpfully NAMED the hydrogen bond, so the sentence "hydrogen bonds between water molecules are overcome during boiling" is acceptable, because the phrase between molecules pins it down as intermolecular. The rule of survival: never let the naked word bond appear in a physical-change answer without declaring which level you mean — bonds within molecules, or forces between them. One precise clause guards the whole answer.

**Bianca:** Next, the paradox: if non-polar molecules have no charged ends, what is doing the attracting? Iodine is a solid, so something must hold it together — but the theory sounds like cheating.

**Tutor:** The paradox dissolves once you notice that "non-polar" is a statement about the AVERAGE, not about every instant. Electrons never park; they swarm continuously around the molecule, and at any given moment the swarm can slosh slightly to one side. For that instant the molecule wears a charged tip — a temporary dipole — even though its long-term average is perfectly balanced. And the flicker is contagious: it distorts the electron cloud of the neighbouring molecule into a matching lean, an induced dipole, and the two momentary dipoles attract. Each event is feeble and gone in a flash, but the flickers never cease, and they run between every pair of molecules in the sample at once. What turns feeble into formidable is scale: more electrons make a bigger, floppier, more polarisable cloud that flickers harder. Watch the halogen parade prove it — fluorine's small tight cloud leaves a gas; chlorine likewise; bromine's larger cloud manages a liquid; and iodine's big soft cloud flickers strongly enough to hold a solid together on your bench at room temperature. One mechanism, one force, growing smoothly with size. So no cheating anywhere: the attraction between non-polar molecules is entirely real — it is simply built out of moments instead of permanence.

**Bianca:** Here is the ranking question everyone fumbles: given a small non-polar molecule, a polar one and a hydrogen-bonding one, when does size decide the boiling point and when does force type decide?

**Tutor:** Apply a strict two-step decision, in order, and the fumbles vanish. Step one: classify the force TYPE in each substance — London only for non-polar molecules; dipole-dipole for polar ones; hydrogen bonding whenever hydrogen sits directly on nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine. If the types DIFFER, the type decides, and it usually crushes any size consideration: a hydrogen-bonded substance outboils a merely polar one, which outboils a comparable non-polar one. The water versus hydrogen sulfide duel is the standard exhibit — hydrogen sulfide carries nearly double the mass yet boils 160 degrees lower, because sulfur is not electronegative enough for hydrogen bonding and water's handshake overwhelms everything mass could contribute. Step two: only when the types MATCH does size take over — along the halogens, or down the alkanes, boiling points rise steadily with molecular mass as London forces grow with the electron count. Then close with the sentence that carries its own mark: the stronger forces require more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is higher. Structure every comparison that way — name the forces in each substance, say which are stronger and why, then the energy conclusion — and ranking questions turn from a gamble into a routine.

**Bianca:** Last one: sugar has no ions at all, yet it dissolves in water beautifully while candle wax refuses completely. Salt at least brings charges to the table — what is sugar's trick?

**Tutor:** Sugar arrives speaking water's own language. A sucrose molecule is studded with O H groups — eight of them — chemically the same oxygen-hydrogen arrangement that water itself is made of, and every one of them can form hydrogen bonds. So when sugar meets water there is no awkward negotiation: water molecules hydrogen-bond straight onto the sugar's O H groups, and the new sugar-water handshakes are of the same kind and comparable strength as the water-water handshakes they displace. The energy ledger balances, the crystal is peeled apart molecule by molecule, and the sugar disperses — no ions required. Note carefully that this is dissolving by hydrogen bonding, not by ion-dipole attraction, which is salt's mechanism; naming the wrong one costs the explanation. Candle wax, meanwhile, arrives with empty pockets: a long non-polar hydrocarbon whose only offering is weak London flickers. Hosting wax would force water to break its strong hydrogen-bond network in exchange for flickers — a terrible trade — so the water declines and closes ranks. That is the machinery inside like dissolves like: dissolving happens when the new solute-solvent attractions can pay, in equal or better currency, for the attractions that must be broken. Sugar pays in water's home currency; wax cannot pay at all. The habit to build: name the forces in the solute, in the solvent, and the new ones formed between them — then judge whether the trade clears.
