Hey, it's Bianca — intermolecular forces looks like a topic drowning in names: London, dispersion, induced dipole, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion-dipole, with van der Waals draped over half of them. Breathe. The entire topic is one sentence, and every question you will ever see is that sentence wearing a different outfit.

The sentence: the grip between molecules decides everything a substance does physically. Melting, boiling, evaporating, dissolving — each one is just asking how strong the grip is. Nothing in this topic ever breaks the molecules themselves; that would be a chemical reaction, and claiming it is the one guaranteed way to lose the mark. Bricks and mortar: bonds inside molecules are bricks, forces between molecules are mortar, and physical changes only ever touch the mortar.

Classifying the grip is a two-question quiz. Question one: is a hydrogen atom bonded directly to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine? Then the substance hydrogen-bonds — top grade. Question two, if not: is the molecule polar? Then dipole-dipole — middle grade. Otherwise it runs on London flickers alone — bottom grade, though big molecules flicker harder, which is why size matters inside a family.

Ranking questions then solve themselves: compare force TYPES first, and only when the types match let SIZE break the tie. Finish every comparison with the energy sentence — stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so the boiling point is higher. That sentence is a mark all by itself.

For solubility, think of a trade deal. Dissolving goes ahead when the new attractions between solute and solvent can pay for the old attractions being broken. Salt pays water in ion-dipole currency; sugar pays in water's own hydrogen-bond currency; wax shows up with nothing but flickers, and water walks away from the deal. Name the forces on both sides and state the verdict, and the marks follow.

One sentence, three grades of grip, one trade deal. That is the whole topic — now go prove it to yourself on the practice questions. You've got this!
