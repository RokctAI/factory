# Part 1 — Expert

Water is a small, light molecule, yet it sits calmly liquid at room temperature while the much heavier carbon dioxide floats around as gas. Surgical spirits vanishes off your skin in seconds; cooking oil stays put for hours. None of these puzzles is answered by looking inside the molecules — the answers live in the spaces BETWEEN them. This session draws the crucial line between forces between molecules and bonds within them, sorts the whole family of intermolecular forces, and then uses them to explain the four properties every exam probes: boiling point, melting point, vapour pressure and solubility.

## Subtopic: Between Molecules, Not Within Them

Every sample of a molecular substance contains two utterly different kinds of attraction, and mixing them up is the topic's original sin.

INTRAMOLECULAR forces are the chemical bonds INSIDE each molecule — the covalent bonds clamping water's two hydrogens onto its oxygen. They are strong, and breaking them is a chemical reaction that destroys the substance itself. INTERMOLECULAR forces act BETWEEN separate molecules — the attractions tying one water molecule to the ones around it. They are dramatically weaker, typically tens of times weaker than covalent bonds, and overcoming them changes only the physical state, never the identity of the substance.

Watch a pot of water boil with that distinction in mind. The steam leaving the pot is still H two O — every covalent bond intact, every molecule whole. All the stove's energy went into loosening the grip BETWEEN neighbouring molecules until they tore free into gas. Melting, boiling and evaporation are battles against INTERmolecular forces only. Had the covalent bonds broken instead, the pot would be venting hydrogen gas and oxygen gas — a decomposition, and an entirely different event.

From this comes the topic's master principle: the strength of a substance's intermolecular forces dictates its physical behaviour. Firm grip between molecules: high melting point, high boiling point, sluggish evaporation. Loose grip: all three reversed. Everything else in this session is that sentence, applied.

One wording habit before you move on: name the force you mean, every time. Writing that the bonds break when water boils is fatal ambiguity; writing that the intermolecular forces between water molecules are overcome is the sentence that scores.

## Subtopic: The Family of Intermolecular Forces

The attractions between molecules form a family — collectively the van der Waals forces — and which member shows up depends on what kind of molecules are involved.

LONDON FORCES, also called induced dipole or dispersion forces, act between ALL molecules, and for non-polar molecules they are the only attraction available. Electrons inside any molecule are perpetually on the move, and at any instant they may crowd briefly toward one side, giving the molecule a fleeting charged tip — a temporary dipole. That flicker nudges the electrons of the next molecule into a matching lean, and for that moment the two attract. Each flicker is feeble, but they never stop, and they grow with molecular SIZE: more electrons make a larger, more easily distorted cloud. Hence the famous parade of the halogens — fluorine and chlorine gases, bromine a liquid, iodine a solid — explained by nothing except London forces swelling with the electron count.

DIPOLE-DIPOLE FORCES act between POLAR molecules, the ones the previous lesson left holding permanent delta plus and delta minus ends. Positive end courts negative end throughout the liquid — a steadier, stronger grip than any flicker. Hydrogen chloride molecules cling this way. The hybrid case is the DIPOLE-INDUCED DIPOLE force, where a polar molecule imposes a temporary dipole on a non-polar neighbour — the mechanism that lets small amounts of oxygen dissolve in water, keeping fish alive.

HYDROGEN BONDING heads the family, under strict entry rules: hydrogen covalently bonded to nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine — the three small, fiercely electronegative atoms — is stripped so bare of electron density that it binds strongly to a lone pair on the N, O or F of a NEIGHBOURING molecule. Water, ammonia and hydrogen fluoride all qualify. The name misleads: this is NOT a chemical bond but an intermolecular force, about one tenth of a covalent bond's strength — yet several times stronger than ordinary dipole-dipole attraction.

Completing the picture, ION-DIPOLE FORCES act between ions and polar molecules — the force that operates every time salt meets water, and the bridge to the solubility story ahead.

The ladder, weakest to strongest: London, dipole-dipole, hydrogen bonding, ion-dipole — remembering that sheer molecular size can hoist London forces surprisingly high for very large molecules.

## Subtopic: Boiling Points, Vapour Pressure and the Water Anomaly

One logic drives every physical property here: stronger intermolecular forces demand more energy to overcome, and each property simply reads that demand differently.

Boiling point first. Boiling means molecules escaping their neighbours completely, so stronger forces push the boiling point up. Two comparison types dominate. WITHIN one family of similar molecules, size decides: the halogens' boiling points climb steadily from fluorine to iodine as swelling electron clouds feed the London forces. BETWEEN different force types, the type decides, and the showcase pairing is water against hydrogen sulfide. Same angular shape, same group of the periodic table — and hydrogen sulfide is nearly twice as heavy, so on size alone it should boil higher. Instead hydrogen sulfide boils at minus 60 degrees Celsius and water at plus 100. The gap of 160 degrees is hydrogen bonding: water forms it, while sulfur is too weakly electronegative to allow it. Force TYPE beats molecular size, spectacularly.

Water's hydrogen bonds sponsor its other famous anomalies: a huge specific heat that lets oceans steady the climate, surface tension strong enough for insects to stride across, and ice that floats — because hydrogen bonds freeze the solid into an open, roomy lattice less dense than the liquid it came from.

Vapour pressure is the same ranking read backwards. In any liquid the fastest surface molecules escape into the space above, and the pressure of that escaped vapour is the vapour pressure. WEAK forces release molecules easily: high vapour pressure, quick evaporation, low boiling point. STRONG forces detain them: low vapour pressure, slow evaporation, high boiling point. Propanone — the solvent in nail polish remover — has only weak attractions and streams off your hand in seconds, taking heat with it; water, netted by hydrogen bonds, lingers and dries slowly.

Melting point completes the set, with the same reading: stronger intermolecular forces hold the solid lattice together to higher temperatures.

## Subtopic: Dissolving, Ion-Dipole Forces and Like Dissolves Like

Stir a spoon of salt into water and the crystal is gone in a minute. The mechanism deserves to be known step by step, because explaining it earns real credit.

Sodium chloride is a rigid lattice of alternating positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions. Water molecules are dipoles — negative at the oxygen corner, positive on the hydrogen side. At the crystal's surface, water molecules mob each exposed ion with their attracting ends turned inward: oxygen corners toward the sodium ions, hydrogen sides toward the chlorides. No single water molecule could compete with the lattice, but dozens pulling in concert overcome its grip, lever the ion out, and close around it as a travelling escort — a hydration shell. Ion by ion, the crystal is dismantled and carried off. The energy bill for breaking the lattice is settled by the new ion-dipole attractions being formed.

The governing rule is LIKE DISSOLVES LIKE. Polar solvents take polar and ionic solutes: water dissolves salt through ion-dipole forces and dissolves sugar by hydrogen-bonding to the many O H groups studding each sugar molecule. Non-polar solvents take non-polar solutes: candle wax dissolves in petrol, both sides trading only London forces of similar worth. The cross-matches fail. Wax will not dissolve in water because water molecules grip one another with hydrogen bonds far stronger than anything wax can offer — the water holds ranks and shuts the wax out, which is exactly why oil and water split into layers in a salad dressing bottle. Salt will not dissolve in oil because the oil's feeble London forces cannot begin to pay the cost of demolishing an ionic lattice.

The complete answer pattern, worth practising until automatic: name the forces within the solute, the forces within the solvent, and the new forces on offer between them, then judge whether the new attractions can pay for the old. Reciting like dissolves like without the forces is half an answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chemistry retold as a story about grip — what clings, what lets go, and who pays for what.

## Subtopic: The Glue Between, Not the Glue Within

Picture a brick wall. Each brick is a solid object in its own right, and a separate, weaker material — the mortar — joins brick to brick. Molecules are built exactly like that. INSIDE each molecule, powerful chemical bonds weld the atoms into one unit: the brick. BETWEEN molecules, a far gentler stickiness joins neighbour to neighbour: the mortar.

Now the insight that unlocks everything: melting and boiling attack ONLY the mortar. Boil water and the steam is still water — every molecule whole, hydrogens still welded to oxygen. The heat merely broke the hold between neighbours so the molecules could scatter. Breaking the bricks themselves would take a chemical reaction, a much fiercer event, and the products would not be steam at all — they would be hydrogen and oxygen, new substances entirely.

So every physical property — how readily something melts, boils or evaporates — is really a gauge of MORTAR strength. Strong mortar: the substance hugs itself, demands serious heat to melt or boil, evaporates grudgingly. Weak mortar: it falls apart easily, boils cold, disappears into the air.

Your own skin can measure mortar. Dab on surgical spirits: cold, then gone within seconds, because its molecules barely hold hands and escape almost instantly, stealing warmth as they leave. Splash on water: it stays, and stays, because water grips itself with the strongest everyday mortar there is. Same skin, same day — different glue.

## Subtopic: Three Grades of Sticky

Mortar comes in grades, and molecules qualify for one of three, according to what kind of molecules they are.

Grade one: the flicker. Even a perfectly even-handed, non-polar molecule has electrons sloshing about inside, and for an instant the slosh can lean to one side — a momentary charged tip. The neighbour's electrons feel it and lean in sympathy, and for a heartbeat the pair attract. One flicker is nothing; billions, never stopping, add up. And bigger molecules — more electrons sloshing — flicker harder, which is why small non-polar molecules drift as gases while a big one like candle wax stands solid: enough weak flickers become a genuine grip.

Grade two: the permanent lean. Polar molecules carry a built-in positive end and negative end, so a crowd of them lines up plus-to-minus throughout — a steady, always-on grip, firmer than any flicker.

Grade three: the special handshake. Bond hydrogen to one of the three fierce pullers — nitrogen, oxygen or fluorine — and that hydrogen is left so exposed and so positive that it locks onto the oxygen or nitrogen of the NEXT molecule with startling strength. That lock is the celebrated hydrogen bond. The name oversells it: it is still mortar, not brick, roughly a tenth of a real chemical bond — but it dwarfs the other grades of sticky.

The handshake is why water is chemistry's great scandal. Set it against hydrogen sulfide: same bent shape, same family of the periodic table, and nearly double the weight, so it ought to cling harder and boil hotter. Reality: hydrogen sulfide boils at minus 60 degrees, water at plus 100. The entire 160-degree gap is grade three grip — water shakes hands; hydrogen sulfide can only flicker and lean. Strip water of its hydrogen bonds and every ocean, river and rain cloud on Earth would be vapour by breakfast.

## Subtopic: Why Salt Disappears and Oil Refuses

Stir salt into water: gone. Stir oil into water: it shrugs and floats. Watch the forces at work and both mysteries unravel.

A salt crystal is a stacked arrangement of charged ions, plus and minus, holding each other tightly. Each water molecule, though, is a little two-ended charged thing: negative at its oxygen corner, positive across its hydrogen side. At the crystal face, water molecules crowd in with their attracting ends first — oxygen corners courting the positive sodiums, hydrogen sides courting the negative chlorides. One water molecule alone would lose that contest; twenty together win it, popping the ion out of the lattice and sweeping it away wrapped in its own watery entourage. Ion after ion, the crystal is carried off. The salt still exists — it is simply travelling in style, fully chaperoned.

Oil gets the opposite reception. Its molecules are non-polar: no charged ends, nothing for water's charged corners to seize. Worse, the water molecules are already holding HANDS with each other through those powerful hydrogen bonds, and admitting an oil molecule would mean breaking good handshakes in exchange for nothing. So the water closes its circle and squeezes the oil out, drop joining drop, until the oil sits exiled in a layer on top. Water and oil do not really repel each other — water simply refuses a bad bargain.

There stands the famous rule, like dissolves like, with its engine exposed. Polar dissolves polar and ionic: water accepts salt and sugar. Non-polar dissolves non-polar: petrol strips candle wax, and dry-cleaning fluid floats grease out of a shirt that water left untouched. The mismatches fail because the attractions on offer cannot pay for the attractions that must be broken.

One rule has run this whole lesson: everything a substance does physically — melt, boil, evaporate, dissolve — is set by the grip between its molecules. Learn to read the grip, and the behaviour stops being a surprise.
