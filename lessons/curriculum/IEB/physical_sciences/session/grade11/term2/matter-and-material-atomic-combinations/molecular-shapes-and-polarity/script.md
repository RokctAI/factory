# Part 1 — Expert

Chemistry's opening question sounds almost childish: why do atoms stick together at all, and what shapes do the stuck-together results take? This session answers it properly. We build the covalent bond out of energy, practise the Lewis diagrams for the molecules the syllabus names, let electron-pair repulsion generate every shape you need, and finish with electronegativity — the single number that decides whether a bond shares its electrons fairly, shares them unfairly, or abandons sharing and hands them over. Intermolecular forces, organic chemistry, acids and bases — all of it stands on today's foundations.

## Subtopic: Why Atoms Bond, and the Lewis Diagram Toolkit

A covalent bond is the sharing of at least one pair of electrons by two atoms, and atoms share because sharing LOWERS their energy. Follow two hydrogen atoms drifting toward one another. Far apart, they ignore each other. Closer, each nucleus starts attracting the other atom's electron as well as its own, and the potential energy falls. At a separation of 74 picometres the energy bottoms out — attraction and the mutual repulsion of the two nuclei exactly balance — and that settling distance is the BOND LENGTH of the hydrogen molecule. Squeeze closer and the two positive nuclei shove each other apart, the energy soaring. The depth of that energy dip is the BOND ENERGY: the energy released when the bond forms, and identically the energy required to tear it apart. Now run the same approach with two helium atoms: each already owns a complete shell of two electrons, sharing lowers nothing, no energy dip appears, and no bond ever forms. Hydrogen pairs up while helium stays solitary — the standard demonstration that bonding is an energy transaction, not a preference.

Lewis diagrams are the bookkeeping. Draw each atom's valence electrons — the outer-shell electrons, the only ones that bond — as dots or crosses around the symbol. The procedure: read each atom's valence count off its group number; place shared pairs between the symbols as bonds; park the leftovers on their owners as LONE PAIRS, aiming for eight electrons per atom, two for hydrogen. Water: oxygen arrives with six valence electrons, each hydrogen with one; two shared pairs hold the hydrogens and two lone pairs stay on the oxygen. Ammonia: nitrogen's five electrons make three bonds and keep one lone pair. Methane: carbon's four electrons make four bonds, none left over.

When one shared pair cannot complete the shells, atoms share more. Two nitrogen atoms, five valence electrons each, share THREE pairs — a triple bond — with one lone pair remaining on each atom. Two oxygens share two pairs, a double bond. Hydrogen cyanide, H C N, joins hydrogen to carbon by a single pair and carbon to nitrogen by a triple. And in a DATIVE covalent bond, one atom donates BOTH electrons of the shared pair: a water molecule's lone pair captures a bare hydrogen ion to build the hydronium ion, and ammonia does the same to build ammonium. Once made, a dative bond cannot be told apart from any other covalent bond.

Attempt this section's questions now — valence electrons from the group number, and every single electron accounted for.

## Subtopic: Molecular Shape from Electron-Pair Repulsion

Molecules live in three dimensions, and one principle predicts their architecture: valence shell electron pair repulsion. Every electron pair around a central atom is a cloud of negative charge; the clouds repel each other and spread into the arrangement that puts them as far apart as geometry allows. Count the pairs, spread them, and read off the shape.

Two repulsion groups spread to opposite sides of the centre: a LINEAR molecule with 180 degrees between bonds — carbon dioxide, whose two double bonds each count as one group. Three bonding groups open into a flat triangle: TRIGONAL PLANAR, 120 degrees — boron trifluoride. Four bonding pairs reach for the corners of a tetrahedron: TETRAHEDRAL, 109,5 degrees — methane. Be suspicious of the flat cross drawn in every workbook: real methane is a tripod with a fourth bond rising from the centre, and the 90 degree angles on paper are an artefact of flat paper.

Lone pairs claim corners too — but invisibly, because a molecule's SHAPE is named from its atoms alone. Ammonia carries four pairs in the tetrahedral arrangement, one of them lone, so the shape you see is a TRIGONAL PYRAMID, nitrogen at the peak. Water carries four pairs of which two are lone, and what remains visible is ANGULAR — bent. Lone pairs also sit closer to their atom and repel more fiercely than bonding pairs, pinching the bond angles: 109,5 degrees in methane, roughly 107 in ammonia, roughly 104,5 in water. That trio — identical electron arrangement, shrinking visible angle — is the comparison to have ready in any exam.

The routine never varies: Lewis diagram first, then count bonding groups and lone pairs on the central atom, spread every pair, and name the shape from the visible atoms. Double and triple bonds each count as one group.

This section's questions are with you now — draw, count, spread, name.

## Subtopic: Electronegativity and the Polarity of Bonds

Electronegativity measures an atom's tendency to attract the shared electron pair in a chemical bond. It is a bare number on the Pauling scale, printed on your data sheet: fluorine rules at 4,0, oxygen 3,5, nitrogen and chlorine 3,0, carbon 2,5, hydrogen 2,1, sodium languishing at 0,9. The number climbs across a period and sinks down a group.

What matters in a bond is the DIFFERENCE — delta E N — between the two partners. Difference zero: the pair sits dead centre and the bond is NON-POLAR covalent. Any element bonded to its own kind shares perfectly — hydrogen with hydrogen, chlorine with chlorine. A moderate difference tilts the sharing: the pair loiters near the stronger attractor, which acquires a small negative charge, delta minus, while the weaker partner is left delta plus. That is a POLAR covalent bond — a bond with two charged ends, a dipole. Hydrogen fluoride: fluorine's 4,0 minus hydrogen's 2,1 leaves 1,9, a ferociously polar bond with fluorine delta minus. Water's oxygen-hydrogen bonds: 3,5 minus 2,1 is 1,4, strongly polar. A carbon-hydrogen bond: 2,5 minus 2,1 is a mere 0,4, only faintly polar. And when the difference stretches to around 2 or beyond, tilting becomes taking: the electron pair transfers outright, ions form, and the bond is IONIC. Sodium chloride: 3,0 minus 0,9 is 2,1, and the electron changes owners completely.

Carry this picture: bonding runs as one continuous slide from perfect sharing through lopsided sharing to outright transfer, with delta E N as the marker showing where a given bond sits. No wall separates covalent from ionic — only a gradient.

The skills to secure: compute differences from data-sheet values, rank bonds by polarity, pin delta plus and delta minus on the correct ends, and classify each bond as non-polar covalent, polar covalent or ionic.

Work through this section's questions now — subtract the smaller value from the larger before you classify anything.

## Subtopic: Polar Molecules, Bond Energy and Bond Length

A polar BOND does not automatically make a polar MOLECULE. The shape holds a veto, and testing that veto is one of the most reliable questions in the topic.

Carbon dioxide holds two strongly polar carbon-oxygen bonds, each oxygen tugging delta minus. But the molecule is LINEAR: the two bond dipoles are equal in size and point in exactly opposite directions, so they cancel completely, like two evenly matched teams pulling one rope. The verdict: a NON-POLAR molecule assembled from polar bonds. Water carries two polar bonds as well — but water is ANGULAR. Its dipoles lean partly the same way, like two ropes pulled at an angle toward the same corner, and they add. Water ends up with a genuinely negative oxygen end and a positive hydrogen side: a POLAR molecule. Generalise it: symmetric shapes — linear, trigonal planar, or a tetrahedron with four identical outer atoms like methane — cancel their dipoles and are non-polar; asymmetric shapes — bent water, pyramidal ammonia, or a tetrahedron with mixed corners such as chloromethane — keep a net dipole and are polar.

Why the fuss? Molecular polarity governs the intermolecular forces of the next topic, and through them boiling points, solubility and the behaviour of every liquid you know. Bank the sentence: a molecule is polar only if it contains polar bonds AND its shape does not cancel them.

Last, the strength of bonds. BOND LENGTH is the nucleus-to-nucleus distance at the energy minimum; BOND ENERGY is the energy needed to break the bond; and they run in opposite directions — shorter means stronger. Two trends control both. Extra shared pairs act as extra glue and pull the nuclei closer: carbon-carbon single, double and triple bonds grow progressively shorter and stronger, and nitrogen's triple bond is precisely why the nitrogen filling our atmosphere is so reluctant to react. Larger atoms bond at larger distances: their valence shells lie further out, so descending a group the bonds to hydrogen lengthen and weaken.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — polar bonds plus a shape that fails to cancel them make a polar molecule; more shared pairs and smaller atoms make shorter, stronger bonds.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chemistry again, told with an energy valley, a bunch of balloons and a playground rope.

## Subtopic: Why Atoms Hold Hands

Why does hydrogen gas travel in pairs — H two, two atoms hand in hand — while helium wanders the universe alone? Comfort. Atoms pair up when pairing makes them more comfortable, and in physics comfortable means LOWER ENERGY.

Watch two hydrogen atoms approach, each holding one electron in a shell with room for two. As the gap closes, each nucleus begins enjoying the other atom's electron along with its own; share both electrons between them and each atom feels its shell complete. The energy slides downhill like a marble finding a hollow, and at the bottom of the hollow they settle — near enough to share, not so near that the two positive nuclei begin shoving. That settling distance is the bond length. The depth of the hollow is the bond energy, and the deeper the hollow, the harder anyone must work to lift the atoms out and apart again.

Repeat the experiment with two helium atoms and nothing happens. Each already carries a full shell of two; sharing adds no comfort, no hollow forms, and the atoms sail past one another unmoved. That is the entire secret of bonding: it happens when it pays, and the payment is always lowered energy — never friendliness.

Chemists track the sharing with dot diagrams: each atom's outer electrons as dots, shared pairs sitting between the symbols like clasped hands, lone pairs resting on their owner like folded arms. Some atoms clasp once, some twice, some three times — two nitrogen atoms lock a triple handshake, which is why prising nitrogen molecules apart is famously hard work.

Your questions on this section are with you now — bonding is a marble settling into an energy hollow, nothing more mystical.

## Subtopic: Balloons Tied at the Centre

Knot four balloons together at their necks and release them: they snap into a tripod with one balloon standing up — a perfect tetrahedron. The balloons know no geometry; each simply shoves the others as far away as possible, and the tetrahedron is where the shoving balances. Electron pairs around an atom behave identically, and for the same reason: all negative, all repelling, all settling where the pushes cancel.

That single picture generates every shape on the syllabus. Two balloons point opposite ways: a LINEAR molecule, like carbon dioxide. Three make a flat triangle. Four make the tetrahedron — methane's true form, with 109,5 degree angles; the flat cross in your workbook is only the shadow that three dimensions cast on flat paper.

Here is the twist that examines well: some balloons are invisible. A lone pair — two electrons owned by the central atom but bonded to nothing — shoves like any balloon, yet when you look at the finished molecule you see only ATOMS. Ammonia holds four balloons with one invisible: what shows is a pyramid, nitrogen on top. Water holds four with two invisible: what shows is three atoms in a bent line — angular. And because lone pairs cuddle closer to their atom, they shove a little harder than bonding pairs, nipping the visible angles smaller: 109,5 in methane, about 107 in ammonia, about 104,5 in water. Four balloons every time; the angle tightens as more of them vanish from view.

So the drill is: draw the dots, count EVERY pair on the central atom, arrange them all as balloons, then name only what is visible.

The questions for this section are coming to you now — balloons shove, lone pairs shove harder, and you name what you can see.

## Subtopic: Fair and Unfair Tug-of-War

Every covalent bond is a tug-of-war over one shared pair of electrons, and each atom's pulling power has a published score — electronegativity, listed on your data sheet from sodium's frail 0,9 to fluorine's crushing 4,0.

Identical twins pull level. Chlorine against chlorine: the flag on the rope hangs dead centre, and the bond is NON-POLAR — a fair contest. Mismatched players tilt the game. Fluorine at 4,0 against hydrogen at 2,1: fluorine hogs the electrons most of the time, its end of the bond turns slightly negative, and the abandoned hydrogen end turns slightly positive. Chemists mark the slight charges delta minus and delta plus, and the bond becomes a dipole — a thing with two charged ends — growing more lopsided as the score gap widens. Push the gap to an adult versus a toddler — chlorine's 3,0 against sodium's 0,9, a gap of 2,1 — and the game stops being tug-of-war at all: chlorine simply confiscates the electron, both players become ions, and the bond is IONIC. Fair sharing, unfair sharing, confiscation: one smooth slide, your position on it read from the score difference.

Then the final twist, where the shape gets its vote: a molecule stuffed with unfair bonds can still be fair OVERALL if its geometry cancels the pulls. Carbon dioxide is a dead-straight line with an oxygen hauling at each end — two strong pulls, exactly opposed, summing to zero. The whole molecule is non-polar. Water's bend wrecks the cancellation: both hydrogens crowd one side, both pulls lean toward the oxygen, and the molecule keeps a negative corner and a positive face. That bend is the reason water is polar — and, as the coming lessons will show, the reason it dissolves salt, clings to glass and boils hot enough for tea.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — score gap for the bond, then shape check for the molecule.
