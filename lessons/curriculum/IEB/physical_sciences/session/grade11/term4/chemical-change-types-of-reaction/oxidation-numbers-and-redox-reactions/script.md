# Part 1 — Expert

The chemistry year ends by completing both of its great reaction families. Acid-base chemistry, which trades PROTONS, gets its final chapter here: designing a named salt from its acid and base parents. Then the lesson turns to the family that will dominate next year's electrochemistry — batteries, electrolysis, corrosion — the reactions that trade ELECTRONS. We build the accounting system called oxidation numbers, pin down oxidation and reduction as exact definitions, and finish with the half-reaction method that balances any redox equation using the table of standard reduction potentials on your data sheet.

## Subtopic: Choosing an Acid and a Base to Prepare a Given Salt

A salt is a child with two parents, and its name tells you exactly who they are. The positive ion — the metal — comes from a base; the negative ion comes from an acid. A preparation question simply asks you to run the family tree backwards: given the salt's name, identify both parents and write the neutralisation equation.

The matching rules are mechanical. The anion names the acid: a chloride needs hydrochloric acid, a nitrate needs nitric acid, a sulphate needs sulphuric acid, an ethanoate needs ethanoic acid. The cation names the base, which arrives as a hydroxide, an oxide or a carbonate of that metal: potassium hydroxide for potassium salts, magnesium oxide for magnesium salts, sodium carbonate for sodium salts.

Now run one of each base type forward. Potassium sulphate from a hydroxide: 2KOH plus H2SO4 gives K2SO4 plus 2H2O — two potassium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid gives potassium sulphate plus two water. Magnesium nitrate from an oxide: MgO plus 2HNO3 gives Mg(NO3)2 plus H2O — magnesium oxide plus two nitric acid gives magnesium nitrate plus water. Sodium chloride from a carbonate: Na2CO3 plus 2HCl gives 2NaCl plus H2O plus CO2 — sodium carbonate plus two hydrochloric acid gives sodium chloride, water and carbon dioxide, the fizz that betrays every carbonate.

The coefficients are not guesses; they are ion charges made visible. Write the salt's formula first. Magnesium carries two plus and nitrate one minus, so magnesium nitrate must be Mg(NO3)2 — and two nitrate ions demand two deliveries of nitric acid. Sulphuric acid carries two acidic hydrogens, so it consumes two hydroxides — hence the 2 in front of KOH. Formula from charges first; coefficients follow without effort.

This section's questions are with you now — both parents read from the name, one equation per base type, and the charges doing the balancing.

## Subtopic: Oxidation Numbers and the Rules for Assigning Them

Electron-transfer reactions have a bookkeeping problem: electrons do not appear in formulas. The OXIDATION NUMBER solves it. It is the charge an atom WOULD carry if every bonding pair were handed entirely to the more electronegative partner — an accounting fiction, not a real charge, but a fiction kept so consistently that it never misreports electron movement.

The rules, in order of authority. One: a free element sits at zero — the atoms in N2, Mg, S8, all zero. Two: a simple ion's number equals its charge. Three: fluorine in any compound is minus one. Four: hydrogen in compounds is plus one — except in metal hydrides, where it is minus one. Five: oxygen in compounds is minus two — except in peroxides, where it is minus one. Six, the closing rule that solves everything: the numbers in a neutral compound must sum to zero, and in a polyatomic ion, to the ion's charge.

Practise the rules on a fresh set. Ammonia, NH3: three hydrogens at plus one give plus three, so nitrogen must be minus three. Hydrogen sulphide, H2S: two hydrogens at plus one force sulphur to minus two. Sulphur dioxide, SO2: two oxygens contribute minus four, so sulphur is plus four. The sulphate ion, SO4 two minus: four oxygens give minus eight, and the total must be minus two, so sulphur sits at PLUS six — one element running from minus two to plus six, which is why sulphur chemistry fills factories. Sodium peroxide, Na2O2: two sodiums at plus one force each oxygen to minus one — the peroxide exception, caught by the sum rule. And potassium permanganate, KMnO4, the deep purple crystal: potassium plus one, four oxygens minus eight, so manganese must be plus seven to close the books.

The discipline never changes: assign the confident atoms by the rule hierarchy, then let the sum rule solve for the stranger. The questions for this section are ready — the six rules, the two exceptions, and the practice set rerun.

## Subtopic: Redox as Electron Transfer — Agents and Their Names

The definitions, stated exactly. OXIDATION is the LOSS of electrons; REDUCTION is the GAIN of electrons. OIL RIG holds it: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain. Translated into bookkeeping: oxidation raises an oxidation number, reduction lowers it. A REDOX reaction is any reaction in which oxidation numbers change — real electrons genuinely moving from one species to another.

Watch the classic silver demonstration. Stand a coil of copper wire in a colourless silver nitrate solution. Glittering silver crystals fur the wire, and the solution slowly turns blue. The equation: Cu plus 2Ag plus gives Cu two plus, plus 2Ag — copper metal plus two silver ions give copper ions plus silver metal. Audit it. Copper went from zero to plus two: it LOST two electrons — oxidation, and the blue colour is the copper ion announcing itself. Each silver ion went from plus one to zero: it GAINED an electron — reduction, and the crystals are the receipts. The two changes are inseparable: every electron surrendered is an electron collected, which is why the process is one word, redox, and why neither half ever happens alone.

Now the naming that inverts in everyone's head. The OXIDISING AGENT is the species that CAUSES oxidation in its partner — which it can only do by taking the electrons, so it is itself REDUCED. Here, the silver ion. The REDUCING AGENT causes reduction by donating electrons, and is itself OXIDISED — here, the copper. Fix the rhythm: the oxidising agent is reduced; the reducing agent is oxidised. An agent is named for what it does to the OTHER species, the way an arsonist is named for what happens to the building, not to himself.

The audit works even when no charges are visible. Magnesium burning: 2Mg plus O2 gives 2MgO. Magnesium rises from zero to plus two — oxidised. Oxygen falls from zero to minus two — reduced. Every combustion, every rusting gate, every cell in every torch is this same trade.

Take this section's questions now — OIL RIG, the copper-and-silver audit, and both agents named without inverting them.

## Subtopic: Balancing Redox Equations with Half-Reactions

A redox reaction is two transactions running simultaneously, and the half-reaction method writes each one on its own line before joining them. The oxidation half-reaction shows the donor releasing electrons; the reduction half-reaction shows the acceptor collecting them. Your data sheet's TABLE OF STANDARD REDUCTION POTENTIALS lists half-reactions written, every one of them, as reductions — ion plus electrons gives element. The species that actually donates must have its line REVERSED before use.

The method in four steps. Step one: select the two half-reactions from the table, reversing the oxidation. Step two: balance the ELECTRONS — multiply each line until both carry the same number, because every electron released must be consumed. Step three: add the lines and cancel the electrons. Step four: audit — atoms equal on both sides, total charge equal on both sides, no electron left visible.

Work magnesium metal reducing iron three ions. Magnesium donates, so reverse its table entry: Mg gives Mg two plus, plus two electrons. Iron's line stays a reduction: Fe three plus, plus three electrons, gives Fe. Electron counts: two against three — lowest common multiple six. Multiply magnesium's line by three: 3Mg gives 3Mg two plus, plus six electrons. Multiply iron's line by two: 2Fe three plus, plus six electrons, gives 2Fe. Add and cancel the six electrons: 3Mg plus 2Fe three plus gives 3Mg two plus, plus 2Fe — three magnesium atoms plus two iron ions give three magnesium ions plus two iron atoms. Audit: magnesium three and three; iron two and two; charge plus six on the left, plus six on the right. Balanced.

Read the roles off the finished equation: magnesium lost electrons — oxidised, the reducing agent; the iron ions gained — reduced, the oxidising agent. The three classic errors: using the donor's line unreversed; adding before the electron counts match; and leaving electrons standing in the final equation, where they may never appear.

The final questions of this part are with you now — table, reverse, match, add, audit, in that order every time.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry told twice more simply: salts as family names, and electrons as coins.

## Subtopic: Assembling a Salt Like a Full Name

Read any salt's name as a full name. The first name — the metal — was inherited from the base. The surname — the family ending — belongs to an acid. So a question saying "describe the preparation of magnesium nitrate" is really a parentage puzzle: magnesium, so the base is magnesium oxide or magnesium hydroxide; nitrate, so the acid is nitric. Put the parents together and the equation writes itself: base plus acid gives salt plus water.

The base shows up in one of three costumes, and the products barely notice. A hydroxide — potassium hydroxide turning sulphuric acid into potassium sulphate: salt plus water. An oxide — the white magnesium oxide powder dissolving in nitric acid: salt plus water again. Or a carbonate — sodium carbonate meeting hydrochloric acid: salt plus water PLUS a rush of carbon dioxide bubbles. The carbonate always fizzes on its way out; the other two costumes leave quietly.

Balancing is charge arithmetic done before anything else. Magnesium is two plus and nitrate is one minus, so the salt must be Mg(NO3)2 — and the 2 in front of HNO3 follows instantly, because two nitrates require two acid molecules to deliver them. Sulphuric acid brings two hydrogens to every encounter, so it needs two hydroxides to absorb them: the 2 in front of KOH is just the second hydrogen made visible.

First name, surname, three costumes, charges before coefficients. That is the whole of salt preparation. Your questions for this section are with you now.

## Subtopic: Electron Bookkeeping — Every Atom Gets an Account

The second family of reactions trades electrons — but formulas hide electrons completely. Chemists fixed this the way banks fixed money: every atom in every formula holds an account, and the balance on that account is its oxidation number. Compare the balances before and after a reaction, and you can see precisely who paid and who collected, even though no electron is ever drawn.

The account rules are short. A pure element — a magnesium ribbon, nitrogen gas — stands at zero: it has traded nothing. A simple ion's balance is its charge. Inside compounds, the regular customers have habits: hydrogen at plus one, oxygen at minus two — with two celebrated exceptions, hydrogen at minus one when bonded to a metal, oxygen at minus one in a peroxide. And the golden rule that audits every account: a neutral compound's balances sum to ZERO; an ion's sum to its charge. That closing rule is your solver — fill in the atoms you know, and the last unknown balance falls out.

Watch it work. Ammonia: three hydrogens at plus one push nitrogen down to minus three. Hydrogen sulphide: sulphur sits at minus two. Burn sulphur into SO2 and it jumps to plus four; lock it into a sulphate ion and it reaches plus six — one atom swinging eight units, which is the books' way of saying serious electrons moved. Sodium peroxide: the two sodiums' plus two forces each oxygen to minus one — the exception detected by arithmetic, not memory. Potassium permanganate: plus one and minus eight leave manganese at plus seven, the highest balance in the school laboratory.

Nothing is memorised except the short rule list; everything else is addition. Balance up means electrons paid out; balance down means electrons collected — and that is the whole of the next section. Your bookkeeping questions are ready now.

## Subtopic: Coins Change Pockets — and the Trade Must Balance

Read a redox reaction as coins changing pockets. Stand copper wire in silver nitrate solution: silver fur grows on the wire, and the liquid blushes blue. The account audit: copper went from zero to plus two — it PAID two electron coins. Each silver ion went from plus one to zero — it COLLECTED one. Paying is oxidation, losing electrons; collecting is reduction, gaining them. OIL RIG. And no coin is ever dropped: every electron paid is pocketed by somebody, which is why oxidation and reduction arrive strictly as a pair, and the pair is called redox.

The naming trap, disarmed with one picture: agents are titled for what they do to the OTHER party. The silver ion, by lifting copper's electrons, caused copper's oxidation — so silver is the OXIDISING agent, and it walked away reduced. Copper, by handing over the coins that reduced the silver, is the REDUCING agent, itself oxidised. The pickpocket is named after the victim's loss. Chant it until it bores you: oxidising agent gets reduced; reducing agent gets oxidised.

Balancing a redox equation is making the till balance. The data sheet's table lists every trader's collecting line. Choose your two traders; the one actually PAYING gets its line flipped. Then match the amounts: magnesium pays in twos, iron three plus collects in threes — so send three magnesiums against two irons, six coins each way, a perfectly even trade. Add the lines, cancel the six coins in transit, and the finished equation shows none: three magnesium plus two iron ions gives three magnesium ions plus two iron atoms. Atoms agree, charges agree, till closed.

That closing audit is redox in one habit — and next year you will run this exact trade through a wire, where the passing coins become an electric current and the beaker becomes a battery. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — balances first, payer and collector named, and never a coin left lying in the equation.
