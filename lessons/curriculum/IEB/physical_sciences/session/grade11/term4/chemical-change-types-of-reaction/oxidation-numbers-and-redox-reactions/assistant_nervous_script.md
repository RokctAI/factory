Hey, it's Bianca — redox looks like a topic designed to trip you: numbers that aren't real charges, agents that seem named backwards, a data-sheet table, half-reactions... I promise the secret is smaller than the reputation: this whole topic is bookkeeping, and bookkeeping is careful, boring steps. Careful, boring steps are exactly the kind of thing you can learn.

Warm up with the salt-preparation piece, because it is genuinely friendly: read the salt's name like a full name. First name is the metal — that came from the base. Surname is the acid's family — nitrate from nitric, sulphate from sulphuric, chloride from hydrochloric. Base plus acid gives salt plus water, and if the base arrived as a carbonate, add the fizz of carbon dioxide. Nothing memorised — just decoded.

Then the electron bookkeeping, in three moves. Move one: assign oxidation numbers from the short rule list — elements zero, ions their charge, hydrogen plus one, oxygen minus two — and let the sum-to-zero rule solve the last unknown atom. The exceptions cannot ambush you, because the sum rule catches them: if minus two for oxygen breaks the total, you are looking at a peroxide, and the arithmetic just told you so.

Move two: compare balances before and after. Up means electrons lost — oxidised. Down means gained — reduced. OIL RIG. For the agents, say the rhythm until it is boring: oxidising agent gets reduced, reducing agent gets oxidised. Named for what it does to the other one — the pickpocket named by the victim's loss.

Move three: half-reactions. Copy both lines from the table, reverse the donor, multiply until the electron counts match, add, cancel, then audit — atoms, charges, zero loose electrons. That audit finds your slips before anyone else can.

One decoded name, three moves, one rhythm. Every question in this topic is one of those wearing different chemicals. Go start the practice questions — you've got this!
