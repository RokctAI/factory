# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the chemistry revision sweep for the final examination — the year's quantitative chemistry and acids and bases rebuilt as four working toolkits: the mole conversions, the gas laws, the stoichiometry chain with its limiting reagents, and the acid-base machinery from definition to titration. Nothing in it is new. Everything in it is the exam-day version — compressed, put in working order, and drilled through fresh worked examples chosen to spring the classic traps in practice, where springing them costs nothing.

## Subtopic: The Mole Toolkit — Mass, Solution and Gas

Every quantitative question the year can ask runs through a single hub: the mole. Three formulas connect that hub to the three things a laboratory can actually measure.

Mass: n equals m over M — moles equal mass over molar mass, with M built from the periodic table. Potassium hydroxide, K O H: 39 plus 16 plus 1, giving 56 grams per mole. So 14 grams of potassium hydroxide is 14 over 56, which is 0,25 moles.

Solution: c equals n over V — concentration in moles per cubic decimetre. Dissolve that same 0,25 moles to make 500 cubic centimetres of solution. Convert the volume FIRST: 500 cubic centimetres over 1000 is 0,5 cubic decimetres. Then c equals 0,25 over 0,5, which is 0,5 moles per cubic decimetre.

Gas at STP: n equals V over 22,4 — because at standard temperature and pressure one mole of ANY gas fills 22,4 cubic decimetres, no matter which gas. Turn it around: 0,25 moles of any gas at STP fills 0,25 times 22,4, which is 5,6 cubic decimetres.

Look at what just happened: 14 grams on a balance, half a litre in a flask and 5,6 cubic decimetres in a gas syringe all collapsed into one number — 0,25 moles. That is the hub doing its job. The standing discipline for every mixed question: convert INTO moles first, do the chemistry in moles, convert OUT last. Grams never speak directly to cubic decimetres; both of them speak to the mole.

This section's questions are with you now — all three conversions run in both directions, the volume converted before the concentration, and the hub discipline stated in one sentence.

## Subtopic: Gases — the Ideal Model and Its Equation

The ideal gas model pays out in two currencies: the assumptions, and the equation. Kinetic theory pictures a gas as a swarm of particles in constant random motion, with particle volumes negligible beside the container, no forces between particles except during collisions, and collisions that are perfectly elastic. In this picture temperature IS the average kinetic energy of the particles — which is why every gas law demands KELVIN, the scale whose zero means zero motion. Convert without fail: kelvin equals degrees Celsius plus 273. Twenty-five degrees Celsius is 298 kelvin, and no gas law accepts Celsius.

Boyle's law prices the fixed-temperature squeeze: p one V one equals p two V two. A gas at 120 kilopascals filling 3 cubic decimetres, compressed at constant temperature into 1 cubic decimetre, climbs to 360 kilopascals — a third of the space, three times the pressure, because the same swarm now batters the walls three times as often. For a fixed sample with everything changing, chain all three: p one V one over T one equals p two V two over T two.

The full ideal gas equation prices any single state: p V equals n R T, with R at 8,31 — and its units are non-negotiable: pressure in pascals, volume in CUBIC METRES, temperature in kelvin. Work one: 0,2 moles of gas at 350 kelvin in a 0,005 cubic metre vessel. Pressure equals n R T over V: 0,2 times 8,31 is 1,662; times 350 is 581,7; divide by 0,005, giving 116 340 pascals — about 116,3 kilopascals.

And the fine print worth marks in any final exam: REAL gases follow the model beautifully at low pressure and high temperature, and drift from it at high pressure and low temperature — precisely where the particles crowd close enough for their own volume to matter and move slowly enough for the forces between them to bite. The ideal gas is a model, and naming where the model bends is a mark.

Take this section's questions now — the assumptions recited, Celsius banished, both gas calculations reproduced, and the deviation conditions named.

## Subtopic: Stoichiometry — Ratios, Limiting Reagents and Yield

Stoichiometry is a four-step bridge, crossed in the same order every time. Step one: balance the equation — its coefficients are the only legal exchange rates. Step two: convert what you are GIVEN into moles. Step three: cross the bridge — apply the mole ratio from the coefficients. Step four: convert the answer OUT into whatever the question requests.

Cross it once with ammonia synthesis: N 2 plus 3 H 2 gives 2 N H 3. Given 2 moles of nitrogen with hydrogen to spare, what mass of ammonia forms? Ratio: one nitrogen to two ammonias, so 4 moles of ammonia. Out: ammonia's molar mass is 14 plus 3, which is 17 — so 4 times 17, giving 68 grams.

Now the limiting reagent — the year's most feared idea, and it is nothing but a comparison. Let the same 2 moles of nitrogen meet exactly 4,5 moles of hydrogen. Interrogate one reactant: how much partner does it demand? Two moles of nitrogen, at one-to-three, demand 6 moles of hydrogen. Available: 4,5 — LESS than demanded, so hydrogen runs out first: hydrogen is the LIMITING reagent and nitrogen is in excess. From that moment the limiting reagent decides everything downstream: 4,5 moles of hydrogen, at three-to-two, make 3 moles of ammonia — 51 grams — and the unreacted half mole of nitrogen simply remains. Every limiting question is that same demand-versus-supply check, and ALL later arithmetic uses the limiting reagent's moles.

Two percentages complete the toolkit. Percentage yield: actual over theoretical, times 100. If the synthesis above delivers only 40,8 grams instead of the theoretical 51, the yield is 40,8 over 51, which is 80 percent — real reactions leak product to side reactions, incomplete mixing and transfer losses. Percentage purity runs the same honesty on the input side: a 10-gram sample of magnesite that is 84 percent pure magnesium carbonate contains 8,4 grams of the real compound — and it is THOSE 8,4 grams, 0,1 moles at 84 grams per mole, that enter the bridge, never the 10.

This section's questions are ready — the bridge crossed in order, the demand-versus-supply check run, and both percentages computed.

## Subtopic: Acids and Bases — Definitions, Pairs and the Titration

The Lowry-Brønsted definitions run the whole topic: an ACID is a proton donor; a BASE is a proton acceptor. One proton — one hydrogen ion — changes hands per event, and tracking that one proton answers every conceptual question.

Track one event. Ammonia dissolving in water: the water molecule donates a proton to ammonia, making N H 4 plus, the ammonium ion, and leaving O H minus. Every acid-base event creates two CONJUGATE PAIRS — species differing by exactly one proton. N H 4 plus and N H 3 are one pair; H 2 O and O H minus are the other. The naming rule: the acid's leftover is its conjugate base; the base's product is its conjugate acid. Notice water DONATED here — yet with hydrochloric acid it ACCEPTS, forming H 3 O plus. A species that can play either role depending on its partner is an AMPHOLYTE, and water is the great example.

Keep two axes strictly apart, because conflating them is the topic's classic error. Strong versus weak is about IONISATION: a strong acid like nitric ionises completely; a weak acid like ethanoic ionises only partially. Concentrated versus dilute is about AMOUNT per volume of solution. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are both perfectly ordinary bottles — the axes are independent.

The reactions, as one family: acid plus metal hydroxide gives salt plus water; acid plus metal oxide gives salt plus water; acid plus metal carbonate gives salt plus water plus carbon dioxide — the fizz that identifies a carbonate on sight.

And the flagship calculation, the titration. The setup: 25 cubic centimetres of 0,2 mole per cubic decimetre potassium hydroxide exactly neutralises 20 cubic centimetres of nitric acid — find the acid's concentration. Moles of base: n equals c V, which is 0,2 times 0,025, giving 0,005 moles. The equation H N O 3 plus K O H gives K N O 3 plus H 2 O runs one to one, so moles of acid: 0,005. Concentration: 0,005 over 0,020, which is 0,25 moles per cubic decimetre. Had the acid been sulphuric, the ratio would be one acid to TWO base — and the skipped ratio step is the single most expensive titration error there is.

The final questions of this part are with you now — donor and acceptor tracked, pairs named, the two axes separated, and the titration crossed via its ratio.

## Subtopic: The Mole as the Only Currency Exchange

Picture the question paper's quantities as currencies of different countries. Grams are one country's money; cubic decimetres of solution another's; gas volumes a third's. None of them exchange directly — every conversion passes through one central currency: the mole.

The exchange rates are printed on things you already hold. The periodic table prices mass: 56 grams buys one mole of potassium hydroxide. The label on a flask prices solutions: at 0,5 moles per cubic decimetre, every cubic decimetre carries 0,5 moles. And STP prices gases with the flattest rate in science: 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole, for ANY gas whatsoever — the mole is the great equaliser, counting particles without asking what they are.

The exchange discipline is the entire method: change INTO moles at the first counter, do all business in moles, change OUT at the last counter. A question that hands you grams of magnesium and asks for cubic decimetres of hydrogen gas is not hard — it is two exchanges with a balanced equation standing between them, quoting the mole-to-mole rate.

The one queue everybody tries to skip: volume units. The concentration counter trades only in cubic decimetres, and questions deliberately pay in cubic centimetres. Divide by 1000 at the door — 500 cubic centimetres is 0,5 cubic decimetres — and only then approach the counter. Skip that queue and every number downstream is mis-priced by a factor of a thousand.

Your questions for this section are ready — every rate matched to its counter, one full exchange run through the hub, and the volume queue joined first.

## Subtopic: Pancakes and the Empty Shelf

The dreaded limiting reagent is a kitchen problem you solved years ago. The recipe: one cup of flour and three eggs make ten pancakes. Your shelf: four cups of flour and nine eggs. How many pancakes?

Instinct glances at the four generous cups of flour and relaxes. But run the demand check: four cups of flour demand twelve eggs, and the shelf holds nine. The eggs run out first: the EGGS are the limiting ingredient, the flour is in excess, and the kitchen makes exactly three batches — thirty pancakes — with a full cup of flour abandoned on the shelf, useless without partners.

That demand-versus-supply check is the complete limiting-reagent method. In the chemistry version the balanced equation is the recipe, the coefficients are the cups and eggs, and the moles the question hands you are the shelf. Pick either reactant, compute how much partner it demands, compare with what is available, and whichever runs short is the limiting reagent. From then on the limiting reagent does ALL the talking: product amounts, leftover amounts, every downstream number scales from it — exactly as the pancake count followed the nine eggs and ignored the flour's generosity.

Percentage yield is the kitchen's closing honesty: the recipe promised thirty pancakes, but two stuck to the pan and one hit the floor, and twenty-seven reached the table. Twenty-seven over thirty, times 100 — 90 percent. Real kitchens and real reaction flasks both leak, and yield simply measures the leak. Percentage purity is honesty about the shelf instead: if a sixth of the flour bag is actually bran, only the true flour enters the recipe arithmetic — and only the pure compound in a sample enters the mole bridge.

Take this section's questions now — the demand check run on the shelf, the limiting ingredient named, and both honesty percentages computed.

## Subtopic: The Proton Pass and the Trap List

An acid-base reaction is a rugby pass: the proton is the ball, the acid is the player releasing it, the base is the player gathering it. One ball, one pass, two players — that is the whole Lowry-Brønsted picture.

Conjugate pairs are simply the players photographed before and after the pass. The passer, ball gone, is the conjugate base — same player, one ball lighter. The receiver, ball in hand, is the conjugate acid — same player, one ball heavier. Two players, four states, always exactly one ball apart: that is why conjugate pairs differ by exactly one proton. And water is the utility player who passes to ammonia but gathers from nitric acid — the ampholyte, choosing a role by reading the opposition.

Strong versus weak is passing COMMITMENT: a strong acid releases the ball completely, every molecule, every time; a weak acid mostly holds on, releasing only a fraction. Concentrated versus dilute is squad SIZE: how many players stand on each cubic decimetre of field. A small squad of total-commitment passers — dilute but strong — is an utterly different team from a huge squad that rarely releases — concentrated but weak. Two independent axes, and questions love to ask about one while waving the other in your face.

Now the trap list — five exhibits, each with its antidote. One: Celsius fed into a gas law — add 273 first, always. Two: cubic centimetres fed into c equals n over V — divide by 1000 at the door. Three: the skipped mole ratio — sulphuric acid demands two hydroxides, and a lazy one-to-one bleeds marks. Four: calculating from the EXCESS reagent — the limiting reagent does all the talking. Five: strong confused with concentrated — commitment is not squad size.

And the final audit: give every answer one moment of disbelief. Moles should be modest numbers, not millions; a concentration should survive comparison with what was actually dissolved; a yield above 100 percent means actual and theoretical swapped places in the fraction. Doubt your own answer for five seconds, and you rob the marking of its favourite catches.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the pass tracked, both axes kept apart, five traps dodged, and every answer disbelieved for a moment first.
