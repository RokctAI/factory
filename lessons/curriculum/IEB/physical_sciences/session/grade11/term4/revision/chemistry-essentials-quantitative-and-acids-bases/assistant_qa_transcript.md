**Bianca:** Revision surfaces the same struggles every year, so let's meet them head-on. First: the mole has three formulas, and learners freeze over which one a question wants. What is the selection method?

**Tutor:** Let the measuring instrument in the question pick the formula, because each formula belongs to exactly one instrument. A balance or a mass in grams: n equals m over M, with the molar mass assembled from the periodic table. A flask, a pipette or anything described as a solution: c equals n over V, with the volume moved to cubic decimetres before a single other step. A gas at STP: n equals V over 22,4. That is the whole decision — no judgement required, only instrument-spotting. The deeper shift is to stop seeing three topics and start seeing one room with three doors: every door opens onto the mole. A question that hands you grams of zinc and wants cubic decimetres of hydrogen is two doors used in sequence — in through the mass door, across the balanced equation's ratio, out through the gas door. Learners who count three formulas run out of time; learners who see one hub with three doors finish early. And close with the sense check: school-scale amounts give modest mole numbers, tenths and units, so an answer in the thousands means a unit slipped somewhere upstream — usually those cubic centimetres that never became cubic decimetres.

**Bianca:** Next, the limiting reagent — the question learners most often leave blank. Why does it frighten people, and what is the dependable method?

**Tutor:** It frightens people because instinct votes for the smaller pile, and stoichiometry votes with the RATIO instead. The dependable method is one comparison, identical every time: choose either reactant, use the coefficients to compute how much partner it demands, and set demand against supply. In the ammonia synthesis, two moles of nitrogen at one-to-three demand six moles of hydrogen; if the flask holds four and a half, hydrogen falls short and is limiting — even when the hydrogen pile looks bigger than the nitrogen pile. That is pancake logic: nine eggs limit the baking while four proud cups of flour watch from the shelf, because the recipe demands three eggs per cup. Then the rule that carries the follow-through marks: once named, the limiting reagent does all the talking — every product mass and every leftover quantity scales from ITS moles, and reaching for the excess reagent's moles afterwards is the classic error the question was built to catch. Write the demand check as one visible line, label the limiting reagent explicitly, and the rest of the question is ordinary bridge-crossing at ordinary speed.

**Bianca:** The acids and bases vocabulary — conjugate pairs, ampholytes, strong versus concentrated — blurs badly under pressure. How should it all be organised?

**Tutor:** Hang everything on the single event the definitions describe: one proton changing hands. The acid donates it, the base accepts it — Lowry-Brønsted in a sentence. Conjugate pairs then name themselves: after the transfer, the acid minus its proton is a conjugate base and the base plus its proton is a conjugate acid, so a legitimate pair differs by exactly one hydrogen and one unit of charge — write the two formulas and check that difference, nothing more. The ampholyte is whichever species can play both roles depending on its partner, and water is the standard example: it donates to ammonia, making ammonium and hydroxide, yet accepts from nitric acid, making hydronium. Then store the two independent axes on separate shelves, because questions deliberately cross them: strong versus weak is completeness of ionisation, a property of the substance itself; concentrated versus dilute is amount dissolved per volume, a property of how the bottle was prepared. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are both everyday laboratory objects, and explaining WHY they differ — every molecule ionised in one, a small fraction in the other — is precisely the two-mark explanation these questions want.

**Bianca:** Last one: any advice for structuring the final weeks of chemistry revision — what genuinely moves marks?

**Tutor:** Three shifts, ranked by payoff. First, make the conversion reflexes frequent rather than long: a short mixed set of mole conversions — mass, solution, gas, both directions — done every second day installs the hub discipline far deeper than one weekend marathon, and that discipline sits inside the majority of all calculation marks. Second, rehearse the two set-piece calculations until their shape runs on rails: the limiting-reagent demand check with its single comparison line, and the titration chain — moles of the known from c times V, across the ratio, out to the unknown concentration — including the diprotic variant with sulphuric acid, because the skipped ratio is the most expensive slip in the topic. Third, keep a personal trap list in your own handwriting and read it before every practice session: kelvin not Celsius, cubic decimetres at the door, the ratio step, the excess reagent silenced, strong kept apart from concentrated. Then end every answer with one deliberate moment of disbelief — a yield above 100 percent or a concentration dwarfing what was dissolved is an error announcing itself while there is still time to fix it. Frequent reflexes, automatic set pieces, a trap list read cold: those three carry more marks than any amount of rereading notes.
