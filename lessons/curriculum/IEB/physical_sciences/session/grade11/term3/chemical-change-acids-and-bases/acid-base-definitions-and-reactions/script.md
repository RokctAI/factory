# Part 1 — Expert

You already know the names and formulae of the everyday acids and bases. Today we interrogate the labels themselves: what is it, precisely, that makes nitric acid an acid and ammonia a base? Chemistry has answered that question twice — once imperfectly with Arrhenius, then properly with Brønsted and Lowry — and the second answer turns the whole topic into the biography of a single travelling proton. Along the way we will harvest conjugate pairs, meet the two-faced substances called ampholytes, drill the three reactions every acid performs, and finish with the indicator colours that let your eyes watch chemistry happen.

## Subtopic: Two Definitions — Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry

Arrhenius made the first serious attempt. His acid is a substance that releases hydrogen ions, H plus, when dissolved in water; his base releases hydroxide ions, OH minus. Test it on nitric acid and potassium hydroxide and it performs beautifully — HNO3 sheds hydrogen ions, KOH sheds hydroxide. But the definition carries two documented weaknesses, and you should be able to state both. First, it chains all acid-base chemistry to water as the solvent. Second, it is struck dumb by ammonia: NH3 has no hydroxide anywhere in its formula, yet dissolve it and the solution is unmistakably basic.

Brønsted and Lowry rescued the theory by ignoring what a substance contains and tracking what it transfers. A hydrogen ion is nothing but a naked proton — hydrogen with its solitary electron removed — so they declared: an acid is a PROTON DONOR, a base is a PROTON ACCEPTOR. Water becomes optional; hydroxide becomes optional; the entire subject collapses into bookkeeping for one proton.

Apply the new lens to two dissolutions. Nitric acid in water: HNO3 plus H2O gives H3O plus, plus NO3 minus. The acid hands its proton to a water molecule, which becomes the hydronium ion, H3O plus, while the nitrate ion is left behind. HNO3 donated, so it is the acid; water accepted, so water played the base. Now ammonia in water, written with the double arrow because it is an equilibrium: NH3 plus H2O in equilibrium with NH4 plus, plus OH minus. This time ammonia is the acceptor — it takes a proton off water and becomes ammonium — so ammonia is the base and WATER is the donor, the acid. And the ammonia puzzle evaporates: a base never needed to carry hydroxide; it only needed to strip a proton from water, and hydroxide appears as the debris.

Look back at water's behaviour across those two equations — acceptor in the first, donor in the second. File that observation; it becomes a whole section shortly. The questions for this section are with you now: state both sets of definitions, write both dissolution equations, and name the two Arrhenius shortcomings.

## Subtopic: Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

Every proton transfer leaves a shadow. The instant an acid gives up its proton, the leftover species is something that could accept that proton back if the reaction reversed — in other words, a base. Acid and leftover together form a CONJUGATE ACID-BASE PAIR: two species separated by exactly one proton, and the member carrying the proton is always the acid of the pair.

Mine the nitric acid equation for its pairs. HNO3 plus H2O gives H3O plus, plus NO3 minus. HNO3 became NO3 minus by losing one proton: that is pair one — HNO3 the acid, nitrate its conjugate base. Water became H3O plus by gaining one proton: pair two — hydronium the acid, water its conjugate base. Every complete acid-base equation hides exactly two pairs, and the extraction routine is mechanical: connect each species on the left to the species on the right that differs from it by a single H, and label whichever partner holds the extra H as the acid.

Do it once more with ammonia: NH3 plus H2O in equilibrium with NH4 plus, plus OH minus. Ammonia gained a proton to become ammonium — NH4 plus is the acid, NH3 the conjugate base. Water lost a proton to become hydroxide — H2O is the acid of that pair, OH minus the conjugate base.

Two errors cost marks here, so disarm them now. Error one: forgetting that a pair differs by ONE proton only. Carbonic acid H2CO3 and the carbonate ion CO3 two minus differ by two protons, so they are NOT conjugates; the genuine pairs are H2CO3 with HCO3 minus, and then HCO3 minus with CO3 two minus — two separate single steps. Error two: pairing the two species standing together on the same side of the arrow. Partners always sit diagonally across the equation, because the two reactants are busy doing opposite jobs in the same direction and can never be each other's conjugate. Sketch the two linking loops under an equation once and the geometry becomes reflex.

This section's questions are ready for you: pull both pairs out of any equation, and enforce the one-proton rule without mercy.

## Subtopic: Ampholytes, and the Three Reactions of Acids

A substance able to donate a proton in one encounter and accept one in another is called AMPHIPROTIC — an AMPHOLYTE. Water is the celebrity example, as the previous two sections proved: it donated to ammonia and accepted from nitric acid. The other ampholyte you must command is the hydrogen carbonate ion, HCO3 minus. Playing acid: HCO3 minus plus H2O in equilibrium with CO3 two minus, plus H3O plus — a proton donated. Playing base: HCO3 minus plus H2O in equilibrium with H2CO3, plus OH minus — a proton accepted. One ion, two performances, and the audience decides which one it gives. The hydrogen sulphate ion, HSO4 minus, has the same double life. When a question says show that a substance is amphiprotic, it is ordering exactly two equations from you — one donating, one accepting.

Now the workhorse of the topic: the three reactions of acids. All three obey a single master template — acid plus base gives salt plus water — and the carbonate case appends one extra product.

Reaction type one, acid plus metal hydroxide: HNO3 plus KOH gives KNO3 plus H2O — nitric acid plus potassium hydroxide gives potassium nitrate plus water, already balanced as written. Reaction type two, acid plus metal oxide: 2HNO3 plus CaO gives Ca(NO3)2 plus H2O — two nitric acid plus calcium oxide gives calcium nitrate plus water. Audit it: two nitrogens both sides, two hydrogens both sides, one calcium, seven oxygens each side. Metal oxides qualify as bases because the oxide ion is a hungry proton acceptor.

Reaction type three, acid plus metal carbonate: salt plus water plus CARBON DIOXIDE. With potassium carbonate: 2HNO3 plus K2CO3 gives 2KNO3 plus H2O plus CO2 — count nine oxygens on each side to confirm. With sulphuric acid on magnesium carbonate: H2SO4 plus MgCO3 gives MgSO4 plus H2O plus CO2. That escaping gas is the signature you can see — the froth on an antacid tablet, the fizz when acid touches limestone, the standard laboratory identification.

Questions for this section are with you now: both HCO3 minus equations, and all three reaction types, balanced, products named.

## Subtopic: Indicators — Making the Chemistry Visible

Protons are invisible, so chemists employ informants. An acid-base INDICATOR is a dye whose colour reports whether its surroundings are acidic or basic, and you are responsible for four of them, with exact colours on both sides.

Litmus: RED in acid, BLUE in base — the oldest informant, and the bluntest, since it only announces which side of neutral you stand on. Methyl orange: RED in acid, YELLOW in base, sliding through orange at its changeover. Bromothymol blue: YELLOW in acid, BLUE in base — and GREEN in a neutral solution, the only one of the four with its own dedicated neutral colour, which is exactly why questions about pure water or a perfectly neutralised mixture reach for it. Phenolphthalein: COLOURLESS in acid, PINK in base — the theatrical one, invisible right up until the solution turns basic, then suddenly, unmistakably pink.

Organise the memory load deliberately rather than hoping. The two reds in acid: litmus and methyl orange. The two blues in base: litmus and bromothymol blue. The outlier: phenolphthalein, the only one that trades a colour for no colour at all.

Why this matters beyond the memorising: indicators are how an invisible event announces itself. Drip acid steadily into a basic solution laced with phenolphthalein and the pink survives, survives, survives — then dies at the exact drop that exhausts the base. Run the same journey with bromothymol blue and you get blue, then a flash of green precisely at neutral, then yellow as acid goes into excess. That changeover moment is the doorway into titration, the precision technique waiting for you next year; reading it correctly is this year's task.

The last questions of this part are with you now: four indicators, eight colours, one green, and a changeover interpreted from a described experiment.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same topic again, but let's shrink it to one playground move: throw, catch, and watch what the catch does to both players.

## Subtopic: Acid-Base Is Pass-the-Ball

Peel away the terminology and one move remains: a tiny particle — a proton, written H plus — is thrown from one molecule to another. The thrower is the acid. The catcher is the base. That single sentence is the whole Brønsted-Lowry theory; every equation in this topic is just that pass written in symbols.

Watch a clean pass. Nitric acid hits water: HNO3 throws its proton, a water molecule catches, and both players are renamed by the play — water plus one proton is now hydronium, H3O plus, and nitric acid minus its proton is just the nitrate ion, NO3 minus. Thrower acid, catcher base. Done.

Now the pass that catches everyone off guard. Ammonia enters water — and the THROWER this time is water. NH3 makes the catch, becoming NH4 plus, and the water molecule that gave up its proton walks away as hydroxide, OH minus. So ammonia earns its base badge not by carrying hydroxide — it owns none — but by being an excellent catcher. Better still, the catch itself manufactures hydroxide out of the robbed water. The old rulebook, which insisted a base must arrive with hydroxide in its kit bag, could never call that play; the pass-the-ball rulebook calls it instantly.

Every question in this topic secretly asks one of two things: who threw, and who caught. Scan the equation for the species that lost an H from left to right — that was your thrower, the acid. Find the species that gained an H — your catcher, the base. Your questions for this section are with you now: track the ball, ignore the jerseys.

## Subtopic: Partners Across the Pass, and the Player Who Plays Both Sides

Pause the game one instant after a pass and study the thrower. His hands are now empty — which makes him the ideal candidate to CATCH the ball if the play reverses. The acid, by throwing, has turned itself into a potential base. That player, photographed with the ball and then without it, is what chemists call a conjugate acid-base pair: one character, two frames, exactly one proton apart. The frame holding the proton is the acid.

Each equation contains TWO of these before-and-after couples — one for the thrower, one for the catcher. In the nitric acid play: HNO3 and NO3 minus, with ball and without; H3O plus and H2O, with and without. Finding them is spot-the-difference: link each left-hand species to the right-hand species one H away from it. Exactly one H — a gap of two protons, like H2CO3 against CO3 two minus, means you skipped a frame, and the mark disappears with it.

And now the most versatile player on any field: the one who throws OR catches depending on the opposition. Water again. Facing nitric acid, a powerful thrower, water catches. Facing ammonia, a hungry catcher, water throws. Identical molecule, opposite roles, decided entirely by the opponent. The technical name is amphiprotic — an ampholyte — and the second one on your list is the hydrogen carbonate ion from baking soda: pair it with an acid and it catches, pair it with a base and it throws. The instruction show that X is an ampholyte translates to: write two short equations, X throwing in one, X catching in the other.

Your questions for this section are ready: before-and-after frames for the pairs, and both equations for the double-role player.

## Subtopic: Three Fizzes and Four Colour Changes

What happens when a thrower finally meets a committed catcher head-on? A trade so total that both players retire. The acid's protons fuse with the base's hydroxide into plain water, and the spectator ions left in solution settle down as a salt. Sour meets soapy and the product is salty water — that is neutralisation, and it is the entire reason a base tablet calms an acid stomach.

Acids run this trade against three kinds of partner, and the results rhyme. Against a metal hydroxide such as potassium hydroxide: salt plus water, no drama. Against a metal oxide such as calcium oxide: salt plus water again, because the oxide ion is a superb proton catcher. Against a metal CARBONATE — baking soda, limestone, the shell of an egg — the same trade happens, then the wounded carbonate collapses and breathes out carbon dioxide gas. That exhale is the fizz. Vinegar foaming on baking soda, acid etching a piece of limestone, a fizzing antacid in a glass: one reaction in three costumes — salt, water, and a sigh of CO2.

But none of this is visible on its own, so you hire spies. Litmus: red among acids, blue among bases — reliable, unspectacular. Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base. Bromothymol blue: yellow in acid, blue in base, and GREEN at the exact moment of neutral — the only spy with a middle colour, so it alone can tell you the instant a neutralisation completes. Phenolphthalein, the show-off: nothing in acid, hot pink in base — drip acid into its pink and the colour switches off at the very drop the base runs out.

Throw and catch, photograph the pairs, respect the double agents, expect a fizz whenever a carbonate is on the receiving end, and trust your spies' colours. That is the entire topic in one breath. The final questions of the lesson are yours now — name each reaction's pattern and the products fill themselves in.
