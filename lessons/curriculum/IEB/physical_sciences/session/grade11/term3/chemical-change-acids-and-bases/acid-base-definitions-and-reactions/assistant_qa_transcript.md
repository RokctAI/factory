**Bianca:** Let's tackle the questions this topic always generates. First up, the two definitions: if Brønsted-Lowry fixed everything Arrhenius got wrong, why do we still have to learn Arrhenius?

**Tutor:** Two reasons — one practical, one deeper. Practically, the syllabus asks for both sets of definitions, so both must be word-perfect. The deeper reason is that a failed definition is a teaching instrument: understanding exactly where Arrhenius breaks shows you what Brønsted-Lowry actually buys. Arrhenius classifies by contents — acids release hydrogen ions in water, bases release hydroxide — and for nitric acid and potassium hydroxide it is flawless. Then you meet ammonia: obviously basic, turns litmus blue, neutralises acids, yet its formula holds no hydroxide whatsoever, and Arrhenius simply has no verdict. Brønsted and Lowry reclassified by behaviour instead: an acid donates a proton, a base accepts one. Now ammonia's case is trivial — NH3 accepts a proton from water, and the water it robbed becomes the very hydroxide Arrhenius went looking for. A favourite assessment question hands you ammonia and asks why one framework can classify it and the other cannot. Give both definitions side by side, then the ammonia equation as your evidence, and the full marks follow.

***

**Bianca:** Next problem: conjugate pairs. Everyone can recite "differ by one proton", but in a real equation people still connect the wrong species. What's the method that never fails?

**Tutor:** Turn it into disciplined spot-the-difference. The rule: a pair takes one species from EACH side of the arrow, the two differing by exactly one H, and whichever member holds the extra H is the acid of that pair. Run it on HNO3 plus H2O gives H3O plus and NO3 minus. Pick up HNO3 from the left and scan the right for the species one proton away — that is NO3 minus. Pair one, HNO3 the acid. Pick up H2O; its one-proton partner is H3O plus. Pair two, hydronium the acid. Now the two classic wrong moves. Wrong move one: pairing the two species that stand next to each other on the left. Reactants can never be conjugates — in the forward direction one is donating while the other accepts, so they are opponents. Pairs always connect diagonally across the arrow. Wrong move two: ignoring the one-proton limit. Carbonic acid H2CO3 and carbonate CO3 two minus are two protons apart, so they are relatives but not conjugates; the legal pairs are H2CO3 with HCO3 minus, then HCO3 minus with CO3 two minus, each a single step. Draw the two diagonal loops under every equation and check each loop spans exactly one H — that habit converts the definition into marks.

***

**Bianca:** Here's the one that feels like a contradiction: water is apparently an acid AND a base. How can one molecule be both?

**Tutor:** It is never both at once — it is one or the other per reaction, and it auditions fresh every time. That is what amphiprotic means. A Brønsted-Lowry label is not a permanent identity; it describes a single transaction. Water owns a donatable proton and also lone pairs capable of catching one, so its role is dictated by its partner. Against a strong donor like nitric acid, water accepts and becomes hydronium — base. Against an eager acceptor like ammonia, water donates and becomes hydroxide — acid. The hydrogen carbonate ion has the same repertoire, and the standard question format is: show, using equations, that HCO3 minus is amphiprotic. The answer is always a matched set of two equations — HCO3 minus donating, to give carbonate and hydronium, and HCO3 minus accepting, to give carbonic acid and hydroxide. Write only one of them and you have proved half the claim; the question demanded both halves. Two equations, two roles, one ion — that is the complete answer every time.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: hydroxides, oxides, carbonates — the three acid reactions merge into mush the night before a test. What's the compression trick?

**Tutor:** One template plus one flag, nothing more. The template: acid plus base gives salt plus water, always. The salt inherits its metal from the base and its family name from the acid; the acid's protons and the base's oxide or hydroxide merge into the water. Hydroxides and oxides follow the template with zero extras: HNO3 plus KOH gives potassium nitrate and water; two HNO3 plus CaO gives calcium nitrate and water. The flag is raised only for carbonates: the template still runs, but the protonated carbonate is unstable and disintegrates into water plus carbon dioxide, so a carbonate always delivers three products — salt, water, and the fizzing gas. Facing any question, classify the partner first: hydroxide or oxide means two products; carbonate means three, with CO2 guaranteed as the third. Then balance carefully — diprotic sulphuric acid needs its partners doubled where charges demand it. And keep the visual clue ready for descriptive questions: of the three reactions, only the carbonate one announces itself to your eyes, because only it fizzes.
