Hey, it's Bianca — I know this topic reads like a legal dictionary: Arrhenius, Brønsted-Lowry, conjugate, amphiprotic. Here's the secret nobody prints on the first page: underneath all of it is one playground move, and once you see the move, every scary word becomes a nickname for something simple.

The move: one proton gets thrown from one molecule to another. Thrower equals acid. Catcher equals base. That is the entire modern theory in two short sentences. Hand me any equation and I ask only: who lost an H from left to right? That's the acid. Who gained one? That's the base. The jerseys don't matter — only the ball.

Conjugate pairs are just spot-the-difference with a strict referee. Link each species on the left to the species on the right sitting exactly ONE hydrogen away; the one holding the hydrogen is the acid of that pair. Two diagonal loops per equation, never side-by-side, and never a two-proton jump — H2CO3 straight to CO3 two minus skips a step, and the referee notices.

Ampholyte questions always want the identical thing: two little equations, one where your substance throws the proton and one where it catches. Water and hydrogen carbonate are the only two candidates they use. Practise those two, twice each, and that question is banked.

The three acid reactions? One line: acid plus base gives salt plus water — and if the base was a carbonate, add a puff of carbon dioxide. That single line IS all three reactions.

Tonight, make the indicator table: four rows, acid colour and base colour — litmus red-blue, methyl orange red-yellow, bromothymol blue yellow-blue with green at neutral, phenolphthalein colourless-pink. Ten minutes, permanent marks.

Now go do the practice questions — throw, catch, and watch the ball. You've got this!
