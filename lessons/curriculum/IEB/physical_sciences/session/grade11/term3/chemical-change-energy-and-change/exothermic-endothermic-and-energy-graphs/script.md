# Part 1 — Expert

Underneath every chemical equation runs a second, invisible ledger: an energy account. Old bonds must be paid apart; new bonds pay energy back in; and the closing balance of that account decides whether a flask warms your hand or chills it. This session audits the whole ledger — the heat of reaction delta H and the rule that fixes its sign, the great sorting of reactions into exothermic and endothermic, the activation energy barrier that guards even the most generous reactions, and finally the potential energy graph, the single diagram on which every question in this topic is built.

## Subtopic: Bond Breaking, Bond Making and the Heat of Reaction

Two laws govern the account, and neither has an exception. To BREAK a chemical bond you must SUPPLY energy: a bond is a mutual attraction, and separating attracted particles is work that must be paid for. To FORM a chemical bond is to RELEASE energy: particles settling into an attraction surrender energy on the way in, just as any falling object surrenders height.

A reaction runs both processes in the same breath — reactant bonds break, product bonds form — and the heat of reaction, delta H, records the net outcome. Its formal definition: the potential energy of the products minus the potential energy of the reactants, delta H equals E products minus E reactants, quoted in kilojoules or kilojoules per mole of the reaction as written.

Two outcomes are possible. If the forming of the new bonds returns MORE energy than the breaking of the old ones consumed, the excess is exported — normally as heat — and the products finish at a LOWER potential energy than the reactants: delta H comes out negative. If the breaking bill outruns the forming refund, the reaction must draw energy in from outside, the products finish HIGHER, and delta H is positive.

Be exact about whose account delta H describes, because the sign lives or dies on it. Delta H belongs to the SYSTEM — the reacting chemicals — never to the surroundings. When the chemicals shed energy, the beaker and the bench around them heat up, yet delta H is negative, because the CHEMICALS finished poorer. Fix the anchor sentence now: a negative delta H means energy left the chemicals. Learners who pin the sign to the warm glass instead of the chemicals invert every answer in this topic.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two bond laws, the definition and units of delta H, and the sign convention pinned to the system.

## Subtopic: Exothermic and Endothermic Reactions

A reaction that exports energy to its surroundings is EXOTHERMIC: delta H negative, products below reactants on the energy axis, surroundings warming. Know the standard examples by name. Combustion — propane burning in a gas heater, C3H8 plus 5O2 gives 3CO2 plus 4H2O, propane plus oxygen giving carbon dioxide and water. Respiration — the slow, controlled combustion of glucose that keeps your body at thirty-seven degrees. And neutralisation — mix an acid and a base and the flask grows noticeably warm.

A reaction that imports energy is ENDOTHERMIC: delta H positive, products above reactants, surroundings COOLING as they are taxed for the difference. The two examples to have ready: photosynthesis — 6CO2 plus 6H2O gives C6H12O6 plus 6O2, six carbon dioxide and six water building one glucose and six oxygen, funded entirely by captured sunlight — and thermal decomposition, such as magnesium carbonate breaking into magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide, which stalls the instant you stop heating it.

The bench-top diagnostic works in both directions. Beaker warms: the chemicals exported energy — exothermic, delta H negative. Beaker chills: the chemicals imported energy — endothermic, delta H positive. A sports first-aid cold pack is an endothermic dissolution in a plastic sleeve; a pocket hand warmer and a camping stove are exothermic chemistry, packaged and priced.

One refinement separates the top answers from the rest. Endothermic does not mean impossible without a flame. It means the products hold more stored energy than the reactants did, so that energy has to be sourced from somewhere — sunlight for the leaf, a sustained burner for the decomposition, or merely the warmth of your ankle for the cold pack. The surroundings are the source; delta H simply records that the system took the energy in.

Take this section's questions now: both definitions, the named examples on each side, the sign of delta H for each, and the warm-beaker cold-beaker diagnostic.

## Subtopic: Activation Energy and the Activated Complex

Paraffin sits calmly in a lamp beside its wick; the gas in a stove streams past glowing metal unburnt until the spark clicks. If combustion is exothermic — a downhill, energy-releasing journey — why does it wait for permission?

Because a barrier stands before the descent. New bonds cannot form until the existing bonds are stretched to breaking, and that investment is demanded IN ADVANCE, regardless of the refund to come. The ACTIVATION ENERGY, E A, is the minimum energy colliding particles must carry for their collision to succeed — measured on the energy diagram as the rise from the reactants' level to the summit.

At that summit lives the ACTIVATED COMPLEX: a fleeting, unstable arrangement in which the old bonds are partially broken and the new bonds partially formed. It is not a compound you could ever isolate; it survives for the duration of one molecular collision and then collapses — forward into products, or backward into the reactants it came from. The definition to give: the unstable transition state of maximum potential energy through which the reaction must pass.

This is the entire physics of the matchstick. The struck match hoists a small population of molecules over the barrier; their reaction refunds far more energy than the match invested; that refund hoists the neighbouring molecules over in turn, and the fire begins paying its own way. The match never funds the fire — it funds the FIRST CROSSING, and the reaction's surplus funds every crossing after that. A low activation energy means a reaction that starts at a touch, sometimes hazardously; a high one means reactants that can share a container for a lifetime, like the paraffin and the air around its wick.

Guard the boundary between the two energies, because questions deliberately smudge it: delta H is the gap between the start and finish levels; E A is the climb from the start to the summit. A reaction can plunge steeply overall and still demand a brutal price of admission. The questions for this section are ready for you now.

## Subtopic: Potential Energy Graphs, Catalysed and Uncatalysed

The potential energy graph carries potential energy on its vertical axis and the progress of the reaction along its horizontal. The curve leaves the reactants' level, climbs to one summit, and settles at the products' level. Three readings, taken the same way every time. Activation energy: reactants' level UP to the summit. Heat of reaction delta H: reactants' level ACROSS to the products' level — downward for exothermic, upward for endothermic. Activated complex: the arrangement at the summit itself.

Work an example with real numbers. Reactants at 80 kilojoules, summit at 170, products at 30. Forward activation energy: 170 minus 80 — 90 kilojoules. Delta H: 30 minus 80 — negative 50 kilojoules, exothermic. Now read the same curve right to left for the reverse reaction: from the products at 30 up to the same summit at 170 is a reverse activation energy of 140 kilojoules, and the reverse delta H is positive 50. Two facts fall out of every such graph: reversing a reaction flips the sign of delta H, and the two activation energies always differ by exactly the magnitude of delta H — here, 140 minus 90 equals 50.

Introduce a CATALYST and watch what moves. A catalyst supplies an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, and emerges chemically unchanged at the end. Graphically, one feature drops and two do not: the summit falls — say from 170 down to 130, trimming the forward climb from 90 kilojoules to 50 — while the reactant and product levels stay bolted in place, leaving delta H untouched. The catalysed reaction is FASTER; it is not one joule more generous, and the reverse climb shrinks by the same amount, from 140 to 100. The standard sketch: a dashed curve with a lower summit nested inside the original, agreeing with it at both ends.

Close with the recurring traps. Measuring delta H up to the summit instead of level-to-level. Claiming a catalyst reduces delta H. Assuming an endothermic reaction has no summit — every reaction climbs, in both directions. And swapping forward and reverse activation energies. The final questions of this part are with you now: every value read off the levels, in kilojoules, sign included.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same topic again, told with a bucket in a well, a stubborn braai, and one drawing of a mountain ridge.

## Subtopic: Paying to Break, Earning to Build

Picture the atoms in a bond as a bucket resting at the bottom of a well. To separate them — to break the bond — you must winch the bucket all the way up, and winching always costs effort. To form a bond is the reverse: the bucket drops back down the well, the rope spins the handle, and energy is handed back out. Those are the only two rules in this topic, and chemistry never breaks either: breaking bonds always costs, forming bonds always pays.

Every reaction is a demolition followed by a rebuild — winch the old buckets up, let new ones drop. So every reaction has a cost and a payout, and only one question matters: which is bigger? When the payout beats the cost, the surplus floods out, usually as heat, and the neighbourhood warms — that is exothermic. A gas heater is a payout machine: the new bonds inside carbon dioxide and water drop into much deeper wells than the propane bonds came out of, and the difference is what warms the room.

When the cost beats the payout, the reaction stalls unless energy is fed in from outside, and it feeds by chilling whatever surrounds it — endothermic. The first-aid cold pack is exactly this deal in a sleeve: crack the inner pouch, the dissolving begins, the cost exceeds the payout, and the shortfall is billed to the heat of your sprained ankle.

The bookkeeping symbol is delta H: what the chemicals hold at the end minus what they held at the start. Payout reaction: the chemicals finish with less in storage, so delta H is NEGATIVE — negative for the chemicals, even while your palms feel the warmth. Cost reaction: the chemicals finish richer, delta H positive, invoiced to the room. The sign always reads the chemicals' account, never your fingertips.

Your questions for this section are ready — winching up costs, dropping down pays, and delta H is the closing balance on the chemicals' statement.

## Subtopic: The Entry Fee — Why the Braai Needs a Match

If burning wood is such a generous payout, here is the awkward question: why is any tree still standing? Why does your braai sit inert — wood touching oxygen all afternoon — until someone finally strikes a match?

Because even the most generous reaction charges admission. The old bonds must be winched open BEFORE the new bonds can drop and pay, and that winching bill lands first. Think of a business with guaranteed profit that still needs startup capital: no capital, no doors open, no profit — ever. That startup demand is the activation energy, and if the colliding molecules cannot raise it between them, the reaction simply does not happen, however rich the eventual payout.

The match is a startup loan. It lifts a few molecules over the admission price; their reaction pays out; the payout covers admission for the molecules beside them; and from that moment the fire is self-financing. One match, a whole evening of coals. It also explains the misery of wet wood: the early payouts get spent boiling water off the logs instead of paying the neighbours' admission, and the chain of financing snaps.

At the very instant of admission, the molecules pass through a strange in-between shape — old bonds half open, new bonds half closed, nothing you could trap in a bottle. That is the activated complex: the wobble at the top of the deal, which tips forward into products or slumps back into reactants.

Steep admission: paraffin and air coexist beside a wick indefinitely. Cheap admission: certain laboratory reagents react on contact, which is why they are stored apart. And notice what the admission price never tells you: the size of the profit. Fee and payout are separate numbers — keeping them separate is the whole of the next section. Your questions are with you now.

## Subtopic: Reading the Hill Between Two Valleys

Compress the entire lesson into one drawing: a mountain path from a high valley, over a ridge, into another valley. Reactants camp in the left valley, products in the right, and the ridge is the admission fee made visible. Chemists call the drawing a potential energy graph — height is stored energy, and walking left to right is the reaction happening.

Three readings, straight off the drawing. The climb from the LEFT valley floor to the ridge crest: that is the activation energy. The height difference between the two valley FLOORS: that is delta H — right valley lower means the journey pays out, exothermic; right valley higher means the journey ran at a loss, endothermic. And the crest itself is the home of the activated complex, balanced for an instant, choosing a side.

Number the drawing: camp at 80, crest at 170, destination at 30. The climb: 90. The overall descent: 50 down, so delta H is minus 50 — a profitable trek. Walk it backwards, products to reactants, and the same crest demands a climb of 140 from the right-hand side, with the walk finishing 50 higher than it began. One drawing, both directions, every number — that is precisely the reading skill assessed.

The final idea is the one this drawing makes effortless: the catalyst. A catalyst bores a tunnel partway up the ridge. The crossing height drops — admission is cheaper, so far more walkers cross per hour and the reaction accelerates. But a tunnel relocates neither valley: the floors stay at 80 and 30, so delta H does not shift by a single joule. Quicker, never richer. On the sketch: same start, same finish, a lower dashed crest inside the solid one.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now. Draw the valleys, mark the crest, read every answer off the heights — the picture remembers everything so you don't have to.
