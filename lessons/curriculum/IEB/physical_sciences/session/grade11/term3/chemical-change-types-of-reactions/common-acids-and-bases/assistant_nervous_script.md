Hey, it's Bianca — nine formulae to reel off from memory sounds like the kind of vocabulary quiz that makes minds go blank on cue. So we're not going to trust memory. We're going to build, because every formula in this lesson reassembles itself from two tiny facts even when your brain is hiding.

Fact one, the pieces: positives are hydrogen, sodium and potassium, one plus each. Negatives: chloride, nitrate, hydroxide and hydrogen carbonate at one minus each — plus the two heavyweights, sulphate and carbonate, at two minus each.

Fact two, the law: charges must cancel to zero. That's the entire kit. Singles pair one-to-one: HCl, HNO3, NaOH, KOH, NaHCO3 — five formulae without memorising a thing. The heavyweights need TWO singles each: H2SO4 and Na2CO3. That subscript 2 everyone drops under pressure isn't decoration — it's the second positive the double charge insisted on. Know WHY it's there and you physically can't leave it out.

Ammonia gets its own line: NH3, the base with no hydroxide anywhere. Store it as one sentence — the base that steals hydrogen from water — and the trick question becomes the one you hope for.

For the household names, take a walk through your own house tonight: vinegar and baking soda in the kitchen, washing soda in the laundry, caustic soda under the sink, that sharp ammonia spray, then pool acid and battery acid in the garage. You've been living with the syllabus all along — the test is introductions, not strangers.

Salt questions read like compound words: first name from the base, surname from the acid. Sodium sulphate? Sodium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid — decoded, not remembered.

Build from charges, walk your house, read the surnames. Three small skills covering every question in the set. Now go meet the practice questions — you've got this!
