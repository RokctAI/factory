# Part 1 — Expert

The whole acid-base chapter rests on nine named substances — four acids and five bases the syllabus requires you to produce from memory, name and formula, without hesitation. This session installs all nine, then does something better than memory: it shows how every formula assembles itself from its ions, so that logic can rebuild anything nerves erase. We end with the naming system that ties each acid to its family of salts — the link on which the remainder of the term hangs.

## Subtopic: The Four Acids You Must Know

Acid one: hydrochloric acid, HCl — a single hydrogen joined to a single chlorine, releasing one hydrogen ion per molecule in water. Your stomach manufactures it daily for digestion; hardware stores sell it diluted as pool acid; every laboratory keeps it on the shelf as the everyday workhorse.

Acid two: nitric acid, HNO3 — one hydrogen, one nitrogen, three oxygens; say it H N O three. One hydrogen ion per molecule. Its industrial life is in fertiliser production, feeding the nitrogen cycle at factory scale.

Acid three: sulphuric acid, H2SO4 — TWO hydrogens, one sulphur, four oxygens; say it H two S O four. It fills car batteries and tops the world's industrial production tables. The two hydrogens are the point: it can surrender two hydrogen ions per molecule, making it DIPROTIC, where the single-hydrogen acids are MONOPROTIC.

Acid four: ethanoic acid, CH3COOH — also called acetic acid, the acid of vinegar; say it C H three C O O H. The formula misleads at first glance: four hydrogens appear, yet only ONE is acidic — the final one, bonded to oxygen. The trio inside the CH3 group is fixed to carbon and never leaves. So ethanoic acid is monoprotic despite its apparent crowd, and it doubles as the syllabus's standard WEAK acid — one that parts with its hydrogen grudgingly and only partially, which is exactly why it is safe on a salad.

Anchor each acid to a place: stomach and pool for HCl, fertiliser works for HNO3, battery for H2SO4, vinegar bottle for CH3COOH. The questions for this section are with you now — name to formula, formula to name, and monoprotic sorted from diprotic.

## Subtopic: The Five Bases You Must Know

Base one: sodium hydroxide, NaOH — sodium, oxygen, hydrogen. On the shelf it is caustic soda, the violent heart of drain cleaner. Base two is its mirror twin: potassium hydroxide, KOH — replace sodium with potassium, change nothing else. Both are strong bases, delivering hydroxide ions, OH minus, the moment they dissolve.

Base three: ammonia, NH3 — one nitrogen, three hydrogens; say it N H three. The renegade of the list, and a favourite test question for that reason: no hydroxide, no oxygen, no metal anywhere in the formula — yet firmly a base, because in water it accepts hydrogen ions. You know it as the sting in the nose from certain household cleaners.

Base four: sodium carbonate, Na2CO3 — two sodiums, one carbon, three oxygens; say it N A two C O three. Its laundry-room name is washing soda. Base five: sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO3 — one sodium, one hydrogen, one carbon, three oxygens; say it N A H C O three. Its kitchen names are baking soda and bicarbonate of soda — the lift in a scone, the relief after an over-rich supper.

That final pair is the great mix-up of the topic, so pin it now: sodium carbonate carries TWO sodiums and no hydrogen; sodium hydrogen carbonate carries ONE sodium and one hydrogen. The names declare it — "hydrogen carbonate" announces its passenger — and the charge arithmetic of the next section will make the difference impossible to forget.

The roll call once more, cleanly: NaOH, KOH, NH3, Na2CO3, NaHCO3 — four white solids and one sharp-smelling gas dissolved in water. This section's questions are ready: names, formulae, household identities, and the carbonate pair kept apart.

## Subtopic: Building the Formulae from Their Ions

Exam pressure erodes memory but leaves construction intact — so construct. Two facts rebuild every formula in this lesson: the identity of the ions, and the law that a compound's charges must sum to zero.

The positive ions each carry one plus: hydrogen H plus, sodium Na plus, potassium K plus. The negative ions split by strength. Single minus: chloride Cl minus, nitrate NO3 minus, hydroxide OH minus. Double minus: sulphate SO4 two minus, and carbonate CO3 two minus. Hydrogen carbonate, HCO3 minus, is a carbonate that already collected one hydrogen, cancelling one of its two charges and leaving a single minus.

Build the acids. H plus with Cl minus: one-to-one, HCl. H plus with NO3 minus: one-to-one, HNO3. H plus with SO4 two minus: the double negative demands TWO hydrogens — H2SO4. That subscript 2 is not typography; it is charge balance written down.

Build the bases. Na plus with OH minus: NaOH. Na plus with CO3 two minus: two sodiums required — Na2CO3. Na plus with HCO3 minus: one-to-one — NaHCO3. And there stands the carbonate pair, derived instead of memorised: full carbonate's double charge conscripts a second sodium; hydrogen carbonate's single charge does not.

Wrong answers now expose themselves. NaCO3 collapses because one sodium leaves a stray minus. H2NO3 collapses because single-minus nitrate has room for only one hydrogen. HSO4 as an acid formula collapses because half of sulphate's charge is still unpaid. Interrogate every formula you write with a single question — do the charges reach zero? — and this entire error family goes extinct.

The questions for this section are in front of you: ions forward to formula, formula back to ions, and the charge audit deployed on impostors.

## Subtopic: Acid Plus Base — the Salt Takes the Acid's Surname

Bring an acid and a base together and they cancel each other — NEUTRALISATION: acid plus base gives salt plus water. The salt assembles at the meeting point: positive ion supplied by the BASE, negative ion supplied by the ACID. That one sentence is the skeleton key for the rest of the term.

Salt families take the acid's surname. Hydrochloric acid founds the CHLORIDES. Nitric acid founds the NITRATES. Sulphuric acid founds the SULPHATES. Ethanoic acid founds the ETHANOATES. Forwards: potassium hydroxide meets hydrochloric acid — KOH plus HCl gives KCl plus H2O, potassium chloride plus water. Potassium from the base, chloride from the acid, and the abandoned H and OH quietly become the water.

More partners. Sodium hydroxide plus nitric acid: NaOH plus HNO3 gives NaNO3 plus H2O — sodium nitrate plus water. Potassium hydroxide plus sulphuric acid: 2KOH plus H2SO4 gives K2SO4 plus 2H2O — two potassium hydroxide per sulphuric acid, because the diprotic acid holds two hydrogens and needs two hydroxides to strip them both; the coefficients record that fact.

Now reverse the reading, because assessments adore the backwards direction: which acid and base would prepare sodium sulphate? Split the name: sodium — from a sodium base, sodium hydroxide; sulphate — the surname of sulphuric acid. Which pair delivers potassium ethanoate? Potassium hydroxide and ethanoic acid. A salt is a compound word, and each half names a parent.

Today is deliberately a first pass — the full machinery of neutralisation, and of acids meeting oxides and carbonates, occupies the coming lessons. What must be reflexive TODAY is reading a salt's two names in both directions. The final questions of this part are with you now: parents to salt, and salt back to parents.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same nine characters again — but this time introduced where you already know them: kitchen, laundry, bathroom cupboard, garage.

## Subtopic: A Tour of the House in Nine Chemicals

Begin at the kitchen counter. The vinegar bottle holds ethanoic acid, CH3COOH — edible only because it is weak, stingy with its one loose hydrogen. Beside it, baking soda: sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO3, the gentle base that lifts a sponge cake and settles an aching stomach. Mix those two and the froth you get is an acid greeting a base — the primary-school volcano was this chapter all along.

The laundry shelf: washing soda, sodium carbonate, Na2CO3 — the burlier sibling of baking soda, carrying two sodiums. Under the sink, drain cleaner: sodium hydroxide, NaOH, caustic soda — a base fierce enough to digest a hair clog, and your fingertips, hence the shouting label. The eye-watering window spray: ammonia, NH3 — the base that walks past the doorman with no hydroxide on its guest pass.

Out to the garage. The white bottle of pool acid: hydrochloric acid, HCl — chemically identical to the acid your stomach brews before lunch. The car battery: sulphuric acid, H2SO4, the only one on the list carrying two acidic hydrogens. And the fertiliser bags stacked at the co-op lead straight back to nitric acid, HNO3.

Nine substances, most already in your possession. The task is simply to greet each by BOTH its names — the label on the shelf and the formula on the page — without pausing to think. The questions for this section are with you now: shelf to symbol and back.

## Subtopic: Charges Snap Together Like Magnets

Retire the memorising; start assembling. Each acid and base here is two charged pieces, and pieces click together until the total charge reads zero — magnets that will not rest while a pole stays unpaired.

The positive pieces are all single-plus: hydrogen, sodium, potassium. Most negative pieces are single-minus: chloride, nitrate, hydroxide, hydrogen carbonate. Singles pair one-to-one — click: HCl, HNO3, NaOH, KOH, NaHCO3. Five formulae earned in one move.

Two negatives are double-strength: sulphate and carbonate, two minus apiece. A double magnet demands TWO singles before it settles: sulphuric acid takes two hydrogens — H2SO4 — and sodium carbonate takes two sodiums — Na2CO3. The mysterious little subscript 2 is demystified: it is the second magnet the double charge summoned.

Fraudulent formulae now trip the alarm on sight. NaCO3? A double-minus carbonate clutched by one sodium — a pole dangling, forbidden. H2NO3? Two hydrogens crammed onto single-minus nitrate — one magnet with nowhere to stick. Run the zero-check on everything you write — pluses and minuses must cancel exactly. Two seconds, and it defuses precisely the wrong options a multiple-choice question will dangle.

Your questions for this section are ready. Assemble, never recall: pieces, charges, click to zero.

## Subtopic: Reading a Salt Like a Compound Word

A salt's name is two words, each one a birth certificate — the first name inherited from one parent, the surname from the other. First word: the metal, always from the BASE. Second word: the acid's family name. Chlorides descend from hydrochloric acid, nitrates from nitric, sulphates from sulphuric, ethanoates from ethanoic.

Read salts as sentences. Potassium chloride announces its parents outright: a potassium base and hydrochloric acid. Sodium nitrate: sodium hydroxide and nitric acid. Potassium ethanoate: potassium hydroxide and the vinegar acid. The name never deceives, and it reads equally well in either direction.

The favourite twist in assessments is the backwards task: "Choose an acid and a base to prepare sodium sulphate." Resist the urge to remember — read instead. Sodium: take the sodium base, NaOH. Sulphate: sulphuric acid's family name, H2SO4. The answer was inside the salt's name the whole time.

And what of the leftover pieces when acid meets base? The acid's hydrogen and the base's hydroxide pair off into water — H plus OH making H2O — which is why neutralisation always delivers the salt AND water. The froth in the kitchen volcano is a bonus that only carbonate bases contribute, and its full story is the business of the lessons ahead.

Today's skill is the reading. First name from the base, surname from the acid, water alongside. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — dismantle each salt like a compound word and the answers assemble themselves.
