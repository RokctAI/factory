# Part 1 — Expert

Last year's stoichiometry assumed a perfect world: reactants in exact proportion, products collected to the last gram, raw materials without a speck of contamination. Industry enjoys none of those luxuries, and Grade 11 stoichiometry adds the three real-world complications that follow: one reactant always runs dry before the other, the collected product always undershoots the prediction, and the sack of raw material is never all raw material. The thread holding the session together: a balanced equation is a promise written in moles, and every complication is judged against that promise.

## Subtopic: The Mole-Ratio Method at Grade 11 Speed

First, the three-leg journey at full speed, on an equation with demanding coefficients. Aluminium burns in oxygen to form aluminium oxide: 4Al plus 3O2 gives 2Al2O3 — four aluminium atoms react with three oxygen molecules to form two units of aluminium oxide. The question: what maximum mass of aluminium oxide can 10,8 grams of aluminium produce, with oxygen freely available?

Leg one, mass to moles. Molar mass of aluminium: 27 grams per mole. So 10,8 grams is 10,8 divided by 27 — 0,4 moles of aluminium.

Leg two, ratio across the equation. The coefficients say aluminium to aluminium oxide is four to two. Write the ratio as a fraction — coefficients of WANTED over GIVEN, two over four — and multiply: 0,4 times two over four is 0,2 moles of aluminium oxide. This is the leg where Grade 11 coefficients punish carelessness; four-to-two and three-to-two ratios invert easily in the head, and the written fraction is your protection.

Leg three, moles to mass. Molar mass of Al2O3: two aluminiums at 27 is 54, plus three oxygens at 16 is 48 — 102 grams per mole. Mass equals n times M: 0,2 times 102 — 20,4 grams of aluminium oxide. That is the theoretical yield, the absolute ceiling the atoms permit.

Every calculation in this session is this same journey with one checkpoint added. Repeat the governing rule as you work: grams never cross a balanced equation — only moles cross. The questions for this section are with you now: molar masses assembled correctly, the wanted-over-given fraction, and the full journey to 20,4 grams.

## Subtopic: The Limiting Reagent — Who Runs Out First

Real mixtures almost never arrive in perfect proportion. Give a reaction two measured quantities and one reactant will be consumed first; the reaction halts the moment it is gone. That reactant is the LIMITING REAGENT, and every product calculation must stand on it. Its partner is in excess, and some of the partner survives the reaction untouched.

The procedure: convert BOTH quantities to moles, then run the ratio test — does the second reactant cover what the first demands? Ammonia synthesis: N2 plus 3H2 gives 2NH3 — one nitrogen molecule reacts with three hydrogen molecules to form two ammonia molecules. Supplied: 14 grams of nitrogen and 2,4 grams of hydrogen.

Both to moles. Nitrogen, molar mass 28: 14 divided by 28 is 0,5 moles. Hydrogen, molar mass 2: 2,4 divided by 2 is 1,2 moles.

The test. The ratio demands three hydrogens per nitrogen, so 0,5 moles of nitrogen calls for 1,5 moles of hydrogen. Available: only 1,2 moles. The hydrogen cannot cover the demand — HYDROGEN is limiting, and nitrogen is in excess, despite nitrogen having the smaller mole count. Sit with that for a second: 0,5 is less than 1,2, yet nitrogen is the one left over, because the reaction devours hydrogen three times as fast. Mole counts alone prove nothing; only the ratio test rules.

Now build on the limiting reagent. Ammonia formed: hydrogen to ammonia is three to two, so 1,2 moles of hydrogen gives 1,2 times two over three — 0,8 moles — and 0,8 times 17 is 13,6 grams of ammonia. Nitrogen consumed: 1,2 divided by 3 is 0,4 moles, so the leftover is 0,5 minus 0,4 — 0,1 moles, which is 2,8 grams of unreacted nitrogen. Close with the mass audit: in, 14 plus 2,4 is 16,4 grams; out, 13,6 grams of ammonia plus 2,8 grams of spare nitrogen — 16,4 again. Conservation of mass is a free self-check, and it earns credit when shown.

This section's questions are ready: both quantities to moles, the ratio test in writing, product from the limiting reagent only, and the leftover excess.

## Subtopic: Percentage Yield and Percentage Purity

Two honesty measures — one aimed at the product, one at the raw material.

Percentage yield compares the laboratory's harvest with the equation's promise: actual yield over theoretical yield, times 100. Our aluminium calculation promised 20,4 grams of oxide; suppose the crucible delivers 17,34 grams. Substitute: 17,34 divided by 20,4, times 100 — 85 percent. Name the reasons yields fall short, because questions ask for them in words: reactions that stop before completion, product lost in transfers and filtrations, side reactions diverting reactant, and reverse reactions reclaiming product. And read a yield above 100 percent correctly: it is never excellence — it is wet product, contaminated product, or unreacted starting material weighed in with the goods.

Percentage purity points the other way: the raw material arrived adulterated. Dolomitic rock is largely magnesium carbonate, but never entirely. The standard interrogation: react the sample with an EXCESS of acid, measure one product precisely, and reason BACKWARDS through the equation to the mass of genuine carbonate the sample must have held.

Work it in full. A 20 gram sample of crushed rock reacts with excess sulphuric acid: MgCO3 plus H2SO4 gives MgSO4 plus H2O plus CO2 — magnesium carbonate plus sulphuric acid gives magnesium sulphate, water and carbon dioxide gas. The gas is collected: 4,48 cubic decimetres at STP. Moles of carbon dioxide: 4,48 divided by 22,4 is 0,2 moles. Carbonate to gas is one to one, so the sample contained 0,2 moles of magnesium carbonate — 0,2 times 84 grams per mole is 16,8 grams. Percentage purity: mass of pure substance over mass of sample, times 100 — 16,8 over 20 — 84 percent pure. The impurities never appear in the arithmetic because they never react; the acid is deliberately in excess so that every grain of true carbonate does.

Take this section's questions now: both formulae, the backwards route from measured gas to hidden carbonate, and the correct reading of a yield beyond 100.

## Subtopic: Airbags and Explosions — Stoichiometry at Speed

To a chemist, an explosion is a reaction that turns a small volume of solid or liquid into an enormous volume of hot gas in a fraction of a second — the damage comes from gas arriving faster than the surroundings can yield. Tame that chemistry, point it somewhere useful, and it becomes the car airbag.

Packed inside the steering wheel is a pellet of sodium azide, NaN3. On impact, a sensor triggers an igniter and the azide falls apart: 2NaN3 gives 2Na plus 3N2 — two sodium azide units decompose into two sodium atoms and three molecules of nitrogen gas. A compact solid enters; a bagful of harmless nitrogen exits, before your head crosses the gap.

The calculation is straight stoichiometry: what volume of nitrogen, at STP, comes from 97,5 grams of sodium azide? Molar mass of NaN3: sodium 23 plus three nitrogens at 14, which is 42 — 65 grams per mole. Moles of azide: 97,5 divided by 65 is 1,5 moles. Ratio across: azide to nitrogen is two to three, so 1,5 times three over two is 2,25 moles of nitrogen. Volume: 2,25 times 22,4 — 50,4 cubic decimetres of gas from a pellet lighter than a cricket ball. Delivered in roughly 30 milliseconds, that expansion is an explosion by definition; the airbag merely catches it in nylon.

Design logic worth knowing in words: the reaction must be violently fast; the gas must be inert — nitrogen, never anything flammable or poisonous; and the pellet is sized by running your three-leg journey in reverse — bag volume to moles of gas, ratio crossed backwards, moles of azide to grams of pellet.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the balanced azide equation, the 50,4 cubic decimetres, and the definition of an explosion as violent gas production.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The whole session again, this time from a lemonade stand on a hot Saturday, where every one of these complications shows up before noon.

## Subtopic: The Ingredient That Runs Out First

The stand's recipe is non-negotiable: four lemons plus one cup of sugar makes one jug of lemonade. A recipe is a ratio — exactly what a balanced equation is — and the ratio counts lemons and cups, not grams, just as an equation's ratio counts moles, never grams.

Saturday's stock: twenty lemons and six cups of sugar. How many jugs? The lazy answer stares at the smaller number — six — and blames the sugar. The correct move is the ratio test: twenty lemons demand five cups of sugar. Do we hold five? Yes — six. Sugar survives; the LEMONS run out first. Five jugs, then the stand goes quiet with one cup of sugar still unopened.

That is the limiting reagent: the ingredient that dies first and closes the stand. Every promise made to a customer is built on IT — never on the leftover sugar. The chemistry procedure is the same picture: both reactants to moles, ratio test, crown the limiting reagent, and let it alone feed the product calculation. And study the trap the stand exposes: sugar was the SMALLER number yet lasted longest, because the recipe swallows lemons four at a time. Small does not mean limiting. The ratio decides — always.

Your questions for this section are ready. Run the ratio test before promising anyone anything, and count the spare sugar while you're at it, because the leftover is its own question.

## Subtopic: What the Kitchen Promised and What Reached the Plate

The recipe promised five jugs. Now watch the Saturday unfold: one jug tips over on the trestle table, and a wasps' raid ends another half-jug before rescue. Four jugs actually reach paying customers. The stand's percentage yield: what was delivered over what was promised, times 100 — four over five — 80 percent.

Chemical kitchens spring the same leaks. Some reactant never reacts — the lemon squeezed only halfway. Product clings to the glassware — lemonade left wetting the jug walls. Side reactions steal ingredients — the wasps. Real yields live below 100 percent, and the formula is the honest scoreboard: actual over theoretical, times 100.

Here is the diagnosis that earns marks: a yield ABOVE 100 percent. Six jugs from a five-jug recipe is not brilliance — something extra got counted: ice cubes topping up the volume, a jug weighed with its lid. In a laboratory, over-100 means the product is wet, or unreacted powder is mixed into the harvest. Atoms fix a ceiling; honest measurements never rise through it.

One more swindle to price in: the raw material itself. The lemon crate you bought turns out to hold a layer of decorative plastic lemons at the bottom. Purity is the same scoreboard aimed at ingredients: mass of real lemons over mass of the whole crate, times 100. Chemists run the check with acid instead of eyesight — feed the sample plenty of acid, capture the gas, and calculate backwards to how much genuine substance had to be there, because the plastic never fizzes.

The questions for this section are with you now: promised versus delivered, why over-100 always signals contamination, and the backwards purity trick.

## Subtopic: The Pillow That Inflates Before You Blink

Now put the day's whole toolkit behind a steering wheel. Inside sits a modest solid pellet — sodium azide, about 97,5 grams, lighter than a cricket ball. Its single party trick: on an electric jolt it collapses into sodium and a torrent of nitrogen gas — the same placid gas filling most of every breath you take.

Crash. The sensor fires and the pellet runs its recipe: two units of sodium azide pay out two of sodium and THREE of nitrogen gas. Lemonade-stand arithmetic: 97,5 grams at 65 grams per crate is one and a half crates of azide. The recipe pays three crates of gas per two crates of azide — one and a half times three over two is 2,25 crates of nitrogen. Each gas crate at standard conditions occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres: 2,25 times 22,4 — just over 50 cubic decimetres. A pillow fully fat, from a palm-sized solid, in the time a wiper blade barely twitches.

That furious unpacking of solid into gas IS an explosion. Quarry charges use it to split rock; the airbag performs the identical chemistry and catches the blast in a bag. Engineers picked sodium azide because its gas is inert nitrogen — an airbag of anything flammable would answer one accident with a second — and they size the pellet by running the stand's arithmetic in reverse: bag volume, to crates of gas, to crates of azide, to grams of pellet.

So the skill you sharpened on lemons and sugar is the same one that decides how a safety pillow meets your face. The last questions of the lesson are yours now: recipe in crates, ratio test first, honest scoreboard afterwards — and every gram accounted for on both sides of the arrow.
