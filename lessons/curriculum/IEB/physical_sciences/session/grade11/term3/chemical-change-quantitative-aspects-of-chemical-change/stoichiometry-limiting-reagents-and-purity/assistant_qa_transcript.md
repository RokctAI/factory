**Bianca:** Time for the questions this topic always generates. First, the trap itself: surely the reactant with fewer moles runs out first? Smaller stockpile, earlier finish — where does that logic break?

**Tutor:** It breaks because reactants are not consumed at equal rates per mole — the coefficients assign each reactant its own consumption speed, and a small stockpile spent slowly outlasts a big stockpile spent fast. Take the lesson's ammonia example: 0,5 moles of nitrogen against 1,2 moles of hydrogen. Nitrogen is the smaller number, yet the equation devours THREE hydrogens for every ONE nitrogen, so the 1,2 moles of hydrogen can only serve 0,4 moles of nitrogen — and nitrogen ends up with spare. The reliable procedure is the ratio test, every time: convert both quantities to moles, choose one reactant, multiply its moles by the ratio to compute the partner it demands, then compare that demand with the partner actually available. Demand covered means your chosen reactant is limiting; demand unmet means the partner is. It is the lemonade stand exactly — six cups of sugar outlasted twenty lemons because the recipe drinks lemons four at a time. A small number proves nothing. The ratio rules.

***

**Bianca:** Next: someone identifies the limiting reagent correctly and then calculates the product from the excess reactant anyway, because its numbers looked friendlier. What exactly goes wrong, and by how much?

**Tutor:** What goes wrong is that the excess reactant never fully reacts — part of it is still sitting untouched when the reaction dies — so a calculation built on its full amount counts phantom material. The error is exactly the surplus fraction: in our example, computing ammonia from all 0,5 moles of nitrogen would promise 1 mole of ammonia, when only 0,8 moles can ever exist — an overstatement of 25 percent that poisons every mark downstream. Adopt this physical discipline: once the ratio test crowns the limiting reagent, box its mole count and permit nothing else to feed the product line. The excess reactant is allowed to answer exactly one question — how much of it remains — and even that answer routes through the limiting reagent: amount used equals the limiting reagent's moles crossed through the ratio, and leftover equals supplied minus used. Two roles, never swapped: the limiting reagent builds products; the excess reagent explains leftovers. Finish with the mass audit — total in equals total out — and the phantom-material error can never survive unnoticed.

***

**Bianca:** The purity method confuses people structurally: we want the carbonate INSIDE the rock, but we measure the gas coming OUT of it. Why not measure the thing we actually want?

**Tutor:** Because the thing we want is physically locked inside its impurities — no sieve or tweezers separates powdered carbonate from powdered grit. Chemistry sidesteps the problem with a selective interrogation: the genuine carbonate answers acid; the impurities stay mute. Flood the sample with acid and every mole of true magnesium carbonate surrenders exactly one mole of carbon dioxide — the balanced equation fixes that exchange rate — while the sand and clay in the rock contribute nothing. Counting the gas therefore counts the carbonate without ever isolating it: 4,48 cubic decimetres at STP is 0,2 moles of gas, hence 0,2 moles of carbonate, hence 16,8 grams, hence 84 percent of the 20 gram sample. Two safeguards make the argument watertight, and both get examined. The acid must be in EXCESS, so no genuine carbonate goes uninterrogated — if the acid ran out first, real carbonate would sit unreacted and the purity would read falsely low. And the measured gas must all come from the reaction, which is why it is collected over water or in a syringe rather than allowed to wander off into the room.

**Bianca:** Last one: the airbag question arrives dressed as a story, and people freeze at the first sentence. What is the fixed route through any story problem like it?

**Tutor:** Undress the story and the same three-leg journey is standing there; the airbag is wardrobe. Fixed route, four lines. Line one: the balanced equation — two sodium azide gives two sodium plus three nitrogen — because without the ratio nothing moves. Line two: given quantity to moles — 97,5 grams at 65 grams per mole is 1,5 moles of azide. Line three: cross with wanted over given — three over two — so 2,25 moles of nitrogen. Line four: convert to the asked-for form — at STP, times 22,4, giving 50,4 cubic decimetres. The one extra muscle worth training is the reverse direction, because design questions flip it: they give the bag volume and want the pellet mass. Then it is volume over 22,4 for moles of gas, ratio inverted to two over three for moles of azide, times 65 for grams. Rehearse the route once forward and once backward, and no storyline can hide the skeleton — sodium azide today, a fizzing tablet or a gas cylinder tomorrow, the same four lines in the same order every time.
