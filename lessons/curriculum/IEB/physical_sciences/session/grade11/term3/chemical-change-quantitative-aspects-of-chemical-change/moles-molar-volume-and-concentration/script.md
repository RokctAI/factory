# Part 1 — Expert

One unit sits underneath every calculation you will do in chemistry this year: the mole. This session rebuilds it properly and then hands you the three conversion tools that the rest of the year runs on — molar mass for anything you can weigh, molar volume for anything gaseous, and concentration for anything dissolved. We finish with percentage composition and the two-step road from an empirical formula to a molecular one. Three tools, one discipline: whatever a question gives you, turn it into moles before you do anything else with it.

## Subtopic: The Mole, Avogadro's Number and Molar Mass

The mole is the SI unit for amount of substance, and its size is fixed by one extraordinary count: one mole of anything contains 6,02 times 10 to the power 23 particles. That count is Avogadro's number, N A. Whether the particles are atoms, molecules or ions changes nothing — the count per mole never moves. The formal definition to memorise: one mole is the amount of substance containing as many particles as there are atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12.

Three symbols run the entire topic. The amount of substance in moles is n. The particle count is N, related by n equals N divided by N A. The molar mass, M, is the mass of one mole in grams per mole — assembled from the periodic table — and n equals m divided by M, the mass over the molar mass.

Molar mass is built atom by atom, and every subscript must be honoured. Ammonia, NH3: one nitrogen at 14 plus three hydrogens at 1 — 17 grams per mole. Sulphur dioxide, SO2: sulphur 32 plus two oxygens at 16 — 64 grams per mole. Potassium carbonate, K2CO3: two potassiums at 39 is 78, plus carbon 12, plus three oxygens at 16 is 48 — 138 grams per mole. The most common error across the whole topic is a dropped subscript, so count the atoms aloud before adding.

Now run the conversions both ways. How many moles is 16 grams of sulphur dioxide? Formula: n equals m over M. Substitution: 16 grams divided by 64 grams per mole. Answer: 0,25 moles. How many molecules is that? Formula: N equals n times N A. Substitution: 0,25 times 6,02 times 10 to the 23. Answer: 1,505 times 10 to the 23 molecules. Reverse direction: 0,3 moles of ammonia has a mass of 0,3 times 17 — 5,1 grams.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of the mole, molar masses with every subscript intact, and both directions of the mass and particle conversions.

## Subtopic: Molar Gas Volume and Avogadro's Law

Weighing a gas is a miserable exercise, so chemistry measures gases by volume instead — a swap made possible by one remarkable fact. At STP, standard temperature and pressure — 0 degrees Celsius and 101,3 kilopascals — one mole of ANY gas fills 22,4 cubic decimetres. That is the molar gas volume, V M, and it supplies the gas conversion: n equals V divided by V M, the volume over 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole.

Why should hydrogen and sulphur dioxide, with molar masses of 2 and 64, occupy identical volumes per mole? Because a gas is almost entirely nothing. The molecules are minute specks separated by vast emptiness, so the volume is fixed by how many specks there are, not by how large each speck is. That is the content of Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.

Run the conversion. How many moles of oxygen gas is 11,2 cubic decimetres at STP? Formula: n equals V over V M. Substitution: 11,2 divided by 22,4. Answer: 0,5 moles. Reverse direction: 1,5 moles of any gas at STP fills 1,5 times 22,4 — 33,6 cubic decimetres.

Avogadro's law also unlocks a superb shortcut: when every substance in a balanced equation is a gas at the same conditions, the coefficients can be read directly as VOLUME ratios. Take 2CO plus O2 gives 2CO2 — two carbon monoxide plus one oxygen gives two carbon dioxide. Then 12 cubic decimetres of carbon monoxide requires exactly 6 cubic decimetres of oxygen and yields 12 cubic decimetres of carbon dioxide, all at the same conditions. No molar masses, no grams — the ratio travels in the volumes themselves. The shortcut is strictly gases-only: the instant a solid or a solution appears in a problem, the route runs through moles again.

Take this section's questions now: the molar volume with its conditions, Avogadro's law word for word, the volume conversion, and the ratio shortcut with its gases-only warning.

## Subtopic: Concentration — How Much Is Dissolved in How Much

Solutions demand their own measure, because a teaspoon of salt in a glass and a teaspoon in a dam are wildly different chemical environments. Concentration is the amount of dissolved substance per volume of solution: c equals n divided by V — moles of solute over the volume of solution in cubic decimetres, giving units of moles per cubic decimetre. A 1 mole per cubic decimetre solution carries one mole of solute in every cubic decimetre.

The unit of volume is where this topic bleeds marks. The formula requires cubic decimetres, but every burette and flask in a laboratory is graduated in cubic centimetres — and 1 000 cubic centimetres make one cubic decimetre. Divide by 1 000 before anything else: 750 cubic centimetres is 0,75 cubic decimetres; 200 cubic centimetres is 0,2 cubic decimetres.

Forwards. Dissolve 8,4 grams of potassium hydroxide, KOH, and make the solution up to 750 cubic centimetres. Molar mass of KOH: potassium 39 plus oxygen 16 plus hydrogen 1 — 56 grams per mole. Moles: 8,4 divided by 56 is 0,15 moles. Formula: c equals n over V. Substitution: 0,15 moles divided by 0,75 cubic decimetres. Answer: 0,2 moles per cubic decimetre.

Backwards, in the form volumetric work loves: how many moles of solute sit in 200 cubic centimetres of a 0,6 mole per cubic decimetre solution? Rearrange: n equals c times V. Substitution: 0,6 times 0,2. Answer: 0,12 moles. Notice the fixed rhythm of every calculation so far — choose the conversion, write the formula, substitute with units, state the answer.

The questions for this section are ready: the definition and unit of concentration, the cubic-centimetre conversion done first, and c equals n over V in both directions.

## Subtopic: Percentage Composition, Empirical and Molecular Formulae

Percentage composition reports what fraction of a compound's mass each element carries: the mass of that element in one mole, over the compound's molar mass, times 100. Sulphur trioxide, SO3, molar mass 32 plus 48 — 80 grams per mole. Sulphur: 32 out of 80, which is 40 percent. Oxygen: 48 out of 80, which is 60 percent. The percentages must add to 100 — 40 plus 60 does — and that free check should be taken every time.

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms, recovered from percentages by reversing the logic. A hydrocarbon is 80 percent carbon and 20 percent hydrogen by mass. Take 100 grams so percentages become grams. Moles of carbon: 80 divided by 12 is 6,67. Moles of hydrogen: 20 divided by 1 is 20. Divide both by the smaller: 6,67 over 6,67 is 1; 20 over 6,67 is 3. Ratio one to three — empirical formula CH3.

The molecular formula is the TRUE atom count in one molecule, always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula — and finding the multiple needs one extra ingredient: the compound's molar mass. Our CH3 compound has a molar mass of 30 grams per mole. The empirical unit CH3 weighs 12 plus 3 — 15 grams per mole. Multiple: 30 divided by 15 is 2. Double every subscript: molecular formula C2H6 — ethane. Verify: two carbons at 12 is 24, six hydrogens is 6, total 30. Confirmed. And notice the percentages still hold: 24 out of 30 is 80 percent carbon.

Keep the vocabulary strict in written answers: CH3 is the ratio, C2H6 is the molecule, and a question will name which one it wants. The questions for this section are with you now — percentages that must close on 100, the divide-by-smallest routine, and the molar-mass multiple that upgrades empirical to molecular.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same tools again, seen from the loading bay of a wholesale warehouse where nothing is ever counted one item at a time.

## Subtopic: Counting by the Boxful

No warehouse counts single eggs. Everything moves in crates, because the packers put the same number in every crate. Chemistry runs its warehouse with exactly one crate size for the entire universe: 6,02 times 10 to the 23 particles per crate. The crate is called a mole. Atoms are so ludicrously tiny that only a count that enormous becomes a usable amount — one crate of ammonia molecules is just 17 grams, less than a matchbox of gas.

Here is the warehouse insight that unlocks everything: a crate of ping-pong balls and a crate of cricket balls hold the same COUNT but weigh completely different amounts. Chemistry's crates behave identically. A crate of hydrogen atoms weighs 1 gram; a crate of sulphur dioxide molecules weighs 64 grams; a crate of potassium carbonate units weighs 138. That per-crate weight is the molar mass, and the periodic table is the warehouse ledger quoting it for every element.

So when a question hands you grams and wants moles, it is really asking: how many full crates is this? Divide the mass by the crate weight. Given 16 grams of sulphur dioxide at 64 grams per crate: 16 divided by 64 is 0,25 crates — 0,25 moles. Need the raw particle count? Multiply crates by the count per crate: 0,25 times 6,02 times 10 to the 23. Grams to crates, crates to items, items back to crates — every question in this chapter is one of those three hops.

Your questions for this section are ready. Think crates: the count per crate is fixed forever; the weight per crate depends on what's packed inside.

## Subtopic: Balloons Are Honest About the Count

Gases perform a trick no solid can match. Inflate two identical balloons to the same size at the same temperature — one with helium, one with carbon dioxide. The gas masses inside differ enormously. The molecule COUNTS inside? Exactly equal. A gas is mostly vacancy — tiny specks tearing through empty space — so a balloon's size reports how many specks are ricocheting inside it, and cares nothing about the mass of each speck.

That honesty carries a law and a number. The law, Avogadro's: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. The number: at standard conditions, one full crate — one mole — of any gas occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres, roughly the space under a school desk chair. Gas questions therefore skip the scale entirely: 11,2 cubic decimetres of oxygen at standard conditions is 11,2 divided by 22,4 — half a crate, 0,5 moles. Volume over 22,4. That is the entire manoeuvre.

Better still, balanced equations become balloon bookkeeping. Two carbon monoxide plus one oxygen gives two carbon dioxide — a two to one to two ratio — so 12 balloons of carbon monoxide consume 6 balloons of oxygen and hand back 12 balloons of carbon dioxide, and no balance was harmed in the process. One label on the trick, in permanent marker: GASES ONLY. A solid or a dissolved substance in the problem sends you straight back to crates — back to moles.

The questions on this section are with you now. Balloons report counts, 22,4 is one gas crate's parking space, and coefficients are balloon ratios.

## Subtopic: How Strong Is the Mix

Two glasses of cordial. Each got the same single shot of syrup — but one glass is a shot glass topped up, and the other is a two-litre jug. Identical syrup, utterly different drinks. Your taste buds are measuring concentration: how much dissolved stuff per how much liquid.

Chemistry writes the taste test as a fraction: concentration equals moles of dissolved substance divided by cubic decimetres of solution. Dissolve 0,15 moles of potassium hydroxide and top up to 0,75 cubic decimetres: 0,15 over 0,75 gives 0,2 moles per cubic decimetre. Pour the same 0,15 moles into three times the volume and the strength drops to a third — same syrup, bigger jug.

Two habits make this section safe. First: laboratory glassware is marked in cubic centimetres, but the formula only accepts cubic decimetres, so divide by 1 000 as your opening move — 200 cubic centimetres becomes 0,2 cubic decimetres, written as its own line. Second: the formula reverses without complaint. A question quoting concentration and volume is quietly asking for moles: n equals c times V — 0,6 moles per cubic decimetre times 0,2 cubic decimetres is 0,12 moles.

One closing bridge. The percentages on a nutrition label are chemistry's ingredient declarations, and from a compound's percentages alone you can reconstruct its simplest recipe — the empirical formula: divide each percentage by that element's crate weight, then simplify the ratio. The true molecule is that recipe scaled up by a whole number, and the compound's molar mass reveals the scale factor.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now. Syrup per jug for concentration, crates for everything else, and every quantity checks in at moles before travelling anywhere.
