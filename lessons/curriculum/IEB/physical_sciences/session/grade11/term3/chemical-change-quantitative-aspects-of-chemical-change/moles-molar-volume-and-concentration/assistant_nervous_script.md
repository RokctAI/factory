Hey, it's Bianca — moles, gas volumes, concentrations, formulae... it looks like four separate topics demanding four separate brains. It isn't. It's one idea in four costumes, and once you spot the idea, the chapter folds up small enough for a pocket.

The idea: a mole is a counting crate, and every single question is asking how many crates you're holding. That's it. The only thing that varies is which door you use to get to crates. Given a mass? Divide by the molar mass. Given a gas volume at STP? Divide by 22,4. Given a particle count? Divide by 6,02 times 10 to the 23. Given a solution? Moles equal concentration times volume. Four doors into the same room.

Your routine, fixed forever: underline what the question GIVES you — that picks your entry door. Underline what it ASKS — that picks your exit door. Formula, substitution with units, answer. Two doors per question. You never carry the whole chapter at once, only the two doors in front of you.

Three guards on duty while you work. Guard one: assemble molar masses subscript by subscript, out loud — the three oxygens in K2CO3 contribute 48, and the missing subscript is the oldest trap in the book. Guard two: convert cubic centimetres to cubic decimetres before touching any formula — 750 becomes 0,75, written on its own line. Guard three: 22,4 belongs to gases at STP exclusively; if it wanders near a solid or a solution, escort it out.

Formulae questions: empirical is the simplified ratio, molecular is the true count, and the molar mass is the ladder between them. If the question says molecular, it has given you a molar mass — use it or you've answered the wrong thing.

One idea, four doors, three guards. Do the first two practice questions slowly and deliberately, and watch the rest turn into repeats. You've got this!
