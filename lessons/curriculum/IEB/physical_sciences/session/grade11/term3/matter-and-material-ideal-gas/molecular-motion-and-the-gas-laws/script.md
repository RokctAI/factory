# Part 1 — Expert

Everything a gas ever does — the stiffness of a pumped tyre, a balloon fattening in the sun, the sigh of a fizzy drink opening — is the collective behaviour of an unimaginable crowd of molecules in perpetual random flight. This session assembles the kinetic model that describes the crowd, defines the ideal gas and marks the borders where reality abandons it, extracts Boyle's law from the standard classroom practical, and finishes with the general gas equation and the ideal gas law, p V equals n R T — one line of algebra holding the entire topic.

## Subtopic: Molecular Motion — the Kinetic Model of a Gas

The kinetic model, in five commitments. A gas is made of particles in continuous, rapid, RANDOM motion. The particles collide — with each other and with the walls — and the collisions are elastic: no kinetic energy is lost in them. The particles are widely separated, so a gas is overwhelmingly empty space. Between collisions the forces the particles exert on each other are negligible. And the average kinetic energy of the particles is directly proportional to the absolute temperature.

Two everyday quantities drop straight out of these commitments. PRESSURE is the summed effect of billions of molecular impacts on the container walls: each collision delivers a minute shove, and the ceaseless hail of shoves, averaged over the wall, is what a gauge registers as steady pressure. Harder impacts, or more frequent ones, mean higher pressure. TEMPERATURE is the crowd's average kinetic energy: warm the gas and the molecules quicken; cool it and they slacken.

Respect the word AVERAGE. At any instant the molecules span a whole DISTRIBUTION of speeds — a few crawling, a few blistering, the bulk clustered in between — because random collisions perpetually strip speed from one molecule and gift it to another. Heating slides the entire distribution toward faster speeds; it does not issue every molecule the same speed. When this topic says the average speed rises, that spread is what the sentence is honouring.

Temperature carries one further obligation: the KELVIN scale. A measure of kinetic energy must place its zero where motion would actually cease — absolute zero, minus 273 degrees Celsius. Convert without exception: kelvin equals Celsius plus 273. A warm afternoon of 30 degrees Celsius is 303 kelvin. Every equation in this topic accepts kelvin and only kelvin.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five commitments of the model, pressure as collisions, temperature as average kinetic energy, and Celsius into kelvin.

## Subtopic: Ideal Gases and Real Gases

The model as stated describes an IDEAL gas — a fiction whose particles occupy zero volume of their own and feel zero forces between collisions. No actual gas meets the fiction exactly, but under ordinary conditions most come remarkably close, which is what earns the model its place.

Reality departs from the fiction under exactly two conditions, and each is one idealisation failing. At HIGH PRESSURE, molecules are crowded so tightly that their own bodies stop being negligible against the shrinking gaps between them — the zero-volume assumption collapses. At LOW TEMPERATURE, the molecules dawdle, and the weak attractions between them finally get time to grip — impacts soften, the crowd huddles, and the no-forces assumption collapses. Drive both conditions far enough and the gas surrenders entirely, condensing into a liquid.

Invert the logic for the conditions of MOST ideal behaviour: LOW pressure and HIGH temperature — molecules far apart and moving too fast for their attractions to matter. Among real gases, the nearest to ideal are the small, light, weakly-attracting ones: helium and hydrogen. Large, clingy, easily-liquefied molecules — ammonia, carbon dioxide — wander furthest from the fiction.

These points are assessed in words, so own the sentences: real gases deviate from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature; at high pressure the molecules' own volume becomes significant; at low temperature the intermolecular forces become significant; ideal behaviour is approached at low pressure and high temperature. Take this section's questions now.

## Subtopic: Boyle's Law from the Practical

The standard practical: a fixed quantity of gas sealed in a syringe or tube, temperature held steady, pressure read from a gauge while the volume is changed in steps. A typical results table: at 80 kilopascals, 3,0 cubic decimetres; at 120 kilopascals, 2,0; at 240 kilopascals, 1,0; at 480 kilopascals, 0,5.

Squeeze the table for its secret. Multiply each pressure by its volume: 80 times 3,0 is 240; 120 times 2,0 is 240; 240 times 1,0 is 240; 480 times 0,5 is 240. The product never moves. Halve the volume and the pressure doubles; cut it to a third and the pressure triples. Pressure is INVERSELY proportional to volume.

That is Boyle's law: the pressure of a fixed amount of gas at constant temperature is inversely proportional to its volume. Symbolically, p V equals k, a constant — hence p1 V1 equals p2 V2 across any two states. The kinetic model supplies the why in a single sentence: cram the same molecules into half the space and each one reaches a wall twice as often, so the collision rate — the pressure — doubles.

Three graphs to command. Pressure against volume: a falling curve, a hyperbola, approaching but never touching either axis. Pressure against 1 OVER volume: a STRAIGHT LINE through the origin — the definitive test of inverse proportionality, and the graph that proves Boyle's law from data. And p V against p: a horizontal line, the constant product laid flat.

A calculation in the standard form. A gas sample at 80 kilopascals occupies 3 cubic decimetres; what pressure results when it is squeezed to 0,75 cubic decimetres at constant temperature? Formula: p1 V1 equals p2 V2. Substitution: 80 times 3 equals p2 times 0,75. Answer: p2 equals 320 kilopascals. Declare the conditions every time — fixed amount of gas, constant temperature — because outside them the law has no force.

The questions for this section are ready: the constant product hunted through a table, the three graphs, the collision explanation, and the two-state calculation.

## Subtopic: The General Gas Equation and the Ideal Gas Law

Boyle froze the temperature. Release it and experiment hands over two further proportionalities: at constant pressure, volume grows in direct proportion to kelvin temperature; at constant volume, pressure grows in direct proportion to kelvin temperature — hotter molecules strike harder and more often, the model again. Fold all three relationships together and you get the GENERAL GAS EQUATION: p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2, valid for a fixed amount of gas, temperatures in kelvin, no exceptions.

One worked pass. A gas fills 4 cubic decimetres at 47 degrees Celsius and 90 kilopascals. What volume does it fill at 87 degrees Celsius and 135 kilopascals? Convert first: 47 plus 273 is 320 kelvin; 87 plus 273 is 360 kelvin. Formula: p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2. Substitution: 90 times 4 over 320 equals 135 times V2 over 360. The left side is 1,125. So 135 V2 equals 1,125 times 360, which is 405 — and V2 equals 3 cubic decimetres. Try it in Celsius and the arithmetic collapses into nonsense; the kelvin conversion is structural, not cosmetic.

The final step nails down the constant itself. For one mole of any gas, p V over T is the same universal number — the gas constant R, 8,31 joules per kelvin per mole. For n moles the constant is n R, and out falls the IDEAL GAS LAW: p V equals n R T — pressure times volume equals moles times R times kelvin temperature. This is the hinge joining the term's mole work to the gas laws: it counts invisible molecules using a pressure gauge and a thermometer.

Units, without mercy: R in joules per kelvin per mole forces pressure into PASCALS, volume into CUBIC METRES, temperature into kelvin. One cubic metre is 1 000 cubic decimetres. Worked: what pressure does 1,5 moles of gas exert at 350 kelvin in a 40 cubic decimetre vessel? Convert: 40 cubic decimetres is 0,04 cubic metres. Formula: p equals n R T over V. Substitution: 1,5 times 8,31 times 350, divided by 0,04. Numerator: 4 362,75. Answer: 109 069 pascals — about 109,1 kilopascals, just above atmospheric pressure. That magnitude is the built-in sanity check: everyday gas pressures cluster near 100 kilopascals.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the general gas equation under kelvin discipline, and the ideal gas law under pascal-and-cubic-metre discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same physics again, felt through your own hands — a squeezed bottle, a hot parked car, and a pump that fights back.

## Subtopic: A Room Full of Super Balls

Imagine a sealed room into which someone has hurled ten thousand super balls at once, and imagine friction has been abolished — nothing ever slows them. They carom off walls, floor, ceiling and each other, forever. That is a gas — except the balls are molecules, their number is beyond counting, and the room might be a deodorant can.

Step into the room. Balls pelt you from every direction, thousands a second — never one great shove, always a relentless drizzle of tiny knocks. That drizzle is PRESSURE. A gauge on the wall is simply counting knocks. More balls, quicker balls, or a smaller room: heavier drizzle, higher pressure.

What speeds the balls up? Heat. TEMPERATURE is nothing more than the crowd's average liveliness — warm the room and the whole swarm accelerates; chill it and the swarm slouches. The word average earns its keep: at any moment some balls dawdle while others streak, the way a school corridor holds both stragglers and sprinters, but heating shifts the entire crowd faster. And if you kept chilling until every ball froze mid-air? That temperature is absolute zero, minus 273 Celsius — the true floor. Science measures from the floor and calls the scale kelvin: a 30-degree summer day is 303 kelvin. Gas formulae accept kelvin only, because only kelvin starts counting where motion itself starts.

One honest footnote on the model: it pretends the balls are sizeless points that never tug on one another. The pretence holds beautifully while they fly far apart and fast — but pack them tight or slow them cold and it frays; push further and the crowd condenses into a puddle. The nearly-true pretence has a name: the ideal gas. Your questions for this section are with you now.

## Subtopic: Squeeze the Bottle — Boyle in Your Hands

Take an empty plastic bottle, screw the cap down, and crush it slowly in one hand. It resists — and the further you crush, the fiercer the resistance. Congratulations: you have just performed this topic's practical with your fingers.

Here is what your grip discovered. The number of balls inside never changed; the temperature never changed; only the room shrank. Same crowd, half the space: every ball reaches a wall twice as often, the drizzle of knocks doubles, and the push-back doubles. Half the volume, twice the pressure. A quarter the volume, four times the pressure. Volume down, pressure up, in exact inverse proportion — Boyle's law. Your bicycle pump preaches the same sermon: the deeper the plunger goes, the harder the trapped air argues back.

The arithmetic compresses to one sentence: pressure times volume never changes. Every question is then a balancing act: 80 kilopascals at 3 cubic decimetres becomes what at 0,75 cubic decimetres? The product must stay 240, so the pressure climbs to 320. Old pressure times old volume equals new pressure times new volume — one written line. Read the fine print first, though: same amount of gas, same temperature, or the balance is void.

And the graphs. Pressure against volume draws a playground slide — steep, then flattening, never touching the ground or the wall. Pressure against ONE OVER volume straightens the slide into a ruler-line through the origin — and a straight line through the origin is science's official signature for inverse proportion. That is the graph that proves it.

Your questions for this section are ready. Crush the bottle in your imagination: room shrinks, drizzle thickens, product stands still.

## Subtopic: One Equation for Every Balloon

A capped water bottle forgotten in a parked car on a blazing afternoon goes drum-tight by three o'clock. Nobody added air. The heat simply quickened the balls inside, and quicker balls knock harder. Pressure climbs with temperature. Let the container stretch — a balloon — and the heat inflates the VOLUME instead. Warmth leans on both dials at once.

So a gas has three dials — pressure, volume, temperature — wired together. One line of physics captures the wiring: pressure times volume, divided by kelvin temperature, is constant for a fixed amount of gas. Old state left, new state right, solve for whichever dial is hidden. The single tollgate: temperature enters in kelvin — add 273 to every Celsius reading first, because 40 degrees is not twice as hot as 20; from the true floor it is 313 against 293, a whisker apart.

Then the masterstroke. Ask what sets the value of the constant and the answer is almost indecently neat: for every mole of gas — every standard crate of 6,02 times 10 to the 23 molecules — the constant is one universal number, R, 8,31 in scientific units. Out comes the ideal gas law: p V equals n R T. Four dials now — pressure, volume, moles, temperature — and any three surrender the fourth. Which means you can take a census of invisible molecules armed with nothing but a gauge, a jug and a thermometer: the drizzle on the walls confesses how many drummers make it.

The whole topic in one image: a tireless crowd of super balls, a drizzle called pressure, a liveliness called temperature, a balancing act called Boyle, and one equation that reads the crowd's headcount from its noise. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — kelvin first, then let the equation carry the weight.
