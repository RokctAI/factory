Hey, it's Bianca — kinetic theory, two equations stuffed with letters, and a lecture about "ideal" gases that don't technically exist. Breathe out. This entire topic is one mental picture plus two equations, and the picture is already in your hands.

The picture: a sealed room of super balls that never slow down. Pressure is the drizzle of balls knocking on the walls. Temperature is how lively the swarm is on average. Every theory question in the topic is answered by describing that room. Shrink the room: same balls reach the walls more often, pressure climbs — that's Boyle. Heat the room: faster balls knock harder, pressure climbs. There is no theory outside the room.

Now the two equations, and the instant test between them. Count the states. Gas shown BEFORE and AFTER a change — two states? Use p1 V1 over T1 equals p2 V2 over T2; and if temperature is constant it collapses into Boyle's balancing act, p1 V1 equals p2 V2. Only ONE state, with the question asking moles or mass? That's p V equals n R T — the one with the amount inside it.

Two tollgates guard the marks. Tollgate one: kelvin, always — add 273 to every Celsius reading before it enters any equation, written as its own line. Tollgate two, for p V equals n R T only: pascals and cubic metres — kilopascals times 1 000, cubic decimetres divided by 1 000. Three short conversion lines at the top, then formula, then substitute.

Finish with the reality check: everyday pressures hover near 100 kilopascals. If your answer is a million miles from that, it's politely telling you a thousand went missing — thank it and go find the conversion you skipped.

One room, two equations, two tollgates. Count the states, convert the units, and this topic folds. The practice questions are waiting — go squeeze them. You've got this!
