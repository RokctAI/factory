# Part 1 — Expert

Last year you learned that charges attract and repel; this year the question becomes how many newtons, exactly. This session states Coulomb's law in wording that earns full definition marks, drills the substitution through microcoulombs and centimetres, then constructs the two problem types every assessment returns to: three charges along a straight line, and three charges arranged at a right angle. The vector work from earlier this term carries the load here, because electrostatic forces add exactly like every other force you have met.

## Subtopic: Charge, the Coulomb, and the Statement of the Law

Charge is measured in coulombs, symbol capital C, and it comes in two varieties, positive and negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Charge is quantised: the smallest free charge is the electron's, 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs, so every real charge is a whole-number multiple of it. A sphere carrying negative 4,8 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs has gained 4,8 times 10 to the minus 6 divided by 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 — that is 3 times 10 to the 13 electrons.

Coulomb's law, word-perfect: the magnitude of the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the magnitudes of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. In symbols, F equals k Q one Q two over r squared, with the constant k equal to 9 times 10 to the 9 newton metre squared per coulomb squared, supplied on the data sheet. Notice whose twin this is: gravitation, met two weeks ago — same product upstairs, same squared distance downstairs, charges standing in for masses.

Work the standard substitution. A charge of positive 3 microcoulombs sits 15 centimetres from a charge of negative 5 microcoulombs. Convert before anything else: 3 microcoulombs is 3 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs, 5 microcoulombs is 5 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs, and 15 centimetres is 0,15 metres. Formula: F equals k Q one Q two over r squared. Substitution: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 5 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,15 squared, which is 0,135 divided by 0,0225. Answer: 6,0 newtons — and since the charges are unlike, the force is attractive.

The discipline that saves the topic: substitute MAGNITUDES only. The signs never enter the formula; they answer one separate question — pull or push — and you state that answer in words at the end.

Attempt this section's questions now: conversions first, magnitudes only, direction in words.

## Subtopic: Three Charges in a Straight Line

With more than one charge present, each PAIR is processed on its own, and the forces are then added as vectors. On a straight line that reduces to addition and subtraction — but only after each direction has been reasoned from attraction and repulsion.

The method: isolate the charge the question asks about. For each neighbour, calculate the magnitude of its force with one Coulomb's law computation, pretending no other charge exists. Draw each force as an arrow on the isolated charge — attraction points toward the neighbour, repulsion points away. Then pick a positive direction and add.

Work it. Three point charges lie on a line. Charge A, positive 3 microcoulombs, on the left. Charge B, negative 2 microcoulombs, sits 0,2 metres to the right of A. Charge C, positive 4 microcoulombs, sits 0,3 metres to the right of B. Find the net force on B.

Force of A on B: unlike charges, attraction, pulling B LEFT, toward A. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,2 squared, which is 0,054 divided by 0,04, giving 1,35 newtons.

Force of C on B: unlike charges again, attraction, pulling B RIGHT, toward C. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 4 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared, which is 0,072 divided by 0,09, giving 0,8 newtons.

The arrows oppose each other, so subtract, and the bigger arrow wins: 1,35 minus 0,8 equals 0,55 newtons, directed LEFT, toward A. Had both arrows agreed, you would have added — which is precisely why the arrows must exist before any arithmetic does.

The classic error: using the wrong separation. B feels A across 0,2 metres and C across 0,3 metres — each pair keeps its OWN r, and the 0,5 metres from A to C belongs to no calculation about B.

This section's questions are with you now: two computations, two arrows, one signed sum.

## Subtopic: Charges at Right Angles — Coulomb's Law in Two Dimensions

Place three charges on the corners of a right angle and the resultant arrives by Pythagoras, exactly as in the vectors topic.

Work the standard arrangement. Charge Q one, positive 2 microcoulombs, occupies the corner. Charge Q two, negative 5 microcoulombs, sits 0,5 metres due north of it. Charge Q three, positive 3 microcoulombs, sits 0,3 metres due east of it. Find the net force on Q one.

Force from Q two: unlike charges, attraction, so Q one is pulled NORTH, toward Q two. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 5 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,5 squared, which is 0,09 divided by 0,25, giving 0,36 newtons.

Force from Q three: like charges, repulsion, so Q one is pushed WEST, away from Q three. Magnitude: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 3 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared, which is 0,054 divided by 0,09, giving 0,6 newtons.

One force north, one west — perpendicular, so Pythagoras finishes it. Magnitude: the square root of 0,36 squared plus 0,6 squared, which is the square root of 0,1296 plus 0,36, the square root of 0,4896, giving 0,7 newtons. Direction: the tangent of the angle north of west is 0,36 over 0,6, which is 0,6, so the angle is 31,0 degrees. The net force on Q one is 0,7 newtons at 31,0 degrees north of west.

The procedure never varies: one Coulomb computation per neighbour, direction from attraction or repulsion, then Pythagoras and the inverse tangent. Draw the corner sketch BEFORE the calculator comes out, and label each arrow with its magnitude as it is found.

Work through this section's questions now, sketch first, calculator second.

## Subtopic: Touching Spheres and the Exam Traps

Identical conducting spheres share their total charge equally on contact. Add the two charges WITH their signs, divide by two, and each sphere leaves the encounter carrying that average.

Work it. Sphere P carries positive 8 microcoulombs; sphere T carries negative 4 microcoulombs; their centres are 0,2 metres apart. Before touching: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 8 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 4 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,2 squared, which is 0,288 divided by 0,04 — 7,2 newtons, attractive. Now they touch and are returned to their positions. Total charge: positive 8 plus negative 4 equals positive 4 microcoulombs; each sphere now holds positive 2 microcoulombs. After touching: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, multiplied by 2 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,04, which is 0,036 divided by 0,04 — 0,9 newtons, now REPULSIVE, both spheres being positive. One touch changed both the size and the character of the force — a complete question in itself, and a favourite.

Now the trap inventory. Trap one: units — microcoulombs become times 10 to the minus 6, nanocoulombs times 10 to the minus 9, and every centimetre or millimetre becomes metres BEFORE squaring. Trap two: signs inside the formula — substitute magnitudes, reason the direction separately, always. Trap three: the distance runs between the CENTRES of spheres, not their surfaces. Trap four: ratio questions run on gravitation logic — halve the distance, quadruple the force; double both charges, quadruple the force. Trap five: charge sharing on contact requires the spheres to be stated IDENTICAL; unequal spheres share unequally.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — hunt each trap on purpose.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same law with the powers of ten resting, told through winter static, a familiar twin, and a tug-of-war.

## Subtopic: The Crackle in Your Jersey

Peel off a jersey on a dry winter evening and listen to it crackle and snap. Rub a balloon on your hair and park it on the ceiling. Shuffle across a carpet, reach for a door handle, and get nipped. All one phenomenon: charge on the move — and nothing exotic is happening. Everything is made of atoms; atoms hold positive protons and negative electrons in equal measure, so ordinary objects are neutral. Rubbing scrapes electrons off one surface onto the other. The surface that GAINS electrons turns negative; the one that LOSES them turns positive. Nothing is created or destroyed — charge merely changes address.

Once separated, one rule governs everything: opposites attract, likes repel. The balloon clings to the ceiling by attraction; freshly rubbed hair lifts and spreads because every strand, carrying the same sign, flees its neighbours.

Two sizes are worth carrying in your head. The electron's charge is almost unthinkably small — which is why real objects carry MICROcoulombs, millionths of a coulomb, each millionth still containing about ten million million electrons. And a whole coulomb is monstrous: two full coulombs at arm's length would push with thousands of millions of newtons. Nature deals in millionths for good reason.

Your questions on this section are with you now — and remember, only the electrons ever move house.

## Subtopic: Gravity's Louder Twin

You already know a law with exactly this shape — you met it two weeks ago. Gravitation: multiply the masses, divide by the distance squared. Coulomb: multiply the charges, divide by the distance squared. Identical skeleton, different skin. Every skill from that topic transfers here without modification.

Double one charge — double the force. Double both — four times. Double the DISTANCE — down to a quarter, because distance is always squared, in both laws. Any ratio question you could do with gravitation, you can already do here.

Two differences deserve respect, though. Gravity only ever pulls; electricity pulls OR pushes, sign-dependent. And the electric constant is gigantic where gravity's was minuscule — which is why a balloon rubbed for three seconds holds itself against the gravitational grip of the ENTIRE planet. Between everyday objects, electricity out-shouts gravity every time; you only fail to notice because most things carry no net charge.

And keep the bookkeeping rule from Part 1 close: charges enter the formula as clean positive numbers — converted millionths — while the plus and minus signs wait outside, deciding one thing only: pull or push. Numbers set the size; signs set the direction. Separate jobs, always.

## Subtopic: The Tug-of-War on the Middle Charge

The favourite picture in this topic: three charges in a row, and the question wants the experience of the middle one. Treat it as a tug-of-war.

The middle charge feels two separate forces, one per neighbour, and each neighbour behaves as if the other did not exist. So interrogate one neighbour at a time. Is this neighbour pulling my charge toward it, or shoving it away? Opposite signs pull; same signs shove. Draw the little arrow. Now the other neighbour: another arrow.

Read the two arrows before touching a calculator. Pointing the same way? The forces cooperate — add them. Pointing at each other? They compete — subtract, and the larger one dictates the direction. The entire strategy is settled by the arrows; the calculator only prices each arrow afterwards, using multiply-the-charges-divide-by-the-distance-squared once per neighbour, each neighbour at its OWN distance.

One more picture for the corner case. If one neighbour sits north of your charge and the other sits east, the two arrows stand at right angles — neither helping nor hindering each other, just sideways. The combined pull is then the diagonal, found with the same square-add-root move as any vector pair: arrows of 0,36 and 0,6 newtons combine into 0,7.

Arrows first, always and forever. The learner who computes before drawing will eventually add forces that should subtract, and the answer is lost at the first line. Arrows, then sizes, then combine.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — draw the tug-of-war before you price a single arrow.
