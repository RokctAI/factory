Hey, it's Bianca — fields can feel like the first properly abstract thing in physics. You cannot see one, you cannot touch one, and suddenly there are two formulas with a big Q and a small q that look identical at midnight before a test. Breathe. There are exactly three ideas here, and you already use all three at the shops.

Idea one: every charge carries an invisible cushion of influence — strong up close, fading with distance. That cushion is the field. A visiting charge only ever feels the cushion at the exact spot where it stands. Arrows on the map show which way a POSITIVE visitor gets shoved: away from positives, toward negatives. Electrons read the map backwards — that reversal is a guaranteed one-marker, so say it out loud in your answer.

Idea two: field strength is a unit price. Not "how big is the shove" — that depends on the size of the visitor — but how many newtons PER COULOMB. Rand per litre, newtons per coulomb, same trick. That is all E equals F over q says: divide the force by the charge that felt it, and the visitor drops out of the story.

Idea three: sorting the two formulas is just asking who is who. Big Q is the CREATOR of the field; small q is the FEELER standing in it. Given a force on a charge? Use E equals F over q. Given a source charge and a distance? Use E equals k Q over r squared — and convert to metres BEFORE squaring. Label creator and feeler in the margin and the formulas choose themselves.

Two-charge questions are the tug-of-war again: one arrow per source, each priced at its own distance, add when the arrows agree, subtract when they clash. Between unlikes the arrows team up; between likes there is a dead spot, closer to the weaker charge.

Cushion, unit price, creator-versus-feeler. Three ideas, and every practice question is one of them in costume. Go unmask them — you've got this!
