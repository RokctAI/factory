# Part 1 — Expert

Coulomb's law prices the force between two charges that both exist. The electric field answers a subtler question: what does a single charge do to the empty space around it, before anything arrives to feel it? This session defines the field precisely, draws the standard field-line patterns, and works the topic's two calculations — E equals F over q where a force on a test charge is known, and E equals k Q over r squared where a source charge and a distance are known. It ends with fields from two charges at once, where the vector discipline from earlier this term earns its keep yet again.

## Subtopic: The Field Concept and the Rules of Field Lines

The definition, examinable: an electric field is a region of space in which an electric charge experiences a force. Every charge dresses the space around itself in such a region, whether or not another charge is present to notice. The field is electrostatics' middleman: charge A never reaches across to touch charge B — charge A builds a field everywhere, and charge B responds to the field at its own location.

The DIRECTION of the field at any point is defined as the direction of the force that a POSITIVE test charge would feel there. That one sentence generates every arrow you will ever draw: fields point away from positive charges, which shove a positive test charge off, and toward negative charges, which reel it in.

Field lines turn the invisible into a drawing, under four rules worth theory marks. They begin on positive charge and end on negative charge. They never cross, because a single point cannot offer two directions of force. The arrowhead shows the way a positive test charge would be pushed. And line SPACING is meaning: crowded lines, strong field; sparse lines, weak field — so every pattern bunches near its charge and thins with distance.

The patterns to master: a lone positive charge, lines radiating straight outward like spokes; a lone negative charge, the same spokes with the arrows reversed, flowing inward; unlike charges, lines leaving the positive and curving around to enter the negative; two like positive charges, lines bending apart with a dead spot between them where the field vanishes; and a charged conducting sphere, whose EXTERNAL field is indistinguishable from a point charge concentrated at its centre.

Attempt this section's questions now — the four rules and the pattern sketches are straightforward marks.

## Subtopic: Field Strength from Force — E Equals F Over q

The electric field strength at a point is defined as the force per unit positive charge placed at that point. In symbols, E equals F divided by q, with units of newtons per coulomb.

Read the definition as a claim about ownership: the field strength belongs to the LOCATION, not to whatever visits it. Double the visiting charge and the force on it doubles as well, so the quotient F over q is untouched. The field was there before the test charge arrived and remains after it leaves.

Work it forward. A small charge of positive 4 times 10 to the minus 6 coulombs placed at point P experiences a force of 0,02 newtons directed north. Formula: E equals F over q. Substitution: 0,02 divided by 4 times 10 to the minus 6. Answer: 5 000 newtons per coulomb, north — the field's direction is the force direction on the positive test charge.

Work it backward. That same 5 000 newton-per-coulomb field now acts on an electron, charge magnitude 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs. Formula: F equals E times q. Substitution: 5 000 multiplied by 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19. Answer: 8 times 10 to the minus 16 newtons — and because the electron is NEGATIVE, the force on it points SOUTH, against the field. That reversal is a one-mark sting that appears constantly.

The classic error: dividing by the source charge instead of the test charge. In E equals F over q, the q is always the charge SITTING IN the field, feeling the force — never the charge that created the field. The creator has its own formula, coming next.

## Subtopic: Field of a Point Charge — E Equals k Q Over r Squared

To find the field a known source charge creates, merge Coulomb's law with the definition. The force on a test charge q at distance r from source Q is k Q q over r squared. Divide by q, as the definition commands, and the test charge cancels out entirely: E equals k Q over r squared, with k the familiar 9 times 10 to the 9.

That cancellation repeats gravitation's lesson: the field at a point depends only on the source and the distance, never on the visitor. And the inverse square rides along — double the distance from the source, quarter the field; triple it, a ninth.

Work it. Find the electric field 0,2 metres from a point charge of positive 8 microcoulombs. Formula: E equals k Q over r squared. Substitution: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 8 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,2 squared. That is 72 000 divided by 0,04. Answer: 1,8 times 10 to the 6 newtons per coulomb, directed away from the charge, because the source is positive.

Two disciplines hold the marks. Convert distances to metres BEFORE squaring, exactly as in Coulomb's law. And attach a direction to every answer: away from a positive source, toward a negative one. A field strength without a direction is half an answer.

Know which formula serves which question, because mixing them is the standard failure. E equals F over q suits a point where a FORCE on some visiting charge is known or wanted. E equals k Q over r squared suits a point at a known DISTANCE from a known SOURCE. Inventory the given quantities and the formula selects itself.

Work through this section's questions now, stating a direction with every field.

## Subtopic: Fields from More Than One Charge

Fields superpose: the net field at a point is the vector sum of the fields each source creates there. With the charges on a straight line the vectors are one-dimensional, but each direction must still be reasoned source by source, exactly like the forces tug-of-war.

The routine mirrors the force routine. Mark the point of interest — usually empty space, no charge required there. For each source, compute the field magnitude at the point from k Q over r squared, using that source's own distance. Draw each contribution's arrow: away from positive sources, toward negative ones. Then combine the arrows.

Work it. Points A and B are 0,6 metres apart. A charge of positive 4 microcoulombs sits at A; a charge of negative 6 microcoulombs sits at B. Find the net field at point P, midway between them, 0,3 metres from each.

Contribution from A: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 4 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared, which is 36 000 divided by 0,09, giving 4 times 10 to the 5 newtons per coulomb. Direction: away from A, since A is positive — from A toward B.

Contribution from B: 9 times 10 to the 9, multiplied by 6 times 10 to the minus 6, divided by 0,3 squared, which is 54 000 divided by 0,09, giving 6 times 10 to the 5 newtons per coulomb. Direction: TOWARD B, since B is negative — which, standing at P, is again from A toward B.

Both arrows agree, so add: 4 plus 6 gives 10, so the net field is 1 times 10 to the 6 newtons per coulomb, directed from A toward B. Between unlike charges the contributions always cooperate like this; between LIKE charges they compete, and somewhere between two like charges the net field passes through zero — nearer to the weaker source.

The trap: source signs never enter the magnitudes. They set arrow directions only, and the addition happens on arrows. Substituting a negative Q and trusting the algebra doubles your ways to fail.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now — one contribution per source, arrows before adding, direction in the answer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea stripped of formality, beginning with a question that sounds philosophical: how can a charge shove something it never touches?

## Subtopic: The Invisible Cushion Around Every Charge

Bring two like charges close and something uncanny happens: a push with no rope, no contact, no visible agent — just refusal. The field is the physics answer to how that works.

Think of every charge as travelling with an invisible cushion of influence, filling the space around it in all directions — dense and forceful up close, thinning with distance, never quite reaching an edge. That cushion IS the electric field. A second charge wandering in does not deal with the faraway first charge directly; it feels the cushion AT THE EXACT SPOT where it stands, and is shoved or tugged accordingly.

To chart a cushion, physicists carry a standard probe: a small positive test charge, moved from spot to spot like a candle through a dark room. Wherever the probe feels a shove, draw an arrow in the shove's direction. Join the arrows and field lines appear — a wind map of the invisible.

Reading the map needs three facts. Arrows point AWAY from positive charges: the positive probe is chased out. Arrows point TOWARD negative charges: the probe is drawn in. And where lines crowd together the field is strong; where they spread out it is weak — which is why every map is dense at the charge and sparse far away. The cushion fades with distance.

Keep one image: a lone positive charge is a spiky seed pod, spikes radiating outward. A lone negative charge is the same pod inhaling.

Your questions on this section are with you now — read every arrow as the shove on a small positive probe.

## Subtopic: How Hard Is the Shove at This Spot?

A field map gives directions. The next question is strength: standing at one exact spot, how hard is the shove?

Here is the snag: the shove depends on the probe you send. A large test charge feels a large force, a small one a small force — at the very same spot. Quoting the force describes your probe, not the place. The repair is to quote force PER COULOMB: divide the force by the charge that felt it, and the probe drops out of the story. What remains — newtons for every coulomb — is the property of the spot itself. That number is the electric field strength.

It is precisely how unit pricing works. A till slip totalling 90 rand says nothing until you know it bought six litres of milk; rand PER litre is what lets you compare shops. Newtons per coulomb compares places in a field. A spot rated 5 000 newtons per coulomb delivers 0,02 newtons to a four-millionths-of-a-coulomb visitor and a different force to a different visitor, but the RATE — 5 000 per coulomb — stands firm until the sources move.

And when you know the charge that built the field, a second route reaches the same number: the source's charge over the distance squared, scaled by the big constant. The inverse square never rests — walk twice as far from the source and the rate drops to a quarter.

One caution before the questions: a negative visitor is shoved BACKWARDS, against the arrows. The map is drawn for positive probes; electrons read it mirror-reversed.

## Subtopic: When Two Charges Both Have a Say

Real questions seldom stop at one charge. With two charges in the room, every point in space hears from both. What does a spot experience? Both influences at once, combined like arrows.

Stand between a positive charge on your left and a negative charge on your right. The positive one blows you rightward, away from itself. The negative one draws you rightward, toward itself. Two votes, both saying right — so the votes add, and the field between unlike charges is strong. That is why the map between them is a thick bundle of lines pouring from the positive into the negative.

Now stand between TWO positive charges. The left one pushes you right; the right one pushes you left. The votes clash. Near the left charge its vote dominates and the field points right; near the right charge, the reverse; and at one particular spot the votes cancel exactly — the field is ZERO. Dead centre if the charges are equal; shifted toward the WEAKER charge if not, since you must stand closer to the weakling for its fading voice to match the stronger one's. That dead spot is a beloved question, and you can now locate it by pure reasoning.

The bookkeeping, once and cleanly: compute each charge's contribution at your spot separately, each with its own distance; draw the two arrows; combine — add when they agree, subtract when they clash. It is the tug-of-war from Coulomb's law again, except the rope now pulls on empty space, at a point where nothing needs to sit.

That is the electric field entire: every charge broadcasting into the space around it, and every point in space totalling what it receives.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — one arrow per charge, then let the arrows vote.
