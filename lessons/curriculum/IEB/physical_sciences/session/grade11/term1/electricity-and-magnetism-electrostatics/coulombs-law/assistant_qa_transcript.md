**Bianca:** Class questions before we close the circuit! First one: why are we forbidden from putting the minus signs into the formula? Surely the calculator can handle a negative number times a positive number?

**Tutor:** The calculator handles it fine; the problem is what the answer then MEANS. Substitute a negative charge and Coulomb's law hands you a negative force — and a negative force is not a physical direction, it is just algebra pointing at nothing. Learners see the minus and do one of two wrong things: they announce "the force is negative" as if that were an answer, or they attach the minus to whichever direction they drew first, which is a coin flip. The deeper issue is that in two-dimensional problems the formula cannot know your geometry. It has no idea that Q two sits north of Q one; only your sketch knows that. So the division of labour is deliberate: magnitudes go into the formula to price the force, and the signs stay outside to answer exactly one question — do these two charges pull together or push apart? You then place the arrow yourself, on the sketch, using that answer. Attraction: arrow toward the neighbour. Repulsion: arrow away. This habit costs nothing on the easy questions and saves everything on the hard ones, where three charges at a corner would turn signed substitution into a tangle no calculator can rescue.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the straight-line problem, both forces on B were attractions — but one pointed left and one pointed right. A classmate wants to know how the same kind of force can point in opposite directions.

**Tutor:** Because direction belongs to the GEOMETRY, not to the type of force. Attraction means "toward the other charge" — and toward is a different compass direction for each neighbour. B sits between A and C. Toward A is leftward; toward C is rightward. Both neighbours attract B, and those two attractions face off against each other precisely because the attractors stand on opposite sides. This is why the tug-of-war picture earns its keep: two teams pulling the same middle object toward themselves are both pulling, yet they pull in opposite directions, and the rope moves toward the stronger team. In our numbers, A's team pulls with 1,35 newtons and C's with 0,8, so B experiences 0,55 newtons toward A. Flip one scenario to test yourself: make C negative like B, and suddenly C repels B — away from C is now leftward too, both arrows agree, and the magnitudes add to 2,15 newtons. Same charges' positions, one sign changed, completely different arithmetic. That is the whole lesson: signs and positions together set each arrow, and the arrows decide whether you add or subtract. Never let the word attraction trick you into thinking all attractions share a direction.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the one everyone flagged: after the spheres touched, the force dropped from 7,2 newtons all the way to 0,9. Why so dramatic? The total charge only went from twelve microcoulombs of stuff to four.

**Tutor:** Careful with that bookkeeping — the total SIGNED charge was never twelve. Before contact the spheres held positive 8 and negative 4, which nets to positive 4 microcoulombs, and after contact they still hold positive 4 in total; charge was conserved to the last electron. What collapsed was the PRODUCT, because the force cares about the product of the two magnitudes, not the total. Before: 8 times 4 is 32 units of product. After the equal split into 2 and 2: just 4 units. The product fell by a factor of 8, and so did the force — 7,2 over 8 is exactly 0,9. There is a lovely extra twist to notice: when the spheres touched, the negative 4 first cancelled 4 of the positive 8 — opposite charges annihilating into neutrality — and only the surviving positive 4 was left to share. Cancellation is what makes touching so destructive to the force. And a final subtlety for full marks: this equal split happens only because the question says IDENTICAL spheres. Different-sized conductors share charge unequally, so always quote the word identical in your justification — it is the licence for dividing by two.

**Bianca:** Last question: for the right-angle problems, is there a quick way to sanity-check the final direction? People keep getting 31 degrees but then writing north of EAST instead of north of west.

**Tutor:** There is, and it takes five seconds: read the direction off your two arrows before trusting any angle. The resultant of two perpendicular forces must live in the quadrant BETWEEN those arrows — always. Our Q one is pulled north by 0,36 newtons and pushed west by 0,6 newtons, so the resultant must point somewhere between north and west; any answer containing the word east is dead on arrival, whatever the number says. Then refine with the sizes: the west arrow is the stronger one, so the resultant leans closer to west than to north — meaning the angle measured FROM west must be the smaller of the two options, less than 45 degrees. That is why 31,0 degrees north of west is consistent: a small tilt away from the dominant direction. If your tangent had produced 59 degrees north of west instead, the arrows would be shouting that you inverted the ratio — 0,6 over 0,36 instead of 0,36 over 0,6. So the ritual is: quadrant from the arrows, lean from the bigger force, then the inverse tangent for the precise number, and finally say the direction in full words tied to the compass. The sketch is not decoration; it is your error detector, and in two-dimensional electrostatics it will catch you before the marker does.
