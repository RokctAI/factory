Hey, it's Bianca — deep breath. I know Newton's laws feel like three slippery sentences that everyone else seems to just "get". Here is the secret: you already know all three from ordinary life. The lesson only gives your experiences their proper names.

Law one, you have lived: the taxi brakes and your body keeps going. Things carry on doing what they are doing until something forces a change. That stubbornness is inertia, and mass is how much of it a thing has. When a question mentions seatbelts, tell that story — the taxi got a backward force, the passenger did not, so the passenger carried on.

Law two is a recipe, not a riddle. Find the LEFTOVER force first: take what pushes forward, subtract what drags backward. Then share the leftover across the kilograms — that share is the acceleration. One hundred and twenty newtons of pull minus forty-five of friction leaves seventy-five, spread over twenty-five kilograms, three each. Done. And the golden shortcut: the words "at rest" or "constant velocity" mean the leftover is ZERO — write that down immediately and half the question solves itself.

Law three: every push is answered by an equal push back, landing on the OTHER object. Push the wall, the wall pushes you. When a question shows a book on a table and asks about pairs, check one thing before anything else: are the two forces on different objects? If they are both on the book, it is not a pair — that trap catches half the class every year, but not you.

Your survival kit for the practice questions: draw the dot, draw every arrow, pick a positive direction and write it down, keep vertical and horizontal in separate sums, and never let kilograms pretend to be newtons — mass is kilograms, weight is mass times nine comma eight.

Three stories: the braking taxi, the leftover push, the wall that answers back. You know them all. Go prove it on the practice questions — you've got this!
