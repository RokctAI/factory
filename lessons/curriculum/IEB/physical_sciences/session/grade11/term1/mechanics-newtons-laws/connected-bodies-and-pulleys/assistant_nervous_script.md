Hey, it's Bianca — I know the feeling when a question has TWO objects, a string AND a pulley, and it seems like the workload just doubled. Here is the good news: connected bodies are actually easier than single ones, because the method never changes. Two moves, every time, in the same order.

Move one: weigh the whole train. Pretend the tied-together objects are one big object. Add the masses. Count only the outside forces — the pull, the friction, a hanging weight — and completely ignore the string, because its two tugs cancel each other inside the system. Leftover force divided by total mass: that is your acceleration, and it belongs to every member of the train.

Move two: zoom in on the laziest object — the one with the fewest forces touching it, usually the hanging mass or the trailing trolley. Write its own little equation with the acceleration you already found, and the tension is the only unknown left. One line, done.

Three facts to keep you honest along the way. The string cannot stretch, so everyone shares one acceleration — never invent a second one. The string cannot push, so its arrows always pull along the string. And when you zoom in on one body, use only THAT body's friction and THAT body's forces — borrowing the pair's total friction is the classic own-goal.

And here is the idea that makes you look like a professional: while anything is accelerating, the string tension is LESS than the hanging weight. It has to be — if the string held the full weight, the mass would balance and nothing would move. Tension only equals the full weight when everything is still or cruising steadily.

For the practice questions: draw a free-body diagram for each body, declare the direction the system actually moves as positive, then move one, move two. Whole train, then one wagon. Say it as you work and the marks stack up. You've got this!
