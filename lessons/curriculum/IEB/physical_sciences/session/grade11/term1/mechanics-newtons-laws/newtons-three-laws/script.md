# Part 1 — Expert

Three sentences written in the seventeenth century still govern every push, pull, collision and launch you will ever analyse. This session states each law in wording a marker can tick, builds the four-step routine that converts any force situation into an equation, and works the standard applications: a crate dragged over a workshop floor, a person on a scale inside a moving lift, and a rocket climbing off its pad. The free-body diagram from last year is the working tool throughout — one dot, labelled arrows — so keep it at your fingertips.

## Subtopic: Newton's First Law and the Meaning of Inertia

The statement, precisely: an object continues in its state of rest, or of uniform velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a net or unbalanced force. The load-bearing words are NET FORCE, not force. Forces act on almost everything almost always; the question is whether they cancel.

INERTIA is the tendency of an object to resist any change in its state of rest or of uniform motion, and MASS is the measure of inertia. A delivery van loaded with cement is harder to pull away at a green light and harder to stop at a red one than the same van empty, with identical engine and brakes, because the load adds mass, and mass is stubbornness.

Seatbelts are the reasoning exercise this topic loves, so build the argument in full. A taxi travels down the main road at 80 kilometres per hour, and so does every passenger inside it. The driver brakes hard for a dog. The brakes and the road apply a large backward force to the TAXI, and the taxi slows. No comparable force acts on the passengers, so the first law takes over: their bodies continue forward at the original velocity until something intervenes — the seat in front, the windscreen, or, if everyone was sensible, the seatbelt, whose entire job is to supply the missing backward force so that body and vehicle slow together. The headrest is the same argument reflected: in a rear-end bump the seat shoves the torso forward, an unsupported head briefly stays behind, and the neck pays the price — that is whiplash.

Two misconceptions to bury today. First, "a force is needed to keep things moving" is false; a force is needed to CHANGE motion, and a puck gliding on smooth ice needs nothing to keep gliding. Second, "uniform velocity" includes direction, so a car rounding a bend at constant speed is not force-free — its direction is changing, and something must be pushing it sideways.

Take this section's questions now: the statement word for word, inertia tied to mass, and the seatbelt argued rather than recited.

## Subtopic: Newton's Second Law and the Fnet = ma Method

The statement: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force, with an acceleration directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass. In symbols, F net equals m a. The unit falls straight out: one newton is the net force that accelerates one kilogram at one metre per second squared, so a newton is a kilogram metre per second squared.

The method is fixed, and writing it out earns method marks even on a day the arithmetic betrays you. Step one: free-body diagram — the object shrinks to a dot, every force becomes a labelled arrow from that dot. Step two: choose a positive direction and say so. Step three: write F net equals m a along that direction, forces with the positive direction added, forces against it subtracted. Step four: substitute, solve, and state the answer with a unit and a direction.

Work it. A 25 kilogram crate is dragged across a rough workshop floor by a horizontal rope pulling with 120 newtons; friction is 45 newtons. Free-body diagram: weight down, normal force up, 120 newtons forward, 45 newtons backward. Forward is positive. Formula: F net equals F applied minus f. Substitution: 120 minus 45. Answer: F net equals 75 newtons forward. Formula: a equals F net divided by m. Substitution: 75 divided by 25. Answer: a equals 3 metres per second squared in the direction of the pull.

Now flip the question: what pull keeps that crate moving at CONSTANT velocity? Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so the net force is m times zero, which is zero, so the pull must exactly match the friction: 45 newtons. The phrase "constant velocity" is never decoration — it is an instruction to set F net to zero.

The recurring error is smuggling the weight into the horizontal equation. Vertical forces live in the vertical equation, horizontal in the horizontal; keep the two ledgers separate and they never corrupt each other.

Work this section's questions now, and lay out every solution as diagram, positive direction, equation, substitution, answer.

## Subtopic: Vertical Applications — Lifts, Cables and Apparent Weight

In vertical problems two forces usually contest: the weight, w equals m g, pulling down with g equal to 9,8 metres per second squared, and a normal force or tension pulling up.

Put a 60 kilogram learner on a bathroom scale in a lift. The scale reads the normal force it supplies. Free-body diagram: weight 60 times 9,8, which is 588 newtons, downward; normal force N upward. Upward is positive.

Case one, lift stationary or at constant velocity: acceleration zero, so N minus 588 equals zero and N equals 588 newtons. The scale tells the truth.

Case two, lift accelerating upward at 1,5 metres per second squared. Formula: N minus m g equals m a. Substitution: N minus 588 equals 60 times 1,5. Answer: N equals 588 plus 90, which is 678 newtons. The scale reads high; the learner feels pressed into the floor; the true weight has not changed by a single newton.

Case three, lift accelerating downward at 1,5 metres per second squared. Upward still positive, so the acceleration enters as negative: N minus 588 equals 60 times negative 1,5, giving N equals 588 minus 90, which is 498 newtons. The scale reads light.

Case four, the cable snaps and the lift falls freely at 9,8 metres per second squared downward. N equals 588 minus 588, which is zero. The scale reads nothing at all. That is apparent weightlessness — the same sensation as an astronaut in orbit — and notice it is the supporting force that vanished, not gravity.

The same bookkeeping launches rockets. A 8 000 kilogram rocket produces 100 000 newtons of thrust. Weight: 8 000 times 9,8, which is 78 400 newtons. Formula: F net equals thrust minus weight. Substitution: 100 000 minus 78 400. Answer: 21 600 newtons upward, so a equals 21 600 divided by 8 000, which is 2,7 metres per second squared upward.

The trap running through all of it is mass against weight: the mass is 60 kilograms, the weight is 588 newtons, and the sentence "the weight is 60 kilograms" loses its mark every single time it is written.

This section's questions are in front of you now: sign convention declared, apparent weight separated from true weight, correct unit on every line.

## Subtopic: Newton's Third Law and Action-Reaction Pairs

The statement: when object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts a force on object A, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

A genuine action-reaction pair has four properties, and questions probe all four. Equal magnitudes. Opposite directions. Acting on DIFFERENT objects — this is the decisive one. And the same type of force: two contact forces, or two gravitational forces, never a mixture.

Collect the honest examples. A rower's oar pushes water backward; the water pushes the oar forward, and the boat goes. A netball player leaps by pushing the court floor downward; the floor pushes her upward. The rocket from the previous subtopic hurls exhaust gas down; the gas shoves the rocket up. You walk by pushing the ground backward, and the ground returns the favour forward.

Now the misconception that separates the top answers from the rest. A dictionary lies on a desk. Its weight, 18 newtons, acts downward; the desk's normal force, 18 newtons, acts upward. Equal and opposite — and NOT an action-reaction pair, because both forces act on the same object, the dictionary, and they are different types of force besides. The genuine pairs are these: the Earth pulls the dictionary down with 18 newtons, and the dictionary pulls the EARTH up with 18 newtons; the desk pushes the dictionary up with 18 newtons, and the dictionary pushes the DESK down with 18 newtons. Two pairs, four forces, each pair split across two bodies.

This also settles the old puzzle of the donkey and the cart: if the cart pulls back on the donkey exactly as hard as the donkey pulls the cart, how does anything move? Because the two forces land on different objects and can never meet in one net-force equation. The cart accelerates according to the forces ON THE CART — the donkey's pull forward against friction backward — and what the cart does to the donkey belongs in the donkey's ledger, not the cart's.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the law stated exactly, true pairs identified by all four properties, and a full-sentence explanation of why weight and normal force on a resting object never qualify.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three laws lived rather than stated, starting inside a taxi on the way to school.

## Subtopic: Why You Jerk Forward When the Taxi Brakes

You are in the taxi, phone in hand, cruising. The driver sees a speed bump late and stamps the brake. Your phone tries to leave your hand and your body lunges at the seat in front. What pushed you?

Nothing did — and that is the whole answer. Your body was never pushed forward; your body simply CARRIED ON at the speed it already had. The taxi is the thing that changed: brakes gripped, tyres bit the road, taxi slowed. Your body missed the memo because no force delivered it, so the seat in front arrived to deliver it instead.

The stubbornness that keeps your body doing what it was doing has a name: inertia. Everything has some, and mass is the amount. Drag a full water bucket across the yard and then an empty one — same grip, same effort, and the full one resists starting and resists stopping. More mass, more stubbornness.

Read the seatbelt through this lens. It is not there for sitting still; it is there for the moment the taxi slows suddenly and your body refuses to. The belt grabs your body and slows it WITH the taxi, playing the role the brakes play for the wheels. The headrest is the same guardian facing backward: when a car taps you from behind, the seat launches your torso forward, and without a headrest your head lags behind for one ugly moment — that lag is whiplash.

And one habit to unlearn today: things do not need a push to keep moving. They need a push to CHANGE — to start, stop, speed up, slow down or swerve. A ball rolling across smooth tiles keeps rolling not because anything drives it, but because almost nothing resists it.

Your questions on this section are with you now — think taxi, think stubbornness, and use the word inertia like you own it.

## Subtopic: Harder Push, Heavier Load

The second law is two pieces of common sense wearing a formula.

Sense one: push a thing harder and it gains speed faster. Sense two: load the thing heavier and the same push gains speed slower. Gaining speed is acceleration, and the law simply says acceleration rises with the push and falls with the mass.

But be careful which push. Not your push — the LEFTOVER push, once everything opposing you has been paid off. The workshop crate: the rope pulls with 120 newtons, the rough floor claws back 45. The leftover is 120 minus 45, which is 75 newtons. Physics calls that the net force, and it is the only number the crate actually responds to.

Now share the leftover across the mass. The crate is 25 kilograms, so each kilogram receives 75 divided by 25, which is 3. The crate gains 3 metres per second of speed every second — an acceleration of 3 metres per second squared. Compressed into symbols, that sharing-out is F net equals m a.

Hidden inside the same idea is the most useful shortcut in the topic. If something sits still, or cruises at a steady speed in a straight line, it is not accelerating, so its leftover push is ZERO — every force is cancelled by another. The instant a question says "at rest" or "constant velocity", write down that the forces balance and start pairing them off. Half the marks in this topic are collected exactly that way.

Two small disciplines keep the method honest: never mix sideways forces with up-down forces in one sum, and pick your positive direction before you write anything, so the minus signs know where they stand.

Questions on this section are coming to you now — find the leftover first, then share it out.

## Subtopic: The Lift Floor and the Wall That Pushes Back

Two final pictures close the topic.

First, the lift. Stand on a bathroom scale in a shopping-centre lift. The scale never measures YOU; it measures how hard it must push up on your feet. Standing still, that push equals your full weight — 588 newtons for a 60 kilogram person. The lift pulls away upward and the floor must push extra to get you moving: the needle climbs to 678. Heavy feeling. The lift sets off downward and the floor relaxes: 498. Light feeling. And if the cable ever failed, floor and person would fall together, the push would drop to zero, and the scale would read nothing — the floating feeling astronauts live with, which is not gravity switched off but support switched off. Through every case your true weight never budged.

Second, the wall. Shove a wall with all your strength; the wall shoves you back exactly as hard — that is why you can push yourself away from it. Everything works like this. You push the ground backward when you walk, and the ground pushes you forward. The rower shoves water one way and glides the other.

Here is the knot everyone tangles: if every push is answered by an equal push back, why isn't everything permanently stuck? Because the two pushes land on DIFFERENT bodies. The donkey pulls the cart forward; the cart pulls the donkey backward — but the cart only answers to forces acting on the cart, and among those the donkey's pull stands unopposed by its twin, which is busy acting on the donkey. The pair never shares one equation, so the pair never cancels.

Same trap in disguise: a dictionary on a desk has weight down and the desk's push up, equal and opposite — but both act on the dictionary, so they are partners in balance, not an action-reaction pair. The true partner of the dictionary's weight is the dictionary pulling the entire Earth upward. Strange, correct, and worth a mark.

Three laws, three pictures: things carry on unless something intervenes, leftover push shared over mass gives acceleration, and every push is returned — onto someone else. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — take them one picture at a time.
