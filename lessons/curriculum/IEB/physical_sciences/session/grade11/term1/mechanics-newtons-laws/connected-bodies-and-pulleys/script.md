# Part 1 — Expert

One body at a time is how Newton's second law is stated; two bodies joined by a string is how it is examined. A trailer behind a car, two crates roped together on a floor, a bucket hanging over a pulley — every version asks for the same two numbers: the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string. This session builds the two-step method that delivers both, then drives it through the three standard arrangements: two bodies pulled along a surface, a body on a table tied over a pulley to a hanging mass, and the incline-plus-pulley combination.

## Subtopic: One String, One Acceleration — the Rules of the System

Three idealising assumptions run this whole topic, and each one buys you a physical fact worth marks.

The string is INEXTENSIBLE — it cannot stretch. Consequence: the connected bodies move as one, so their accelerations share the SAME MAGNITUDE. If the front body gains speed at 2 metres per second squared, so does the back one, instantly. Say "same magnitude" rather than "same direction", because around a pulley one body may accelerate vertically while its partner accelerates horizontally.

The string is LIGHT — its mass is negligible. Consequence: the tension is the SAME at both ends. A heavy rope would need net force to accelerate its own mass, and the tension would then vary along its length; a massless string transmits the pull undiminished.

The pulley is FRICTIONLESS and light. Consequence: it changes only the DIRECTION of the tension, never its magnitude. The pull dragging a block toward the table edge equals the pull holding the hanging mass, to the newton.

One more fact prevents the commonest drawing error: a string can only PULL. It cannot push. On every body, draw the tension arrow along the string, pointing away from that body toward the pulley or partner.

Finally, sort the forces by citizenship. Tension acts between members of the two-body system, so it is an INTERNAL force. Applied forces, friction and the weights arrive from outside — EXTERNAL. That distinction is not vocabulary for its own sake; it is the engine of the method in the next subtopic.

Take the questions for this section now, making sure you can justify each assumption, not merely recite it.

## Subtopic: The Two-Step Method — Whole System, Then One Body

Step one: fuse the bodies into ONE object with their combined mass. The tension pulls forward on one body and backward on the other with equal magnitude, so in the combined bookkeeping the two tensions cancel and disappear. What survives is F net external equals total mass times acceleration — an equation containing no tension at all. Solve it for a. That is precisely why the system step comes first: it is the only equation available in which the unknown tension is absent.

Step two: pick ONE body — by preference the one carrying the fewest forces — and apply F net equals m a to it alone, using the a you have just found. Now the tension is the only unknown and surrenders in one line.

Run the cleanest possible case. Two trolleys on a smooth, effectively frictionless floor: trolley A of 6 kilograms in front, trolley B of 4 kilograms behind, joined by a light cord. A horizontal force of 40 newtons pulls trolley A forward.

Step one, the system. Formula: F net equals total mass times a. Substitution: 40 equals 10 times a. Answer: a equals 4 metres per second squared, forward.

Step two, trolley B alone. The cord's tension is the only horizontal force on B. Formula: T equals m B times a. Substitution: 4 times 4. Answer: T equals 16 newtons.

Audit with the other body — the professional's habit: on trolley A, 40 minus T equals 6 times 4, so 40 minus 16 gives 24, and 6 times 4 is indeed 24. The ledger balances.

Two disciplines. The tension NEVER enters the system equation — starting with "40 plus T equals 10 a" is the classic wrong opening. And draw a separate free-body diagram for each body before writing anything, because method marks live there.

The questions on this section are with you now: whole system first, one body second, tension banished from the first equation.

## Subtopic: Two Crates on a Rough Floor

Introduce friction and the method stands unchanged; only the bookkeeping thickens.

The same pair — 6 kilograms leading, 4 kilograms trailing, light cord, 40 newton pull — now crosses a rough floor with a coefficient of kinetic friction of 0,15. Each body suffers friction, and each body's friction is built on ITS OWN normal force.

Step one, the system. Combined weight: 10 times 9,8, which is 98 newtons, so the combined normal force is 98 newtons. Total friction: formula f k equals mu k times N, substitution 0,15 times 98, answer 14,7 newtons backward. Formula: F net equals F applied minus f total. Substitution: 40 minus 14,7. Answer: 25,3 newtons. Then a equals 25,3 divided by 10, which is 2,53 metres per second squared forward.

Step two, the trailing 4 kilogram crate, which carries only two horizontal forces. Its own normal force: 4 times 9,8, which is 39,2 newtons. Its own friction: 0,15 times 39,2, which is 5,88 newtons backward. Formula: T minus f B equals m B times a. Substitution: T minus 5,88 equals 4 times 2,53, which is 10,12. Answer: T equals 16,0 newtons.

Audit on the leader: its friction is 0,15 times 58,8, which is 8,82 newtons; then 40 minus 16 minus 8,82 gives 15,18 newtons, and 6 times 2,53 is 15,18. Balanced.

The expensive, subtle trap: dropping the TOTAL friction into a single-body equation. The trailing crate feels only its own 5,88 newtons, never the pair's 14,7. Every number inside a one-body equation must belong to that body and no other.

Work this section's questions now, keeping each crate's friction glued to that crate.

## Subtopic: Over the Pulley — Table Edge and Incline

Change the furniture; keep the method.

A 3 kilogram block rests on a rough bench, coefficient of kinetic friction 0,2. A light string runs from the block, horizontally over a frictionless pulley at the bench's edge, down to a 5 kilogram mass hanging free. The system is released: the mass descends, the block slides.

Free-body diagrams first. On the block: weight 29,4 newtons down, normal force 29,4 newtons up, tension toward the pulley, friction away from it. On the hanging mass: weight 49 newtons down, tension up.

Step one, the system, taking the direction of motion as positive throughout. The driver is the hanging weight: 5 times 9,8, which is 49 newtons. The resister is friction on the block: 0,2 times 29,4, which is 5,88 newtons. The block's weight and normal force sit perpendicular to its motion and cancel, so they stay out. Formula: F net equals total mass times a. Substitution: 49 minus 5,88 equals 8 times a, so 43,12 equals 8 a. Answer: a equals 5,39 metres per second squared.

Step two, the hanging mass alone, downward positive. Formula: m g minus T equals m a. Substitution: 49 minus T equals 5 times 5,39, which is 26,95. Answer: T equals 22,05 newtons.

Stare at that answer, because it carries the most-tested idea in the topic. The tension is 22,05 newtons while the hanging weight is 49 newtons: tension is LESS than the hanging weight, and it must be — a mass held by its full weight would hang in balance, not accelerate downward. Tension equals the hanging weight only when the acceleration is zero.

Now the incline version. A 5 kilogram block sits on a frictionless slope of 30 degrees; its string runs up the slope, over a pulley at the crest, down to an 8 kilogram hanging mass.

Step one. Driver: 8 times 9,8, which is 78,4 newtons. Resister: the block's weight component along the slope, m g sine theta, which is 5 times 9,8 times 0,5, giving 24,5 newtons. Formula: F net equals total mass times a. Substitution: 78,4 minus 24,5 equals 13 a, so 53,9 equals 13 a. Answer: a equals 4,15 metres per second squared.

Step two, the hanging mass: 78,4 minus T equals 8 times 4,15, which is 33,2. Answer: T equals 45,2 newtons.

Three named traps. One: setting the tension equal to the hanging weight — almost never true. Two: entering the block's FULL weight into the system equation on an incline, instead of only m g sine theta. Three: sign chaos, cured by declaring "the direction the system actually moves" as positive for every member, so the falling mass going down and the block going along the surface both count positive.

The final questions of this part are with you now: system equation, one-body equation, and a sign convention that follows the motion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with the fear removed, starting behind two supermarket trolleys.

## Subtopic: Two Trolleys Tied Together

Picture two trolleys nose to tail in a supermarket aisle, joined by a short cord. You push the front one; the back one tags along.

Three facts, and they are the entire subtopic. First: they move together. Whatever speed the front trolley gains, the back one gains in the same breath, because the cord cannot stretch. One acceleration for the pair — find it once, use it twice.

Second: the cord pulls equally at both ends. Being light, the cord needs no force for itself; it passes your pull through undiluted. The tug it gives the back trolley equals the tug it gives the front one, backwards.

Third — the fact that rescues every diagram: a cord can only PULL. Never push. On each trolley, the cord's arrow points along the cord, away from that trolley, toward its partner.

Now the clever observation hiding in plain sight. The cord drags the back trolley forward and holds the front trolley back, with the same strength. Zoom out and treat the two trolleys as one long vehicle, and those two tugs face each other and cancel completely. They are INSIDE the system, so the system as a whole never feels them. Only your push and the floor's friction — the outside forces — move the pair.

That cancellation is not a shortcut trick; it is the entire reason the method in the next section works, so say it once in your own words: the cord only matters when you look at one trolley at a time.

Small win in the bag. Questions on this section are with you now — three facts, and the reason the cord vanishes when you zoom out.

## Subtopic: Weigh the Whole Train First

The recipe has exactly two moves.

Move one: pretend the connected objects are one object. Add the masses. Count only the OUTSIDE forces — pushes, friction, hanging weights — and ignore the cord entirely, because its two tugs cancel. Leftover outside force divided by total mass gives the acceleration. One number, now known for every member.

Move two: return to ONE object, choosing the laziest — the one with the fewest forces touching it. List its forces, cord included this time, set them equal to its own mass times the known acceleration, and the cord's tension is the only unknown left standing.

Do the trolleys on a floor with a little grip. Six kilograms leading, four trailing, pulled with 40 newtons, grip factor 0,15.

Move one: together 10 kilograms, pressing down 98 newtons, so friction drags back 0,15 times 98, which is 14,7 newtons. Leftover: 40 minus 14,7, which is 25,3 newtons. Acceleration: 25,3 over 10 — 2,53 metres per second squared.

Move two: the trailing trolley alone. Its own press is 39,2 newtons, its own friction 0,15 times 39,2, which is 5,88 newtons. The cord tugs it forward with T. So T minus 5,88 equals 4 times 2,53, which is 10,12 — and T is 16 newtons.

The pothole to steer around: when zoomed in on one trolley, use THAT trolley's friction, never the pair's total. Its own numbers, always.

Your questions for this section are coming now — whole train first, one wagon second.

## Subtopic: The Bucket Down the Well

The final picture is the one your exams adore: something on a surface, a string over a wheel at the edge, something hanging.

A 3 kilogram block on a bench, a 5 kilogram mass dangling past the edge, a touch of friction on the bench. Who drives? The dangler, hauling down with 5 times 9,8, which is 49 newtons. Who resists? Friction under the block: 0,2 times its 29,4 newton press, which is 5,88 newtons. Nothing else counts — the block's weight pushes into the bench, the bench answers, and that little duel cancels itself.

Move one: leftover is 49 minus 5,88, which is 43,12 newtons, shared across 8 kilograms of total stuff — 5,39 metres per second squared.

Move two: zoom in on the dangler. Gravity pulls it down with 49; the string holds up with T; it accelerates down at 5,39. So 49 minus T equals 5 times 5,39, which is 26,95 — and T is 22,05 newtons.

Sit with that number, because it is the punchline of the whole lesson. The string carries 22,05 newtons while the dangling mass weighs 49. The string holds LESS than the full weight — necessarily, because if it held the full weight the mass would be in balance and would not fall at all. A string only carries the full weight when nothing accelerates.

Same story tilted: a 5 kilogram block on a smooth 30 degree slope, tied over a pulley to an 8 kilogram dangler. The dangler drives with 78,4 newtons; the slope resists with only the block's downhill share, 5 times 9,8 times a half, which is 24,5 newtons. Leftover 53,9 newtons across 13 kilograms gives 4,15 metres per second squared, and the string settles at 45,2 newtons.

Two moves, one shared acceleration, a string that pulls and never pushes, and a tension that always undercuts the hanging weight while anything accelerates. The last questions of the lesson are yours now — start every single one by weighing the whole train.
