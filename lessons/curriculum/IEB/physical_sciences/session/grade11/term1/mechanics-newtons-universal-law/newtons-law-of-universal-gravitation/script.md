# Part 1 — Expert

A single equation covers the falling of a mango, the orbit of the Moon and the reading on a bathroom scale carried to another planet. This session states Newton's law of universal gravitation in wording that earns full definition marks, works the substitution problems that always accompany it, derives the surface gravity of any planet from two numbers on a data table, and finally cuts the ancient knot between mass and weight. Keep a calculator with an exponent key at hand — every quantity in this topic travels with a power of ten.

## Subtopic: The Law of Universal Gravitation and Its Equation

The statement, in full: every particle in the universe attracts every other particle with a force that is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centres. In symbols, F equals G m one m two divided by d squared. The gravitational constant G is 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11 newton metre squared per kilogram squared — printed on your data sheet, so read it rather than trust memory.

Three properties of the law carry theory marks. First, the force is exclusively attractive; gravity has no repulsive mode. Second, the two bodies pull on each other with equal magnitudes in opposite directions — a flawless Newton's third law pair even when one body is a planet and the other a person. You pull the Earth upward exactly as hard as it pulls you down. Third, the distance d runs between the CENTRES of the two bodies, never between their surfaces, and for planets that distinction is enormous.

Feel the scale of the constant. Two learners of 50 kilograms and 70 kilograms sit 2 metres apart in a classroom. Formula: F equals G m one m two divided by d squared. Substitution: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 50, multiplied by 70, divided by 2 squared. Answer: about 5,8 times 10 to the minus 8 newtons — a twentieth of a millionth of a newton, hopelessly below anything a human can feel. That is why gravity between ordinary objects goes unnoticed. Swap one learner for the Earth — mass 5,98 times 10 to the 24 kilograms, centre-to-centre distance one Earth radius, 6,38 times 10 to the 6 metres — and the force on a 60 kilogram person computes to 588 newtons. Which is exactly m g. No coincidence: weight IS this force wearing its everyday name.

## Subtopic: Inverse-Square Reasoning and Ratio Questions

A favourite question style changes a mass or a distance and asks for the new force with no numbers at all. These ratio questions are fast marks once you see their skeleton.

Force is proportional to each mass: double one mass, double the force; double both, and the force multiplies by two times two, which is four. Force is inversely proportional to the SQUARE of the distance: double the distance and the force divides by four; triple it, divides by nine; halve it, multiplies by four.

Work a combined case. The force between two spheres is F. One mass is quadrupled and the distance is doubled. From the mass: times four. From the distance: divide by two squared, which is four. New force: four divided by four — exactly F again, unchanged. Present the reasoning in that shape — mass factor, distance factor, multiply — and the marks follow the structure.

Now with a number. The gravitational force between two objects is 8 times 10 to the minus 7 newtons. The distance between their centres is halved. Halving the distance multiplies the force by two squared, which is four: the new force is 3,2 times 10 to the minus 6 newtons.

The trap has one name: the forgotten square. A learner who halves the force when the distance doubles has treated gravity as inversely proportional to plain distance, and every mark in the question dies with that line. Chant it while working: distance changes always get squared.

This section's questions are with you now — most need no calculator, only the two proportionalities handled in the correct order.

## Subtopic: Gravitational Acceleration from g Equals G M Over r Squared

Set the universal law beside the definition of weight and something elegant falls out. The gravitational force on a mass m at the surface of a planet of mass M and radius r is G M m over r squared. But that same force is the object's weight, m g. Equate them: m g equals G M m over r squared. The object's mass m cancels from both sides, leaving g equals G M divided by r squared.

The cancellation IS the physics. Because m vanishes, every object at a given location falls with the same acceleration — a hammer and a feather dropped together on the airless Moon strike the ground together. Massive objects are pulled harder, but they resist acceleration in exactly the same proportion, and the two effects annihilate.

Compute it for Earth. Formula: g equals G M over r squared. Substitution: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 5,98 times 10 to the 24, divided by the square of 6,38 times 10 to the 6. Answer: 9,8 metres per second squared — the number you have carried since last year, now derived from first principles instead of handed down.

Now Venus, mass 4,87 times 10 to the 24 kilograms, radius 6,05 times 10 to the 6 metres. Substitution: 6,67 times 10 to the minus 11, multiplied by 4,87 times 10 to the 24, divided by the square of 6,05 times 10 to the 6. Answer: 8,87 metres per second squared — close to Earth's, because Venus is nearly Earth's twin in bulk.

Altitude matters, because r is measured from the CENTRE. A satellite parked two Earth radii ABOVE the surface sits three radii from the centre, so g there is 9,8 divided by three squared, which is 1,09 metres per second squared. And at ordinary orbital height, just a few hundred kilometres up, g is still nearly 9 metres per second squared — file that fact for the weightlessness discussion ahead.

Work this section's questions now, and write out the cancellation argument in sentences at least once.

## Subtopic: Weight on Other Planets and True Weightlessness

Mass is the amount of matter in a body, measured in kilograms, and it is identical everywhere in the universe. Weight is the gravitational force acting on that matter, w equals m g, measured in newtons, and it changes whenever g changes. Test questions return to this distinction relentlessly.

Follow a 60 kilogram astronaut. On Earth: weight equals 60 times 9,8, which is 588 newtons. On the Moon, where g is 1,62 metres per second squared: 60 times 1,62, which is 97,2 newtons. On Venus, using the 8,87 we derived: 60 times 8,87, which is about 532 newtons. Three planets, three weights, one unchanging mass of 60 kilograms.

Now dispose of the greatest myth in the syllabus: that astronauts float because there is no gravity in space. At orbital height gravity is nearly as strong as at your desk. The station and everyone aboard are in permanent FREE FALL around the Earth — falling toward the planet while moving sideways so fast that the surface keeps curving away beneath them. In free fall no floor presses on your feet, no chair on your back; the sensation we call weight is exactly that supporting push, and in orbit it is absent. Weightlessness is therefore the experience of gravity WITHOUT a support force, not the absence of gravity. A snapped lift cable or the top of a trampoline bounce delivers the identical sensation for a shorter time.

The sentence to own for the exams: mass is constant everywhere; weight equals m g and varies with location; weightlessness is free fall, not zero gravity.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same law with the exponents put away, told through desks, torchlight and a bathroom scale that refuses to tell the same story twice.

## Subtopic: Everything Pulls on Everything

Here is a true sentence that sounds invented: your desk is pulling you toward it right now, and you are pulling it back. You are also pulling on your school building, on the ocean, on the Moon — and every one of them pulls on you. Anything with mass tugs on everything else with mass. That is what the word universal is doing in the law's name.

Why does nobody feel a desk's pull? Because gravity is astoundingly feeble unless one of the partners is planet-sized. Two learners sitting two metres apart attract each other with roughly a twentieth of a millionth of a newton — a force that would lose an arm-wrestle with a falling dust grain. Gravity only becomes noticeable when a partner brings kilograms by the trillion trillion, which is what the Earth does.

And take the strange part seriously, because it is exam gold: the Earth pulls you down with 588 newtons, and you pull the Earth UP with 588 newtons. Same size, opposite directions, landing on different bodies — a perfect matched pair. The Earth ignores your pull only because 588 newtons spread over its colossal mass produces an acceleration too tiny to detect, while the same 588 newtons on your 60 kilograms is very persuasive indeed.

Two dials control the strength of any gravitational handshake: how much mass, and how far apart. More mass, stronger pull. More distance, weaker pull — and the next section reveals exactly how much weaker.

Your questions on this section are with you now — picture a quiet thread of attraction between every pair of objects in the room.

## Subtopic: Double the Distance, Quarter the Pull

Gravity fades with distance in one very specific way, and the pattern earns marks year after year.

Shine a torch at a wall. Step back to double the distance and the same beam paints four times the area, so each patch of wall receives a quarter of the light. Gravity spreads exactly like torchlight. Double the separation between two objects and the pull falls to a quarter. Triple it — a ninth. The rule in one line: whatever number you multiply the distance by, you divide the force by that number SQUARED.

Masses are gentler: no squaring. Double one mass, double the pull. Double both, four times the pull. The square belongs to distance alone.

Play the ratio game the way the questions do. The pull between two objects is F. Quadruple one mass and double the distance. Mass factor: times four. Distance factor: divide by two squared, four. Result: F, exactly as before — the two changes cancelled. No calculator, just the two rules applied in order.

One warning, because this is where the marks bleed: when the distance doubles, the force does NOT halve. It quarters. If a single sentence survives from this section, let it be that one.

The questions for this section are coming to you now — solve them with the two rules, never with the full formula.

## Subtopic: Same You, Different Weight

Step on the bathroom scale at home: it reads, say, 60 kilograms. Airlift that same scale to the Moon and step on again: it reads about 10. Did two thirds of you stay behind on Earth? Not a gram of you.

Two words are being confused, and separating them finishes the topic. MASS is how much of you exists — the count of matter, in kilograms, identical in your bedroom, on the Moon, and drifting past Jupiter. WEIGHT is how hard gravity pulls on that matter — a force, in newtons, entirely dependent on where you stand. On Earth every kilogram is pulled with about 9,8 newtons. On the Moon, only 1,62 newtons per kilogram, because the Moon is a far smaller ball of rock. Same you, weaker pull, smaller weight — and a scale is only a pull-meter wearing a mass costume.

What sets a planet's pull? Two competing quantities: how much mass the planet owns, and how far its surface stands from its centre. The Moon loses on mass so badly that even its smaller radius cannot save it: about a sixth of Earth's pull. Venus, nearly Earth's twin in both quantities, lands at about 8,9 newtons per kilogram — visit Venus and your weight barely changes. The comparison is always a fraction fight: mass on top, radius squared underneath.

Last picture: the floating astronaut. The famous claim — no gravity in space — is simply false; a few hundred kilometres up, gravity keeps nearly all its strength. Astronauts float because they are perpetually FALLING around the planet, with their floor falling beside them. Nothing pushes up on their feet, and that missing push is the whole sensation of weightlessness. You have sampled it yourself, for half a second, at the top of a trampoline bounce when nothing touched your soles.

The last questions of the lesson are yours now — kilograms for what you are, newtons for how hard you are pulled.
