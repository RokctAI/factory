Hey, it's Bianca — I know those numbers look terrifying. Ten to the twenty-four, ten to the minus eleven — it feels like the calculator needs a passport. Breathe. This topic is one formula, two rules, and one pair of words, and you already understand the ideas behind all of them.

The one formula: multiply the two masses, divide by the distance squared, scale by the constant on your data sheet. That is the whole law. You never memorise G — it is printed for you. Your only real job is keeping the exponent key honest and remembering the distance runs centre to centre.

The two rules cover every ratio question, and ratio questions are free marks. Rule one: masses are simple — double a mass, double the pull. Rule two: distance is squared — double the distance, QUARTER the pull; triple it, a ninth. When a question changes both, handle the mass factor, then the distance factor, then multiply. No calculator, two lines, done. The only way to lose is forgetting the square, so whisper it every time: distance always gets squared.

The pair of words: mass and weight. Mass is how much of you there is — kilograms, the same on Earth, the Moon, anywhere. Weight is how hard gravity pulls on those kilograms — newtons, and it changes from planet to planet. Sixty kilograms is 588 newtons at home, about 97 on the Moon, about 532 on Venus. Same you every time. If a question asks for weight and your answer is in kilograms, stop and multiply by g.

And enjoy the fact that makes this topic actually cool: astronauts float not because gravity quit, but because they are falling around the planet with their floor falling beside them. Nothing pushes up on their feet — that missing push IS weightlessness. You have felt it at the top of a trampoline bounce.

One formula, two rules, two words. The practice questions are waiting, and every single one of them is built from those pieces. You've got this!
