# Part 1 — Expert

A transverse wave shakes its medium across the direction of travel. This session meets the second family — waves that shake the medium back and forth ALONG the direction of travel — and then follows that family straight to its most important member: sound. We define compressions and rarefactions, establish why sound is longitudinal and why a vacuum kills it, let the medium set the speed, sort out what pitch, loudness and quality each measure, and finish with echo and sonar calculations set out so the there-and-back factor can never slip away.

## Subtopic: Compressions, Rarefactions and the Longitudinal Wave

A LONGITUDINAL WAVE is one in which the particles of the medium vibrate parallel to — along the same line as — the direction in which the wave travels. Hold it against the transverse definition, where the vibration is perpendicular to the travel. That one contrast, parallel versus perpendicular, is the entire distinction between the two families, and questions ask for it in exactly those words.

Stretch a slinky along a bench and give the free end one sharp shove forward — along the spring, not across it. A knot of bunched coils sprints away down the spring. In that bunched region the coils are crowded together, so the pressure and the density there are HIGHER than normal: that region is a COMPRESSION. Directly behind it the coils have been dragged further apart than usual, so pressure and density are LOWER than normal: that stretched region is a RAREFACTION. Shove and pull in a steady rhythm and the spring carries an alternating parade — compression, rarefaction, compression, rarefaction — away from your hand.

Every measuring tool from the transverse work survives the change of picture; only the geometry is redrawn. The WAVELENGTH is the distance between two successive compressions — or two successive rarefactions — which is once again the distance between successive points in phase, in metres. The AMPLITUDE is still the maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position, in metres; drive the coils harder and the compressions pack tighter. The PERIOD is the time for one complete wave to pass a fixed point, in seconds; the FREQUENCY is the number of complete waves passing per second, in hertz.

The two equations survive untouched as well: f = 1/T, frequency is one over the period; and v = fλ — v equals f lambda, speed equals frequency times wavelength. Changing the direction of the wiggle changes none of the mathematics.

One extra fact to bank: longitudinal waves travel through solids, liquids and gases alike, because every state of matter pushes back when it is squeezed. Take the questions for this section now, holding the definitions of compression and rarefaction, and the parallel-versus-perpendicular contrast, word for word.

## Subtopic: Sound as a Longitudinal Wave, and the Speed of the Medium

Sound is a longitudinal wave produced by a VIBRATING OBJECT. A violin string, a loudspeaker cone, a siren's diaphragm, your own vocal cords — each surges forward, crowding the air molecules ahead of it into a compression, then swings back, leaving a rarefaction behind. Those alternating pressure regions spread outward until they reach an eardrum and set it vibrating in step. Sound is a travelling pattern of pressure, not a wind: each molecule nudges its neighbours and returns to where it started.

The first consequence is non-negotiable: sound REQUIRES A MEDIUM. Seal a ringing electric bell inside a glass jar and pump the air out. The ringing thins, fades, and in a good vacuum disappears entirely — while the hammer visibly keeps striking. No particles means no compressions, and no compressions means no sound. Space is perfectly silent, and every film that gives a spaceship a roaring engine is doing fiction, not physics.

The second consequence: the speed of sound belongs to THE MEDIUM, never to the source. Whisper or bellow, squeak or growl — the sound leaves at the same speed. What changes the speed is the material the sound crosses, and the ranking never varies: FASTEST IN SOLIDS, slower in liquids, slowest in gases, because the closer and more tightly bound the particles, the sooner each one hands the disturbance to the next. Working values: about 340 m·s⁻¹ in air at ordinary temperatures, roughly 1 500 m·s⁻¹ in water, and around 5 000 m·s⁻¹ in steel. Within a gas, warming it raises the speed, since faster-moving molecules collide sooner and pass the message quicker.

Two numbers to memorise. The AUDIBLE RANGE of a healthy human ear runs from about 20 Hz to about 20 000 Hz — below it lies infrasound, above it ultrasound. And unless a question supplies its own value, the speed of sound in air is taken as 340 m·s⁻¹.

The questions for this section are with you now: justify why sound cannot cross a vacuum, and rank solid, liquid and gas by sound speed with the particle-spacing reason attached.

## Subtopic: Pitch, Loudness and Quality

Your ear distinguishes three separate things about a sound, and each one is controlled by a different property of the wave. Keep the three pairings apart and this whole section is banked credit.

PITCH is how high or low a note sounds, and it is set by FREQUENCY alone. Many vibrations per second: high pitch. Few: low pitch. A short, thin, tightly-wound string vibrates quickly and sings high; a long, thick, slack string vibrates slowly and rumbles low. How hard you pluck has no vote in the matter.

LOUDNESS is how strong the sound seems, and it is set by AMPLITUDE. A bigger amplitude means the source shoved the air harder, the compressions arrive more tightly packed, more energy lands on the eardrum, and the sound is louder. Strike the same piano key gently and then firmly: identical pitch, different loudness. Loudness has a second ingredient — the ear itself, which hears mid-range frequencies best, so equal amplitudes at very different frequencies are not judged equally loud.

QUALITY — also called tone or timbre — is what lets you pick your best friend's voice out of a corridor full of chatter, or tell a saxophone from a flute holding the same note. It is set by the WAVEFORM, the shape of the wave. No real source produces one clean frequency; each stacks fainter, higher frequencies on top of the main note, and that particular recipe gives every source its own waveform and its own fingerprint.

Now connect pitch to spacing with a calculation. An ambulance siren holds a note of 680 Hz. Taking the speed of sound in air as 340 m·s⁻¹, find the wavelength. Formula: v = fλ, so λ = v ÷ f. Substitute: λ = 340 ÷ 680. Answer: λ = 0,5 m. Push the pitch higher and the wavelength must shrink, because the air is the same air and its speed will not move.

Work through this section's questions now, matching pitch to frequency, loudness to amplitude and quality to waveform without a moment's doubt.

## Subtopic: Echoes, Ultrasound, and the Factor of Two

An ECHO is sound reflected off a hard surface and heard as a distinct, later sound. Every echo problem has the same skeleton — and the same trap buried in it.

The trap first: the sound travels to the reflector AND BACK AGAIN. The measured time covers double the distance you are asked for. So v = 2d ÷ Δt, which rearranges to d = (v × Δt) ÷ 2 — distance to the reflector equals speed times time, divided by two. Skip the two and every answer comes out exactly double; that single dropped factor is the most expensive habit in the topic.

Worked example. A climber facing a quarry wall in the Magaliesberg claps once and hears the echo 0,9 s later. Take the speed of sound in air as 340 m·s⁻¹ and find the distance to the wall. Formula: d = (v × Δt) ÷ 2. Substitute: d = (340 × 0,9) ÷ 2 = 306 ÷ 2. Answer: d = 153 m.

ULTRASOUND is sound whose frequency lies above the ceiling of human hearing — above about 20 000 Hz. It is ordinary sound obeying ordinary physics; our ears simply cannot register it. Bats and dolphins fire ultrasound pulses and clock the returning echoes to find moths and fish in total darkness — echolocation. Ships run the same trick electronically and call it SONAR.

Worked example. A survey boat on the Knysna lagoon pings a sonar pulse straight down and receives the echo 0,6 s later. The speed of sound in seawater is 1 500 m·s⁻¹. Find the depth. Formula: d = (v × Δt) ÷ 2. Substitute: d = (1 500 × 0,6) ÷ 2 = 900 ÷ 2. Answer: d = 450 m.

In medicine, different tissues bounce ultrasound back differently, and a scanner assembles those echoes into a live image — the scan of an unborn baby being the familiar example. Its great advantage over X-rays is that ultrasound is NOT ionising radiation, so it does not damage cells. The same high-frequency sound shatters kidney stones, cleans jewellery and precision instruments, and exposes invisible cracks inside welded joints.

Guard the units as jealously as the factor of two: 60 kHz means 60 000 Hz, never 60. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — halve every round trip, convert every kilohertz, and name one ultrasound application together with its reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same physics again, now living in a slinky on a bench, a queue at the taxi rank and one clap at a quarry wall — plain language first, symbols only where they help.

## Subtopic: The Slinky and the Taxi Queue

Friday afternoon at the rank, and the queue for the last taxi is long. Somebody at the back loses their balance and bumps the person ahead. That person lurches into the next, who nudges the next — and a little squash of people travels the whole length of the queue to the front. Watch carefully what happened: nobody walked up the line. Every person rocked forward, then stepped back into their own spot. Only the squash made the journey.

That travelling squash is a compression — a patch where people are packed closer than normal. And look just behind it: everyone there has rocked forward and left a stretched-out gap, a patch where the queue is thinner than normal. That gap is a rarefaction. Keep bumping the back of the queue in rhythm and the pattern marches forward endlessly: squash, gap, squash, gap.

Here is the sentence that defines the whole family. The people rocked FORWARD AND BACK — along the very same line the squash travelled. Compare the stadium wave from the transverse lesson: there, people bobbed up and down while the wave rolled sideways, across their movement. In the queue, the movement and the travel share one line. That is what longitudinal means. A slinky does it perfectly when you shove its end along the spring instead of flicking it sideways.

And the measuring kit is unchanged. Squash to the next squash: one wavelength. How far each person rocks from their spot: the amplitude. Squashes arriving at the front per second: the frequency. Time for one squash to pass: the period. Four old measurements, one new picture.

Quick check before we move on — some questions on the queue are with you now, especially about which way the people move compared with which way the squash goes.

## Subtopic: Why Sound Is a Queue of Air

Swap every person in that queue for an air molecule, and you are no longer imagining — you are hearing.

Something vibrates: a violin string, a speaker cone, a siren, your voice box. As it swings forward it squashes the air in front of it; as it swings back it leaves a thin, stretched patch. Forward, back, forward, back — and squashes and thin patches stream across the room until one squash lands on your eardrum and presses it inward. That press is what your brain calls sound.

Which is exactly why sound must have something to travel through — air, water, brick, bone, anything built of particles. No particles, nothing to squash. The ringing bell sealed in a jar goes quiet as the pump drags the air out, even though you can still see the hammer hammering. Space is dead silent; every roaring space-battle you have ever watched is theatre.

And the material decides the speed — never the shout. Go back to the queue. Shoulder-to-shoulder queue: the squash reaches the front almost instantly. Loose queue with two-metre gaps: the squash dawdles. Solids are the shoulder-to-shoulder queue, so sound rips through steel at about five thousand metres each second. Water queues a little looser: about one and a half thousand. Air is the loosest queue of all: three hundred and forty metres each second. The order never changes — solid first, liquid second, gas last.

You have seen the proof on a stormy evening. The flash and the boom are born together, but light arrives virtually instantly while the boom plods over at 340 metres every second — which is exactly why the gap between flash and thunder tells you how far off the storm sits.

Your questions on this part are up now: what sound actually is, why a vacuum stays silent, and which state of matter carries sound fastest, with the reason.

## Subtopic: High, Loud, and the Round Trip

Your ear runs three separate dials, and each dial reads a different part of the wave.

The HIGH-LOW dial is frequency: how many squashes arrive each second. Many per second and the note sits high; few per second and it sits low. That is pitch, and nothing but frequency moves it.

The LOUD-SOFT dial is amplitude: how hard the source shoved the air. Hard shove, dense squashes, more energy delivered to your ear, bigger sound. Strike the same drum softly, then hard — same note, more of it. So hold the pair firmly: pitch is how OFTEN, loudness is how HARD. Swapping those two is the classic slip in this topic, and now it cannot catch you.

The third dial is the one that recognises people. Your coach shouting across a field, your neighbour's radio through a wall — you know them instantly, even at the same pitch and volume, because no real sound is one clean note. Every source piles faint extra tones on top of its main note, and the mixture gives the wave a shape all its own. That shape is the sound's quality.

Now the sums — and there is only one thing to remember: sound goes there AND comes back. Clap at the quarry wall and catch the echo 0,9 seconds later. In that time sound covered 340 times 0,9 — 306 metres. But that is the full round trip, so the wall stands at half of it: 153 metres. Same move on the water at Knysna: the sonar ping returns after 0,6 seconds at 1 500 metres a second, so the journey totals 900 metres — and the bottom lies 450 metres down. Halve before you write, every single time.

Bats run this exact calculation in the dark using squeaks pitched too high for human ears — ultrasound, anything above about 20 000 hertz — and hospitals use the same gentle echoes to watch a baby grow before it is born, safely, because ultrasound is not the cell-damaging kind of radiation that X-rays are.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: pitch on the frequency dial, loudness on the amplitude dial, and every echo distance halved before it goes on paper. Nail the halving and this topic pays out in full.
