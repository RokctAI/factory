# Part 1 — Expert

All of electricity grows out of one property of matter: charge. This session pins that property down — what charge is, the two laws it never breaks, conservation and quantisation, the three routes by which an object becomes charged — and then upgrades "like charges repel" from a slogan into a force measured in newtons. The calculations live in prefixes and powers of ten, and nothing in this topic punishes carelessness like a missed conversion or an unsquared distance.

## Subtopic: Charge, Its Unit, and Quantisation

Electric charge comes in exactly TWO kinds, labelled positive and negative. Protons, locked in the nucleus, carry positive charge; electrons carry negative charge; neutrons carry none. An object holding equal numbers of protons and electrons is NEUTRAL — not empty of charge, but perfectly balanced.

The SI unit of charge is the COULOMB, symbol C. One coulomb is a gigantic quantity by laboratory standards, which is why this topic speaks mostly in microcoulombs and nanocoulombs: one microcoulomb, μC, is 10⁻⁶ C; one nanocoulomb, nC, is 10⁻⁹ C.

Charge is QUANTISED: it exists only in whole-number multiples of one fundamental packet — the magnitude of the charge on a single electron, symbol e, value 1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. An object can hold the charge of fifty electrons or fifty billion, but never of half an electron. The working relationship is Q = nq, read aloud as "Q equals n q — the total charge equals the number of charged particles times the charge on one particle".

Worked example. A tiny sphere carries a charge of −8 × 10⁻¹⁸ C. How many excess electrons sit on it? Formula: Q = nq, so n = Q ÷ q. Substitute magnitudes: n = (8 × 10⁻¹⁸) ÷ (1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹). Answer: n = 50 electrons. The negative sign on the charge announces EXCESS electrons — gained, not lost — and the answer carries no unit, because it is a count.

Reverse the gears too. An object that has LOST 2,5 × 10¹¹ electrons carries Q = nq = (2,5 × 10¹¹)(1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 4 × 10⁻⁸ C — and the sign is POSITIVE, because a shortage of electrons leaves the protons in surplus.

The questions for this section are with you now: define quantisation in your own words, drive Q = nq in both directions, and fix every sign by reasoning about electrons gained or lost.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Charge an Object

First, the iron rule that governs every explanation in this topic: only ELECTRONS move. Protons stay riveted inside their nuclei through every process here — so a charging answer must always be phrased as electrons gained or electrons lost. Write "the cloth gained electrons"; never write "the rod gained protons".

CHARGING BY FRICTION — tribo-electric charging — is the insulators' method. Rub two different insulating materials together and the one that grips electrons more strongly strips them from the weaker gripper. Drag a plastic ruler through a woollen sleeve and the ruler typically comes away with extra electrons, negative, while the sleeve, robbed of them, is left positive. Which partner wins depends on the pair: a tribo-electric series ranks materials by how fiercely they hold their electrons.

CHARGING BY CONTACT is the conductors' method. Press a charged object against a neutral conductor and the charge spreads across both — so the two part company carrying the SAME SIGN. A negative rod touched to a neutral metal sphere shares its surplus electrons; the sphere leaves the encounter negative as well.

CHARGING BY INDUCTION, also for conductors, manufactures the OPPOSITE sign without a single touch. Bring a negative rod close to a neutral metal sphere. The sphere's electrons flee to the far side, leaving the near face positive — the sphere is polarised, though still neutral in total. Now earth the far side for a moment with a fingertip: the crowded electrons drain away to earth. Lift the finger first, then withdraw the rod, and the sphere is left holding a net POSITIVE charge — opposite to the rod that never touched it.

Insulators cannot pass charge along, but they still answer a nearby charge: their molecules POLARISE, each electron cloud easing slightly to one side, so the surface closest to the charged object wears the opposite charge. That is how a charged ruler lifts neutral paper confetti, and why a rubbed balloon clings to a painted wall.

Take the questions for this section now, writing each explanation strictly as electron traffic, and naming friction, contact or induction together with the sign it delivers.

## Subtopic: Conservation of Charge and the Sharing Rule

The PRINCIPLE OF CONSERVATION OF CHARGE: charge can be neither created nor destroyed, only transferred from one object to another. In an isolated system, the total charge after any interaction equals the total before it. Rubbing manufactures nothing — every electron that appears on the ruler is one the sleeve lost, so equal and opposite charges appear on the pair.

From that principle comes the rule this topic leans on constantly. When two IDENTICAL conducting spheres touch and separate, the total charge divides equally: Q = (Q₁ + Q₂) ÷ 2 — each sphere afterwards carries the sum of the original charges divided by two. Two conditions guard the rule: the spheres must be identical, and the original charges are added WITH THEIR SIGNS.

Worked example. Sphere P carries −5 nC and sphere R carries +1 nC. They touch, then separate. Find the final charge on each, and how many electrons moved. Final charge: Q = (Q₁ + Q₂) ÷ 2 = ((−5) + (+1)) ÷ 2 = (−4) ÷ 2 = −2 nC on each sphere. Conservation check: −2 plus −2 gives −4 nC, exactly the starting total. Now the electron count. Sphere P moved from −5 nC to −2 nC, surrendering 3 nC of negative charge — 3 × 10⁻⁹ C. Electrons: n = Q ÷ q = (3 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (1,6 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1,875 × 10¹⁰ electrons, transferred from P to R.

Two ambushes wait here. Ambush one: adding sizes and ignoring signs — 5 plus 1 over 2 gives 3 nC, which conjures charge from nowhere and breaks conservation. Ambush two: halving between spheres of DIFFERENT sizes — the equal split belongs to identical spheres only.

Work through the questions for this section now, keeping every sign inside the bracket and auditing conservation before any answer is committed to paper.

## Subtopic: Coulomb's Law and the Inverse Square

Like charges repel, unlike charges attract — true, but toothless without a number. COULOMB'S LAW supplies the number, and although its formal examination comes in Grade 11, meeting it now cements everything above.

The law: the electrostatic force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. In symbols: F = kQ₁Q₂ ÷ r². In words: F equals k Q one Q two over r squared — force equals Coulomb's constant times the product of the charges, divided by the distance squared. The constant k is 9 × 10⁹ N·m²·C⁻², and r is the centre-to-centre distance in metres.

Worked example. A charge of +2 × 10⁻⁶ C sits 0,20 m from a charge of −6 × 10⁻⁶ C. Find the magnitude of the force. Formula: F = kQ₁Q₂ ÷ r². Substitute magnitudes only: F = (9 × 10⁹)(2 × 10⁻⁶)(6 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ (0,20)². Numerator: 0,108. Denominator: 0,04. Answer: F = 2,7 N — and since the charges are unlike, it is a force of ATTRACTION.

Now the inverse square, the favourite twist. Slide the same pair out to 0,40 m — double the distance. The denominator becomes 0,16, and F = 0,108 ÷ 0,16 = 0,675 N. Doubling the gap collapsed the force to a QUARTER, not a half. Triple the gap and the force falls to a ninth.

The traps, by name. One: failing to square r — the single most common error in the topic. Two: substituting centimetres; 20 cm must enter as 0,20 m, and because r gets squared, that slip inflates or deflates the answer ten-thousand-fold. Three: feeding minus signs into the formula and reporting a meaningless negative force — substitute magnitudes, then declare attraction or repulsion in words. Four: dropping a prefix, since micro is 10⁻⁶ and nano is 10⁻⁹. Five: an answer without its unit — force is measured in newtons.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: quote the law, substitute SI magnitudes, square the distance, and close with the direction in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same physics from a balloon on a jersey, a gate latch that shocks you in July, and two friends evening out their pocket money — plain words first, formulas after.

## Subtopic: The Balloon and the School Jersey

Rub a balloon up and down your jersey, press it to the classroom wall, and take your hand away. It stays. No glue, no tape, no trick — so what is holding it?

Everything around you is built of pieces carrying a tiny electrical property called charge, in two flavours, positive and negative. Ordinarily an object holds the two flavours in exactly equal amounts, cancelling to neutral. Rubbing upsets the balance.

Here is the fact the whole topic stands on: only the negative pieces — the electrons — can move. The positive pieces sit locked in the cores of the atoms and never travel. So as balloon scrubs against jersey, electrons are scraped off the wool and pile onto the rubber. The balloon, holding extras, is now negative; the jersey, running short, is positive. Nothing new was made — charge was only relocated. Say that sentence and you have said the conservation law.

But the wall was never rubbed — why does it grab the balloon? Because the balloon's extra electrons shove the wall's own electrons a little way back from the surface, leaving the wall's face slightly positive. Opposites pull, and the balloon hangs on. The same move lets a rubbed ruler hoover up paper confetti, and makes loose hair chase a comb on a crackling dry winter morning.

One more gift from the electrons: since charge moves only as whole electrons, charge only ever comes in whole steps — never half an electron's worth. That is all the textbook word "quantised" means. Count charge like counting marbles: three, or three thousand, but never two and a half.

Quick check before we go on — questions on the balloon are with you now, especially on which pieces move and which never do.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Get Charged, and Sharing the Total

Three ways to load an object with charge, each with its own picture.

RUBBING is the balloon story: two insulators, one grips electrons harder, electrons cross over, and the pair walk away with opposite charges. It is also why July is shock season — dry air, woollen jerseys, synthetic carpets. Shuffle across the classroom floor and you harvest electrons the whole way; the steel gate latch is simply the first exit those electrons find, and the exit stings.

TOUCHING is the metals' way. Bring a charged object against a neutral piece of metal and the surplus electrons spill across and spread out. The two objects end up sharing, so they finish with the SAME sign — a negative rod leaves the metal negative too.

BRINGING NEAR — no contact — is the sly one. Hold something negative just off a metal ball. The ball's electrons scurry to the far side, leaving the near side positive. Touch the far side with a fingertip for one second: the huddled electrons escape through you into the ground. Finger away first, rod away second, and the ball is left positive — the OPPOSITE of the rod that never laid a finger on it. That is induction.

And sharing between two identical metal balls is playground arithmetic: pool the charges and split them evenly, like two friends tossing their pocket money into one pile and dividing it back fairly. If one ball holds minus 5 and the other plus 1, add them WITH signs — minus 5 plus 1 is minus 4 — then halve: minus 2 each. Never add the bare sizes: 5 plus 1 giving 3 each would mean fresh charge appeared out of thin air, and charge is never created — only moved.

Your questions on this part are up now: the three methods, the sign each one produces, and signs added before anything is halved.

## Subtopic: Close Is Strong

Last block: how hard do two charges actually push or pull on each other?

Two levers control it, and both make sense the moment they are said aloud. Lever one: bigger charges, bigger force — double either charge and the force doubles. Lever two: distance — and it works harder than anyone expects. Pull the charges twice as far apart and the force does not halve; it crashes to a QUARTER. Three times as far, and it is down to a ninth. The distance gets squared, so it punishes twice.

Picture a braai fire on a cold evening. One step back and the warmth fades far faster than the step deserved, because the heat is spreading over a wider and wider front as it travels. Electric force thins out exactly the same way — hence its tidy name, the inverse square rule.

Bolt the two levers together and you have Coulomb's law: force equals a constant, times the two charges multiplied, divided by the distance squared. The constant is nine times ten to the nine, and the answer lands in newtons.

Run one through. Two charges — two millionths of a coulomb and six millionths — sitting twenty centimetres apart. First move, before anything else: twenty centimetres becomes 0,20 of a metre, because that number is about to be squared and a slip there is a slip of ten thousand. Top of the fraction: nine times ten to the nine, times two millionths, times six millionths — 0,108. Bottom: 0,20 squared is 0,04. Divide: 2,7 newtons. One charge positive, one negative: they pull together — attraction.

Now slide them out to forty centimetres, double the gap. The bottom becomes 0,16, and the force drops to 0,675 newtons — precisely a quarter, exactly as the rule promised.

Two habits carry this whole topic: metres before you square, and minus signs kept OUT of the sum — work with sizes, collect your newtons, then say in words whether the pair pulls or pushes. Last questions of the lesson are with you right now, and if only one thing survives the week, let it be this: squaring the distance is where the marks are won.
