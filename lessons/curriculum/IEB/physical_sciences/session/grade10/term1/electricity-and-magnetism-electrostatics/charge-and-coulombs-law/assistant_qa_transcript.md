**Thandi:** Before you go — the four questions this topic always generates. First: every explanation is supposed to be about electrons moving, but people keep writing "the rod became positive because it gained positive charge." Why is that wording a problem, and what should it say?

**Tutor:** It is a problem because it describes something that physically cannot happen, and the wording is exactly what gets checked. The positive charges in ordinary matter are protons, and protons live buried in the nucleus of every atom, bound there by forces enormously stronger than anything rubbing or touching can supply. No everyday process moves a proton anywhere. The only mobile players are electrons — lightweight, on the atom's outskirts, and easy to strip or share. So every change of charge is a story about electron traffic, told from one side or the other: an object becomes negative by GAINING electrons, and becomes positive by LOSING them. The rod did not gain positive charge; it lost electrons, and the loss exposed a proton surplus that was there all along. Practise converting every statement into that grammar. "The cloth is negative" becomes "the cloth gained electrons from the rod". "The sphere became positive by induction" becomes "electrons were repelled to the far side and drained to earth, leaving the sphere short of electrons". If your sentence's verb belongs to a proton, rewrite it. One bonus check rides along free: because only electrons move, and each carries exactly 1,6 times ten to the minus nineteen coulombs, every legal charge is a whole multiple of that number — that is quantisation, spoken in electron grammar.

***

**Thandi:** Next, induction keeps tangling people: why must the finger leave before the rod? Half of us reversed the order in the practice question and could not see why it mattered.

**Tutor:** Follow the electrons through both orderings and the difference jumps out. Set the scene: negative rod held near a metal sphere, the sphere's electrons crowded onto the far side, near side positive, sphere still neutral overall. You touch the far side, and the crowded electrons — repelled by the rod, offered an escape — drain through your finger to earth. Now the fork. Correct order: lift the finger FIRST, while the rod still stands guard. The escape road is destroyed while the repulsion still holds, so when the rod finally leaves, the missing electrons have no way home — the sphere is stuck with an electron shortage, permanently positive. Wrong order: lift the rod first, with your finger still planted. The instant the rod's repulsion vanishes, nothing holds those electrons away any more — and your finger is still a road, this time running inward. The sphere's positive regions pull electrons straight back up from earth through your hand, the shortage refills, and the sphere lands exactly where it started: neutral. All that careful staging, undone in a blink. The way to hold it: the rod is the guard, the finger is the gate. Close the gate while the guard still stands; a gate left open after the guard walks away lets everything back in. And note the payoff that makes induction test-worthy — it produces the OPPOSITE sign to the rod, without contact, while touching produces the SAME sign. Answer the sign question by naming the method first.

***

**Thandi:** Then the sphere-sharing sums: the rule says add with signs, but why does adding the sizes fail? Plus 5 and minus 1 giving 3 each feels so natural.

**Tutor:** It feels natural because it treats charge like two piles of sand, and charge is not sand — it is a ledger with credits and debits. Minus 5 nanocoulombs is not "five units of stuff in a negative costume"; it is five units of electron surplus, while plus 1 is one unit of electron shortage. Put the two spheres in contact and the first thing that happens is cancellation: surplus electrons flood into the shortage until it is gone. One unit of surplus wipes out the one unit of shortage, leaving four units of surplus — minus 4 — as the TOTAL the pair now shares. Identical spheres split it evenly: minus 2 each. Now watch what the sizes-only method claims: 5 plus 1 is 6, shared as 3 each — a total magnitude of 6 where the system genuinely holds 4. Two nanocoulombs of charge invented from nothing, and conservation broken on the spot. That is why the conservation check is not decoration: add your two answers and they must reproduce the original signed total, minus 2 plus minus 2 giving minus 4. Do it every time; it costs five seconds and catches the error instantly. And keep the rule's small print visible — the equal split is for IDENTICAL conducting spheres. Different sizes still share until they reach agreement, but not in half-half proportions, and a question that mentions different radii is warning you not to halve.

**Thandi:** Last one, on Coulomb's law: the inverse square keeps ambushing people. Force at a quarter when distance doubles — what is the fastest way to get these scaling questions right without redoing the whole calculation?

**Tutor:** Use the ratio method: never recompute, just ask what factor each ingredient changed by, and push those factors through the formula's shape. The formula is F equals k Q one Q two over r squared, so force scales UP with each charge, and DOWN with the SQUARE of the distance. Distance doubled? The bottom of the fraction gains a factor of two squared — four — so the force divides by four: 2,7 newtons becomes 0,675. Distance tripled: divide by nine. Distance halved: multiply by four, because the square works both ways. One charge doubled: multiply by two, no squaring — squaring belongs to distance alone, and gluing it onto the charges is the mirror-image error to forgetting it on r. Combinations just multiply their factors: both charges doubled AND distance doubled means times two, times two, divided by four — no change at all, a favourite twist. Write the factor chain down: "F times 2 times 2 over 4 equals F". For the full calculations, keep the drill from the lesson: centimetres to metres BEFORE squaring, since 20 versus 0,20 is a factor of ten thousand once squared; magnitudes only inside the formula, with attraction or repulsion declared in words afterwards; and newtons on the answer. Scaling questions by ratios, number questions by the three-line layout — two lanes, never mixed, and Coulomb's law stops ambushing anyone.
