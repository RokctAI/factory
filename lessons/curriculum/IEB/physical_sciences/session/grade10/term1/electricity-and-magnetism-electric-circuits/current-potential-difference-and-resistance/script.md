# Part 1 — Expert

Electrostatics studied charge at rest; this session releases it. Four foundations carry every circuit you will ever draw: electric current and the coulomb-per-second that defines it, potential difference and the joule-per-coulomb that defines it, the two meters that read those quantities and the strict wiring rule attached to each, and resistance — what it is at the level of colliding electrons, and the three dials that set how much of it any conductor has.

## Subtopic: Current — Charge on the Move

ELECTRIC CURRENT is the rate of flow of charge — the amount of charge passing a point in the circuit per unit time. As an equation: I equals Q divided by delta t, with I the current in amperes, Q the charge in coulombs and delta t the time in seconds. One AMPERE means one coulomb passing the point every second. The coulomb itself arrives from electrostatics unchanged: the SI unit of charge, and a giant one — a single coulomb bundles the charge of roughly six billion billion electrons.

Drive the definition in both directions. If 40 C of charge flows through a heater element in 8 s, the current is I equals Q over delta t: 40 divided by 8, which is 5 A. Reversed: a torch bulb drawing 0,3 A for 5 minutes passes charge Q equals I times delta t. Convert first — 5 minutes is 300 s. Then Q equals 0,3 times 300, which is 90 C.

Next, the direction convention — pure history, and pure marks. CONVENTIONAL CURRENT is defined to flow from the POSITIVE terminal of the battery, around the external circuit, into the NEGATIVE terminal. The electrons — the charges that actually drift in a metal — travel the other way, negative toward positive, because their charge is negative. The arrow was agreed before anyone had found the electron, and physics never repainted it. So unless a question names electron flow specifically, every arrow you draw is conventional current: positive to negative, around the outside.

And bank this truth immediately: current is never used up. Every coulomb that leaves one terminal arrives at the other. A bulb does not eat charge — it unloads energy from the charge passing through it. Take this section's questions now: the definition, both rearrangements, and the direction given without a flicker of doubt.

## Subtopic: Potential Difference, Emf and the Volt

Charge only moves because energy is spent moving it, and POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE is the bookkeeping of that energy. The potential difference between two points is the WORK DONE per unit charge — the energy transferred for each coulomb travelling between the points. As an equation: V equals W divided by Q, with W the work, or energy transferred, in joules, and Q the charge in coulombs. The unit is the VOLT: one volt is one joule per coulomb.

Put numbers through it. If 36 J of work moves 6 C of charge through a resistor, the potential difference across the resistor is V equals W over Q: 36 divided by 6, which is 6 V. Read battery labels with the same eye: a 1,5 V cell hands every coulomb 1,5 J of energy; a 12 V battery hands every coulomb 12 J. Rearranged, W equals V times Q: pushing 4 C through a 12 V system transfers 48 J.

Now the distinction this topic insists on: EMF versus TERMINAL POTENTIAL DIFFERENCE. Both are volts, both measured across the same battery — what differs is the circumstance. The emf is the voltmeter reading across the battery when NO current flows: the open-circuit reading, the full energy-per-coulomb promised by the chemistry inside. The terminal potential difference is the reading across those same terminals while the battery is DELIVERING current — and it always sits slightly BELOW the emf, because the battery's own internal resistance taxes each coulomb on its way through the battery itself. Emf is the promise, read at rest; terminal pd is the delivery, read under load. A battery labelled 12 V may read 12 V on the shelf and 11,4 V while driving headlights — nothing is faulty; the missing volts are being spent inside the battery.

The questions for this section are with you now: joules per coulomb, both rearrangements, and emf cleanly fenced off from terminal pd.

## Subtopic: Ammeters and Voltmeters — Reading the Circuit

Two instruments, two quantities, two opposite wiring rules — and circuit questions probe those rules relentlessly.

The AMMETER measures current. Current is a flow THROUGH a point, so the ammeter must sit in the flow: it is connected IN SERIES, cut into the line so that every coulomb it counts genuinely passes through it — a turnstile installed in the road itself. Sitting in the main path, it must disturb the traffic as little as possible: an ideal ammeter has a VERY LOW resistance, effectively zero, so its presence barely changes the current it reports.

The VOLTMETER measures potential difference — an energy comparison BETWEEN two points. So it connects IN PARALLEL, across the component: one lead on each side of the resistor or bulb, reading the energy per coulomb before against after, the way you measure a waterfall's height with one mark at the lip and one at the pool. Offering a side-path around the component, the voltmeter must poach as little current as possible: an ideal voltmeter has a VERY HIGH resistance, effectively infinite.

Trade the rules and the circuit punishes you. A voltmeter spliced in series brings its enormous resistance into the main line and strangles the current to nearly nothing — the circuit dies. An ammeter clipped in parallel lays its near-zero resistance across the component and invites a violent current through itself — a short circuit that can blow the meter's fuse or cook the battery. So the drill before every diagram: ammeter in the line, in series, low resistance; voltmeter across the component, in parallel, high resistance.

Reading discipline belongs here too: check which scale the meter is set to, read amperes or volts off the correct scale, and attach the unit every single time. Take the questions for this section now — connection, resistance, and the reason behind each.

## Subtopic: Resistance — the Friction of the Wire

RESISTANCE is a material's opposition to the flow of electric charge. Symbol R, unit the OHM. A conductor has a resistance of one ohm when a potential difference of one volt across it pushes a current of one ampere through it.

One microscopic picture explains the whole concept. A metal is a scaffold of fixed positive ions soaked in free electrons. Drive those electrons along with a potential difference and they do not slide cleanly — they smash again and again into the vibrating ions of the scaffold. Every collision hands energy from electron to ion, shaking the scaffold harder. That handed-over energy is HEAT: the reason a working resistor warms, the reason a stove plate glows, and the reason energy must be fed in continuously to keep current moving. Resistance IS that collision story, priced in ohms.

From the picture, the three official factors fall out by pure logic. LENGTH: a longer wire is more scaffold to cross, more collisions per trip — resistance grows with length; double the length, double the resistance. THICKNESS: a fatter wire offers more side-by-side lanes, more cross-sectional area for the same flow — resistance FALLS as thickness grows, as a wide corridor passes a crowd more easily than a narrow one. TEMPERATURE: heat a metal and its ions shake more violently, so electrons collide more often — for metallic conductors, resistance climbs as temperature climbs. A filament at the cold instant of switch-on resists far less than the same filament glowing at working temperature.

Material chooses sides too: copper barely resists, nichrome resists hard — so houses are wired in copper to waste little, while kettles and heaters are wound in nichrome to convert electrical energy into heat on purpose. Long, thin, hot: high resistance. Short, thick, cool: low. This part's final questions are in front of you now — the definition, the collision picture, and all three factors with their directions.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same four ideas again — now running on a water feature in a courtyard, a stadium turnstile, and the corridor outside your classroom at break.

## Subtopic: The Water Pump on the Roof

Picture a courtyard water feature: a pump hoists water up into a tank on the roof, and the water falls back down a pipe, spinning a small paddle wheel on its way to the bottom, where the pump lifts it again. The same water, around and around, all day.

The pump is the battery. Its one job is LIFTING — giving every litre some height, some stored energy, before releasing it into the loop. A battery does precisely this to charge: it loads every coulomb with a parcel of energy and sends it out of the positive terminal. The number on the label is the size of the parcel — a 1,5 V cell loads 1,5 joules into every coulomb, a 12 V battery loads 12. That is all a volt is: joules per coulomb, the energy each unit of charge carries as it sets out.

The paddle wheel is the bulb or resistor. Falling water cashes in its height to spin the wheel; travelling charge cashes in its energy parcel to heat a filament white. Watch closely what is spent and what is not: the WATER survives every lap — each litre returns to the pump for another lift. Charge in a circuit is the same: never consumed, only relieved of the energy it carries. A battery goes flat not because it ran out of charge, but because its chemical lifting power gave out.

And one honest detail rounds off the picture. Inside the pump itself there is a little drag, so the water reaches the roof with slightly less height than the pump's full effort promised — exactly why a battery under load reads slightly below its open-shelf figure. The shelf reading is the emf, the promise; the working reading is the terminal pd, the delivery.

Questions on the pump picture are with you now — what the battery actually gives, and what never gets used up.

## Subtopic: Counting Heads at the Gate

Current next, on the friendliest picture in the subject: the turnstile at a stadium on derby day.

Current is COUNTING AT A POINT: how many supporters click through the turnstile each second. Not how fast anyone walks, not how full the stadium is — clicks per second at the gate. In the circuit, the supporters are coulombs, and the count is the current: one coulomb through per second is one ampere. Forty coulombs through the heater in eight seconds? Five per second — 5 A. A torch bulb sipping three-tenths of a coulomb each second for five minutes? Three-tenths times three hundred — 90 coulombs delivered in total.

The counting picture also dissolves the oldest beginner's fear — that current gets eaten along the way. Watch the turnstile: every supporter who enters comes out the other side; the gate swallows nobody. Current is identical on both sides of a bulb. What the bulb collects is the ENERGY each charge carries — the ticket money, never the people.

As for direction: it is an old arrow on an old map. The current arrow — positive terminal around to negative — was drawn before anyone knew that in a metal it is the negative electrons drifting, in the opposite direction. The arrow was never redrawn. So your diagrams always carry the official arrow, conventional current, positive to negative around the outside — and electrons get a mention only when a question asks for them by name.

Take the gate questions now — counting per second, and who actually moves versus which way the arrow points.

## Subtopic: The Narrow Corridor

The last idea, resistance, is taught every break time by the corridor outside your classroom.

Push down a corridor packed shoulder to shoulder. You bump, you stall, you squeeze past — and every bump costs you energy and leaves the crowd a little more stirred up. Electrons in a wire live that exact story: the corridor is the metal's scaffold of ions, the bumping is the collisions, and the stirred-up crowd is heat. That is why current warms a wire, and why a kettle element — a corridor built deliberately awkward — runs hot enough to boil water. Resistance is the corridor's difficulty rating.

Three changes make any corridor harder, and they are precisely the three factors. Make it LONGER: more corridor, more bumps — a long wire resists more, in direct proportion; double it and the difficulty doubles. Make it NARROWER: fewer lanes, worse crowding — a thin wire resists more than a thick one, so a fatter wire means an easier passage and lower resistance. Make the crowd JITTERY: a corridor of milling, restless learners is far harder to cross than a calm one — that is temperature, because heating the metal sets its ions rattling, and a hot wire resists more than a cold one.

Then pick the corridor to match the job. Copper is the wide, calm hallway — charge strolls through, so houses are wired with it and little energy is lost on the way. Nichrome is the obstacle course — installed in kettles, toasters and heaters exactly because charge fighting through it turns electrical energy into heat, which is the whole point of the appliance.

Long, thin and hot: maximum resistance. Short, thick and cool: minimum. The lesson's last questions are with you right now — the corridor's three dials, plus the two meters from earlier: counter in the line, height-gauge across the ends. Hold those five pictures and every circuit, this year and beyond, rests on solid ground.
