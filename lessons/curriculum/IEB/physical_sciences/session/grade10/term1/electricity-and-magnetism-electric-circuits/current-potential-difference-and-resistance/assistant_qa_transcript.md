**Thandi:** Before you head out — the four questions this lesson always leaves behind. First: if the electrons in the wire actually drift from negative to positive, why do we still draw the arrow the other way? Doesn't using a wrong arrow break the physics?

**Tutor:** The arrow is not wrong — it is a convention, and conventions only need consistency, not electrons. Here is the history and then the reassurance. The direction of current was defined — positive terminal, around the outside, to negative — decades before anyone knew what actually moves in a metal. When the electron turned up, negatively charged and drifting the opposite way, physics faced a choice: repaint every arrow in every book and instrument, or keep the convention and note the electron's direction separately. It kept the convention, and here is why that costs nothing: a flow of negative charge one way is mathematically identical, in every effect it produces, to a flow of positive charge the other way. The same energy is delivered, the same readings appear on every meter, the same circuit laws hold. So the official arrow gives right answers everywhere, provided everyone uses the same one — and everyone does. Your working rule: every arrow you draw is conventional current, positive to negative around the external circuit; the phrase "electron flow" enters your answer only when the question uses it first, and then you state that electrons drift opposite to conventional current because their charge is negative. Two directions, one physics — and the marks go to whoever keeps the labels straight.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the one that muddles everyone: current and voltage feel like the same thing — both get bigger when the circuit works harder. What is the cleanest way to keep them separate in definitions and in units?

**Tutor:** Anchor each one to its own question, because they answer different questions about the same charge. Current answers HOW MANY: how many coulombs pass a chosen point per second. It is a counting rate, measured in amperes, one ampere being one coulomb per second, and you can only measure it by standing IN the flow — hence the ammeter in series. Potential difference answers HOW MUCH EACH: how much energy every coulomb gains or spends between two points. It is a per-coulomb energy price, measured in volts, one volt being one joule per coulomb, and it only exists BETWEEN two places — hence the voltmeter in parallel, straddling the component. Notice each definition smuggles in its own unit and its own meter rule; learn them as a package and nothing can drift. Test yourself with the turnstile and the ticket: the turnstile's clicks per second is current; the ticket price each supporter pays at the gate is the potential difference across it. A busy gate with cheap tickets, a quiet gate with expensive ones — the two numbers move independently, which is exactly why a circuit needs both. And when a calculation appears, the units police themselves: coulombs divided by seconds can only be a current; joules divided by coulombs can only be a voltage. Write units on every line and a swapped formula announces itself before it costs anything.

***

**Thandi:** Then emf: the lesson says a 12 volt battery can read 11,4 volts while working, and nothing is broken. Where do the missing volts physically go?

**Tutor:** They are spent inside the battery, on the battery's own awkwardness — and once you see that, emf questions become easy. A real battery is not a perfect pump: the charge it drives around the circuit must also travel THROUGH the battery, through its chemical paste and plates, and that internal passage has resistance of its own — internal resistance. While current flows, every coulomb pays a small energy toll crossing that internal passage, and the toll appears as gentle warmth inside the battery itself. What is left over per coulomb at the terminals is the terminal potential difference: the full chemical promise, minus the internal toll. Now the two readings make sense. On the shelf, with no current, nobody is crossing the internal passage, no toll is collected, and the voltmeter sees the whole promise — that reading is the emf, 12 volts. Under load, with current flowing, the toll is being paid, and the terminals show only 11,4 — the delivery. The 0,6 volts is not lost from the universe; it is heating the inside of the battery. Two exam-ready consequences: the harder the battery works — the bigger the current — the bigger the toll and the lower the terminal reading, which is why headlights dim slightly when a car's starter motor runs; and emf can only be measured with the circuit open, because the act of drawing current immediately starts the toll. Promise at rest, delivery under load, difference spent inside — three phrases that answer every version of this question.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, practical: in the test there will be a circuit to draw or fix, with both meters. What is the checking routine before moving on to the next question?

**Tutor:** Run a four-point inspection, in the same order every time. Point one — the loop: trace the circuit with a finger from the battery's positive terminal all the way around and back to the negative. There must be one unbroken conducting path; if your finger has to jump a gap, the circuit cannot work before any meter question even starts. Point two — the ammeter: it must lie ON the path you just traced, spliced into the line so your finger passes straight through it. If you can reach the far side of the circuit without crossing the ammeter, it is not in series, and it is not counting the current it claims to count. Remember its character: very low resistance, a turnstile that never slows the crowd. Point three — the voltmeter: it must sit OFF the main path, on a little side branch of its own, with its two leads landing one on each side of the component being measured. Your tracing finger should never pass through a voltmeter; if it does, the voltmeter is in series, and its enormous resistance has just strangled the circuit. Point four — the labels: arrow drawn from positive around to negative, every reading paired with its unit, amperes for the counter, volts for the price-gauge. Then the sanity glance: does each meter's placement match its question — the ammeter asking how many, in the line; the voltmeter asking how much each, across the ends? Trace, splice, straddle, label. Thirty seconds, and the wiring marks — which come up in some form nearly every year — are safely banked.
