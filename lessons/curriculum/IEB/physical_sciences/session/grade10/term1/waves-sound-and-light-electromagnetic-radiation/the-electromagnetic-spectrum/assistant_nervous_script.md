Hey, it's Thandi — I know how this one looks: powers of ten flying everywhere, a constant with thirty-four zeros hiding under it, and seven bands to hold in order. Breathe. This topic is secretly one of the kindest in the course, because it runs on just two ideas.

Idea one: it is all one family, and the family has one speed. Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma — every one of them crosses empty space at three hundred million metres a second. Nothing to compare, nothing to rank. The only difference is packing: long lazy waves at the radio end, short furious waves at the gamma end. And you already know the middle of the queue, because infra-red means below red and ultra-violet means beyond violet — light's neighbours are named after it.

Idea two: shorter waves punch harder. Radio taps like a feather, ultraviolet flicks like a fingernail, gamma hits like a hammer. That single line generates every use and every danger — why radio is harmless, why sunscreen exists, why the radiographer stands behind the screen. No table to memorise: just ask how hard the packet hits.

The calculations are two small formulas: c equals f lambda for the see-saw between length and count, E equals h f for the punch. The only real enemy is the prefixes — write nano as ten to the minus nine ON PAPER, tidy into standard form, then substitute.

Seven names in order, one speed, one punch rule, two formulas. That is the entire topic. Go and open the practice questions right now while the order is still humming in your head. You've got this!
