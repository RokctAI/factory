# Part 1 — Expert

Sound demanded a medium; this session introduces the waves that laugh at that demand. We build the electromagnetic family from its two intertwined fields, establish why every member crosses a vacuum at exactly one speed, learn the spectrum's seven bands in strict order from radio waves to gamma rays, and master the two equations that convert between frequency, wavelength and the energy of a single photon. The arithmetic lives entirely in scientific notation, so command of prefixes and powers of ten decides whether an answer earns its marks or bleeds them.

## Subtopic: What an Electromagnetic Wave Is

An ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVE is produced by an ACCELERATING ELECTRIC CHARGE. Drive electrons up and down a transmitter mast and their changing electric field generates a changing magnetic field, which regenerates a changing electric field a little further out — and the two fields leapfrog each other outward through space forever. The wave consists of an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, perpendicular to each other, and both perpendicular to the direction of travel. That final geometry makes all electromagnetic radiation TRANSVERSE.

Now the consequence that towers over everything else: because the two fields sustain each other, an electromagnetic wave needs NO MEDIUM. Sound falls silent in a vacuum; sunlight crosses roughly one hundred and fifty million kilometres of vacuum to land on your arm. That contrast is the sharpest dividing line between the two wave families, and questions return to it again and again.

In a vacuum, every electromagnetic wave moves at one identical speed — the speed of light, symbol c, value 3 × 10⁸ m·s⁻¹, three times ten to the eight metres per second. Radio waves do not lag behind gamma rays; violet light does not outrun red. In a vacuum they differ in frequency and wavelength only, never in speed. Inside a material like glass or water they all slow down, but that story belongs later in the course.

Electromagnetic radiation also carries a DUAL NATURE. The WAVE MODEL — travelling, self-sustaining fields — accounts for reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference. The PARTICLE MODEL pictures the radiation as a stream of discrete energy packets called PHOTONS, and it explains why radiation is emitted and absorbed in fixed amounts, and why dim ultraviolet light can kick electrons out of a metal surface when blinding red light cannot. Neither picture is complete on its own; radiation sits both exams, and physics keeps both models.

The questions for this section are with you now: state what produces an electromagnetic wave, name the two fields with their orientations, and argue properly why no medium is needed.

## Subtopic: The Spectrum in Order

The ELECTROMAGNETIC SPECTRUM is the whole range of electromagnetic radiation laid out in order of frequency, and the sequence is non-negotiable memory work. From lowest frequency and longest wavelength to highest frequency and shortest wavelength: RADIO WAVES, MICROWAVES, INFRARED, VISIBLE LIGHT, ULTRAVIOLET, X-RAYS, GAMMA RAYS.

Say the order twice, because two rulers run along it in opposite directions. Radio waves stretch metres to hundreds of metres between crests at the lowest frequencies. Gamma rays crowd their crests closer than the width of an atomic nucleus, at frequencies beyond ten to the twenty hertz. Each step along the spectrum multiplies frequency and shrinks wavelength, because their product is chained to c.

VISIBLE LIGHT occupies one slender band near the middle — roughly 400 to 700 nanometres — the only slice any human eye registers. Inside the band the same law repeats: RED owns the longest wavelength and lowest frequency; orange, yellow, green, blue and indigo follow; VIOLET owns the shortest wavelength and highest frequency. Below red, just outside the band, sits infrared; beyond violet sits ultraviolet — the neighbours are literally named after their positions.

Two quantities climb steadily as you walk the spectrum toward gamma rays, and together they will explain every use and every danger in this lesson. First: ENERGY PER PHOTON rises with frequency. Second: PENETRATING ABILITY rises with that energy — radio waves drift through brick walls and through your body without incident, while gamma rays punch through concrete and through living cells, ripping electrons off atoms on the way. Radiation energetic enough to strip electrons from atoms is called IONISING RADIATION, and the family's ionising members are ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.

Take the questions for this section now — the seven bands in order, which end carries the long wavelengths, and red and violet placed correctly inside the visible strip.

## Subtopic: The Two Equations — c = fλ and E = hf

Every calculation in this topic runs on two formulae, and both must be written down before any number is substituted.

First, the wave equation with its speed pinned: c = fλ. In words: c equals f lambda — the speed of light equals frequency times wavelength. With c constant, frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional: treble the frequency and the wavelength drops to a third.

Worked example. A two-way radio transmits at 150 MHz. Find the wavelength. Formula: c = fλ, so λ = c ÷ f. Convert: 150 MHz = 150 × 10⁶ Hz = 1,5 × 10⁸ Hz. Substitute: λ = (3 × 10⁸) ÷ (1,5 × 10⁸). Answer: λ = 2 m. Two metres — which is why such radios wear aerials the better part of a metre long, while a microwave oven humming at about 2,45 × 10⁹ Hz makes waves only around 0,12 m long, and the fine metal grid in its door, with holes far smaller than that, traps them inside.

Second, the energy of a single photon: E = hf, where h is Planck's constant, 6,63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. In words: E equals h f — photon energy equals Planck's constant times frequency. Marry it to c = fλ and you get the alternative form E = hc/λ — energy equals h c over lambda.

Worked example. Ultraviolet radiation of wavelength 300 nm. Find the frequency and the energy of one photon. Convert first: 300 nm = 300 × 10⁻⁹ m = 3 × 10⁻⁷ m. Frequency: f = c ÷ λ = (3 × 10⁸) ÷ (3 × 10⁻⁷) = 1 × 10¹⁵ Hz. Photon energy: E = hf = (6,63 × 10⁻³⁴)(1 × 10¹⁵) = 6,63 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Now put the family side by side. One photon of that 150 MHz radio wave carries E = hf = (6,63 × 10⁻³⁴)(1,5 × 10⁸) = 9,95 × 10⁻²⁶ J — nearly seven million times less than the ultraviolet photon. That ratio, and never any difference in speed, is why one band delivers music into a car and the other delivers sunburn onto a shoulder.

Work through this section's questions now, converting every prefix into standard form before substitution, and closing every answer with its unit.

## Subtopic: Uses, Dangers and the Traps

Every band's uses and dangers are consequences of its photon energy and penetrating power — learn the logic and the list writes itself.

RADIO WAVES: broadcasting, television, cellphone and two-way signals. Feeblest photons, non-ionising, no established harm at broadcast strengths. MICROWAVES: cooking — water molecules absorb them hungrily and the food heats — plus satellite links, cellphone towers and radar. Intense beams heat living tissue. INFRARED: heaters and heat lamps, television remotes, thermal-imaging and night-vision cameras; overdo the exposure and skin burns. VISIBLE LIGHT: sight itself, photosynthesis, optical-fibre communication; concentrated beams such as lasers can wreck a retina.

Then the ionising trio. ULTRAVIOLET: sterilising water and instruments, making vitamin D in skin, exposing the hidden security marks on banknotes. Its menace is real in South Africa, where the ultraviolet index runs high most of the year: sunburn, early ageing of skin, skin cancer, cataracts. X-RAYS: imaging bones and teeth, scanning luggage at airports, checking welds; being ionising, they damage living cells — hence the radiographer behind the screen and the lead apron over the patient. GAMMA RAYS: radiotherapy that destroys tumour cells, sterilising surgical equipment, irradiating food to kill bacteria. The most penetrating, most ionising member of the family; high doses bring severe cell damage and radiation sickness.

Now the traps, all five. One: prefixes — nanometres, megahertz and gigahertz must become metres and hertz before substitution; 300 nm is 3 × 10⁻⁷ m, never 300. Two: the speed error — in a vacuum every band moves at the same c, so gamma rays are more energetic than radio waves, never faster. Three: calculator discipline — enter powers of ten with the exponent key, not by typing "times ten to the". Four: the lambda in E = hc/λ must be in metres, nothing else. Five: photon energy is answered in joules — an answer in hertz is a frequency that wandered into the wrong question.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: pair every band with one use and one danger, justify both from photon energy, and convert every prefix before anything is substituted.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same family, met across one ordinary day — the radio at breakfast, the sun on the walk home, the plate at the dentist — same order, same sums, home language first.

## Subtopic: One Family, Same Speed

Everything in this topic is one family of waves. All of them are made the same way, all of them cross empty space without needing air or water or anything else — and here is the fact that catches everyone: all of them travel at exactly the same speed. Three hundred million metres every second. The breakfast radio signal, the glow of your phone screen, the X-ray at the clinic — in empty space, one speed, no exceptions, no overtaking.

Then what separates them? Only how tightly the waves are packed. Some arrive long and lazy, sprawled over metres. Others arrive short and furious, billions upon billions crammed into each second. That packing is the entire difference between a music broadcast and a gamma ray.

Parade them from longest and laziest to shortest and sharpest and the family lines up: radio, microwave, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. Seven members, and the order outranks everything else in the topic. Here is the trick for anchoring it: visible light's two neighbours carry its name. Infra-red means below red — the invisible warmth rolling off braai coals onto your face. Ultra-violet means beyond violet — the invisible burn that finds you at the beach even under cloud. So light stands in the middle of the queue with warmth on one side and sunburn on the other, and the rest of the family extends outward from there.

And inside the thin visible strip the same pattern repeats in miniature: red is the long lazy end, violet the short sharp end, and the rainbow fills the space between in the order you have known since primary school.

Small check before we go on — questions on the family are with you now, especially the order of the seven and the single shared speed.

## Subtopic: The See-Saw and the Punch

Two pictures run this entire topic, and neither needs a formula to make sense.

Picture one: a see-saw. The speed is bolted at three hundred million metres per second, so wavelength and frequency must trade against each other exactly. Shorten the waves and more must arrive per second; lengthen them and fewer arrive. One side down, other side up — always, without exception. That is everything c equals f lambda says: speed equals frequency times wavelength, and with the speed bolted down, the other two balance on the pivot.

Ride the see-saw with numbers. A two-way radio chirps at one hundred and fifty million cycles a second. Divide light's three hundred million metres per second among those cycles and each wave measures two metres — which is exactly why the handset's aerial is a decent stick of metal rather than a stub. Now the microwave oven, screaming at nearly two and a half billion cycles a second: vastly more waves each second, so each is far shorter — about twelve centimetres. Peer at the mesh in the oven door: its holes are far smaller than twelve centimetres, so the waves are simply too broad to squeeze through. Supper gets the energy; your face does not.

Picture two: the punch. The shorter and sharper the wave, the harder each individual packet of it lands. Radio packets arrive like feathers brushing you all day — you never feel one. Ultraviolet packets arrive like fingernail flicks — enough of them and skin cells take real damage. X-ray and gamma packets arrive like hammer blows, straight through skin into the cells beneath. Do the division and one ultraviolet packet lands nearly seven million times harder than one radio packet — same speed on the road, wildly different force on arrival.

Your questions on this part are up now: the see-saw trading length against count, and the punch explaining who is harmless and who is not.

## Subtopic: Sunscreen, the Remote and the Lead Apron

Walk one ordinary day and you will greet all seven members, each doing its job, each with its warning label.

The radio wakes you — the morning show, the TV news, every message that reaches your phone rode in on radio waves. Gentle, harmless, and happy to drift through walls, which is why the signal finds you indoors. Then breakfast: the microwave reheats the leftovers by rattling the water molecules inside the food until they run hot. The same band carries your calls to the tower and satellite television down to the dish.

You aim the remote at the TV: infrared does the pressing, invisible but real — the same radiation you feel as heat from coals or a wall heater. Around you all day, visible light: the one thin band your eyes were built for, the band plants eat to grow, and the band that carries the internet as flashes down fibre.

On the walk home, ultraviolet is waiting. It has honest work — building vitamin D in your skin, sterilising water, lighting up the hidden marks on a banknote — but under a South African sky it is fierce most of the year, and an overdose means sunburn, damaged eyes and, over years, skin cancer. Hence the hat and the sunscreen; and no, cloud cover does not switch it off.

At the dentist, X-rays sail through your cheek's soft tissue and stop in teeth and bone — that contrast is the image. But energy that can pass through you can also injure cells in passing, so the radiographer steps out of the room and a lead apron settles over your chest. And in the hospital's oncology wing, gamma rays — the family's heavyweight — are aimed deliberately into tumours to kill cancer cells, and used to sterilise instruments that must be perfectly clean.

One family, one speed, running from harmless to hazardous — and the only thing that changed along the line was how tightly the waves were packed. Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: match each band to a use and a danger, and explain both with the punch idea. Once the logic is yours, the list never needs memorising again.
