**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions this topic always raises. First one: the lesson keeps saying only energy arrives at the far end. But energy is invisible. How would I actually show, in a test answer, that the medium stayed behind?

**Tutor:** Describe the marker experiment — it is one sentence of apparatus and one sentence of result, and it proves the whole claim. Stick a piece of coloured tape to one coil of the spring, or watch a single peg on the washing line, and send pulse after pulse past it. Result: the marker rises and falls each time, and after any number of pulses it is exactly where it started — it has zero net displacement along the spring. Meanwhile the far end visibly jerks when each pulse arrives, so something real was delivered there: the far end gained energy it did not have before. Put those two observations side by side and the conclusion writes itself — the disturbance and its energy travelled the full length, while every particle of the medium only made a short round trip at right angles to the travel. That is why the definition says a pulse transfers energy without transferring matter. When a question asks what a pulse or a wave transfers, the core of the answer is one word, energy — and if you have two more marks to earn, add the tape evidence: the medium's particles return to their rest positions, so no matter is carried along.

***

**Thandi:** Next: friction makes a pulse's amplitude shrink as it travels down the line. A few people then wrote that the pulse slows down as it fades. Why is that wrong?

**Tutor:** Because they let two different quantities share one word in their heads — "weaker" — and then let weaker mean slower. Untangle them. Amplitude is the energy account: friction steadily withdraws energy from the pulse, and the balance shows in the shrinking height of the hump. Speed is a completely different account, owned not by the pulse but by the medium: it is fixed by the tension in the line and the mass of each metre of it. As the pulse fades, has the line's tension changed? No. Has the line gotten heavier or lighter? No. Then the speed cannot have changed either — the fading pulse keeps covering its 2 metres every second right to the end; it just arrives shorter than it left. Here is a check you can do mentally: send two identical pulses one second apart. If fading really slowed a pulse down, the fresher one behind would catch up to the tired one in front, and pulses would pile up on every long rope — which nobody has ever observed. The gap stays constant, because both travel at the medium's one speed. Write the rule as two separate sentences and you will never merge them again: friction reduces amplitude, because amplitude stores energy; friction leaves speed alone, because speed belongs to the medium.

***

**Thandi:** Then the argument that never dies: when the crest and the equal trough cancel and the spring lies flat, my friend says the pulses are gone and new ones get created afterwards. How do I beat that answer?

**Tutor:** Beat it with the energy audit, because your friend's version breaks the most protected rule in physics. If the pulses were truly gone at the flat instant, their energy would be gone too, and if new pulses were created afterwards, that energy would appear from nothing — destroyed then created, twice impossible. So the energy must be somewhere in the flat spring, and it is: in motion. A photograph of the flat instant shows you displacement only, and displacement genuinely is zero everywhere. But point a speed camera at the coils instead. On one side of the meeting point the coils are sweeping upward, driven by the crest still arriving; on the other side they are sweeping downward, driven by the trough. The spring is flat and furiously moving — like a skipping rope at the moment it passes through the straight position: no shape, maximum motion. All the pulse energy is banked as kinetic energy of the medium for that one instant, and that motion is exactly what rebuilds the crest on one side and the trough on the other a moment later, each with its original amplitude and heading. The full-credit sentence is: the displacements sum to zero, but both pulses still exist — their energy is momentarily stored in the motion of the medium, and they continue through each other unchanged.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, about the diagrams. Interference questions usually come as two pulse shapes drawn on a grid, and people lose marks even when they understand the rule. What is the working method for the picture itself?

**Tutor:** Treat the grid as a table of numbers and stop trusting your eye. Column by column, the method has four steps. Step one: write the sign convention above your working — up positive, down negative — because it is often a mark on its own and it forces discipline. Step two: at the instant the question freezes, read off each pulse's displacement at every gridline in the overlap region, counting squares, sign attached: the crest gives plus 2 squares here, plus 1 there; the trough gives minus 1, minus 2. Step three: add each column algebraically and plot the answers as dots — plus 2 minus 1 is plus 1, so one square up at that gridline — then join your dots. That joined curve IS the resultant; you never guessed its shape, you computed it point by point. Step four: outside the overlap region, copy each pulse exactly as drawn, and if the question asks for a later moment, redraw both originals past each other, unchanged. Two warnings from marked work: do not add the amplitudes of the whole pulses when the peaks are not aligned — superposition happens point by point, not pulse by pulse; and never leave the after-picture empty, because the survival of both pulses usually carries its own mark. Columns in, dots out, both pulses alive at the end — that method turns every interference diagram into arithmetic.
