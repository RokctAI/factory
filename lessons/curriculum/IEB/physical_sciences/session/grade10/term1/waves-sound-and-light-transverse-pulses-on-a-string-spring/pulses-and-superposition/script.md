# Part 1 — Expert

Before this course can talk about waves — the swell rolling into a harbour, the hum of a plucked bass string, the light reaching your desk — it has to nail down one smaller object: the pulse. This session does exactly that. We define the pulse and settle what actually travels when one moves, we pin down amplitude and where it is measured from, and then we meet the rule that decides what a medium does when two pulses share a point at the same instant: the principle of superposition, together with its two named outcomes, constructive and destructive interference.

## Subtopic: The Pulse — a Single Disturbance That Travels

A PULSE is a single disturbance that moves through a medium. One disturbance — not a repeating train of them. Give the free end of a long stretched spring one sharp upward jerk and a lone hump sets off down the spring toward the fixed end. That travelling hump is a pulse. Jerk the end rhythmically and hump after hump follows — that is a wave, and waves are simply pulses in procession, which is why every wave rule you meet later rests on what a single pulse does here.

Now the definition that earns full credit. A TRANSVERSE PULSE is one in which the particles of the medium move at RIGHT ANGLES — perpendicular — to the direction in which the pulse travels. Stick a small piece of bright tape to one coil of the spring and watch only the tape. As the hump reaches it, the tape lifts straight up; as the hump moves on, the tape sinks straight back to its starting height. The tape's journey is vertical. The pulse's journey is horizontal. The medium moves across the line of travel — and that crossing is precisely what transverse means.

Here is the idea students resist at first, and the one exam questions probe hardest: THE MEDIUM DOES NOT TRAVEL WITH THE PULSE. The tape never creeps toward the fixed end, not by a coil's width, no matter how many pulses pass it. Each particle of the spring makes a brief round trip — up, down, home — while the DISTURBANCE, and the ENERGY it carries, completes the whole journey. A pulse is a transfer of energy through a medium without any transfer of the medium itself. Learn that sentence exactly; it is the standard definition-style answer.

And the speed? The speed of a pulse is fixed by the MEDIUM — the mass of the spring and how firmly it is stretched — never by the violence of the jerk that launched it. A pulse that travels 8 m along the spring in 4 s has speed equal to distance divided by time: 8 divided by 4, which is 2 m·s⁻¹. Every other pulse on that same spring, huge or tiny, clocks the same 2 m·s⁻¹.

## Subtopic: Amplitude, and What the Medium Actually Does

The AMPLITUDE of a pulse is the MAXIMUM displacement of a particle of the medium from its REST position. Every word in that definition is load-bearing, and each can be tested on its own.

Maximum: the furthest any particle gets from where it began — not the hump's width, not some average height. Displacement: a change of position, in metres, carrying a direction — above the undisturbed line counts positive, below it counts negative. Rest position: the straight, quiet line the spring lies along before anything disturbs it, also called the equilibrium position. Amplitude is measured FROM that rest line to the point of greatest displacement — never from the bottom of a dip to the top of a hump, and never along the spring.

Put numbers on it. If the crest of a hump stands 0,4 m above the rest line, the amplitude is 0,4 m. If instead you send a dip whose lowest point sits 0,25 m below the rest line, the displacement there is minus 0,25 m — but the amplitude is quoted as its size, 0,25 m. Keep the sign vocabulary warm, though: up positive, down negative is the language the next two sections are written in.

What is amplitude physically? It is the medium's receipt for energy. A harder jerk feeds more energy into the spring, and that extra energy appears as a greater maximum displacement. What amplitude does NOT buy is speed. A 0,4 m giant and a 0,05 m ripple launched together on the same spring travel shoulder to shoulder, because speed belongs to the medium. At 2 m·s⁻¹, a pulse asked to cover 5 m needs time equal to distance divided by speed: 5 divided by 2, which is 2,5 s — tall pulse or tiny one, the answer is the same.

One last observation for sketches: on a real spring, friction bleeds energy out of a travelling pulse, so its amplitude gently shrinks along the way — while its speed does not change at all. Energy fades; the medium's speed limit stands.

The questions for this section are with you now — the full definition of amplitude, the line it is measured from, and what it does and does not control.

## Subtopic: The Principle of Superposition

Launch a pulse from each end of the spring at the same moment, heading toward each other. Somewhere near the middle they arrive at the same coils at the same time. What does the spring do where they overlap? One rule answers every version of this question.

THE PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION: when two or more pulses occupy the same point of a medium at the same time, the displacement of the medium at that point is the ALGEBRAIC SUM of the displacements of the individual pulses. Algebraic sum is the key phrase — it means add the displacements WITH their signs. Upward displacements enter the sum as positives, downward ones as negatives, and the spring simply performs the arithmetic at every point.

Run it with numbers. A crest of plus 0,4 m travels right while a crest of plus 0,2 m travels left. At the instant their peaks line up, the spring at that point stands at plus 0,4 plus 0,2 — a displacement of 0,6 m. Swap the second pulse for a dip of minus 0,1 m and repeat: plus 0,4 minus 0,1 leaves plus 0,3 m — a hump, but a reduced one.

Now the half of the story that separates careful answers from careless ones: once the overlap ends, the pulses PASS THROUGH each other entirely unchanged. Each emerges on the far side wearing its original shape, its original amplitude and its original direction of travel, as though the meeting never happened. Superposition is a momentary sharing of space, not a crash. Pulses never bounce off each other, never absorb each other, never permanently erase each other. Two beams of light crossing in mid-air continue untouched; pulses on a spring behave identically.

So every overlap question is answered in two stages: DURING the overlap, add displacements with their signs; AFTER it, redraw both original pulses continuing on their ways. Answers that bury the pulses after the meeting throw away the second half of the credit.

Pause for this section's questions: the principle word for word, the signed addition, and what becomes of the pulses once they separate.

## Subtopic: Constructive and Destructive Interference

The two celebrity cases of superposition carry names, and their definitions deserve to be memorised precisely.

CONSTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE occurs when the overlapping pulses displace the medium in the SAME direction — both upward, or both downward. The displacements reinforce, and the resultant is LARGER than either pulse alone. Crests of 0,4 m and 0,2 m coincide: for an instant the spring towers 0,6 m high, taller than either parent. Two troughs of minus 0,15 m each coincide: the spring plunges to minus 0,3 m. Notice that the double trough is also constructive — construction means the disturbances built each other up, whichever direction they point.

DESTRUCTIVE INTERFERENCE occurs when the pulses displace the medium in OPPOSITE directions — one up, one down. The displacements cancel, partly or completely, and the resultant is SMALLER than the larger pulse. A crest of 0,4 m meets a trough of minus 0,1 m: the algebraic sum is plus 0,3 m, a shrunken hump. And the showpiece case: a crest of 0,3 m meets a trough of minus 0,3 m — equal sizes, opposite signs. The sum is zero, and for one instant the spring lies COMPLETELY FLAT. Yet both pulses are still present inside that flatness, their energy carried for that moment by the motion of the coils. A breath later the crest surfaces on one side, the trough on the other, both intact and still travelling. The flat spring is the classic trick diagram: flat does not mean gone.

The working method never varies. Step one: at the instant in question, write each pulse's displacement at the overlap point, sign attached. Step two: add them algebraically — that total is the medium's displacement. Step three: if the question moves past the overlap, restore both original pulses, unchanged, on their original headings. Drill it until it is reflex: same signs build, opposite signs cancel, and equal-and-opposite cancels to one momentary flat line.

This part's final questions are in front of you now — name each case, compute each sum, and insist that both pulses survive.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same physics again, but now it lives in your backyard — a washing line, a skipping rope, and a friend with good timing. Same rules, same answers, everyday pictures.

## Subtopic: One Flick of the Washing Line

Saturday afternoon, the line is strung across the yard, and a bored pigeon has parked itself right at the far pole. You snap your end of the line once — one crisp flick — to move it along. Look at what actually travels: a single hump takes off from your hand and runs the whole line, past every peg, all the way to the pole. That running hump is a pulse — one bump with a ticket to the far end.

Now stop watching the hump and watch one peg instead. As the hump arrives underneath it, the peg hops straight up; as the hump moves on, the peg settles straight back down exactly where it hung. The peg never slides along the line toward the pigeon — not once, not ever. Up and down is the peg's whole life; sideways is the hump's. That is why it is called a transverse pulse: the line moves across the direction of travel. And it is also why your towels stay pegged where you left them — the line itself goes nowhere. Only the bump, carrying the energy of your flick, makes the trip.

Here is the part your wrist cannot change: the hump's speed is decided by the LINE — how heavy it is, how tightly it is strung between the poles — not by how hard you snapped. Snap softly or snap like you mean it: on that line, every hump travels at the same pace. If a hump crosses the 10 m yard in 5 s, it is covering 2 metres every second, and so will every hump after it. Want the pulse to move faster? Winch the line tighter. That is the only lever you have.

Some questions on the washing line are with you now — especially about what the peg does, and what the peg never does.

## Subtopic: When Two Bumps Meet in the Middle

Fetch the skipping rope and a friend. On a count of three you both flick upward — your end and their end at the same instant. Two humps leave home, run toward each other, and meet dead centre — and for one moment the rope lifts a single tall hump, taller than anything either of you sent. Your 25 centimetres and their 15 centimetres pile up into one 40-centimetre tower.

That is the entire rule, and it is nothing but addition. Wherever two pulses occupy the same piece of rope at the same moment, the rope adds their heights. Two ups stack into a bigger up. Two downs dig a deeper down. The rope never picks a favourite — it obeys both flicks at once by adding them.

Now keep your eyes on the middle for one more heartbeat, because this is where most answers go wrong. The tower does not stay, and the humps do not wreck each other. Your 25-centimetre hump climbs out the far side, still 25 centimetres, still heading for your friend. Their 15-centimetre hump climbs out your side, still 15 centimetres, still coming at you. They slipped THROUGH each other — like two songs playing across the same room, overlapping in the air and coming out the other side unmixed. Nothing bounced back. Nothing died.

So every pulse meeting is a story in two scenes. Scene one, the overlap: add the heights. Scene two, straight afterwards: both pulses stroll on exactly as they were. Give both scenes every time and the full credit is yours.

Quick check before the last stretch — your questions on the meeting in the middle are up now.

## Subtopic: Adding Heights With Signs

One final round, and it is the sly one. This time you flick UP while, on the same count, your friend flicks DOWN. A hump sets off one way, a dip the other. What happens when up meets down?

They settle it by ordinary adding with signs — the same arithmetic as steps on a staircase. Your hump is plus 30 centimetres: thirty steps up. Their dip is minus 10 centimetres: ten steps down. Where they meet: plus 30 minus 10 leaves plus 20. For an instant the rope shows a modest 20-centimetre bump — smaller than your hump, because the dip ate part of it. That eating-away is destructive interference; the stacking-up from the last section is constructive interference. Same signs build, opposite signs cancel — that single line is the whole dictionary for this topic.

Now tune it to perfection. You flick up 20 centimetres; your friend flicks down 20 centimetres. Equal sizes, opposite directions. At the meeting: plus 20 minus 20 makes zero. For one instant the rope lies dead flat, as if the afternoon never happened. But both pulses are still in there — like two runners crossing on a narrow bridge, hidden from a distant camera for one stride — and a moment later the hump pops out on one side and the dip on the other, both perfect, both still travelling. A flat rope at that instant is not an empty rope. When a question shows you the flat picture and asks where the pulses went, the answer is: nowhere — they are mid-crossing, and they will reappear unchanged.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: same signs build, opposite signs cancel, and after every meeting both pulses carry on. Add with signs, tell both scenes, and this topic is yours for keeps.
