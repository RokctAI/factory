# Part 1 — Expert

One short list of quantities describes every wave this course will ever show you — the swell running into a harbour wall, the note from a bass guitar, the sunlight on your desk. This session assembles that list carefully: what a pulse is, how a train of pulses becomes a wave, how amplitude, wavelength, period and frequency are each defined and measured, and how the wave equation welds any two of them into the third. None of the arithmetic is heavy. The marks sit in the discipline — right symbol, right SI unit, and an unbreakable wall between period and frequency.

## Subtopic: From a Single Pulse to a Travelling Wave

Start with the smallest disturbance physics bothers to name. A PULSE is a single disturbance that moves through a medium. Stretch a long rope across the lawn, grip one end, and snap your wrist once. One hump leaves your hand and runs the length of the rope. That travelling hump is a pulse.

It is a TRANSVERSE pulse because of the direction the medium moves. The particles of the medium move at right angles — perpendicular — to the direction in which the pulse travels. Clip a clothes peg onto one strand of the rope and watch only the peg: it lifts, drops, and settles exactly where it began. The pulse crosses the lawn; the rope does not. What reaches the far end is ENERGY, relayed from particle to particle down the line.

The AMPLITUDE of a pulse is the maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position — its equilibrium position — measured in metres. Snap harder and the amplitude grows, because your hand fed the rope more energy.

When two pulses meet, the PRINCIPLE OF SUPERPOSITION takes over: at every point, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the displacements each pulse would produce alone. Two humps on the same side of the rest line add up — CONSTRUCTIVE interference, one taller hump. A hump meeting a dip subtracts — DESTRUCTIVE interference, and equal opposites leave the rope flat for a single instant. Then both pulses walk on unchanged. An overlap is a meeting, never a collision.

Finally, stop snapping once and snap steadily, again and again. Hump follows hump down the rope in a continuous train of transverse pulses — and that train is a TRANSVERSE WAVE. A wave is a repeated disturbance transferring energy through a medium without transferring the medium itself. Every quantity in the rest of this lesson measures either the size of that disturbance or its timing.

The questions for this section are with you now: give the definitions of pulse, transverse and amplitude exactly as stated, and be clear about what travels down the rope and what stays behind.

## Subtopic: Crest, Trough, Amplitude and Wavelength

Draw a transverse wave and you get a repeating shape climbing above and dipping below a horizontal rest line. Every feature of that picture has a name, and full answers give those names in full.

A CREST is a point of maximum positive displacement — the peak of a hump. A TROUGH is a point of maximum negative displacement — the floor of a dip. The horizontal rest line is the equilibrium position, the level of the undisturbed medium.

AMPLITUDE, symbol A, is the maximum displacement of a particle of the medium from its equilibrium position. SI unit: the metre. Now read the definition again slowly, because the single most common lost mark on this topic comes from measuring a crest DOWN to a trough. Crest to trough is TWO amplitudes. If a crest rises 8 centimetres above the rest line and the trough sinks 8 centimetres below it, the amplitude is 8 centimetres — 0,08 metres — and the crest-to-trough distance of 16 centimetres is double, not the answer.

WAVELENGTH, symbol λ, the Greek letter lambda, is the distance between two successive points that are IN PHASE. SI unit: the metre. Crest to the very next crest is one wavelength; trough to the very next trough is one wavelength; and so is the gap between any point and the next point copying it exactly.

That phrase "in phase" has its own definition, and questions target it directly. Two points are IN PHASE when they have the same displacement AND move in the same direction — both sitting at crests, or both climbing through the rest line together — which puts them a whole number of wavelengths apart. Two points are OUT OF PHASE when they do opposite things — one cresting while the other bottoms out in a trough — which puts them an odd number of half wavelengths apart: half a lambda, one and a half, two and a half.

Keep amplitude and wavelength in separate pockets. Amplitude runs up and down, perpendicular to the travel, and records the wave's energy. Wavelength runs along the travel, and records the wave's spacing.

Take the questions for this section now — expect an amplitude read off a sketch, a wavelength measured between two named points, and a pair of points to be judged in phase or out.

## Subtopic: Period, Frequency and the Wave Equation

Two more quantities capture the wave's TIMING, and each is the other one flipped over.

The PERIOD, symbol T, is the time taken for one complete wave to pass a fixed point — equally, the time for one particle to finish one full oscillation. SI unit: the second.

The FREQUENCY, symbol f, is the number of complete waves passing a fixed point each second. SI unit: the hertz, written Hz, meaning one per second — seconds to the power minus one.

In symbols, f = 1/T; in words, frequency is one divided by the period. Flip it and T = 1/f — the period is one divided by the frequency. If five waves pass every second, each one takes a fifth of a second. One fact, read from both ends.

A first calculation. A cork bobs in a tidal pool while 10 complete waves pass it in 4 seconds. Frequency is the number of waves divided by the time: f = 10 ÷ 4 = 2,5 Hz. Then the period: T = 1/f = 1 ÷ 2,5 = 0,4 s.

Now the equation that ties timing to spacing. In exactly one period, a wave advances exactly one wavelength — that is what the period means. Speed is distance over time, so v = λ ÷ T; and since one over T is the frequency, speed equals frequency multiplied by wavelength. In symbols: v = fλ. In words: v equals f lambda — wave speed equals frequency times wavelength. The units cooperate: hertz times metres gives metres per second, m·s⁻¹.

Worked example. A ripple tank's vibrator beats at 25 Hz and the ripples on the surface are 2 cm apart. Find the speed. Formula: v = fλ. Convert first — 2 cm = 0,02 m. Substitute: v = (25)(0,02). Answer: v = 0,5 m·s⁻¹.

Worked example, run backwards. Swells enter a harbour at 2,4 m·s⁻¹ with a period of 0,8 s. Find the wavelength. Frequency first: f = 1/T = 1 ÷ 0,8 = 1,25 Hz. Then from v = fλ, λ = v ÷ f = 2,4 ÷ 1,25 = 1,92 m. And notice the shape of both solutions — formula, substitution, answer with unit — because each of those written lines earns credit on its own.

Work through this section's questions now, and lay out every calculation in that three-line form.

## Subtopic: The Traps That Cost Marks

Most of the marks lost on this topic disappear into four holes, and every one of them has a cover.

Hole one: unit conversion. Questions hand you wavelengths in centimetres, periods in milliseconds, frequencies in kilohertz — while the formula eats only metres, seconds and hertz. Convert on the substitution line, in writing, not in your head: 2 cm becomes 0,02 m; 40 ms becomes 0,04 s; 2 kHz becomes 2 000 Hz. A flawless method with one centimetre still lodged in it keeps the method credit and forfeits the answer.

Hole two: amplitude measured crest to trough. Recite the definition before touching the ruler: maximum displacement FROM THE REST POSITION. Crest to trough is double the amplitude, every time.

Hole three: swapping period and frequency. They are reciprocals with different units answering opposite questions — the period asks how long one wave takes; the frequency asks how many waves arrive per second. A period of 0,2 s belongs with a frequency of 5 Hz. Write the unit beside every number and the swap self-destructs: a quantity in seconds can never be a frequency.

Hole four — the priciest: the graph-axis error. A displacement-versus-POSITION graph is a snapshot of the whole rope at one instant, so the distance after which it repeats is the WAVELENGTH. A displacement-versus-TIME graph tracks a single particle bobbing, so the interval after which it repeats is the PERIOD. The two curves are visually identical twins. Read the horizontal axis label before you measure anything, every single time.

And one conceptual trap to finish. Staring at v = fλ, many students decide that turning up the frequency turns up the speed. It does not. Wave speed is the medium's property — the rope's tension, the water's depth. Shake the source faster and the speed holds firm while the wavelength shortens in exact proportion. The source chooses the frequency; the medium chooses the speed; the wavelength is whatever makes the equation balance.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: convert before substituting, name what each axis measures, and keep period and frequency on opposite sides of the wall.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same physics, new scenery: a stadium crowd, a skipping rope tied to a fence pole, and a tub of water — the definitions unchanged, the symbols left outside until we need them.

## Subtopic: The Wave in the Stands

Friday night game, stands packed to the top row. One block of supporters leaps up, arms high, and drops back down. The block beside them copies a beat later, then the next block, then the next — and a ripple of standing people sweeps clean around the ground while the noise follows it.

Slow the replay down. Nobody changed seats. Every single supporter went straight UP and came straight DOWN into the same chair. Yet the wave itself travelled SIDEWAYS around the stadium. People moving vertically, wave moving horizontally — crossways to each other, at right angles. That crossways relationship is everything the word "transverse" means, and it is exactly what your rope does when you flick it.

One ring of stand-and-sit running around the ground once is a pulse. The whole crowd repeating it, ring after ring chasing each other around the stands — that is a wave.

And hold on to the deepest part: what actually travelled around the stadium was not a single person. It was the energy of standing up, handed from block to block. Every wave in physics does the same job — it delivers energy across a distance without delivering the material. Your shout reaches a teammate across the field without one puff of air making the trip from your mouth to their ear.

Small check before we move on — some questions on the stadium are coming to you now, especially about what goes around the ground and what only goes up and down.

## Subtopic: Measuring a Wave with a Skipping Rope

Knot a skipping rope to the fence pole and shake the free end steadily. Every quantity in this lesson is now something your own hands control.

How HIGH you shake is the amplitude — measured from the flat line the rope relaxes into when you stop, up to the top of a hump. Gentle shake, small amplitude. Big shake, big amplitude — and your shoulder burns sooner, because amplitude is where your energy goes. Guard one thing only: amplitude is rest line to hump-top, never top to bottom. Top of a hump to bottom of a dip is two amplitudes, and that doubling error spills more marks than anything else here.

How far apart the humps ride is the wavelength — top of one hump to the top of the next. You control that too, through your rhythm. Shake lazily and the humps stretch out long. Double your rhythm and twice as many humps must share the same rope, so each one comes out half as long.

Now post a friend next to the rope and count. How long does one complete hump take to pass her shoe — up, down, and back to flat? That time is the period. How many complete humps pass her shoe in one second? That count is the frequency.

And those two numbers are one fact in two outfits. If five humps pass her shoe every second, then each hump takes one fifth of a second — 0,2 of a second. Flip either number and the other appears. That is the entire content of f equals one over T: frequency is one divided by the period.

Your measuring questions are up now: rest line to hump-top for amplitude, hump-top to hump-top for wavelength, and the flip between how-many-per-second and how-long-each-one-takes.

## Subtopic: One Sum That Does Everything

The topic owns exactly one real calculation, and you can build it yourself from the rope instead of memorising it.

In one second your hand launches a certain number of humps — the frequency. Each hump has a certain length — the wavelength. So how much ground does the front of the wave gain in that second? The number of humps multiplied by the length of each. Distance covered in one second IS speed. That is the whole wave equation: v equals f lambda — wave speed is frequency times wavelength. Counting humps, times how long each hump is. Nothing more.

Test it in the ripple tank. The vibrator taps the water 25 times each second, and each ripple measures 2 centimetres. Centimetres become metres first — 2 centimetres is 0,02 of a metre — then multiply: 25 times 0,02 gives 0,5. The ripples advance at 0,5 metres per second. Sanity check in plain words: twenty-five ripples a second at two centimetres each is fifty centimetres of surface every second. Same sentence, said twice.

Now run it in reverse when the question supplies the speed instead. Harbour swells arrive at 2,4 metres per second, and one full swell takes 0,8 of a second to pass the wall. That period flips into a frequency: one divided by 0,8 is 1,25 — one and a quarter swells each second. Share the 2,4 metres among them: 2,4 divided by 1,25 gives 1,92. Each swell is 1,92 metres long.

Two habits keep all of this safe. Convert every centimetre to metres and every millisecond to seconds BEFORE you multiply anything. And when a graph turns up, say the bottom axis out loud before you measure: distance along the bottom means the repeat you see is a wavelength; time along the bottom means the repeat is a period. Identical pictures, different questions. Hold those two habits and this whole topic sits in your pocket.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: convert first, multiply second, and read the bottom axis before trusting anything you measure off a graph.
