# Part 1 — Expert

The groundwork on sound — compressions, rarefactions, pitch, loudness — is done. This session makes that groundwork earn its keep. We put the wave equation v equals f lambda to work on real sound, combine echo timing with the wave equation inside sonar problems, meet the two animals that invented sonar millions of years before ships did, and end in the hospital, where the very shortness of ultrasound's wavelength is the reason a doctor can watch an unborn baby move.

## Subtopic: The Wave Equation Working for Sound

One equation powers this entire session: v equals f lambda — wave speed equals frequency times wavelength. Applied to sound, each symbol answers to a different master, and knowing who owns what is where the credit sits.

The speed v is owned by the MEDIUM. In air at everyday temperatures, take 340 m·s⁻¹ unless told otherwise; in water, about 1 500 m·s⁻¹; in steel, around 5 000 m·s⁻¹. Warm a gas and the speed climbs, because faster molecules hand the compression on sooner. The frequency f is owned by the SOURCE — a speaker driven at 250 Hz stamps out exactly 250 compressions per second, and no medium can alter that count. The wavelength lambda is owned by nobody: it is the outcome of the negotiation, lambda equals v divided by f.

Run the numbers. A speaker at 250 Hz in air: lambda equals 340 divided by 250, which is 1,36 m — compressions striding across the room spaced a little more than a metre apart.

Now the question that sorts real understanding from memorising: that same 250 Hz sound passes from air into a swimming pool. The frequency CANNOT change — every compression reaching the surface crosses into the water, 250 arriving per second, 250 continuing per second. But the speed leaps to 1 500 m·s⁻¹, so the wavelength must stretch to match: lambda equals 1 500 divided by 250, which is 6 m. Same note, same frequency, a wavelength more than four times longer. When sound changes medium: frequency stays, speed and wavelength change together. That one sentence unlocks a whole family of questions.

And keep the unit drill sharp: kilo means thousand, so 85 kHz enters every equation as 85 000 Hz. Take the questions for this section now — who owns each symbol, and what survives the crossing between media.

## Subtopic: Sonar — Timing Plus the Wave Equation

SONAR — sound navigation and ranging — is the art of asking the sea a question and timing its reply. A ship's transducer fires a sharp pulse of sound straight down; the seabed bounces it back; a detector clocks the return. Solving a sonar problem takes two different tools working side by side: the echo rule for the depth, the wave equation for the wavelength.

The echo rule carries its famous factor: the pulse travels down AND back up, so the depth is d equals v times delta t, divided by 2.

Worked example, start to finish. A survey ship off Gqeberha sends a 25 kHz sonar pulse into seawater, where sound travels at 1 500 m·s⁻¹. The echo returns after 1,2 s. First, the depth. Formula: d equals v times delta t divided by 2. Substitute: d equals 1 500 times 1,2, divided by 2 — that is 1 800 divided by 2. Answer: d equals 900 m. Second, the wavelength of the pulse in the water. Convert: 25 kHz is 25 000 Hz. Formula: lambda equals v divided by f. Substitute: lambda equals 1 500 divided by 25 000. Answer: lambda equals 0,06 m — six centimetres.

Look hard at how DIFFERENT the two calculations are. The depth ran on travel time and demanded the factor of two. The wavelength ran on the wave equation and contains no factor of two at all — halving belongs to round trips, never to wavelengths. Blending the two logics is the classic wreck, so caption each line of working: this line is timing; this line is wave equation.

Why fire at 25 kHz instead of a foghorn's 250 Hz? Because a 250 Hz sound in seawater stretches 6 m between compressions — it would flow straight past a sunken container or a shoal of pilchards and report back nothing. The six-centimetre pulse snaps crisply off anything around its own size. Short wavelengths see small things — hold that sentence; two animals have been living off it for millions of years.

This section's questions are with you now: the two-step sonar problem, each step wearing its own formula and its own logic.

## Subtopic: Bats and Dolphins — Nature's Sonar

Evolution shipped sonar twice before humans got there — once into the night air, once into the sea.

A bat hunting moths after sunset emits pulses of ULTRASOUND — sound above the human ceiling of about 20 000 Hz, with these hunting calls typically running from 20 kHz up toward 100 kHz. Take a bat calling at 85 kHz in air. Wavelength: lambda equals v divided by f — 340 divided by 85 000. Answer: 0,004 m — four millimetres. Moth-sized, and that is the whole strategy. A wave reflects usefully only off objects roughly its own wavelength or larger, so a four-millimetre wave turns a moth into a mirror. If the bat called at a deep audible pitch, its metre-long waves would wrap around the insect like a swell around a jetty pole and come back empty.

The bat also times every echo, running the pure echo rule in its head. Say a moth hangs 2,55 m away. Round trip: 2 times 2,55, which is 5,1 m, at 340 m·s⁻¹. Time equals distance divided by speed: 5,1 divided by 340, which is 0,015 s. Fifteen thousandths of a second — refreshed many times per second as the bat closes in, flying by ear through absolute darkness. This is ECHOLOCATION: locating objects by emitting sound and reading the returning echoes.

Dolphins run the same programme underwater. A dolphin clicking at 50 kHz in seawater at 1 500 m·s⁻¹ makes wavelengths of 1 500 divided by 50 000 — 0,03 m, three centimetres, sharp enough to isolate a single fish and even judge its size from the echo's strength. And notice the bonus the dolphin's medium pays out: at 1 500 m·s⁻¹, its answers return more than four times faster than a bat's would across the same gap.

One physics, three operators — ship, bat, dolphin: send high frequency for short wavelength, time the echo, halve the round trip. Take this section's questions now.

## Subtopic: The Ear's Limits, and Ultrasound in Medicine

The human ear spans roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz — and even within that band it plays favourites. Sensitivity peaks in the middle of the range, around the frequencies of ordinary speech; out toward either extreme, a sound needs a far larger amplitude to seem equally loud. That is precisely why loudness and amplitude are not synonyms: loudness is the ear's verdict, assembled from the amplitude AND the ear's sensitivity at that particular frequency. Age and noise damage erode the top of the range first — which is why a 17 kHz ringtone can drill into a teenager's skull while the adult at the front of the room hears nothing at all.

Above 20 kHz the ear resigns, and medicine takes over. An ultrasound scanner presses a transducer to the skin and fires pulses of very high-frequency sound into the body, where they travel at about 1 500 m·s⁻¹ — acoustically, soft tissue behaves much like water. At every boundary between different tissues — muscle meeting bone, fluid meeting skin — a fraction of the pulse REFLECTS. The machine times each echo, applies the echo rule to convert time into depth, and stacks thousands of those depths into a moving picture.

Feel the timescale it works on. A boundary 0,045 m down returns its echo after time equals 2 times 0,045 divided by 1 500 — that is 0,09 divided by 1 500, which is 0,000 06 s. Sixty millionths of a second, measured routinely, over and over across every sweep.

Why sound rather than X-rays for a pregnancy? Because ultrasound is a mechanical pressure wave, NOT ionising radiation. X-rays and gamma rays pack enough energy per photon to tear electrons off atoms and damage cells; ultrasound cannot do that, so a developing baby can be scanned safely, and as often as the doctors need. Past imaging, focused ultrasound pounds kidney stones into fragments without a single incision, rattles the grime off precision instruments, and hunts hairline cracks inside welded steel — every one of them an echo story at heart.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: the ear's range and its uneven favouritism, the scanner's echo logic, and the safety argument in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same physics, three new scenes: a storm you can measure by counting, a wavelength chosen the way you choose a tool, and a torch that shines sound instead of light.

## Subtopic: Counting the Storm

An afternoon storm stacks up over the ridge. The sky flashes — and you start counting. One... two... three... four... five — and the thunder lands. How far off is the storm?

The flash and the bang were born together, at the same place and the same instant. The light crossed to you practically instantly. The sound slogged over at 340 metres every second. Five seconds of slogging: 340 times 5 is 1 700 metres. The storm sits about one and a seven-tenths kilometres away. Count again after the next flash: a shorter count means it is coming your way. Roughly every three seconds of gap is another kilometre.

Now the contrast this whole lesson turns on. In storm counting you halve NOTHING. The thunder made a ONE-WAY journey — lightning to your ears, no bounce, no return. Halving is reserved for ECHOES, where the sound leaves and comes back to the same place: a shout returning off a cliff, a ping returning to the ship, a squeak returning to the bat. So before every sound sum, ask the one deciding question — did the sound come back to where it started? One-way trip: use the distance whole. Round trip: halve it. Write your answer to that question down first and the factor of two never bites again.

And keep clear whose number the 340 is: the air's, not the storm's. Furious thunder and distant grumble cross the suburb at identical speed — sound cannot hurry by being loud.

Some questions on the storm count are with you now, and the one-way-versus-round-trip decision is the heart of every one.

## Subtopic: Shopping With Four-Millimetre Waves

Why does a bat squeak at a pitch no human will ever hear? Not for drama — it is shopping for small things, and it has chosen its wavelength the way you choose a tool.

The rule of the shop: a wave only bounces properly off things about its own size or bigger. Watch a harbour. Long rolling swells slide past a mooring pole as if the pole were not there — but flick tiny ripples at the same pole and they scatter off it visibly. To the long swell the pole is beneath notice; to the short ripple it is a wall.

Now price the bat's purchase. Calling at 85 000 vibrations per second in air buys waves four millimetres long — moth-sized waves. To a four-millimetre wave, a moth is a billboard: the squeak slaps off it and sprints home. Had the bat called at our comfortable speaking pitch, its waves would run about a metre long and would pour around the moth like the swell around the pole — the moth simply would not exist. So: the smaller the target, the shorter the wave you must send, and since the air fixes the speed, shorter wave means higher frequency. That is the entire reason ultrasound exists in nature.

The dolphin shops the same aisle underwater with three-centimetre waves — fine enough to pick one fish out of the blur. And the timing half is your storm count plus the echo halving: the bat hears its squeak return after fifteen thousandths of a second, works out the round trip was 5,1 metres, halves it, and locks onto a moth 2,55 metres ahead.

Squeak short, catch the return, halve the trip. Your questions on nature's sonar are up now.

## Subtopic: Seeing With Echoes

A torch shows you a dark room by bouncing light off the furniture into your eyes. An ultrasound scanner shows a doctor the inside of a body by bouncing SOUND off its structures into a detector. It is a torch made of sound, shone straight through skin.

The scanner fires tiny clicks pitched far beyond any ear. Inside the body, each time a click crosses from one tissue into another — muscle into bone, fluid into baby — a small echo splashes back out. The machine is the world's best storm counter: it times every echo, and because sound in the body moves at about 1 500 metres a second, an echo arriving after sixty millionths of a second marks a boundary four and a half centimetres deep — out and back, halved, exactly the cliff rule. Stack thousands of timed echoes side by side and a picture assembles itself on the screen: a heart valve flickering open and shut, or a baby stretching months before anyone will hold it.

Why not simply X-ray? Because X-rays are the harsh kind of ray — energetic enough to damage the cells they pass through, the very last thing a growing baby should meet. Ultrasound is only sound: pushes and squashes, mechanical and gentle, with no power to break cells. That is why the scan is safe, and why it can be repeated at every check-up without a second thought.

And once you can see with echoes, you can work with them too. Focus strong ultrasound onto a kidney stone and the pressure waves hammer it into grit the body can flush out — no scalpel involved. Engineers fire the same clicks through welds hunting invisible cracks; jewellers shake the week's dirt off rings in buzzing ultrasonic baths.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: echoes timed, round trips halved, short waves for small things, and sound chosen over X-rays for the kindest of reasons. That is sound, on the job.
