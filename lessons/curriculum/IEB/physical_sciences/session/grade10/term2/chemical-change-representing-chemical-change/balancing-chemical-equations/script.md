# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the skill that turns a description of a reaction into a lawful statement of chemistry: balancing chemical equations. The problem carrying the work is the combustion of propane — C three H eight plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water. One equation, three elements to track, and a strategy that makes the whole craft mechanical. Every stoichiometry calculation from next term to the end of school opens with a balanced equation, so today's method is permanent equipment.

## Subtopic: Why Equations Must Balance — Conservation of Mass

A chemical equation is a sentence: reactants on the left, an arrow meaning "react to form", products on the right. Propane plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water: C3H8 plus O2, arrow, CO2 plus H2O. Every formula in that sentence is correct — and yet, as it stands, the sentence lies.

Run the census. Left of the arrow: three carbons, eight hydrogens, two oxygens. Right of the arrow: one carbon, two hydrogens, three oxygens. Two carbons have evaporated from the record; six hydrogens are unaccounted for; an oxygen has materialised out of thin air. The unbalanced equation quietly claims that atoms were created and destroyed.

That claim breaks a law — the law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed; they are only REARRANGED. Every atom that walks in on the left must walk out on the right, dressed in new molecular clothing but present and countable. The mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants, because they are the very same atoms.

Balancing is the act of correcting the COUNTS until the equation obeys the law. We adjust how MANY of each molecule takes part, never what the molecules are, until every element tallies across the arrow. When the census agrees, the equation stops lying and starts being chemistry.

Keep the physical picture alive, because meaning makes memory: propane — the gas in a camping cylinder — burns in oxygen; its carbons depart as carbon dioxide, its hydrogens depart as water vapour. The flame rearranges every atom of the fuel into those two products, and our task is to bookkeep that rearrangement honestly.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now: the law stated precisely, and the census of the unbalanced equation used to point at exactly where it lies.

## Subtopic: The One Commandment — Coefficients, Never Subscripts

Before touching a single atom, learn the commandment that rules the whole craft: you may change only the COEFFICIENTS — the large numbers written in FRONT of formulas. You may NEVER edit the subscripts inside a formula.

Why so absolute? Because the two kinds of number do entirely different jobs. A subscript is part of a substance's IDENTITY: H2O — two hydrogens, one oxygen — is water. Edit that subscript to H2O2 and you have not adjusted a count; you have manufactured a different chemical — hydrogen peroxide, the wound antiseptic, definitely not for drinking. An equation "balanced" by editing subscripts describes a reaction that never happened anywhere in the universe.

A coefficient, by contrast, is a MULTIPLIER of the entire formula: three CO2 means three complete carbon-dioxide molecules — three carbons, six oxygens. Coefficients scale amounts without touching identities. That is precisely the freedom balancing needs, and the only freedom it is granted.

Drill the reading of coefficient against subscript, because every audit depends on it. Four H2O: four molecules, each holding two hydrogens and one oxygen — eight hydrogens and four oxygens in total. The coefficient multiplies EVERY atom in the formula behind it. Five O2: ten oxygen atoms. And a formula with no written coefficient carries an invisible one.

One strategic rule completes the toolkit: in combustion equations, balance in the order carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen — C, H, O. The logic: carbon and hydrogen each land in exactly ONE product, so their coefficients settle instantly and stay settled. Oxygen has fingers in BOTH products and also stands alone as O2 on the left — its count depends on everyone else's. Balance the dependent element last, and the lone O2 lets you set it with one coefficient that disturbs nothing already done. Balance oxygen first and you enter the classic tail-chase: every later fix breaks it again.

This section's questions are in front of you now: coefficient distinguished from subscript, a coefficient multiplied across a whole formula, and the reason oxygen waits until last.

## Subtopic: Balancing the Propane Equation — C, then H, then O

Now the equation itself, worked with the discipline of the order.

Carbon first. Left: three carbons, locked inside C3H8. Right: each CO2 carries exactly one. Three carbons demand three CO2 — coefficient three. The line now reads: C3H8 plus O2 gives three CO2 plus H2O.

Hydrogen second. Left: eight hydrogens. Right: each water molecule carries two. Eight divided by two — four waters. Coefficient four: C3H8 plus O2 gives three CO2 plus four H2O.

Oxygen last — and count the RIGHT side first, because that is where the demand sits. Three CO2 hold three times two — six oxygens. Four H2O hold four times one — four more. Total demand: ten oxygen atoms. The left delivers oxygen in pairs — O2. Ten atoms at two per molecule: five O2. Coefficient five.

The balanced equation: C3H8 plus five O2 gives three CO2 plus four H2O.

Now the audit — never optional, always the closing move. Carbon: left three; right, three CO2 — three. Match. Hydrogen: left eight; right, four waters at two each — eight. Match. Oxygen: left, five O2 — ten; right, six in the carbon dioxides plus four in the waters — ten. Match. Three elements, three matches: the equation obeys conservation of mass, and you have PROVEN it rather than assumed it.

Notice how the order carried the load. Carbon settled in one move because it lived in one product; hydrogen the same; and oxygen — the element with a share in everything — waited until the rest was frozen and then balanced in a single clean step. The order is the difference between a solution that runs straight through and a loop that eats your time.

Stop for this section's questions now — each coefficient justified in turn, and the closing audit run element by element until all three counts match.

## Subtopic: Fractions, Final Form, and the Road to Stoichiometry

Sometimes the oxygen step lands on an odd number — and hands you a fraction. Balance butane, the lighter fuel: C4H10 plus O2 gives CO2 plus H2O. Carbon: four CO2. Hydrogen: ten over two — five H2O. Oxygen demand: eight in the carbon dioxides plus five in the waters — thirteen atoms — which is thirteen halves of O2. The equation balances with the coefficient thirteen over two — but Grade 10 convention demands whole numbers. The cure: double EVERY coefficient. Two C4H10 plus thirteen O2 gives eight CO2 plus ten H2O. Audit it: carbons eight and eight; hydrogens twenty and twenty; oxygens twenty-six and twenty-six. Whole numbers, lawful, done.

That closing idea — the smallest set — is the final-form rule. Coefficients must be the smallest whole numbers that balance. Consider four H2 plus two O2 gives four H2O: it audits perfectly, yet every coefficient divides by two, so the honest final line is two H2 plus one O2 gives two H2O. Check every final answer by asking: does any single number divide all my coefficients? For the propane line — one, five, three, four — nothing does. Final.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one — the capital crime: balancing by subscript, turning water into peroxide; identity theft, never legal. Exhibit two: forgetting that a coefficient multiplies the WHOLE formula — reading three CO2 as three carbons and two oxygens instead of three and six; audits die on this rock. Exhibit three: oxygen first — the tail-chase; respect C, H, O. Exhibit four: leaving a fraction, or an unreduced set, as the final answer — clear fractions by doubling, then reduce to the smallest set.

The complete method, six steps. One: write correct formulae — from that moment they are locked; balancing never edits them. Two: count every element on both sides. Three: balance the elements that appear in only one place — for combustion, carbon and then hydrogen. Four: balance the everywhere-element — oxygen — last, using the lone O2 to set its count in one move. Five: clear any fraction by doubling everything, and reduce to the smallest whole-number set. Six: audit every element aloud — left count, right count, match — before you call it finished.

Where does this lead? Next term the coefficients take centre stage: one, five, three, four become MOLE RATIOS — one mole of propane consumes five moles of oxygen and delivers three of carbon dioxide and four of water. Stoichiometry, the mass-and-mole arithmetic of Grade 11 and the engine of senior chemistry, reads its ratios straight off the balanced equation — which is why nearly every chemistry calculation you will ever write opens with one. Six steps, one commandment, one audit — practise until the propane equation runs without hesitation, and that opening line is yours for the next three years.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the fraction cleared by doubling, the smallest whole-number set identified, and the coefficients read as the ratios they will become.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same equation from the campsite, where the gas cylinder, the flame and the steam all have to add up.

## Subtopic: Nothing Goes Missing at the Braai

Light the gas braai and it looks like a disappearing act: the cylinder slowly empties, the flame roars, and nothing seems to remain. But nothing has left the universe. Weigh the gas that flowed out of the cylinder, weigh the oxygen the flame pulled from the air, then capture every trace of gas and steam the fire released — the before pile and the after pile weigh exactly the same. Atoms cannot be burned out of existence. They are taken apart and rebuilt, like the bricks of a demolished wall reappearing in a new one.

That is the only idea behind this whole topic. The gas in the cylinder is propane: three carbons and eight hydrogens holding hands. The air supplies oxygen, which always travels in pairs. The flame pulls the propane apart and hands every carbon to oxygen — that pairing is carbon dioxide, riding up in the heat shimmer. Every hydrogen is handed oxygen too — hydrogen with oxygen is water, leaving as steam you can see on a cold evening. Three carbons in, three carbons out. Eight hydrogens in, eight hydrogens out. Nobody slips away.

So when we write the reaction down and the two sides refuse to tally, chemistry is not wrong — our counting is. Read the first attempt honestly: propane plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water. Left side: three carbons, eight hydrogens, two oxygens. Right side: one carbon, two hydrogens, three oxygens. Two carbons missing, six hydrogens missing, one oxygen invented — like sending three parcels with the courier and having one arrive, along with a parcel nobody ever posted. Impossible.

Balancing is simply fixing the count until both sides tally. The formal name is the law of conservation of mass, but the courier version says it better: everything collected must be delivered.

Questions on this piece are with you right now, so take them slowly: what actually burns, what actually leaves, and where the first count goes wrong.

## Subtopic: Buy More Packets, Don't Repack Them

Here is the one rule that decides whether your balancing is legal — and it is a shopping rule.

At the shop you may buy more packets. Three six-packs of rolls, five cans of cooldrink, four boxes of matches: nobody blinks, because each packet stays exactly what it is. What you may NOT do is open a packet and change what is inside. A six-pack of rolls with two rolls squeezed out of it is not a six-pack any more; it is a different product wearing the wrong label.

Chemistry has exactly those two numbers. The big number in front of a formula is the number of packets — five O2 means five packets of oxygen, each holding two atoms, so ten oxygen atoms in the trolley. Change that number freely; oxygen remains oxygen. The little number tucked inside the formula is the label — H2O is water precisely because it holds two hydrogens and one oxygen. Fiddle with that little number and you have not balanced anything; you have swapped the product for something else. H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide — the brown-bottle antiseptic for scraped knees — not a drink. An equation "repaired" that way describes a reaction that never happened, and it earns you nothing.

So: the big numbers out front are yours. The little numbers inside are locked. That is the entire legal code of the craft — and the formal words are coefficient for the big number and subscript for the small one.

One counting habit rides along: the big number multiplies everything in the packet. Four H2O is four packets, each with two hydrogens and one oxygen — eight hydrogens and four oxygens, never four and four. Three CO2 is three carbons and six oxygens. And a formula with no big number in front carries an invisible one, the way a single roll on the counter is still one roll.

Nail that habit and half the battle in this topic is already won. Your questions for this part are up now: which number you may touch, and what the big number does to every atom behind it.

## Subtopic: Easy Ones First, Then the Awkward One

When a family packs the car for a trip, the fixed things go in first — the suitcases, the cooler box — and the awkward soft bags squeeze into whatever space is left. Balancing works the same way, and for a burning fuel the order is carbon, then hydrogen, then oxygen.

Why that order? Carbon has exactly one destination — the carbon dioxide. Hydrogen has exactly one destination — the water. Each settles in a single move and never needs revisiting. Oxygen is the awkward soft bag: it ends up inside the carbon dioxide AND inside the water, and it arrives on its own side in pairs. Pack oxygen first and every later change squashes it — that is the loop that steals twenty minutes on a bad day.

So, propane. Three carbons on the left, one in each carbon dioxide: three carbon dioxides. Eight hydrogens on the left, two in each water: eight divided by two is four waters. Now total the oxygen the products demand: three carbon dioxides at two each is six, four waters at one each is four — ten oxygen atoms altogether. Oxygen comes in pairs, and ten divided by two is five pairs. Five oxygen packets.

The finished line: one propane plus five oxygen gives three carbon dioxide plus four water. Say it out loud as a sentence and it loses all its teeth.

Then the closing move, which is never optional — count both sides aloud. Carbons: three and three. Hydrogens: eight and eight. Oxygens: ten and ten. Three matches, and now you know instead of hope.

Two last snags. Sometimes the oxygen demand comes out odd — butane from a lighter needs thirteen oxygen atoms, which is six and a half pairs. You cannot buy half a packet, so double the whole shopping list: two butane, thirteen oxygen, eight carbon dioxide, ten water. And once everything is whole, check that no single number divides every coefficient — if the whole list can be halved, halve it, because the final answer must be the smallest set that works.

That is the whole trade: fix the easy ones, leave the awkward one for last, clear any halves, count out loud. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the order defended, the propane numbers rebuilt from scratch, and a fraction cleared the honest way.
