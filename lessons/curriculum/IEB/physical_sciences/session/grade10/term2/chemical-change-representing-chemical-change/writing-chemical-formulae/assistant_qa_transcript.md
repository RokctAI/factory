**Thandi:** Before you pack up — the questions this topic always leaves behind. First: the crossover method feels like magic. If it always works, why did we bother learning the neutrality law at all?

**Tutor:** Because the crossover is a servant of the law, and a servant without a master makes famous mistakes. What the crossover really does is manufacture equal totals mechanically: crossing calcium's two under the chlorine guarantees two one-minus charges against one two-plus — zero by construction. As long as the situation is simple, the machine runs perfectly. But watch it meet calcium oxide: it prints Ca2O2, which is wrong as a final answer, and the machine has no idea why. Only the law tells you: two-plus against two-minus already cancels one-for-one, so the honest ratio is one-to-one, CaO. The machine also cannot audit itself. When you finish any formula, the proof of correctness is never "I crossed correctly" — it is the charge count: one calcium at plus two, two chlorides at minus one each, sum zero. That audit is the law speaking, and it catches every slip the machine makes, including the ones you have not imagined yet. So use the crossover for speed, absolutely — but keep the law in charge: simplify what the machine prints, and close every formula with the zero. Speed from the servant, truth from the master.

***

**Thandi:** Next: polyatomic ions. Why does a team of atoms get to carry one charge, and how do we know the team stays together in a reaction?

**Tutor:** Think about what the charge actually belongs to. In sulphate, the sulphur and four oxygens are covalently bonded into one tight cluster, and the cluster as a whole has gained two extra electrons — the two-minus belongs to the team, not to any single atom. The bonds inside the team are strong; the attraction between the team and its partner ion is a separate, weaker matter. So in most reactions the partner changes and the team survives intact — sulphate can leave one compound and appear unchanged in another, the way a five-a-side team changes tournaments without changing players. That is why your data sheet can list sulphate once, with one charge, and why the bracket rule matters so much: when a formula needs two of the team, the bracket keeps the team whole while the subscript multiplies it. Practise reading the common ones as single passengers — hydroxide O H minus, nitrate N O three minus, carbonate C O three two-minus, ammonium N H four plus — and always check the ending: an "-ide" on a non-metal usually means a lone atom, while an "-ate" means oxygens are aboard. Sulphide is plain S two-minus; sulphate is the team. Confuse those two and every formula downstream inherits the error.

***

**Thandi:** Then the Roman numerals. Why do only some metals need them, and what should we do when a name has no numeral at all?

**Tutor:** The numeral exists to resolve ambiguity, and only some metals are ambiguous. Group one and group two metals are creatures of habit: potassium always hands out exactly one electron, calcium always two — their charges are fixed by their group, so the name needs no reminder and never carries one. The transition metals in the middle block are the flexible ones: iron manages plus two and plus three, copper plus one and plus two, and each option builds a genuinely different compound. For those metals the name must specify, and the Roman numeral is the specification: copper(II) sulphate means Cu two-plus, iron(III) chloride means Fe three-plus. Read the numeral as the charge, never as an atom count — iron(III) oxide contains two irons, not three; the three describes each ion's charge, and the subscripts then follow from neutrality. When a name arrives without a numeral, reason by elimination: if the metal is from group one or two, or is aluminium, the charge is fixed — sodium oxide can only be Na2O. If a transition metal arrives with no numeral in a careful text, convention usually intends its common ion, but in your own writing, always include the numeral for a flexible metal. Precision costs one bracket; ambiguity costs the whole compound.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the five steps feel slow. Once someone is confident, can they skip the audit at the end?

**Tutor:** The audit is the one step confidence should never retire, because it is the only step that checks the others. Understand what kind of errors this topic produces: they are almost never errors of ignorance — they are transcription slips. A two-minus harvested as one-minus from a crowded data sheet. A crossover written diagonally the wrong way in a hurry. A bracket dropped because the hand moved faster than the head. Every one of those produces a formula that LOOKS finished, sitting quietly on the page, wrong. The audit costs perhaps eight seconds: total the positive charge, total the negative, confirm zero; then sweep the atom count once, respecting every bracket — for (NH4)2SO4, two nitrogens, eight hydrogens, one sulphur, four oxygens. Eight seconds against the alternative: a wrong formula flows into a balanced equation, the equation flows into a stoichiometry calculation, and one dropped bracket has now cost you an entire question chain — because in balancing, formulae are locked, and no coefficient can repair a broken one. The strongest chemists I know are not the ones who never slip; they are the ones whose slips never survive to the next line. The audit is how. Keep it forever, and it will keep you.
