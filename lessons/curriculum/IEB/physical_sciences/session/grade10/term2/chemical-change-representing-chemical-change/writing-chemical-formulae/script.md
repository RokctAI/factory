# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the skill of turning a compound's NAME into its exact symbolic recipe: writing chemical formulae. Three compounds carry the work — calcium chloride, ammonium sulphate, and iron three oxide: a simple ionic pair, a polyatomic ion demanding brackets, and a metal whose charge hides in a Roman numeral. Behind all three sits one law and one five-step method. Every chemical equation you will ever balance begins with formulae written correctly, so this hour is foundation work for everything quantitative chemistry does from here to the end of school.

## Subtopic: Charges Must Cancel — the One Law of Formula Writing

A chemical formula is not spelling to memorise — it is arithmetic to perform. Ionic compounds are built from ions: atoms or groups of atoms carrying electric charge. Metals lose electrons and become positive ions — cations. Non-metals gain electrons and become negative ions — anions.

And here is the single law that writes every formula: the compound as a whole must be electrically NEUTRAL. Total positive charge plus total negative charge must come to exactly zero. A formula's entire job is to declare the ratio of ions that lands on that zero.

Where do the charges come from? The periodic table, backed by the ion table on your data sheet. Group one metals — lithium, sodium, potassium — form one-plus ions. Group two — magnesium, calcium — two-plus. Aluminium in group thirteen: three-plus. Across the table: group seventeen, the halogens — fluorine, chlorine, bromine — form one-minus ions. Group sixteen — oxygen, sulphur — two-minus. Group fifteen's nitrogen: three-minus.

Before writing anything new, practise reading a known formula through the law. Potassium bromide: KBr. Potassium is one-plus, bromide one-minus — one of each gives plus one, minus one: zero. Neutral, so the ratio is one-to-one and no subscripts appear. Calcium oxide: Ca two-plus meets O two-minus — one of each cancels: CaO. But aluminium chloride: Al three-plus against Cl one-minus — one chloride cannot absorb three-plus, and neither can two. THREE can. AlCl3. That subscript three is not decoration; it is the arithmetic of neutrality made visible.

That is the entire theory. Everything that follows is technique for running this arithmetic quickly and without error.

Pause here — the questions for this section are with you now: charges harvested correctly from the table, and the neutrality law applied to a known formula until it sums to zero.

## Subtopic: Calcium Chloride and the Crossover Method

First working compound: calcium chloride — the drying agent in those moisture-trap tubs in a damp cupboard. Harvest the ions. Calcium: group two — Ca two-plus. Chloride: chlorine's ion, group seventeen — Cl one-minus.

Reason it from the law: one calcium supplies plus two. Each chloride brings minus one. Reaching zero takes two one-minuses against the two-plus. Formula: CaCl2. Done by pure logic.

Now the speed technique everyone uses: the crossover method. Write the ions side by side with their charges: Ca two-plus, Cl one-minus. Take the SIZE of each charge — ignore the sign — and cross it over diagonally: calcium's two becomes chlorine's subscript; chlorine's one becomes calcium's subscript. Result: Ca one Cl two — written CaCl2, because a subscript of one is never shown.

Why does crossing work? Because it manufactures equal totals automatically: two chlorides at one-minus exactly match one calcium at two-plus. The crossover is the neutrality law running on rails.

But the rails carry two safety rules. Safety rule one: SIMPLIFY after crossing. Cross calcium two-plus with oxide two-minus and the machine prints Ca2O2 — but two-to-two is one-to-one wearing a disguise: the correct formula is CaO. Always reduce subscripts to the smallest whole-number ratio. Safety rule two: never write a subscript of one — CaCl2, never Ca1Cl2.

And the professional's habit: verify by charge audit. CaCl2 holds one calcium at plus two — total plus two — and two chlorides at minus one each — total minus two. Sum: zero. Neutral, proven. Run that silent audit on every formula you ever write; it catches everything.

Stop for this section's questions now — the crossover performed, the result simplified to its smallest ratio, and the charge audit closed at zero.

## Subtopic: Ammonium Sulphate — Polyatomic Ions and the Bracket Rule

Second compound: ammonium sulphate — a nitrogen fertiliser scooped out by the bag at any farming co-op. Neither word names a single element — both name POLYATOMIC ions: charged teams of atoms that travel and react as one unit. Your data sheet lists them; learn today's two.

Ammonium: N H four plus — one nitrogen, four hydrogens, net charge one-plus. Note it well: ammonium is the rare POSITIVE polyatomic ion, a cation with no metal in sight. Sulphate: S O four two-minus — one sulphur, four oxygens, charge two-minus. And keep sulphate apart from sulphide, the simple S two-minus ion — the "-ate" ending signals oxygen aboard.

Balance the charges: ammonium brings plus one; sulphate demands minus two. Two ammoniums per sulphate. The crossover agrees: cross the one and the two — two ammoniums, one sulphate.

Now the rule this compound exists to teach: when a POLYATOMIC ion takes a subscript, wrap the whole ion in BRACKETS first. Two ammonium ions: open bracket, N H four, close bracket, subscript two. The full formula: (NH4)2SO4.

Why are the brackets non-negotiable? Read the disasters without them. NH42SO4 claims forty-two hydrogens hanging off one nitrogen. NH8SO4 — someone doubling the hydrogens by hand — wrecks the ammonium ion's identity and loses the nitrogen count. The bracket says: THIS ENTIRE TEAM, taken twice. It is the only honest way to multiply a team.

Audit the finished formula, atoms and charges both. Atoms: the bracket's subscript two multiplies everything inside — two nitrogens, eight hydrogens — then one sulphur and four oxygens follow. Charges: two ammoniums at plus one give plus two; one sulphate at minus two gives minus two. Zero. Neutral, verified.

Sulphate stays bracket-free here because only ONE sulphate appears — brackets earn their keep only where a subscript must multiply a team. Magnesium hydroxide, with two hydroxide teams, is Mg, open bracket O H close bracket, subscript two — Mg(OH)2 — the same rule pointing the other way.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: the two polyatomic ions named with their charges, the bracket placed where a subscript multiplies a team, and the atom count of the finished formula read correctly.

## Subtopic: Iron(III) Oxide — Roman Numerals and the Complete Method

Third compound: iron three oxide — written iron, Roman numeral three in brackets, oxide. Why the numeral? Because iron is a transition metal, and transition metals are two-faced: iron forms BOTH Fe two-plus and Fe three-plus ions, and both build real compounds. The name must therefore announce which iron attended — and that is exactly the Roman numeral's job. Iron three means Fe three-plus, by instruction. The numeral IS the charge: assembly instructions hidden inside the name.

So: Fe three-plus meets oxide, O two-minus. Neutrality arithmetic: the lowest common multiple of three and two is six. Two irons supply plus six; three oxides supply minus six. Zero. Formula: Fe2O3. The crossover delivers the same — cross the three under the iron's partner, the two under the iron — Fe2O3 — and this time nothing simplifies, because two and three share no factor.

Contrast: iron TWO oxide. Fe two-plus with O two-minus — one each cancels — FeO. Different numeral, different compound entirely: FeO and Fe2O3 differ in colour, in behaviour, in mass. One Roman numeral separates two different substances, which is why it can never be skimmed past.

Charge audit of Fe2O3: two irons at plus three — plus six; three oxides at minus two — minus six. Zero. Verified.

Now assemble the complete method — five steps for any ionic formula. Step one: name the two ions, data sheet and periodic table open; a Roman numeral dictates a transition metal's charge; an "-ate" ending flags a polyatomic ion. Step two: write both ions WITH their charges. Step three: find the neutral ratio — by logic or by crossover. Step four: simplify to the smallest ratio, bracket any polyatomic ion that carries a subscript, and hide every subscript of one. Step five: audit — total plus must cancel total minus, and the atom count must respect every bracket.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: crossing without simplifying — Ca2O2 standing where CaO belongs. Exhibit two: missing brackets — NH42SO4 and its forty-two hydrogens. Exhibit three: the ignored numeral — writing FeO for iron three oxide on the excuse that iron is iron; it is not. Exhibit four: charges misread from the table — sulphate harvested as minus one, say — which poisons every later step; the charges are your raw material, so gather them carefully.

Where does this skill flow? Straight into balancing chemical equations — where the formulae you write are LOCKED, and only coefficients may move. Write a formula wrongly and no amount of balancing can rescue the equation built on it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the Roman numeral read as a charge, iron two oxide and iron three oxide told apart, and the five steps applied in order to a name you have not met before.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three formulae from behind the counter of a corner shop, where the till must come to zero before anyone goes home.

## Subtopic: The Till Must Balance

Closing time at the shop. Whatever went into the drawer must match the slips — over by twenty rand or short by twenty rand, somebody is staying late until the count comes to zero. A chemical formula is that till, and charge is the money.

Some atoms hand out and some take in. The metals are the givers: potassium hands out one, calcium hands out two, aluminium hands out three — and giving leaves them positive, like a person who has lent money and is owed. The non-metals are the takers: bromine takes one, oxygen takes two, nitrogen takes three — and taking leaves them negative, like a person carrying a debt. The columns of the periodic table hand you those amounts for free.

Now the shop arithmetic on calcium chloride. Calcium hands out two. Each chloride can take only one, and one taker cannot absorb what two-plus is handing over — so call a second chloride. Plus two, minus one, minus one: the drawer closes at zero. Two chlorides for every calcium — CaCl2, the little two tucked under the chlorine.

The shortcut does the thinking for you. Put the pair down with their numbers — calcium two, chloride one — and swap the numbers across, diagonally: calcium's two drops under the chlorine, chlorine's one drops under the calcium. One calcium, two chlorines, and nobody writes the little one. That diagonal swap is the crossover method, and all it ever does is make both sides of the till match.

Two habits keep it honest. Cancel down: calcium gives two and oxygen takes two, so the swap prints Ca2O2 — like pricing two sweets for two rand when you mean a sweet a rand. Same deal, clumsy words: CaO. And never a little one — CaCl2, not Ca1Cl2. Then count the drawer out loud: one calcium at plus two, two chlorides at minus one each — zero. That zero is you being right, and you can feel it.

Questions on this piece are coming to you right now, so take your time: givers and takers, the swap, and the drawer counted to zero.

## Subtopic: The Team Rides in One Taxi

Some ions never travel alone. Ammonium is five atoms — one nitrogen with four hydrogens — that get in, ride and get out together, as ONE passenger paying ONE fare of plus one. Sulphate is another: one sulphur with four oxygens, one passenger, fare minus two. One ammonium cannot settle a minus two; two of them can, so the mix is two ammonium groups to one sulphate. The only question left is how to say "two of that group" without wrecking the group.

Think of the rank. When two full groups travel, you do not scribble the head-count into the middle of a group — you load each whole group into a taxi and say "two taxis". The brackets are the taxi: open the bracket, N H four, close the bracket, and the two rides outside; sulphate follows on its own. That is (NH4)2SO4.

Skip the taxi and watch the crash. N H four two S O four reads as forty-two hydrogens on one nitrogen — a passenger chemistry has never met. Doubling only the hydrogens by hand leaves the nitrogen stranded at the rank. The bracket is the one honest way to say "this whole group, twice".

Counting is then easy: the two outside multiplies everything inside — two nitrogens, eight hydrogens — and sulphate rides once, so one sulphur and four oxygens. Money check: plus one twice against minus two once — zero.

Notice sulphate got no bracket: only one sulphate is travelling, and you never say "one taxi-load of one passenger". Need two hydroxide groups, as in magnesium hydroxide — the milky stomach remedy? Then hydroxide gets the taxi: Mg(OH)2. And the name whispers which passenger you have — "-ide" is a lone atom, so sulphide is plain S at minus two, while "-ate" means oxygens are aboard, so sulphate is S O four at minus two. Chemists call the travelling groups polyatomic ions; the brackets are simply their taxi.

Nice work — that was the hardest bracket in grade ten. Your questions for this part are up now: which passenger, how many taxis, and the atom count once the taxi is doubled.

## Subtopic: When the Name Whispers the Charge

Most metals have a fixed price: potassium always hands out one, calcium always two, and you never have to ask. Iron is not like that. Some days iron hands out two, some days three, and both versions build real substances you have touched — so the name tells you which iron showed up, in brackets, in Roman numerals. Iron three means the version handing out three. That numeral is not decoration, and it is not a count of atoms; it is the charge, printed free of charge inside the name.

So build iron three oxide. Iron hands out three, oxygen takes two, and three and two do not match — find the smallest number both fit into: six. Two irons hand out six; three oxygens take six. Zero. Fe2O3 — two irons, three oxygens — and you know this substance well: it is rust, the red-brown crust on a farm gate after a wet season. Change one numeral and you change the substance: iron two oxide is two against two, one each cancels, FeO — black, and different in every way. "Iron is iron" is exactly the thought that writes the wrong compound.

Here is the whole recipe in kitchen language. One: name the two ions, letting the name help — a Roman numeral gives the metal's number, an "-ate" ending means a travelling group from the data sheet. Two: put both down with their numbers. Three: find the mix that comes to zero, by thinking or by swapping across. Four: cancel down, taxi any group that gets multiplied, drop every little one. Five: count the till — pluses against minuses, atoms with the brackets respected.

Four ways people go wrong, all avoidable: crossing and forgetting to cancel; forgetting the taxi and inventing forty-two hydrogens; ignoring the Roman numeral; and misreading a charge off the table, which spoils everything downstream of it.

Look what you can do now — hand you any name in the syllabus and you can build it, bracket it and prove it. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the numeral read as a charge, rust built from scratch, and the five-step recipe run start to finish.
