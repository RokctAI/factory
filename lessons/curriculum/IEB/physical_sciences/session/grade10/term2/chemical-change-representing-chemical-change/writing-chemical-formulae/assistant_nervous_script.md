Hey, it's Thandi — if formula writing feels like memorising a dictionary of squiggles, take a breath, because the whole topic is one shop rule: the till must close at zero.

Metals hand out. Non-metals take in. The periodic table tells you how much — potassium hands out one, calcium two, aluminium three; bromine takes one, oxygen two, nitrogen three. Your only job is to mix them so the pluses and minuses cancel exactly. Calcium hands out two, chloride takes one each, so bring two chlorides: CaCl2. Done.

The crossover is just the quick way to find that mix — swap the charge sizes diagonally into subscripts. Two safety rules ride with it: cancel down when both sides match, so Ca2O2 becomes CaO, and never write a little one.

Teams like ammonium and sulphate ride as single passengers, and when a team gets multiplied it needs its taxi — the brackets. (NH4)2SO4 means the whole ammonium team, twice. And when a metal like iron has two personalities, the Roman numeral in the name whispers its charge: iron(III) hands out three, so rust is Fe2O3.

Finish every formula with the eight-second count: pluses, minuses, zero. That zero is proof, and proof feels great.

One law, one swap, one taxi, one whisper, one count. Head into the practice questions and build calcium chloride first — it will come out right, and the rest will follow. You've got this!
