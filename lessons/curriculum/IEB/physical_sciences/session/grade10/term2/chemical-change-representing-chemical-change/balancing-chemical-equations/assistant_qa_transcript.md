**Thandi:** Before you go — the four questions this topic always raises. First: why is changing a subscript such a serious crime? It feels like such a small edit compared to writing a big five in front.

**Tutor:** Because the small edit changes what thing exists, while the big edit only changes how much of it there is — and those are crimes of completely different orders. Think of the formula as a recipe locked behind glass. H2O is not a suggestion; it is the definition of water — two hydrogens bonded to one oxygen, a specific molecule with specific properties. Nudge the subscript and you have written the definition of a different molecule: H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a bleaching antiseptic with its own boiling point, its own reactions, its own dangers. Your equation might now tally beautifully, but it tallies about a reaction that never occurred — you have described propane burning into peroxide, which no flame on Earth does. The coefficient carries no such risk because it lives outside the glass: five O2 is five copies of the same locked recipe. Amounts are negotiable in chemistry; identities are not. So before balancing anything, write the correct formulae and mentally weld them shut. From that moment the only question you are ever answering is "how many of each?", and the only numbers you may write are the big ones out front.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the audit at the end. If the method was followed correctly, isn't counting both sides again just wasted time?

**Tutor:** The audit is the cheapest insurance in all of chemistry, and it pays out constantly. The method is sound, but the hands executing it are human: a three written where a four was meant, a coefficient multiplied across only half a formula, an oxygen demand added as six plus four but scribbled as eight. Every one of those slips produces a confident-looking final line that is silently wrong — and every one of them is caught by thirty seconds of counting. The audit also has a special power the method lacks: it verifies the whole chain at once. You do not audit steps; you audit the finished equation against the law itself — carbons left and right, hydrogens left and right, oxygens left and right. If all three match, then whatever route you took, the line obeys conservation of mass, and you know it the way you know a sum by checking it backwards. And when a count does not match, the mismatch tells you where to look: a carbon shortfall points at the CO2 coefficient, a hydrogen shortfall at the water. Do it out loud or in writing, every single time, even on lines that look obvious. The students who audit are not slower — they are the ones who never hand in a lying equation.

***

**Thandi:** Then the fraction. Thirteen halves of oxygen for butane feels like the method broke. Why is doubling everything legal, and why does nothing else change?

**Tutor:** The method did not break — it told you the truth in an inconvenient currency, and doubling is just changing currency. Understand what the equation actually asserts: a ratio. One butane to thirteen halves of oxygen to four carbon dioxides to five waters is a perfectly true statement about proportions — for every two butanes, thirteen oxygen pairs. The fraction is awkward only because we like to speak in whole molecules; you cannot hand a flame half an O2. Multiplying EVERY coefficient by two rescales the recipe without altering the ratio, exactly the way doubling a cake recipe changes no proportion of flour to sugar. Two butane, thirteen oxygen, eight carbon dioxide, ten water — same chemistry, whole numbers, and the audit still gives three matches: eight and eight, twenty and twenty, twenty-six and twenty-six. Two warnings attach. First, the multiplication must touch every coefficient — doubling only the oxygen manufactures a new lie. Second, doubling is only for clearing fractions; if you end with coefficients that share a common factor, divide it out, because convention wants the smallest whole-number set. Fractions cleared by scaling everything, final answers reduced to smallest terms — both moves preserve the one thing the equation exists to state: the ratio.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: does the C, H, O order work on everything, or only on burning fuels? What do we do when an equation has no carbon at all?

**Tutor:** The letters are specific to combustion; the principle underneath them is universal, and the principle is what you should carry. Stated generally: balance first the elements that appear in exactly one formula on each side, and save for last any element that appears in several places — especially one that also stands alone as a pure element, because a lone element's coefficient can be set at the very end without disturbing anything. In propane's combustion that ranking happens to spell C, then H, then O. But hand me magnesium burning in oxygen — magnesium plus O2 gives magnesium oxide — and the same principle says: magnesium lives in one place on each side, oxygen stands alone on the left, so settle magnesium's bookkeeping and let the lone O2 close the account: two magnesium plus one O2 gives two magnesium oxide. Audit: two and two, two and two. Or iron with sulfur, hydrogen with nitrogen — scan the equation, rank the elements from most-constrained to least, and work down the ranking. The scan takes ten seconds and replaces memorised letter-orders with understanding: easy ones first, the everywhere-element last. That habit scales to every equation you will meet, long after the propane example has faded — and it is exactly how you will read equations next term, when the coefficients turn into mole ratios and start carrying your calculations.
