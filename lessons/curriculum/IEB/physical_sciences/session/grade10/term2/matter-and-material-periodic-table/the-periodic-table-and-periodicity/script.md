# Part 1 — Expert

The periodic table is not a poster of facts — it is a machine for predicting chemistry. Built on the electron configurations of the previous topic, this session covers how the table is organised and why position reveals electron structure, where the metals, non-metals and metalloids sit, the four great trends that run from lithium to argon, and the family chemistry of groups 1, 2, 17 and 18 — the reactivity stories every question in this topic draws on.

## Subtopic: The Table's Architecture — Atomic Number, Groups and Periods

The modern periodic table arranges the elements in order of increasing ATOMIC NUMBER — proton count, never mass. Read it like a book, left to right, top to bottom: hydrogen 1, helium 2, lithium 3, and onward — no gaps, no exceptions.

The rows and columns are where the meaning lives. A horizontal row is a PERIOD, and the period number equals the number of ENERGY LEVELS the element's electrons occupy. Magnesium's configuration — 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s two — spreads over three levels, so magnesium sits in period 3. A vertical column is a GROUP, and elements sharing a group carry the same number of VALENCE electrons — the outermost ones — which is why they share chemical behaviour. Number the main groups 1 and 2 on the left and 13 to 18 on the right; for groups 13 to 18 the units digit gives the valence count — group 16 elements hold six valence electrons, group 18 hold eight, a full outer level.

Position and electron structure are two spellings of one fact, and you must translate both ways. Given the configuration 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s two, 3p four: three occupied levels — period 3; six valence electrons — group 16; the element is sulphur. Given a position — period 2, group 1 — write the configuration back: two levels, one valence electron: 1s two, 2s one — lithium.

Between the two blocks of main groups runs the long central slab of TRANSITION METALS — iron, copper, zinc and their neighbours — treated at this level as one family of typically hard, dense metals.

Take this section's questions now: order by atomic number, period equals levels, group tracks valence electrons, and the translation run in both directions.

## Subtopic: Metals, Non-Metals and Metalloids

Draw a staircase line starting beside boron and stepping down to the right, and the table splits into its three broad kingdoms.

To the LEFT of the staircase — the great majority of elements — lie the METALS: typically shiny, malleable rather than brittle, ductile enough to draw into wires, and good conductors of heat and electricity, because their structure holds a sea of mobile electrons. Most main-group metals form POSITIVE ions: group 1 metals shed their single valence electron to become plus-one cations; group 2 shed two for plus-two.

To the UPPER RIGHT lie the NON-METALS: dull, brittle when solid, poor conductors — insulators — and inclined to GAIN electrons, forming negative anions: group 17 gains one electron for minus-one; oxygen's family typically gains two for minus-two. Those two habits — metal loses, non-metal gains — are the engine room of the ion table you used for writing formulae.

ALONG the staircase itself sit the METALLOIDS — the in-betweeners, silicon and germanium chief among them — with mixed properties. Electrically they are SEMICONDUCTORS: better conductors than insulators, worse than metals, and controllably so — which is exactly why silicon runs the entire electronics industry, from cellphones to solar panels on farm rooftops.

The syllabus also tags magnetic behaviour: a small club of metals — iron, cobalt, nickel — is strongly magnetic, while most materials are not. And keep the conductor spectrum as a single sentence: metals conduct, non-metals insulate, metalloids semiconduct.

This section's questions are with you now: the three kingdoms located, their properties contrasted, and the ion habits of each side of the staircase.

## Subtopic: Trends Across and Down — Radius, Ionisation Energy, Electronegativity

From lithium to argon, four properties change in step, and one physical idea explains all four: crossing a period adds protons to pull on the SAME outer level, so the nuclear grip on the outer electrons strengthens; descending a group adds a whole NEW level, so the outer electrons sit further out, screened by more inner electrons.

Trend one: ATOMIC RADIUS — the size of the atom. ACROSS a period, radius DECREASES: more protons pulling on the same shells reels them inward, so chlorine is smaller than sodium despite carrying more electrons. DOWN a group, radius INCREASES: each row adds a level, like adding a ring to an onion.

Trend two: IONISATION ENERGY — the energy needed to remove an electron from the atom. Across a period it INCREASES: the stronger grip holds the outer electrons tighter, so stripping one costs more. Down a group it DECREASES: the outer electron sits further away, well screened, and leaves cheaply — which is why the big group 1 metals shed electrons so willingly.

Trend three: ELECTRON AFFINITY — the energy change when an atom GAINS an electron. Across a period, atoms accept incoming electrons ever more readily; the halogens, one electron short of a full level, are the most eager catchers on the table.

Trend four: ELECTRONEGATIVITY — the strength with which a bonded atom pulls SHARED electrons toward itself. It rises across a period and falls down a group, making fluorine, top right, the strongest puller in chemistry, and the huge soft metals of the bottom left the weakest. The noble gases sit this trend out — with full outer levels they rarely bond at all.

One mental picture holds all four: across a period, the nucleus wins; down a group, distance and screening win. Take the questions for this section now, stating each trend in both directions with its reason.

## Subtopic: Group Portraits — Alkali Metals to Noble Gases

Four families, four personalities — each one written entirely by its valence electrons.

GROUP 1, the ALKALI METALS — lithium, sodium, potassium. One lonely valence electron, cheap to lose, so these are the most reactive metals known: soft enough to cut with a table knife, stored under oil because they attack air and water on contact. Dropped into water they fizz, melt into a skating bead, and can burst into flame, leaving an alkaline solution behind — hence the family name. Reactivity INCREASES down the group: potassium's outer electron sits a level further out than sodium's, better screened, and leaves even more easily. All form plus-one ions.

GROUP 2, the ALKALINE EARTH METALS — magnesium, calcium. Two valence electrons, forming plus-two ions. Reactive, but a clear step calmer than group 1 — surrendering two electrons costs more than surrendering one. Magnesium burns with a blinding white flare; calcium compounds build cement, bones and teeth.

GROUP 17, the HALOGENS — fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine. Seven valence electrons, one short of a full level, so they hunt a single extra electron — forming minus-one ions — and rank as the most reactive NON-metals. Here reactivity DECREASES down the group: fluorine, tiny and barely screened, seizes new electrons ferociously; iodine, large and well screened, only mildly. Notice the mirror: metal families grow MORE reactive downward, non-metal families LESS.

GROUP 18, the NOBLE GASES — helium, neon, argon. Full outer levels: nothing to lose, nothing to gain. They are chemically UNREACTIVE — inert for this course — and drift about as single atoms. Their role in chemistry is to be everyone else's ambition: ions form and bonds form precisely to reach a noble-gas arrangement, the idea the entire bonding topic stands on.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: each family's electron count, ion charge, and reactivity direction with its reason.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same table as a street map, a tug-of-war, and four families from your own neighbourhood — same chemistry, plain words first.

## Subtopic: The Street Map of the Elements

Think of the periodic table as the street map of a town where every element has an address — and the address TELLS you about the resident, the way a real address tells you about schools and taxi routes before you ever knock on the door.

The house numbers run in strict order: 1, 2, 3, up the proton count, left to right, row by row. No number is ever skipped. The STREET you live in — the row, called a period — tells you how many floors your house has: row 1 houses have one floor of electrons, row 3 houses have three. Magnesium lives on row 3 because its electrons fill three levels.

The SIDE of town — the column, called a group — tells you who your relatives are, because everyone in a column keeps the same number of electrons on the top floor. And the top floor is all that matters socially: those valence electrons are the only ones other atoms ever meet. One top-floor electron: column 1, restless and generous. Six top-floor electrons: column 16, two short and grasping. Eight: column 18, complete and content.

So a single address answers two questions instantly. Where is sulphur? Row 3 — three floors; column 16 — six electrons upstairs. And the reverse reading works too: an element whose configuration ends 2s one lives on row 2 with one electron upstairs — that is lithium's address. Learn to read addresses in both directions and the table starts working for you instead of staring at you.

Questions on the map are with you now: streets, sides of town, and the two-way address reading.

## Subtopic: The Tug-of-War for Electrons

All four famous trends are one playground game: the nucleus hauling on the outer electrons like a tug-of-war team hauling a rope. Two things decide the contest — how many pullers on the team, and how long the rope.

Walk ACROSS a row, sodium to chlorine. Every step adds a proton to the team, but the outer electrons stay on the same floor — the rope stays the same length. More pullers, same rope: the grip tightens. So going across, atoms get SMALLER — the rope is hauled in; electrons get harder to steal — ionisation energy climbing; and the atom drags harder on shared and incoming electrons — electronegativity and electron affinity rising. One story, four properties.

Now walk DOWN a column, lithium to potassium. Every step adds an entire new floor, so the outer electrons sit further from the nucleus — a longer rope — and the crowded inner floors stand in between, muffling the pull. Longer rope, muffled pull: the grip weakens. Atoms get BIGGER going down; the top-floor electron leaves more easily; the atom's tug on electrons fades away.

Put the two walks together and the corners of the table explain themselves. Top right — fluorine — short rope, big team: the fiercest electron-puller in chemistry. Bottom left — the giant soft metals — long rope, muffled pull: electrons practically fall off on their own. You never memorise four separate trends; you replay one tug-of-war and read off whichever property the question happens to name.

Have a go at this section's questions now — every answer is either "more pull" or "longer rope".

## Subtopic: Four Families You Already Know

Meet the four families whose stories run all of introductory chemistry — personality first.

Column 1, the alkali metals, are the kid with one marble. One valence electron rattling around alone, and they cannot wait to be rid of it. Sodium hits water and fizzes furiously; potassium does the same but wilder. Why wilder? Potassium's marble sits a floor higher — longer rope, weaker grip — so it drops at the slightest bump. The giving family gets MORE reactive as you walk down the column. And once the marble is gone, each becomes a plus-one ion — chemistry's version of travelling light.

Column 2 are the careful givers: two marbles, and parting with two takes real persuading, so magnesium and calcium are reactive but never reckless — steady citizens who end up as plus-two ions, quietly building cement, bones and teeth.

Column 17, the halogens, are the sticker collectors with one empty slot. Seven of eight slots filled, and they will snatch the missing sticker from almost anyone — the most reactive non-metals, finishing as minus-one ions. But collectors lose their appetite going DOWN: fluorine, tiny with a short rope, snatches hardest; iodine, big and muffled, hardly bothers. Givers strengthen downward, collectors weaken downward — hold that mirror, because comparisons between the two families test exactly it.

Column 18, the noble gases, are the family that needs nothing: full album, complete top floor. Helium, neon and argon react with essentially nobody — which is why party balloons and glowing shop signs are filled with them. And their contentment is the secret motor of everything else: every ion formed and every bond built in the bonding topic is some atom's attempt to look like a noble gas.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: givers, careful givers, collectors, and the content ones — each with its ion charge and reactivity direction attached. Read the map, feel the rope, and the table stops being a poster and becomes a prediction machine.
