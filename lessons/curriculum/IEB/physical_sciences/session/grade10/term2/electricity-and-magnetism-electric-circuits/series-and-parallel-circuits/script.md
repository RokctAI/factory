# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the two circuit patterns that every electricity question for the next three years is assembled from: series and parallel. The worked problem runs both patterns on the same components — an 18-volt battery with a 3-ohm resistor and a 6-ohm resistor — first joined in one loop, then joined side by side, and we calculate every current, every potential difference and every total resistance in both. The rules are short. Nearly every error in this topic comes from mixing them up.

## Subtopic: What a Circuit Actually Moves — Charge, Energy and Resistance

Three quantities describe every circuit, and each deserves its definition word for word.

Current, symbol I, is the rate of flow of charge. The formula: I equals Q over delta t — current equals the charge passing a point, divided by the time it takes. One ampere is one coulomb passing a point every second. Conventional current runs from the positive terminal, around the external circuit, back to the negative — even though the electrons themselves drift the other way.

Potential difference, symbol V, is the work done per unit charge. The formula: V equals W over Q — potential difference equals the energy transferred, divided by the charge that carried it. Joules per coulomb, and one joule per coulomb is one volt. Say the meaning aloud: 18 volts across a resistor means every coulomb passing through hands over 18 joules. The emf of a battery is that same quantity at the source, and terminal potential difference is what remains once the battery's internal resistance has taken its share.

Follow the energy, because that is what a circuit is for. Chemical potential energy in the cell becomes electrical energy carried by moving charge; in a resistor it becomes heat and light. The charge is never used up — the same coulombs circulate endlessly — but the energy is spent, and that is exactly why a battery goes flat.

Resistance, symbol R, unit the ohm, is the opposition a conductor offers to charge flow: drifting electrons collide with the vibrating atoms of the metal lattice and surrender energy as heat. A longer conductor resists more, a thicker one resists less, a hotter metal resists more, and copper resists far less than nichrome.

Two instrument rules to weld in place. An ammeter measures current, so the charge must flow through it: connect it in SERIES, and its resistance is very low so it steals almost nothing. A voltmeter measures the energy difference across a component, so it connects in PARALLEL with that component, and its resistance is very high so it diverts almost no current.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two defining formulas stated in words and symbols, the energy path through the circuit traced, and the ammeter and voltmeter each connected correctly.

## Subtopic: Ohm's Law and the Series Circuit — One Path, Shared Current

The relationship linking the three quantities is Ohm's law: V equals I R — the potential difference across a conductor equals the current through it multiplied by its resistance. Rearranged: I equals V over R, and R equals V over I. At constant temperature the current through a metal conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it — double the volts, double the amps.

Now build the series circuit, and picture it as I describe it. Start at the positive terminal of an 18-volt battery. One wire leaves it and runs to the first resistor, 3 ohms. A single wire leaves that resistor and enters the second, 6 ohms. One wire returns to the negative terminal. One unbroken loop, no junctions anywhere — that is what "series" means.

Two rules follow from having one path only. Rule one: the current is the SAME everywhere. Charge cannot pile up or leak away, so every coulomb passes through the 3-ohm resistor and then the 6-ohm resistor — not shared, not consumed, identical at every point. Rule two: the potential differences ADD to the emf, and the total resistance is the plain sum — R series equals R one plus R two, and so on.

Work the numbers. Total resistance: R series equals 3 plus 6, which is 9 ohms. Total current: I equals V over R, which is 18 volts over 9 ohms, giving 2 amperes — flowing through both resistors.

Potential difference across the 3-ohm resistor: V one equals I times R one, which is 2 amperes times 3 ohms, giving 6 volts. Across the 6-ohm resistor: V two equals 2 amperes times 6 ohms, giving 12 volts. Check: 6 volts plus 12 volts equals 18 volts — the battery's full emf accounted for. A series circuit is a potential-difference divider, and it divides in proportion to resistance: twice the ohms, twice the volts.

Two consequences. Add another resistor in series and the total resistance rises, so the current everywhere falls and every component dims. Break the loop at any single point and the whole circuit dies, because there is no second path.

Stop for this section's questions now: Ohm's law stated and rearranged, the two series rules justified, and the 6-volt and 12-volt split reproduced from the resistances alone.

## Subtopic: The Parallel Circuit — Shared Potential Difference, Divided Current

Rewire the same two resistors. From the positive terminal of the same 18-volt battery a wire runs to a junction, where the path splits: one branch carries the 3-ohm resistor, the other the 6-ohm resistor. The branches rejoin at a second junction, and a single wire returns to the negative terminal. Two separate paths between the same pair of junctions — that is what "parallel" means.

Rule one: the potential difference is the SAME across every branch. Both branches begin at one junction and end at the other, so both experience the full 18 volts. This is the rule that gets swapped most often — in parallel, volts are equal on every branch, never divided up. Rule two: the branch currents ADD to give the total leaving the battery, because a junction splits the flow and loses nothing. Rule three: the total resistance obeys the reciprocal formula — one over R parallel equals one over R one plus one over R two.

Work the numbers. One over R parallel equals one over 3 plus one over 6. Put both over six: two over six plus one over six equals three over six — one half. Now invert — the step everyone forgets — R parallel equals 2 ohms.

Look hard at that answer: 2 ohms is SMALLER than either 3 ohms or 6 ohms. Not an error — the defining feature of parallel. A second path gives the charge somewhere else to go, so the combination opposes flow less than either resistor alone. Your instant sanity check: the parallel total is always less than the smallest branch.

Total current: I equals V over R, which is 18 volts over 2 ohms, giving 9 amperes. Through the 3-ohm branch: 18 volts over 3 ohms, giving 6 amperes. Through the 6-ohm branch: 18 volts over 6 ohms, giving 3 amperes. Check the addition: 6 plus 3 equals 9 amperes — exactly the total. A parallel circuit is a current divider, and it divides inversely with resistance: the smaller resistance takes the larger current.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: the reciprocal formula applied and then inverted, the branch currents calculated and summed, and the reason a parallel total falls below the smallest resistor explained.

## Subtopic: Series Against Parallel — the Rules, the Traps and the Wiring in Your Walls

Set the two results side by side, because the contrast is the whole topic. Same battery, same two resistors. In series: total resistance 9 ohms, current 2 amperes everywhere, potential differences 6 volts and 12 volts. In parallel: total resistance 2 ohms, 18 volts across both branches, currents 6 amperes and 3 amperes, total 9 amperes. Identical components, four and a half times the current — because the connection pattern, not the components, sets the total resistance.

Compress the rules into a pair you can recite. SERIES: same current everywhere; potential differences add to the emf; resistances add; the total is larger than any one resistor; one break kills everything. PARALLEL: same potential difference on every branch; branch currents add to the total; reciprocals add; the total is smaller than the smallest branch; one break leaves the others working.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: writing the reciprocal down as the answer — one half is one over the resistance; invert it to get 2 ohms. Exhibit two: swapping the "same" quantity — equal currents claimed in parallel branches, or equal potential differences across series resistors. Fix it at the source: same PATH means same CURRENT; same PAIR OF JUNCTIONS means same VOLTS. Exhibit three: unit slips — milliamperes fed into a formula expecting amperes; convert before you substitute. Exhibit four: an answer with no unit, which is not an answer at all.

Then the reason this matters outside the classroom. The plugs and lights of a South African home are wired in PARALLEL: every appliance receives the full 230 volts regardless of what else is running, and switching off the kettle does not kill the lights, because each sits on its own branch. The cost is the third rule — every extra appliance lowers the total resistance and raises the total current, which is why an overloaded multiplug turns hot and why the breaker in the distribution board trips.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the two rule sets recited without mixing them, the reciprocal inversion performed correctly, and household parallel wiring justified in the language of current and potential difference.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two circuits from the road — one route through town, then two — because current behaves remarkably like traffic, and every rule you just met is something a taxi driver already knows.

## Subtopic: One Road Through Town

Think of charge as taxis and the battery as the rank where they load up.

The battery does one job: it hands every taxi a full load of energy. Eighteen volts means every coulomb leaves carrying eighteen joules — that is all "volts" ever means. The taxis drive the route, drop energy wherever the road gets rough, and return empty for another load. The taxis never wear out; the energy does. That is why a battery goes flat yet never runs out of charge.

Now the single-road circuit. One road leaves the rank, crosses a rough patch, then a rougher patch, then returns. No turn-offs. Because there is only one road, every taxi must cross both patches — so the flow of traffic is identical at every point. Count taxis at the start, in the middle, at the end: the same number per minute. Nothing is consumed, because taxis are not fuel.

What DOES get shared out is the energy. The first patch shakes some of it out of every taxi; the rougher patch shakes out more — the rougher the patch, the bigger its slice of the eighteen.

Put numbers on it. The patches are 3 ohms and 6 ohms — ohms simply measure roughness. On one road the roughness adds: 3 plus 6 is 9 ohms to fight through. Eighteen volts pushing against nine ohms settles the traffic at two amps. The energy split then follows the roughness: 6 volts dropped at the 3-ohm patch, 12 volts at the 6-ohm patch, and 6 plus 12 hands back the full 18. Double the roughness, double the slice.

Two things fall out of "one road only". Add another rough patch and the whole route slows — everything dims together. And close the road anywhere, like one blown globe in an old string of fairy lights, and every taxi stops. The proper name for one-road wiring is a series circuit.

Quick check before we carry on — the questions on this piece are coming to you right now: same traffic everywhere, shared-out energy, and the roughness simply added up.

## Subtopic: Two Roads, Two Queues

Now open a second road, and everything flips.

Same rank, same eighteen joules per taxi. But this time the road splits at a junction: one route runs through the 3-ohm patch, the other through the 6-ohm patch, and the two rejoin further on. Both routes start at the same corner and finish at the same corner.

Here is the flip. Every taxi, whichever route it takes, sets out with eighteen joules and arrives home empty — so BOTH routes drop the full eighteen volts. Not nine each. Eighteen each. Two paths between the same two corners share the same energy drop, and that is the rule people swap most: on separate branches, everybody gets the full voltage.

What splits instead is the traffic. Eighteen volts through the 3-ohm route gives 18 divided by 3, which is 6 amps. Eighteen volts through the 6-ohm route gives 18 divided by 6, which is 3 amps. Add them where the roads rejoin: 6 plus 3 is 9 amps leaving the rank altogether. Less roughness, more taxis — the easy road is always the busy one.

And now the fact that surprises everyone. What is the roughness of the WHOLE two-road system? Not nine. Not even three. Opening a second road makes the town easier to cross than either road alone — so the combined roughness must be less than the smaller one. The sum runs on the upside-down numbers: a third plus a sixth is a half. But a half is upside down — flip it back and you get 2 ohms. Check it: 18 volts over 2 ohms gives 9 amps, the same total we counted at the junction.

Write that flip on your hand, because it is the step that dies most often in this topic: add the upside-down numbers, then turn the answer the right way up again. If your final resistance came out bigger than the smallest road, you forgot to flip. Two-road wiring is called a parallel circuit.

Your questions for this part are up now: everyone gets the full eighteen, the traffic splits with the easy road busier, and the upside-down sum turned back the right way up.

## Subtopic: Why Your House Is Wired the Clever Way

Last piece — and it is standing in your kitchen.

Look at what changed when the second road opened. Same battery, same two rough patches, and yet: one road gave 9 ohms and 2 amps; two roads gave 2 ohms and 9 amps. Four and a half times the traffic, purely from how things were joined.

So which does a house use? Imagine your home wired as one road: kettle, TV, lights and fridge on a single loop. Everything shares out the voltage, so nothing runs at full strength. Switch off the kettle and you have broken the road — the lights die too. Blow one globe and the whole house goes dark. Nobody would live like that.

Your house is wired the two-road way — parallel. Every plug point and every light sits on its own branch between the same two corners, so each receives the full supply voltage whether the rest of the house is on or off. Switch the kettle off and the lights carry on, because breaking one branch leaves the others untouched.

There is a price, and it is the third rule. Every appliance you switch on is another road opened, so the total roughness drops and the total current climbs. That is why cramming heaters and kettles into one multiplug makes the cord dangerously hot, and why the trip switch in the DB board snaps off — it is counting amps, and it has had enough. And one-road wiring still keeps its place: the wall switch sits in series with its light, because there you WANT one break to stop everything.

So carry two short sentences out of this lesson. One road: same traffic everywhere, energy shared out, roughness adds. Two roads: same energy drop on every branch, traffic splits, roughness falls below the smallest — after you flip the fraction back. Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the two rule sets kept apart, the flip done properly, and your own kitchen explained in volts and amps.
