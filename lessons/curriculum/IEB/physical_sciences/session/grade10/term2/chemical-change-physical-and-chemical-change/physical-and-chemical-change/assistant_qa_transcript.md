**Thandi:** Before you move on — the questions that come up again and again with this topic. First one: a fizzy cold drink bubbles, and a headache tablet in water bubbles. Why is one physical and the other chemical?

**Tutor:** Because the two gases have completely different histories, and history is what the deciding question interrogates. The cold drink was bottled under pressure with carbon dioxide already dissolved in it — the gas existed before you ever touched the cap. Open the bottle, the pressure drops, and the dissolved gas simply comes out of hiding as bubbles, the same way sugar comes back out of tea when the water evaporates. Nothing new was manufactured; a mixture separated. That is physical, and here is the give-away: seal the bottle again, chill it, even pressurise it, and the gas dissolves right back in — a round trip. The tablet is a different story entirely. Solid ingredients in the tablet react with each other the moment water frees them to meet, and the product of that reaction is carbon dioxide that never existed in the tablet as a gas. You cannot coax the fizz back into the tablet, and evaporating the water afterwards does not return the original tablet — it returns the leftover products. Same-looking bubbles, opposite verdicts. So never classify by what your eyes see; classify by the inventory: did the gas exist beforehand, or was it made on the spot? Existing gas escaping — physical. New gas manufactured — chemical.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the steel wool experiment breaks people's brains. Everyone expects burning to lose mass, but burnt steel wool weighs MORE. Where does the extra mass come from?

**Tutor:** From the air — and the surprise only exists because the air was left off the books. Burning steel wool is iron atoms combining with oxygen molecules to build iron oxide. Every particle of that oxide contains iron you started with PLUS oxygen captured from the atmosphere, so of course the solid on the balance gets heavier: it has been annexing invisible mass the whole time. Wood pulls the same trick in the opposite direction — its products are mostly gases, carbon dioxide and water vapour, which drift away and take their mass with them, so the ash left behind weighs less and the fire seems to destroy matter. Neither fire is cheating. Do the accounting honestly, in a sealed container, weighing every reactant including the oxygen and every product including the gases, and the totals match to the limit of your balance — every time, for every reaction ever measured. That is the law of conservation of mass: atoms are never created or destroyed in a chemical change, only regrouped. And notice what the two illusions teach jointly: an open system hides mass in whatever escapes or arrives unseen. The moment you close the system, both illusions vanish and the one law stands. Hold onto that instinct — it becomes the entire logic of balancing equations, where every atom on the left must be found again on the right.

**Thandi:** Then the sugar question. Dissolving sugar is physical, fine. But heating sugar in a pan — it melts, then it browns. Is that one change or two?

**Tutor:** Two changes, back to back, and the pan is the cleanest place in any kitchen to watch the border being crossed. Stage one: gentle heat melts the white crystals into a clear, colourless liquid. That liquid is still sugar — cool it and it solidifies back, sweet as before, molecules intact. Melting is physical, exactly like the chocolate in your pocket. But keep the heat coming and stage two begins: the liquid turns gold, then amber, then dark brown, and a sharp caramel smell fills the kitchen while wisps of steam escape. Those are the fingerprints of chemistry. The sugar molecules themselves are now breaking apart and regrouping into dozens of new substances — the browns and aromas of caramel, water vapour leaving, and, if you push on stubbornly, black carbon that no cooling will ever whiten. Notice the evidence kit lighting up: permanent colour change, new smell, gas released. And notice the reversibility test agreeing: cooled caramel is not sugar; it is a new material with new properties. So one saucepan holds the whole topic — the same substance passing first through a physical change and then through a chemical one, with the border crossed at the moment new substances started to form, not at the moment heating began. Heat was present in both stages; only stage two made anything new.

**Thandi:** Last one: is there a quick way to know when the reversibility test is safe to use? People hear that chemical changes are hard to reverse, and then they meet one that seems reversible and panic.

**Tutor:** Treat reversibility as a helper, never as the definition — the definition is always the new-substance question. The helper works beautifully in one direction: if SIMPLE means bring the original back — cooling, evaporating, filtering, sieving, dissolving and re-crystallising — then no new substance can have formed, and the change was physical. Melted chocolate sets; sunned Epsom-salt water returns its crystals; the fizzy drink re-absorbs its gas under pressure. Those recoveries need no chemistry, so none happened. The panic cases sit on the other side: changes that ARE chemical yet can be undone — but look closely at what the undoing costs. Hydrogen and oxygen from electrolysis can be turned back into water, but only by a whole new chemical reaction, a combustion, not by any amount of cooling or filtering. Rust can be smelted back to iron in a furnace — again a fresh chemical reaction at brutal temperatures. So the honest phrasing is the one from the lesson: chemical changes are hard or impossible to reverse BY SIMPLE MEANS. If reversal needs another reaction, the change was chemical, and its difficulty confirms rather than contradicts the classification. Keep the order of operations: first ask whether a new substance formed — that settles everything; then use easy recoverability as supporting evidence for physical. Used in that order, the helper never betrays you.
