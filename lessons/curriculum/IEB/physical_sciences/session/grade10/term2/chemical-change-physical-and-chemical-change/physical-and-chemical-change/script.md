# Part 1 — Expert

A chocolate bar melts in your pocket, and it is still chocolate. A match is struck, and it is never a match again. Between those two sentences runs the border between the physics and the chemistry of matter, and this session walks that border from end to end: the definitions of physical and chemical change, the particle-level story behind each, the observable evidence that chemistry has happened, the conservation laws that hold on both sides — and the tricky cases designed to catch an answer that was memorised instead of understood.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Change — the Deciding Question

Matter never sits still. It melts, burns, dissolves, rusts, boils, bakes. Chemistry sorts every one of those events with a single deciding question: IS A NEW SUBSTANCE FORMED?

A PHYSICAL CHANGE is a change in which NO new substance forms. State, shape or size may change — chocolate softening in the sun, a chalk stick ground to dust, sugar stirred into warm milk — but the particles themselves come through untouched. Melted chocolate is still chocolate; chalk dust answers every test that the stick did; the sugar is still sugar, and gently evaporating the milk would hand it back. Because the particles survive, a physical change is usually EASY TO REVERSE: cool the chocolate and it sets again.

A CHEMICAL CHANGE — a chemical reaction — is a change in which one or more NEW substances form, with properties DIFFERENT from the originals. Strike the match and the head becomes smoke, gases and a blackened stump — none of which is match. Leave steel wool in a damp corner and it becomes crumbly brown iron oxide, nothing like the springy grey metal. In a chemical change, bonds between atoms BREAK and NEW bonds FORM; the atoms regroup into different molecules. Because the original substance no longer exists, a chemical change is usually DIFFICULT OR IMPOSSIBLE to reverse by simple means — no amount of cooling un-strikes a match.

So the working method for any change is a before-and-after inventory: name the substance you started with, name what you ended with, compare. Same substance — physical. New substance — chemical. Every question in this topic is that comparison in a different costume. The questions for this section are with you now — the two definitions, and the deciding question applied cleanly.

## Subtopic: Physical Change Up Close — Same Particles, New Arrangement

Put a physical change under the particle microscope and one fact organises everything: the PARTICLES THEMSELVES DO NOT CHANGE — only their arrangement, their spacing or their energy changes.

Changes of STATE lead the list, straight out of the kinetic molecular theory. Melting, evaporation, condensation, freezing, sublimation: in each one the molecule stays exactly itself. Washing drying on the line does not destroy the water in the fabric; the intact H two O molecules simply gain enough energy to drift off as vapour, and the very same kind of molecules bead up as dew the next cold morning. Vapour, liquid and ice are one substance wearing three outfits, which is why state changes reverse so cleanly.

DISSOLVING is the second family. Stir Epsom salts into a basin of water and the crystals seem to vanish — but every ion and every water molecule survives, merely spread out and mingled. The proof is recoverability: leave the basin in the sun, let the water evaporate, and chemically identical crystals grow back. Nothing new was ever made; the ingredients were only shuffled together very finely.

Changes of SHAPE and SIZE are the third: tearing, cutting, grating, grinding, bending. Grate a block of cheese and the pile looks nothing like the block — yet shred and block answer every chemical test identically, because the particles are the same.

Two footnotes for precision. First, MIXING is generally physical: stir clean sand into salt and both are recoverable — add water to dissolve the salt, filter out the sand, evaporate the water, and both ingredients return unchanged. Second, a physical change CAN move energy — melting absorbs heat, freezing releases it, the washing line runs on sunshine — so an energy change on its own NEVER proves that chemistry happened. Only a new substance proves that. The questions for this section are ready for you now.

## Subtopic: Chemical Change Up Close — New Substances, and the Evidence

Chemical change is atoms REGROUPING: bonds break, atoms swap partners, new molecules assemble. Nobody can watch a single bond break, so you are armed instead with the observable EVIDENCE that regrouping has happened. Five signs, each worth naming precisely.

One: a GAS is produced — fizzing or bubbling where nothing is boiling. Squeeze lemon juice onto bicarbonate of soda and it foams with carbon dioxide; drop a strip of magnesium into dilute acid and hydrogen streams upward.

Two: a PRECIPITATE forms — an insoluble solid appearing where two clear solutions meet. Two water-clear liquids are poured together and a bright yellow solid snows out of nowhere: a new substance, beyond argument, because neither original solution contained any solid at all.

Three: a permanent COLOUR CHANGE — transformation, not dilution. A copper roof slowly turning green; a cut avocado browning on the plate; grey steel wool crumbling to red-brown rust.

Four: a TEMPERATURE change that nobody caused from outside. A camping hand warmer heats itself for hours as the iron inside combines with oxygen — bonds forming, energy released, an EXOTHERMIC change. Sherbet fizzing cold on your tongue is the opposite — the reaction drinks in energy, an ENDOTHERMIC change. Either direction, energy moving on its own is chemistry doing its bookkeeping in heat.

Five: LIGHT or SOUND from the change itself — the white blaze of a sparkler, the squeaky pop when a flame meets hydrogen gas.

Everyday chemical changes to recognise on sight: any fuel burning; rusting; bread dough rising as yeast breathes out new carbon dioxide; toast blackening; concrete setting; milk turning sour. Each is one-way traffic — no cooling, filtering or evaporating brings the original back.

One caution to carry: any single sign can occasionally mislead — bubbles also rise in boiling water, and boiling is physical. The signs are evidence; the verdict is always the same question — has a NEW substance appeared? Take the questions for this section now: five signs, named examples, and the verdict question.

## Subtopic: Mass, Energy and the Tricky Cases

Two conservation laws hold across BOTH kinds of change — and then the tricky cases test whether your definitions are real or recited.

First: CONSERVATION OF MASS. In a physical change it feels obvious — melt eighty grams of ice and you hold eighty grams of water. But it holds just as strictly in every chemical change: atoms are never created or destroyed, only regrouped, so the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants. Two directions of illusion. Burning wood SEEMS to lose mass — but only because smoke and gases escape unweighed; trap them and the books balance. Steel wool burned in oxygen SEEMS to gain mass — the ash weighs more than the metal did — but only because oxygen from the air joined the iron; put the oxygen on the input side of the ledger and the totals match exactly. This law is the foundation on which the next topic, balancing equations, is built.

Second: ENERGY IS CONSERVED, but it moves. Physical changes move energy without making anything new — melting absorbs, freezing releases. Chemical changes move energy by breaking and forming bonds — a burning match releases stored chemical energy as heat and light; photosynthesis locks light energy into new bonds. The direction of energy flow never decides the classification. Only the new-substance question does.

Now the tricky cases. Boiling water versus ELECTROLYSIS of water: boiling gives steam — still water, physical. Pass an electric current through water instead and it splits into hydrogen gas and oxygen gas — two new substances, chemical. Same starting material, opposite verdicts. Sugar, twice: sugar DISSOLVING in tea is physical — evaporate and it returns. Sugar HEATED in a pan is a double act — it first melts to a clear liquid, physical, and then, as heating continues, darkens into caramel and finally black carbon with steam rising — new substances, chemical, and no cooling turns caramel back into white sugar. And the fizzy cold drink versus the fizzing tablet: bubbles rushing from a freshly opened cold drink are dissolved carbon dioxide escaping — it was already there, physical. A headache tablet fizzing in water is manufacturing carbon dioxide that never existed in the tablet as gas — chemical.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: both conservation laws, the two mass illusions, and every tricky case judged by the one deciding question.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same border drawn through a Saturday at home — a freezer, a fire, a detective's clue kit, and one candle flame.

## Subtopic: The Ice Tray and the Braai Fire

Two weekend scenes carry the whole topic.

Scene one: the ice tray. A heatwave weekend — you fill the tray, freeze it, drop blocks into the jug, watch them melt, and refill the tray from the same jug. Freeze, melt, freeze, melt, all afternoon, and at every single stage it is WATER. Nothing new was ever made; the molecules only huddled into a solid or loosened into a liquid. That is physical change: same stuff, different outfit, and the outfit comes off again. Grating cheese, tearing bread, stirring Epsom salts into a footbath — same family. The crystals vanish from sight, but leave the basin in the sun and they climb right back out as the water leaves.

Scene two: the braai fire. Wood goes on — solid, pale, splintery, smelling of resin. The fire works through the evening, and by morning the grid holds grey ash, with smoke and invisible gases long gone into the night air. Now try to reverse it. Cool the ash: ash. Soak it, press it, wait a week: ash. The wood is not hiding in there — it is GONE, rebuilt atom by atom into new substances with new properties. That is chemical change: the old substance stops existing, and there is no outfit to take off because the actor has been replaced.

So the take-home test is one question: can I get the original back by simple means — cooling, evaporating, filtering, sieving? If yes, physical. If the original no longer exists, chemical. Ice tray, or braai fire. Your questions on the two scenes are with you now.

## Subtopic: Clues at the Scene

You cannot see atoms regrouping, so you work like a detective, reading the clues a chemical change leaves behind. Five clues make the full kit.

Clue one: unexpected bubbles. Foam where nothing is boiling means a gas is being born — lemon juice hissing on bicarbonate of soda, dough slowly inflating in a warm kitchen as yeast makes gas that was never in the flour.

Clue two: a solid from nowhere. Two clear liquids meet and a coloured solid snows down. You could filter either original liquid all day and catch nothing, because the solid did not exist until they met.

Clue three: a colour that will not come back. The browning face of a cut avocado, the green skin creeping over a copper roof, the red-brown crumble taking over wet steel wool. Scratch, rinse, cool — the change goes deep and stays.

Clue four: heat nobody added, or cold nobody ordered. The hand warmer in your pocket cooking along happily for hours; the sherbet that turns your tongue cold as it fizzes. Energy moving by itself says bonds are breaking and forming.

Clue five: light or sound from the change itself — the sparkler's white blaze, the pop of hydrogen meeting a flame.

One caution, detective: a single clue can frame an innocent change. The bubbles streaming out of a just-opened fizzy drink look exactly like clue one — but that gas was already dissolved in the liquid and is simply escaping. Nothing new was made: physical. Clues raise suspicion; the conviction always needs the verdict question — is there a NEW SUBSTANCE at the scene? The slice of toast that went in as bread and came out black and stays black answers yes. Quick questions on the clue kit are up now.

## Subtopic: The Candle Doing Both at Once

One candle flame runs both kinds of change at the same time, a few millimetres apart — which is exactly why it makes the perfect final test of your definitions.

Watch the top of the candle. The flame's heat MELTS the wax into a small clear pool. Tip the candle and a drop runs down the side, cools, and hardens back into wax — you can pick it off with a fingernail and squash it back on. Melted, hardened, still wax: physical change, the ice tray in miniature.

Now watch the wick. Liquid wax climbs the wick, meets the flame, and BURNS — combines with oxygen from the air and becomes carbon dioxide and water vapour, both invisible, both gone into the room. That wax is never coming back. No freezer, no filter, no patience will reassemble it. Chemical change, the braai fire in miniature. And that is why a candle grows shorter evening after evening: the melted wax returns, the burnt wax never does.

Hold the pair together and you can answer in two sentences: melting at the top — physical, no new substance, reversed by cooling. Burning at the wick — chemical, new substances formed, not reversible.

One last fact to close the loop: even in the burning, not one atom was destroyed. Weigh the candle before and after, capture every gas the flame gave off, count the oxygen the flame took from the air — and the ledger balances exactly. Atoms regroup; they never vanish. That bookkeeping is the law of conservation of mass, and it is precisely the rule you will enforce, line by line, when you balance chemical equations in the sessions ahead.

The last questions of the lesson are with you right now: the one deciding question, the five clues, and a candle doing both changes at once. Same stuff back — physical. New stuff made — chemical. That line runs through the whole of chemistry, and you can now draw it anywhere.
