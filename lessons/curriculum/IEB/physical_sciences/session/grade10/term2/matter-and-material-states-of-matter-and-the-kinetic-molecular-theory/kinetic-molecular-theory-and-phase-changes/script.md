# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the model that explains every physical property you have ever observed: the Kinetic Molecular Theory. The worked problem carrying it is the heating curve of water — half a kilogram of ice at minus fifteen degrees Celsius heated steadily until it is steam above one hundred degrees — and the question this topic always turns on: why does the temperature stop rising while the heating continues? Answer that in the language of particles, energy and forces, and the whole topic is yours.

## Subtopic: Matter Is Particles — and Here Is the Evidence

The Kinetic Molecular Theory rests on five statements. Learn them as a list, because "explain in terms of the kinetic molecular theory" is an instruction that expects them.

One: all matter is made of particles — atoms, molecules or ions. Two: those particles are in constant, random motion. Three: the particles possess kinetic energy, and the average kinetic energy of the particles increases as the temperature rises. Four: there are spaces between the particles. Five: there are forces of attraction between the particles — intermolecular forces — and the strength of those forces relative to the particles' kinetic energy decides which state the substance occupies.

Two pieces of observable evidence support the model, and both are named in the syllabus.

Diffusion is the spreading of one substance through another because of the random motion of its particles. Peel an orange in one corner of a quiet classroom and within a minute the far corner knows about it. Let a single drop of food colouring fall into a glass of perfectly still water and the colour drifts outward until the whole glass is tinted. Nothing stirred it; the particles moved themselves. Diffusion runs faster in gases than in liquids, and faster at higher temperature, because higher temperature means greater average kinetic energy.

Brownian motion is the sharper evidence. Tiny pollen grains suspended in water, watched under a microscope, jerk along jagged, unpredictable paths — and smoke specks in air do the same. The grains are far too large to be propelling themselves; they are being bombarded, unevenly and from every side, by water or air particles too small to see. That is direct evidence for statement two: invisible particles exist, and they never stop moving.

Keep one distinction sharp from the very start. Temperature measures the AVERAGE kinetic energy of the particles; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. A substance can absorb a great deal of heat with no change in temperature at all — as the heating curve is about to prove.

The questions for this section are with you now: the five statements listed, diffusion and Brownian motion each explained as evidence, and heat distinguished from temperature.

## Subtopic: Solid, Liquid and Gas in the Kinetic Molecular Theory

Three states, and for each the syllabus wants four things described: the arrangement of the particles, their motion, the strength of the forces between them, and the resulting bulk properties.

A SOLID: particles packed closely in a fixed, regular arrangement — a lattice. They cannot move from place to place; they vibrate about fixed positions. The forces between them are strong, and the particles' kinetic energy is too small to overcome those forces. Consequences: fixed shape, fixed volume, very hard to compress because there is almost no space between particles, and high density.

A LIQUID: particles still close together, so the spaces stay small, but the arrangement is irregular and the particles slide past one another. The forces remain significant, yet the particles now carry enough kinetic energy to escape fixed positions while staying held within the body of the liquid. Consequences: a fixed volume but no fixed shape — a litre of milk takes the shape of the bottle, the jug or the spill — and still very hard to compress.

A GAS: particles far apart, in rapid, random, straight-line motion, colliding with one another and with the container walls. The forces between them are negligible, because the particles carry far too much kinetic energy to be held. Consequences: no fixed shape and no fixed volume — a gas fills any container completely — easily compressed because most of its volume is empty space, and very low density. Gas pressure is nothing more than those particles colliding with the container walls.

Read the pattern running through all three: a contest between the particles' kinetic energy and the forces of attraction between them. Forces winning gives a solid; forces and energy roughly matched gives a liquid; energy winning outright gives a gas. Heating supplies kinetic energy, cooling removes it, and every change of state is the moment the contest tips.

Stop for this section's questions now: arrangement, motion, forces and bulk properties given for each state, and the energy-against-forces contest used to explain which state a substance occupies.

## Subtopic: The Six Changes of State and the Energy They Need

Six changes, six names — and both the name and the direction must be exact.

Melting: solid to liquid. Freezing, also called solidification: liquid to solid. Evaporation and boiling: liquid to gas. Condensation: gas to liquid. Sublimation: solid straight to gas, skipping the liquid entirely — solid carbon dioxide, sold as dry ice, does exactly this at ordinary pressure, and mothballs in a cupboard slowly vanish the same way. Deposition: gas straight to solid — which is how frost coats a farm fence on a clear winter night: water vapour becoming ice crystals without ever being liquid.

Sort the six by energy, because that sorting anchors half the questions in this topic. Melting, evaporation and sublimation all carry a substance toward a state with weaker forces and more freedom, so all three ABSORB energy — endothermic. Freezing, condensation and deposition all move toward tighter holding, so all three RELEASE energy — exothermic. That is why sweat cools your skin: the evaporating water takes its energy from you.

Two definitions must be word-perfect. The melting point is the temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid, and it is identical to the freezing point of the same substance approached from the other side. The boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid changes into a gas throughout the liquid, when its vapour pressure equals the surrounding atmospheric pressure. Pure water melts at zero degrees Celsius and boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level.

Distinguish evaporation from boiling. Evaporation happens at ANY temperature, only at the SURFACE, and only for particles with above-average kinetic energy escaping upwards — which is precisely why the liquid left behind grows cooler. Boiling happens only AT the boiling point, throughout the liquid, with bubbles of vapour forming inside it.

And a South African consequence of that pressure definition: atmospheric pressure falls with altitude, so water boils at very nearly one hundred degrees beside the sea but at roughly ninety-five degrees in Johannesburg — less air pressing down, lower boiling point, and rice that genuinely takes longer to cook inland.

This section's questions are in front of you now: the six changes named with their directions, sorted into endothermic and exothermic, and evaporation properly distinguished from boiling.

## Subtopic: Reading a Heating Curve — Why the Temperature Stops Rising

Now the worked curve. Half a kilogram of ice at minus fifteen degrees Celsius, heated at a steady rate, with temperature plotted upward and time along the bottom. The graph has five parts, and you must be able to name and explain every one.

Part one: a rising line from minus fifteen degrees to zero. The energy supplied increases the average kinetic energy of the particles, which vibrate faster; the sample is entirely solid ice. Part two: a horizontal plateau at zero degrees — melting, with ice and liquid water present together and the temperature pinned while any ice remains. Part three: a rising line from zero to one hundred degrees, all liquid, kinetic energy and temperature climbing again. Part four: a much longer horizontal plateau at one hundred degrees — boiling, liquid and steam together. Part five: a rising line above one hundred degrees, all steam.

Now the question the topic turns on: energy is still being supplied during the plateaus, so why does the temperature not rise? Because temperature measures average KINETIC energy, and during a change of state the supplied energy is not becoming kinetic energy at all. It is spent overcoming the forces of attraction between the particles — increasing their POTENTIAL energy, prising them apart into a freer arrangement. Particles are being separated, not sped up. Only when the last of the ice has melted does further energy raise the kinetic energy again, and only then does the thermometer move.

Note the shape detail worth pointing out every time: the boiling plateau is markedly longer than the melting plateau, because separating liquid particles completely into a gas is a far bigger job than loosening a lattice into a liquid.

The cooling curve is the exact mirror: steam cools, plateaus at one hundred while condensing, cools as liquid, plateaus at zero while freezing, then cools as ice — the plateaus now marking energy RELEASED as the forces re-form.

Reading state off the curve is the other standard task, and the rule is simple: below the melting point, solid only; on the melting plateau, solid and liquid together; between the plateaus, liquid only; on the boiling plateau, liquid and gas together; above it, gas only.

Three traps. One: writing that "no energy is being added" during a plateau — energy is added continuously; it is doing a different job. Two: confusing heat with temperature, and so expecting every joule to move the thermometer. Three: kelvin and Celsius muddled — add 273 to the Celsius reading, so zero degrees Celsius is 273 kelvin and one hundred degrees Celsius is 373 kelvin.

The final questions of this part are with you now: each section of the curve named and explained, the plateau accounted for in terms of potential energy and forces, and the state identified at any stated temperature.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same particles from school assembly, a jam-packed corridor and an open field — because you have already watched all three states of matter behave, without knowing that was what you were watching.

## Subtopic: The Assembly, the Crowd and the Open Field

Everything around you is made of tiny particles, they never stop moving, and there are gaps and pulls between them. That is the entire model. What changes between ice, water and steam is not the particles — it is how tightly they are held and how much they are jiggling.

Picture a school assembly. Learners stand in neat rows, each in an assigned spot, shoulder to shoulder. Nobody wanders off, but nobody stands frozen either — there is constant small fidgeting on the spot. The rows keep their shape, and you could not pack the crowd much tighter. That is a SOLID: fixed places, fixed shape, fixed size, particles vibrating where they stand.

Now assembly ends and everyone pours into the corridor. Still shoulder to shoulder, still packed — but now people slide past one another, and the crowd flows into the shape of whatever corridor holds it. Same bodies, same closeness, completely different behaviour. That is a LIQUID: still close, no longer fixed, keeping its volume while taking the shape of its container.

Then break time, and everyone sprints onto the field. Far apart now, moving fast in every direction, colliding only now and then, spreading until the whole field is used. Move the fences outward and the crowd fills the new space too, because it is mostly emptiness out there. That is a GAS: far apart, fast, filling whatever it is given, and easy to squeeze.

One idea decides which picture you are in: a tug-of-war between how much the particles jiggle and how strongly they pull on each other. Pull winning, everyone holds their rows — solid. Roughly even — liquid. Jiggle winning, everyone scatters — gas. Heating turns the jiggle up; cooling turns it down.

And how do we know invisible particles are truly moving? Because a peeled orange announces itself across a still classroom with nobody fanning the smell along. And because a pollen grain under a microscope jitters as if being shoved — which it is, by water particles far too small to see.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on this piece are coming to you right now: the three pictures matched to the three states, and the tug-of-war used to say why each behaves as it does.

## Subtopic: Why the Pot Sticks at One Hundred

Here is the thing that trips almost everybody, and a kettle proves it in your own kitchen.

Put a pot of water on the stove with a thermometer in it. The temperature climbs steadily — forty, sixty, eighty, ninety-nine, one hundred. Then it stops. The plate is still glowing, the electricity is still costing somebody money, and the reading refuses to pass one hundred until the pot has nearly boiled dry. Where is all that energy going?

Not into making the particles faster. Temperature only measures how hard the particles jiggle on average — so if the thermometer is stuck, the jiggling is not increasing. The energy is doing a different job: prising the particles apart. Water particles grip one another, and turning liquid into steam means breaking every one of those grips and setting each particle loose. That costs an enormous amount of energy, and while it is being spent on separation, none is left over to speed anything up.

Think of it as paying off a debt before you can save. Every joule goes straight to the debt collector — the forces holding the particles together — and only once the debt is fully settled does the savings balance start climbing again.

Exactly the same happens at the bottom of the scale. Ice from the chest freezer at minus fifteen warms steadily to zero, then sticks at zero as a slushy mix of ice and water until the last sliver of ice is gone. Only then does the water begin its climb toward one hundred.

Draw the whole journey as temperature against time and you get a staircase with two flat steps: a short one at zero while it melts, a long one at one hundred while it boils. The boiling step is much longer because setting particles completely free is a far bigger job than loosening them out of their rows.

Run the journey backwards — steam meeting a cold windowpane — and you get the same shape upside down, the flat parts now energy handed BACK as the particles grab hold of each other again. That is why a steam burn is so much worse than a splash of boiling water: condensing steam dumps its whole separation debt into your skin.

Your questions for this part are up now: the flat parts explained, the energy accounted for, and the reason the boiling step is the long one.

## Subtopic: Six Words for Changing, and the Traps That Cost Marks

Last piece: the six words, and the three mistakes that undo more answers than anything else in this topic.

The six changes, most of them straight from your kitchen. Ice to water is melting. Water to ice is freezing. Water to steam is boiling — or evaporating, when it happens quietly from a surface. Steam to water on a cold window is condensation. Then the two that skip the middle: solid straight to gas is sublimation — dry ice smoking away without ever leaving a puddle, mothballs shrinking year by year — and gas straight to solid is deposition, which is exactly how frost gets onto the grass overnight. No rain fell; the water vapour turned solid where it landed.

Sort them by energy with a single question: am I loosening the grip, or tightening it? Loosening — melting, evaporating, subliming — needs energy in: endothermic. Tightening — freezing, condensing, deposition — hands energy out: exothermic. That is why sweating works: the evaporating layer takes its energy from your skin.

Now the traps. Trap one, the biggest: writing "no energy is going in" during a flat part of the graph. Energy pours in the whole time; it is simply being spent pulling particles apart instead of speeding them up. Say "the energy overcomes the forces between the particles, raising their potential energy" and the answer stands.

Trap two: treating heat and temperature as one word. Heat is energy on the move; temperature is the average jiggle. A cup of boiling water and a bathtub of boiling water sit at the same temperature — but the bathtub holds vastly more heat.

Trap three: using evaporation and boiling interchangeably. Boiling happens only at the boiling point, all through the liquid, with bubbles. Evaporation happens at any temperature, only from the surface — which is how washing dries on the line on a mild day.

And one local detail worth carrying: water boils at about ninety-five degrees in Johannesburg instead of one hundred at the coast, because less air presses down at altitude.

Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the six words matched to their directions, sorted into energy-in and energy-out, and the three traps named before they can catch you.
