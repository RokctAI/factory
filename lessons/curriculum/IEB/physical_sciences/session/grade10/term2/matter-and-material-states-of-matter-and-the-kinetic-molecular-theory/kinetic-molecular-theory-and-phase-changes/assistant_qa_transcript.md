**Thandi:** Before you head out — the four questions this topic always leaves simmering. First: the plateau. Everyone can recite "the energy breaks the forces", but what does that actually look like down among the particles while the ice melts?

**Tutor:** Zoom all the way in and watch one particle at the edge of the ice. It sits in the lattice, gripped by its neighbours on every side, vibrating in place — and the stove's energy has been feeding that vibration, which is why the temperature climbed from minus fifteen to zero. At zero, the vibration reaches the point where the grips can just barely hold. Now every joule arriving faces a choice, and the physics makes the choice automatically: it is spent prising a particle out of the lattice — lifting it over the wall of attraction into the liquid, where it can slide — rather than shaking the whole crowd faster. Think of the energy as a work crew: while there is still lattice to dismantle, the whole crew works demolition, and none is left to push the speed up. That is why the thermometer freezes at zero even though the stove pours energy in: temperature only reports SPEED — average kinetic energy — and the incoming energy is being banked as POTENTIAL energy in the freshly separated particles instead. The moment the last block of lattice is dismantled, the crew has nothing left to demolish, every joule goes back into speed, and the reading climbs again. Same story at one hundred, only the demolition is total — every remaining grip broken — which is why that plateau runs so much longer.

**Thandi:** Next: evaporation. If boiling needs one hundred degrees, how does a puddle disappear on a twenty-degree day — and why does evaporating make things colder?

**Tutor:** Because temperature is an AVERAGE, and averages hide a spread. In that twenty-degree puddle the particles do not all move at the same speed: most are middling, some are sluggish, and a lucky few at any instant are moving far faster than average. When one of those fast particles finds itself at the surface, aimed upward, its kinetic energy can beat the grip of its neighbours — and it escapes into the air. No bubbles, no hundred degrees, just the top tail of the speed distribution leaking away, particle by particle. That explains both halves of your question at once. The puddle dries at any temperature because there are always SOME particles fast enough to escape — the process merely runs slower when fewer qualify. And the puddle cools as it dries because of who leaves: evaporation is selective emigration of the fastest particles, so the average speed of those left behind drops — and a lower average speed IS a lower temperature. That is the entire engineering of sweating: your body pushes water onto the skin precisely so its fastest particles will desert, taking above-average energy with them. Boiling is the different, violent case: at one hundred degrees the escape is no longer reserved for the lucky few at the surface — vapour bubbles form throughout the body of the liquid, because the vapour pressure now matches the atmosphere pressing down. Any temperature, surface only, fastest few: evaporation. One temperature, everywhere, everyone: boiling.

**Thandi:** Then the altitude fact. Why exactly does water boil at about ninety-five degrees in Johannesburg — and what does that do to cooking?

**Tutor:** Start from the honest definition of boiling: a liquid boils when its vapour pressure — the push of its own escaping particles — equals the atmospheric pressure pressing down on its surface. Boiling is therefore a negotiation between the liquid and the atmosphere, and the atmosphere's side of the deal changes with altitude. At the coast, the air column above the pot weighs in at full sea-level pressure, and water must be driven all the way to one hundred degrees before its vapour pushes back hard enough. In Johannesburg, some one thousand seven hundred metres up, a substantial slice of that air column is simply missing; the lid of atmospheric pressure is lighter, so the water's vapour pressure matches it earlier — at roughly ninety-five degrees — and the water boils there, refusing to get any hotter no matter how fierce the flame. That last clause is the cooking consequence: boiling water cannot exceed its boiling point, so inland cooks simmer rice and potatoes in ninety-five-degree water rather than hundred-degree water, and everything takes noticeably longer. A pressure cooker is this same physics run in reverse: seal the pot, trap the vapour, raise the pressure above atmospheric, and the boiling point climbs past one hundred — hotter water, faster supper. One definition, and both the mountain and the pressure cooker fall out of it.

**Thandi:** Last one: heat versus temperature keeps costing people. Is there a picture that locks the difference in permanently?

**Tutor:** Use money, and the lock never slips. Temperature is the average wealth per person in a room; heat is money actually changing hands. A cup of boiling water and a bathtub of boiling water have the same average — every particle jiggling equally hard, one hundred degrees in both — but the bathtub holds thousands of times more particles, so its total energy account is enormously larger. Tip each over your foot and you will feel the difference between an average and a total. Now run the picture through the topic's claims and watch them turn obvious. Heat flows from hot to cold: money flows from the wealthy room to the poorer room when they trade, regardless of which room has more people — a spark at a thousand degrees gives heat to a swimming pool even though the pool's total energy dwarfs it. The plateau: money pours into the room, but it is all spent paying off the debt of separation — the average wealth per person cannot rise until the debt clears; that is heat entering with temperature stuck. And the steam burn: condensing steam is a crowd paying its entire separation debt back out, into your skin, on top of being at one hundred degrees. Whenever a question mentions either word, translate: temperature — average per particle; heat — energy actually transferred. Answer in the translation, then convert back, and the two words will never blur again.
