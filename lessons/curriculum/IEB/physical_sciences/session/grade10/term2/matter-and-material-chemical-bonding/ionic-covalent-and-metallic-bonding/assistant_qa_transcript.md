**Thandi:** Before you close your books — the four questions this topic always leaves hanging. First: if the electron is handed over in ionic bonding, what is actually holding the compound together afterwards? The handover sounds like the bond, but you said it isn't.

**Tutor:** The handover is the setup; the attraction is the bond — and keeping those separate rescues a lot of confused answers. Walk the sequence slowly. Before the transfer, sodium and chlorine are two neutral atoms: nothing pulls them together. The transfer changes their status: sodium, minus one electron, becomes a plus-one cation; chlorine, plus one electron, becomes a minus-one anion. Only NOW does a force exist — the electrostatic attraction between opposite charges — and that force, not the historical event of the handover, is the ionic bond. The distinction matters practically. First, it explains why the bond acts in ALL directions: a charge attracts every opposite charge around it, not just the atom it once traded with, which is exactly why ions stack into a lattice instead of pairing into molecules. Second, it explains dissolving: water molecules can work their way between ions and cushion the attractions, freeing ions that never "give back" any electron — the transfer is not undone, the attraction is merely outcompeted. If you ever write "the ionic bond is when an electron is transferred", upgrade the sentence: the transfer creates the ions; the bond is the attraction between them. Cause, then force. Two different things, one clean answer.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why do the sharers make soft, low-melting stuff like wax and water, when a covalent bond is supposed to be strong?

**Tutor:** Because two entirely different forces are being compared, and the melting point only tests the weaker one. Inside a water molecule, the oxygen-hydrogen bonds are genuinely strong — boiling a kettle does not tear a single one of them; the steam leaving the spout is still intact H two O. What melting and boiling must overcome is the grip BETWEEN separate molecules — the intermolecular forces — and those are feeble compared with the bonds inside. Picture beads on strings: each bead is welded solid, but the beads barely cling to each other, so the pile pours easily. Wax slumping on a hot day is molecules sliding past one another with every internal bond intact. Now run the same comparison on the other two structures and the property table writes itself. In an ionic lattice there is no "between molecules" — the electrostatic attractions ARE the structure, running plus to minus in every direction, so melting salt means fighting the full bonding network: hence the high melting point. In a metal, the kernels sit in the communal electron sea, again bonded throughout the piece. So when a question asks why covalent substances melt low, never answer "covalent bonds are weak" — they are not. Answer: the molecules are internally strong but externally loosely held, and melting only has to loosen the external grip.

**Thandi:** Then the electron sea. It sounds like a nice story — what would convince a skeptic that copper really has loose, shared electrons?

**Tutor:** Ask the skeptic to explain three everyday observations without the sea, and watch the story become the only one that works. Observation one: flick a light switch and the lamp responds essentially instantly, metres of copper away. Something charged must be free to move throughout the entire wire the moment a push is applied — bound electrons, locked to individual atoms as in salt or wax, cannot do that, and indeed solid salt conducts nothing. Delocalised electrons, already drifting loose among the kernels, respond at once. Observation two: a wire can be drawn thinner and bent around corners without crumbling, while a salt crystal explodes into fragments under a hammer. If bonding belonged to fixed neighbour-pairs, shifting a layer of atoms would break those pairs and the metal would shatter like the crystal does; instead the sea simply flows around the rearranged kernels and the bonding survives — which is precisely what malleable means. Observation three: the shine. Loose electrons interact strongly with light across a whole range of colours, giving metals their mirror-like lustre; substances with tightly bound electrons are typically dull or transparent. One model, three predictions, all confirmed on your kitchen table. That is what a good scientific picture does — and "delocalised" is the single word that unlocks all three explanations in any answer you write.

**Thandi:** Last one: with three bond types and all these formulas, what is the fastest way to decide, in a test, which bonding a substance has and what properties to expect?

**Tutor:** Use the two-step scan: composition first, properties as confirmation. Step one, read what kinds of element are present. Metal with non-metal — think ionic: transfer, ions, lattice. Non-metal with non-metal — think covalent: shared pairs, molecules. Metal alone — metallic: kernels in a sea. That single glance at the periodic table classifies almost everything in Grade 10. Step two, let the expected properties confirm or veto. Ionic: high melting point, brittle crystals, conducts only when molten or dissolved — never as a solid. Covalent molecular: low melting point, soft or waxy or liquid or gas, does not conduct in any state. Metallic: conducts as a solid, bends and stretches, shines. The confirmation step matters because it works in reverse too — a question may hand you only properties and ask for the bonding. Then you read backwards: conducts molten but not solid points at ions locked in a lattice; conducts solid points at the sea; melts at forty degrees and conducts never points at molecules. Practise until the scan takes five seconds each way — substance to bonding, bonding to properties, properties to bonding. Those three little journeys, plus a formula balanced to zero and a mass added with its subscripts, cover the overwhelming majority of everything this topic can ask.
