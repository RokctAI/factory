# Part 1 — Expert

Why does table salt sit as a brittle crystal that dissolves in water, candle wax as a soft solid that slumps on a hot day, and copper as a shiny metal that bends and conducts? Because their atoms are held together in three different ways. This session covers the three types of chemical bond — covalent sharing, ionic transfer, and the metallic sea — plus the Lewis diagrams that draw them, the naming and formula rules for each, and the formula-mass calculations that close every bonding question.

## Subtopic: Why Atoms Bond — Valence Electrons and Lewis Diagrams

A CHEMICAL BOND is a force that holds atoms together, and every bond in this course exists for one reason: atoms interact so as to reach a FULL OUTER ENERGY LEVEL — the stable electron arrangement of a noble gas. For most atoms that means eight electrons in the outer level, the octet; for the smallest atoms, hydrogen above all, it means two, matching helium. Full outer levels are low-energy, settled arrangements, and chemistry is the business of getting there.

Only the VALENCE electrons — the outer-level electrons from the previous topic — take part in bonding. So chemistry keeps a shorthand that shows just those, and that is the LEWIS DIAGRAM, also called an electron-dot diagram: the element's symbol stands for the nucleus plus every inner electron, and the valence electrons are drawn as dots around it, placed singly on the four sides before any pairing begins.

Describe a few aloud. Hydrogen: the symbol H with one dot. Carbon, group 14: C with four single dots, one per side. Nitrogen, group 15: N with five dots — one pair and three singles. Oxygen, group 16: O with six dots — two pairs and two singles. Fluorine, group 17: F with seven dots — three pairs and one single. Potassium, group 1: K with a lone dot. The pattern to bank: the dot count equals the group's valence count, and the UNPAIRED dots are the atom's bonding hooks — carbon has four hooks, oxygen two, fluorine one.

Now preview the three strategies for filling the level. Two non-metals, neither willing to surrender electrons: they SHARE — covalent bonding. A metal and a non-metal, one eager to lose and one eager to gain: they TRANSFER — ionic bonding. Metal atoms alone, all wanting to lose: they pool their electrons — metallic bonding. Take this section's questions now: the reason for bonding, and dots counted for any main-group element.

## Subtopic: Covalent Bonding — Sharing Pairs

COVALENT BONDING is the sharing of one or more PAIRS of electrons between two non-metal atoms, so that each atom counts the shared pair toward its own outer level. The bonded unit is a MOLECULE, and each shared pair is one covalent bond.

Build the syllabus molecules in words. Hydrogen, H two: each H brings one electron; together they hold one shared pair — a single bond — and each hydrogen now counts two electrons, helium's arrangement. Fluorine, F two, and chlorine, C l two: each atom offers its single unpaired dot, forming one bond, with three lone pairs left sitting on each atom. Hydrogen fluoride, H F, and hydrogen chloride, H C l: one shared pair joining the two. Water, H two O: oxygen carries two hooks, so it takes one hydrogen on each, keeping two lone pairs for itself — two single bonds. Ammonia, N H three: nitrogen's three hooks each catch a hydrogen, one lone pair remaining. Methane, C H four: carbon's four hooks, four hydrogens, no lone pairs — a perfect cross of four single bonds.

Some atoms must share MORE than one pair. Oxygen, O two: each oxygen still needs two electrons, so the pair of atoms shares TWO pairs — a DOUBLE bond, leaving two lone pairs on each atom. Nitrogen, N two: each needs three, so they share THREE — a TRIPLE bond, one lone pair each. That triple bond ranks among the strongest in chemistry, which is why nitrogen makes up most of every breath you take and reacts with almost nothing on the way through.

Naming covalent compounds runs on counting prefixes, always on the second element and, when needed, the first: carbon dioxide — one carbon, two oxygens; sulphur dioxide; carbon monoxide; dinitrogen tetroxide announces two nitrogens and four oxygens. The formula records the true atom count in a single molecule.

This section's questions are with you now: pairs shared, lone pairs left, and single, double and triple bonds assigned correctly.

## Subtopic: Ionic Bonding — Transfer and the Lattice

IONIC BONDING begins with a TRANSFER: a metal atom hands one or more valence electrons to a non-metal atom. The metal becomes a positive CATION, the non-metal a negative ANION, and the bond is the electrostatic ATTRACTION between the oppositely charged ions.

Sodium chloride in words. Sodium — one valence electron, hopeless to complete a level, cheap to empty one — releases its single outer electron: N a plus, with neon's stable arrangement underneath. Chlorine — seven valence electrons — receives it: C l minus, with argon's arrangement. Plus attracts minus, and the ions bind. The Lewis picture: the sodium symbol with no dots and a plus charge, beside a bracketed chloride with eight dots and a minus charge.

Predict every ion charge straight off the periodic table: group 1 forms plus one; group 2, plus two; group 16, minus two; group 17, minus one. Then build formulae by BALANCING CHARGE to zero. Lithium fluoride: plus one meets minus one — L i F, one to one. Magnesium chloride: magnesium's plus two needs TWO chloride minus ones — M g C l two. Sodium oxide: oxide's minus two needs TWO sodium plus ones — N a two O, and notice the subscript lands on the METAL this time. Speak the Lewis diagram of magnesium chloride: a magnesium symbol, no dots, two-plus; and two separate bracketed chlorides, eight dots each, minus one apiece.

Now the structural point that separates ionic from covalent. Ionic compounds do NOT form molecules. The ions stack into a giant three-dimensional CRYSTAL LATTICE — every plus surrounded by minuses, every minus by pluses, attraction pulling in all directions. So the formula N a C l is a FORMULA UNIT: the simplest ratio of ions in the crystal, one to one, never a two-atom particle. The lattice explains the properties: strong attractions in every direction mean high melting points; rigid ion positions mean brittle crystals; and the ions conduct electricity only once freed — molten, or dissolved in water. Naming is simple: metal first, unchanged; non-metal second with the ending i d e — sodium chloride, magnesium chloride, sodium oxide.

Take the questions for this section now: transfer, charges, lattice, and formulae balanced to zero.

## Subtopic: Metallic Bonding, and Formula Masses

The third bond belongs to metals alone. In a piece of copper every atom is a metal atom; nobody can hand electrons to anybody, and nobody will take them. Instead, every atom RELEASES its valence electrons into a shared pool. METALLIC BONDING is the attraction between the resulting lattice of POSITIVE METAL IONS — sometimes called kernels — and the SEA OF DELOCALISED ELECTRONS drifting freely among them. Delocalised is the load-bearing word: the electrons belong to the whole metal, not to any single atom.

The sea explains the metal personality in one breath. Conduction: the delocalised electrons are already mobile, so charge — and heat — flow easily. Malleability and ductility: push one layer of ions past another and the sea flows around the new arrangement; the bonding survives, so metal bends where an ionic crystal would shatter. Shine: the loose electrons interact strongly with light. Three properties, one cause — say "delocalised electrons" and the explanations write themselves.

Close the topic with the calculation that finishes nearly every bonding question: RELATIVE MOLECULAR MASS for covalent molecules, RELATIVE FORMULA MASS for ionic formula units — same method, different name. Add the relative atomic masses from the periodic table, honouring the subscripts. Methane, C H four: 12 plus four ones — 16. Hydrogen chloride, H C l: 1 plus 35,5 — 36,5. Water, H two O: two ones plus 16 — 18. Potassium bromide: 39 plus 80 — 119. Magnesium chloride, M g C l two: 24 plus two times 35,5 — 24 plus 71 — 95. Sodium oxide, N a two O: two times 23 plus 16 — 46 plus 16 — 62 — and there the subscript sits on the metal, waiting to catch anyone skimming. The classic error is ignoring a subscript — one chloride counted in magnesium chloride instead of two. Read every formula as an atom inventory, multiply before adding, and the answer takes care of itself.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: the sea described, the properties explained by it, and masses added with every subscript honoured.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three bonds as three ways people share at home — same chemistry, kitchen-table pictures first.

## Subtopic: Sharing the Data, Swapping the Chairs

Why do atoms bother bonding at all? Because every atom wants its outer shelf of electrons FULL — eight for most, two for the tiny ones. A full shelf is calm and settled, like the noble gases, those neighbours who need nothing from anybody. Every bond in chemistry is an atom's plan for filling its shelf, and between neighbours there are only two plans: share, or swap.

Plan one — sharing — is two friends with one data bundle between them. Neither can afford the full bundle alone, so they pool: both phones run off the shared data, and both count it as theirs. That is covalent bonding: two non-metals, each contributing an electron to a shared pair that sits between them, counted by both. Water is oxygen sharing with two hydrogens; methane is carbon sharing with four. Some friendships need more than one bundle: two oxygens pool two pairs — a double bond — and two nitrogens pool three, the famous triple bond that lets nitrogen drift lazily through every breath.

Plan two — swapping — is a chair trade that suits both sides. Sodium owns one awkward extra chair it never wanted; chlorine has seven chairs and a single gap. Sodium simply GIVES its chair away. Now sodium's full shelf below is on display and it wears a plus badge; chlorine's row is complete and it wears a minus badge. Plus and minus attract — and THAT attraction, not the handover itself, is the ionic bond.

How do you know which plan two atoms will choose? Read the table: two non-metals, they share; a metal with a non-metal, they swap. Questions on the two plans are with you now.

## Subtopic: The Salt Grid and the Electron Sea

What happens AFTER the swap explains everything about salt. The plus and minus ions never pair off into little couples — they stack. Every plus surrounds itself with minuses, every minus with pluses, outward in all directions, millions upon millions deep: a giant grid, the crystal lattice. A single grain of salt is one tiny piece of that grid.

The grid explains salt's whole personality. Hard to melt, because melting means breaking attractions that run in every direction at once. Brittle — knock it and it shatters rather than bends, because sliding a layer forces plus against plus, and the grid tears itself apart. And the formula N a C l is not a molecule; it is the RECIPE RATIO — one plus for every minus. Magnesium chloride's recipe reads M g C l two because magnesium wears a two-plus badge and needs two minus-one chlorides to reach zero; sodium oxide flips it — N a two O — two plus-one sodiums settling one minus-two oxide. Charge always balances to zero; that single rule writes every ionic formula.

Metals play a third game entirely. In a copper wire every atom is a metal — nobody to swap with. So they all toss their outer electrons into one communal pot, and the loose electrons wash freely around the grid of positive leftovers: the electron sea. The sea is why metal is metal. Nudge the sea and charge flows — electrical conduction. Bend the wire and the sea simply flows around the rearranged ions — the metal dents and stretches where salt would shatter. Even the shine is that restless sea playing with light.

Three structures, three personalities: molecules for the sharers, a rigid grid for the swappers, a sea for the metals. Your questions on the grid and the sea are up now.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Recipe

The last skill is pure supermarket arithmetic: weighing a recipe by adding up its ingredients.

Every formula is a recipe, and the periodic table is the price list — each element with its relative atomic mass printed in the corner of its block. To weigh a recipe, read the atom counts honestly and add. Methane, C H four: one carbon at 12, four hydrogens at 1 each — 16. Hydrogen chloride: 1 plus 35,5 — 36,5. Water: two ones plus 16 — 18.

Same game for the swapped compounds. Potassium bromide: 39 plus 80 — 119. Magnesium chloride, M g C l two: mind the little two — TWO chlorides at 35,5 each is 71, plus magnesium's 24 — 95. And sodium oxide, N a two O, plants the trap on the other side: the two belongs to the SODIUM — two twenty-threes is 46, plus oxygen's 16 — 62. The name shifts with the bond — molecular mass for sharers, formula mass for swappers — but the adding never changes.

The one error that ruins these sums: skimming past a subscript, on either side of the formula. Read every formula out loud as an inventory before touching numbers — "two sodiums, one oxygen" — then multiply each count by its mass, then add. Inventory, multiply, add. Three beats, every time.

And why does this little sum matter so much? Because next topic, the mole turns these numbers into GRAMS you can weigh on a scale: 119 becomes the grams in one mole of potassium bromide. Every stoichiometry calculation from now to the end of school opens by weighing a recipe exactly like this.

Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: share or swap decided from the table, the grid and the sea explained, and recipes weighed with every subscript counted. Three bonds, three pictures — and the foundations of your whole chemistry course are now poured.
