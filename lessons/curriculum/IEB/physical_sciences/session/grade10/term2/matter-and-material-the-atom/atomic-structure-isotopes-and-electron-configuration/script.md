# Part 1 — Expert

Every formula, every reaction and every periodic-table trend for the next three years is decided inside a space a tenth of a billionth of a metre wide. This session maps that space: the nucleus and its two occupants, the numbers that identify an element and its isotopes, the weighted average behind the masses printed on the periodic table, and the strict filling rules — energy levels, orbitals, Aufbau diagrams and sp notation — that arrange the electrons and, with them, the whole of chemistry.

## Subtopic: Inside the Atom — Protons, Neutrons and Electrons

The modern picture of the atom took shape in stages. The earliest model imagined a soft ball of positive charge with electrons scattered through it like pips in a watermelon. Then came the famous gold-foil experiment: alpha particles fired at a whisper-thin gold sheet mostly sailed straight through — but a tiny fraction ricocheted almost straight back. The conclusion rewrote the atom: nearly all its mass, and all its positive charge, is crammed into a minute central NUCLEUS, with the electrons occupying the vast, mostly empty space around it. Later refinements placed those electrons into specific energy levels, and that is the working model for this course.

Three particles build every atom. In the nucleus: the PROTON, charge plus one, relative mass one; and the NEUTRON, no charge, relative mass also one. Around the nucleus: the ELECTRON, charge minus one, with a mass so tiny — about one two-thousandth of a proton's — that we ignore it when totalling an atom's mass. The nucleus therefore holds essentially all the mass in a fraction of the space, while the electrons define the atom's size and conduct all of its chemistry.

Hold onto the scale, because it shapes everything later: enlarge an atom to the size of a sports stadium and the nucleus is a pea on the centre spot. Matter is overwhelmingly empty space, stitched together by electric attraction — negative electrons held to the positive nucleus.

In a NEUTRAL atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons: the charges cancel exactly. Tip that balance and you have ions, arriving in the next section. Take the questions for this section now — the three particles, their charges and masses, and where each one lives.

## Subtopic: Atomic Number, Mass Number, Notation and Ions

Two whole numbers identify any atom, and the standard notation writes both beside the element's symbol.

The ATOMIC NUMBER, symbol Z, is the number of PROTONS in the nucleus — and it is the element's identity. Every atom with 13 protons is aluminium; every atom with 9 is fluorine; change the proton count and you have changed the element itself. The MASS NUMBER, symbol A, is protons PLUS neutrons — the total count of heavy particles in the nucleus. From the pair: neutrons equal A minus Z.

The notation stacks the mass number as a superscript and the atomic number as a subscript, both to the LEFT of the symbol. Spoken aloud: aluminium twenty-seven — A equal to 27 above, Z equal to 13 below, written before the symbol A l. Unpack it: 13 protons, because Z is 13; 27 minus 13, which is 14 neutrons; and, in the neutral atom, 13 electrons balancing the 13 protons.

Now the ions. Atoms gain or lose ELECTRONS — never protons — to form charged particles. Lose electrons and the positives outnumber the negatives: a positive ion, a CATION. Magnesium surrendering two electrons becomes M g two-plus: still 12 protons, still magnesium, but only 10 electrons remain. Gain electrons and the negatives win: a negative ion, an ANION. Oxygen — 8 protons — collecting two extra electrons becomes O two-minus with 10 electrons. The charge sits as a superscript on the right: plus, two-plus, minus, two-minus. And drill the accounting rule: a two-plus charge means two electrons LOST, never gained; the nucleus never changes in ordinary chemistry.

The questions for this section are with you now: unpack the notation into protons, neutrons and electrons, for neutral atoms and for ions both.

## Subtopic: Isotopes and Relative Atomic Mass

Look up boron on the periodic table and its mass reads 10,8. No nucleus can hold eight-tenths of a neutron — so where does the fraction come from? From the fact that an element is a family, not a single atom.

ISOTOPES are atoms of the SAME element — same atomic number, same proton count — with DIFFERENT numbers of neutrons, and therefore different mass numbers. Boron comes in two natural varieties: boron ten, with 5 protons and 5 neutrons, and boron eleven, with 5 protons and 6 neutrons. Same Z, different A. Because chemistry is run entirely by the electrons, and both isotopes carry identical electron arrangements, isotopes are chemically IDENTICAL — they differ only in mass.

The number printed on the table is the RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS: the weighted average of the isotope masses, weighted by each isotope's natural abundance. Weighted is the crucial word — this is not the halfway point; the more common isotope counts for more, exactly as a test that counts double drags your average toward it.

Calculate boron's. In nature, 20 percent of boron atoms are boron ten and 80 percent are boron eleven. Relative atomic mass equals 20 over 100 times 10, plus 80 over 100 times 11. That is 0,2 times 10, which is 2 — plus 0,8 times 11, which is 8,8. Sum: 10,8. There is the fraction: no atom weighs 10,8; the FAMILY averages 10,8, four parts heavy to one part light.

The reverse reading matters just as much: an average of 10,8 sitting far closer to 11 than to 10 announces at a glance that the heavier isotope dominates — and chlorine's famous 35,5, much nearer 35 than 37, whispers the opposite story. ## Subtopic: Energy Levels, Orbitals and Electron Configuration

The electrons around a nucleus are not free to sit anywhere. They occupy ENERGY LEVELS — numbered 1, 2, 3, 4 outward from the nucleus — and within each level, regions called ORBITALS. An orbital is a region of space where an electron is most likely to be found, and each orbital holds AT MOST TWO electrons. Two kinds concern Grade 10: the s orbital, a sphere around the nucleus, one per level, holding up to 2 electrons; and the p orbitals, arriving in sets of three from level 2 upward, together holding up to 6. So level 1 holds 2 electrons; level 2 holds 2 plus 6 — 8; level 3, for our purposes, offers 3s and 3p — another 8 — before the 4s begins.

The energy ORDER is the heart of it: 1s, then 2s, then 2p, then 3s, then 3p, then 4s. Mark the twist at the end — 4s fills BEFORE 3d, which is why the order stops at 4s for every element up to Z equals 20. Filling obeys three rules. Lowest energy first — the AUFBAU principle, the building-up principle. At most two electrons per orbital, and a pair must spin oppositely. And across a set of equal-energy p orbitals, electrons spread out singly before any pairing begins — Hund's rule; phosphorus, with three 3p electrons, parks one in each p orbital, all unpaired.

An AUFBAU DIAGRAM draws each orbital as a block or circle, stacked by energy, with electrons as arrows — an up arrow and a down arrow marking the opposite spins of a pair. Describe aluminium's aloud: 1s holds a pair; 2s a pair; the three 2p orbitals hold three pairs; 3s a pair; and one lone arrow sits in 3p. Count: 2 plus 2 plus 6 plus 2 plus 1 — 13 electrons, matching aluminium's 13 protons.

SP NOTATION compresses the same information into one line, electron counts as superscripts: aluminium is 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s two, 3p one. Practise toward the top of the range: potassium, 19 electrons — 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s two, 3p six, 4s one — the 4s quirk in action. Calcium, 20 electrons — the same line closing with 4s two. Always check by adding the superscripts back to Z. The outermost electrons — the VALENCE electrons — are the ones chemistry will spend, and the coming topics, the periodic table and bonding, are built entirely on counting them. ## Subtopic: The Stadium and the Pea

Put an atom on the centre spot of a big soccer stadium and inflate it until the atom fills the whole ground. The nucleus — the part holding all the protons and neutrons — is a single pea on the kickoff dot. Everything else, out to the highest row of seats, is the electrons' territory: almost completely empty space, patrolled by particles so light no scale bothers with them.

That pea is absurdly important for its size. It owns essentially ALL the mass of the atom — protons and neutrons each count one full unit, while an electron weighs about one two-thousandth of that, loose change nobody counts. And the pea owns all the POSITIVE charge, one plus per proton, while the stands hold the matching negatives — one electron for every proton — so from outside, the whole stadium reads neutral.

Why do the electrons stay in the stands? Opposite charges attract — the same electrostatics you met last term. The positive pea pulls on every negative electron, and that pull is the glue of the atom.

And how do we know the pea is really there? Scientists fired tiny, fast particles at foil thinner than a page, expecting them all to punch through soft, spread-out atoms. Nearly all did — an empty stadium — but roughly one in several thousand slammed into something and bounced back hard. You only bounce off a pea if there IS a pea: tiny, dense, charged. That bounce is how humanity discovered the nucleus.

Some questions on the stadium are with you now — what sits on the centre spot, what fills the stands, and who owns the mass.

## Subtopic: Twins With Heavier Backpacks

Two learners are identical twins: same face, same voice, same jokes — but one carries a heavier backpack. Across the playground you cannot tell them apart, and every teacher treats them identically. Only the clinic scale knows the difference.

That is isotopes. Two atoms of boron both carry 5 protons — 5 is boron's identity number, its face. Both run 5 electrons doing identical chemistry — the voice and the jokes. But one packs 5 neutrons in its nucleus and the other packs 6 — the backpacks. We name them by the total nucleus count: boron ten and boron eleven. Same element, same behaviour, different mass. Neutrons carry no charge, so the extra neutron changes nothing about bonding or reacting — only the weigh-in.

Now, the mysterious 10,8 on the periodic table. Nature's boron is a crowd of these twins: out of every 100 atoms, about 20 are the lighter twin and 80 the heavier. The table prints the crowd's AVERAGE mass — a weighted average, the same arithmetic as school marks when one assessment counts four times. Work it like marks: 20 hundredths of 10 is 2; 80 hundredths of 11 is 8,8; add them — 10,8. No single atom weighs that. The average leans hard toward 11 because the heavier twin outnumbers the lighter four to one — exactly the way a term mark leans toward the assessment that counted four times. And when you meet chlorine's 35,5 sitting close to 35, read it backwards the same way: there, the LIGHTER twin runs the playground.

Your questions on the twins are up now: what makes them the same, what makes them differ, and the average done the school-marks way.

## Subtopic: Filling the Hotel From the Ground Floor

Electrons around a nucleus live in a strange hotel, and three house rules decide every room allocation in chemistry.

The hotel: floors numbered 1, 2, 3, 4 going up — the energy levels, the ground floor nearest the nucleus and cheapest in energy. On each floor, rooms — the orbitals. Floor 1 has a single small room, 1s. Floor 2 has its own s room plus a row of three p rooms — 2s and 2p. Floor 3 repeats the layout: 3s and three 3p rooms. Every room, no exceptions, sleeps at most TWO electrons.

Rule one — fill from the bottom: electrons are lazy and always take the cheapest room available. The order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p — and then the famous quirk: the 4s room is actually slightly cheaper than the remaining floor-3 rooms, so 4s fills next. For every element up to number 20, that order is the whole story. Rule two — two per room, and roommates must spin opposite ways: drawn as one arrow up and one arrow down. Rule three — across the three identical p rooms, guests take empty rooms before anyone doubles up: strangers spread out first. Phosphorus proves it, one guest in each 3p room, all unpaired.

Now check in aluminium's 13 electrons. Two into 1s. Two into 2s. Six fill the three 2p rooms. Two into 3s. That is twelve; the thirteenth takes a 3p room alone. The register — sp notation — reads: 1s two, 2s two, 2p six, 3s two, 3p one. Potassium's 19 guests fill everything through 3p and then send one guest up to the bargain 4s room: the register ends 4s one; calcium's twentieth guest joins to close it at 4s two. Add the little numbers and you must recover the atomic number — that is your free self-check, every single time.

Why care about the register? Because the guests on the TOP floor — the valence electrons — are the only ones other atoms ever meet. Aluminium's three top-floor guests are the entire reason it forms a three-plus ion, and that story is exactly where the periodic table topic picks up. Last questions of the lesson are with you right now: floors, rooms, three rules, and a register that always adds up.
