# Part 1 — Expert

This session moves chemistry into its true home ground: water. Almost every reaction that matters to a living cell, a river or a laboratory happens in aqueous solution, and today builds the four skills that unlock all of it. First, WHY water pulls ionic solids to pieces — the polar molecule at work, and the dissolution equations that record the pulling. Second, electrolytes — which solutions carry an electric current, and precisely what a conductivity reading can and cannot claim. Third, precipitation — the startling moment when two clear liquids meet and a solid appears — written as balanced equations with every phase label in place. Fourth, the standard ion tests: the drill that names an invisible ion in an unknown sample, and the four reaction types that file the whole of chemical change into a tidy cabinet. Running through it all is one puzzle: a storeroom bottle whose label has washed off, holding a clear, colourless solution. By the end of the session we will have interrogated that bottle with two reagents and given its hidden ion a name.

## Subtopic: Water the Puller-Apart — Ions in Solution

Start with the solvent itself. A water molecule is electrically lopsided: its oxygen corner carries a small negative charge and its hydrogen corners carry a small positive charge. One molecule, two oppositely charged faces — chemists call this POLAR. A polar molecule is a grappling hook for anything built out of charge.

Now offer it something built entirely out of charge. Blue copper sulfate is an ionic solid: positive copper ions and negative sulfate ions bound into a rigid lattice. Drop a crystal into water and the water molecules swarm it, each one choosing its target by charge — oxygen faces turn toward the positive copper ions, hydrogen faces toward the negative sulfate ions. Pulled from every direction at once, ions are levered off the lattice edge one after another, and each escaping ion is immediately encased in a shell of clinging water molecules. That water shell is called HYDRATION; the whole breakup of the crystal into free ions is called DISSOLUTION, or dissociation. The spreading blue colour in the glass is not dye — it is the hydrated copper ion itself, drifting free.

Write the event as chemistry: CuSO4 solid, arrow, Cu two plus aqueous, plus SO4 two minus aqueous. The label s says solid; the label aq — aqueous — says dissolved in water, wearing its hydration shell. Phase labels are not decoration: an equation without them tells only half the story and loses credit accordingly.

Every dissolution equation must survive a double audit. Atoms must balance — and so must CHARGE. Copper contributes plus two, sulfate minus two, and the sum on the right is zero, matching the neutral solid on the left. Practise on magnesium chloride, where a coefficient appears: MgCl2 solid, arrow, Mg two plus aqueous, plus TWO Cl minus aqueous. Charge audit: plus two, plus twice minus one, equals zero. Balanced both ways. One more, the easiest of the day: potassium nitrate, KNO3 solid, arrow, K plus aqueous plus NO3 minus aqueous.

The questions for this section are with you now: the polar molecule described with its two charged faces, dissolution and hydration defined, the copper sulfate and magnesium chloride equations written with phase labels, and the charge audit performed on each.

## Subtopic: Electrolytes — Solutions That Conduct

Build this apparatus in your head and keep it for life: a beaker of solution, two carbon electrodes dipped in, wired through a battery to a small bulb. A glowing bulb means charge is crossing the liquid; a brighter bulb means more charge is crossing per second. This conductivity tester is the topic's central instrument.

Run three liquids through it. Distilled water: the bulb stays dead — pure water barely conducts. A spoon of table salt stirred in: the bulb lights strongly. A spoon of sugar in fresh distilled water: dead again, indistinguishable from pure water. Three clear, colourless liquids, two dark bulbs and one bright one — and explaining that difference is the entire concept.

A current in a solution is carried by mobile CHARGE CARRIERS, and in water those carriers are IONS. Salt dissociates into sodium ions and chloride ions, so salt water is a moving crowd of charges and the circuit completes. A substance whose solution conducts is called an ELECTROLYTE. Sugar dissolves generously — but as a molecular substance it enters the water as WHOLE, neutral molecules. Plenty of dissolved particles, none of them charged: no carriers, no current, no light. Sugar is a NON-ELECTROLYTE.

Brightness carries quantitative information: it tracks the CONCENTRATION of ions. Double the dissolved salt and the bulb burns brighter, because twice the carriers are available. That also lets conductivity rank how completely substances split into ions — strong electrolytes such as sodium chloride dissociate essentially totally; weak electrolytes only partially; non-electrolytes not at all.

Now the distinction that catches candidates every year: conductivity is NOT a general measure of solubility. Solubility asks how much of a substance the water can take in; conductivity asks whether what went in carries charge. Sugar scores spectacularly on the first test and zero on the second. A dark bulb never proves an empty solution — it proves only that nothing charged is swimming in it.

Take this section's questions now: the tester circuit described, electrolyte and non-electrolyte defined through ions, the salt-versus-sugar result explained at particle level, and the solubility-conductivity distinction stated without wobbling.

## Subtopic: Precipitation — When Two Clear Solutions Make a Solid

Two beakers, both holding clear, colourless liquid: barium chloride solution in one, sodium sulfate solution in the other. Pour them together and the mixture instantly clouds white — a fine solid materialises where there was only liquid. That solid is a PRECIPITATE, and the event is a precipitation reaction.

Zoom to the particles. Before mixing, each beaker was a crowd of free, hydrated ions: barium and chloride in the first, sodium and sulfate in the second. Mixing lets the crowds mingle, and one of the new pairings — barium with sulfate — forms a compound water CANNOT keep dissolved. Insoluble means the water shells are too weak to hold that pair apart, so every barium ion that finds a sulfate ion locks into solid barium sulfate and drops out of the liquid. The other new pairing, sodium with chloride, stays comfortably dissolved and never leaves the water — spectator ions, present but unchanged.

The full balanced equation, phase labels working hard: BaCl2 aqueous plus Na2SO4 aqueous, arrow, BaSO4 solid plus two NaCl aqueous. Audit it: one barium, two chlorines, two sodiums, one sulfur, four oxygens on each side — and the s on barium sulfate IS the white cloud you watched form.

This reaction family is called ION EXCHANGE: the positive ions swapped partners, and the swap was DRIVEN by one product escaping the solution as a solid. No insoluble pairing means no reaction at all — mix potassium chloride with sodium nitrate and every possible couple stays soluble; the ions simply mingle and nothing whatsoever happens.

So the practical question is which pairings are insoluble, and Grade 10 needs a short working list, not a database. Memorise this much: all nitrates are soluble — nitrate never, ever precipitates. Common sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds are soluble. The celebrated insolubles: the silver halides — silver chloride, silver bromide, silver iodide; barium sulfate; and most carbonates, such as calcium carbonate and barium carbonate. For colour, add one showpiece: lead iodide, the brilliant yellow precipitate that falls like golden snow when lead nitrate solution meets potassium iodide solution.

Work this section's questions now: precipitate defined, the barium sulfate equation balanced with labels, the driving force of ion exchange named, and the short-list applied to fresh pairs of solutions.

## Subtopic: The Ion Test Kit and the Four Reaction Types

Back to the storeroom bottle. A clear solution and no label — but precipitation, run deliberately, becomes identification. Each standard test forces a KNOWN insoluble pairing with a chosen reagent, and each begins with an acid step that eliminates impostors.

The halide tests: add dilute nitric acid to the sample, then a few drops of silver nitrate solution. A WHITE precipitate announces chloride — silver chloride. CREAM announces bromide — silver bromide. YELLOW announces iodide — silver iodide. White to cream to yellow, deepening down the group: the halide colour code.

The sulfate test: dilute nitric acid, then barium nitrate solution. A white precipitate that PERSISTS in the acid is barium sulfate, and the verdict is sulfate.

The carbonate test: add barium nitrate and a white precipitate appears — barium carbonate. Now add the nitric acid: this precipitate DISSOLVES with brisk effervescence, releasing carbon dioxide gas. White solid, gone in acid, with a fizz — that is carbonate's signature, and no other common ion fakes it.

Why does acid open every test? Because carbonate is the serial impostor. Silver carbonate and barium carbonate are both whitish and insoluble, so an untested carbonate could masquerade as chloride or as sulfate and send you home with the wrong name. Nitric acid destroys carbonate on contact — fizzing it away as carbon dioxide — so any precipitate that forms afterwards is genuine. The drill is fixed: acid first, reagent second, colour read third.

Interrogate the bottle. Dilute nitric acid in: no fizz — carbonate is eliminated. Silver nitrate in: the sample snows WHITE. Verdict: chloride. As a cross-check, a fresh portion with acid and barium nitrate gives nothing at all — no sulfate hiding underneath. Two reagents, one snowfall, and the bottle can finally be relabelled: a chloride solution.

Close the topic by filing all of chemical change into its four reaction types. Type one: PRECIPITATION — ion exchange driven by a solid leaving the solution; today's headline act. Type two: GAS-FORMING reactions — ion exchange driven by a gas escaping, exactly like carbonate fizzing in acid. Type three: ACID-BASE reactions — driven by protons handed from acid to base, giving salt and water. Type four: REDOX reactions — driven by ELECTRONS transferred between particles, and recognisable because atoms CHANGE CHARGE: a neutral magnesium atom becoming a plus-two magnesium ion has surrendered two electrons, and that charge change is the redox fingerprint. The first three are partner-swap cousins in which every ion keeps its charge; redox alone rewrites the charges. The full machinery of acids, bases and electron bookkeeping arrives in Grade 11 — today you carry the four names, their four driving forces, and the fingerprint.

The final questions of this part are with you now: each test matched to reagent, colour and conclusion, the acid step justified, the bottle's chloride verdict re-derived, and the four reaction types sorted by what drives them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chemistry at the kitchen sink — a salt cellar, a sugar bowl, and a glass of water with two very different plans for them.

## Subtopic: What Water Does to Salt

Stir a spoon of salt into water: gone. Stir a spoon of sugar into a fresh glass: also gone. Two identical disappearing acts on the surface — and two completely different crimes underneath. The difference between them powers this whole lesson.

First, meet the water molecule properly. It is a tiny two-faced thing: the oxygen side sits slightly negative, the hydrogen side slightly positive. The word is polar. Each face of the molecule is permanently hunting for the opposite kind of charge, like the two ends of a toy magnet.

Salt is a tower of charges — a crystal scaffold of positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions gripping each other. Lower that scaffold into a sea of two-faced molecules and the dismantling starts immediately: oxygen faces gang up on every sodium, hydrogen faces gang up on every chloride, and ion after ion is levered off the scaffold. The instant an ion comes free, water molecules close around it like a security detail. The escort has a name — hydration. The dismantling has a name — dissolution. Nothing was destroyed: the ions are all still there, separated, escorted, and swimming.

The one-line version: NaCl solid becomes Na plus aqueous plus Cl minus aqueous. Aqueous simply means "swimming in water". Read it aloud as a sentence: solid sodium chloride splits into swimming sodium ions and swimming chloride ions.

Sugar's crime is different. Water carries sugar molecules away from each other — but each molecule leaves WHOLE, intact and electrically neutral. Dissolved, absolutely. Split into charged pieces, never. Keep that difference warm; in the next section it switches a light bulb on.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: the two-faced molecule, who gets dismantled and who floats away whole, and what the little labels s and aq are telling you.

## Subtopic: The Light Bulb Detective

Here is a gadget simple enough to sketch in ten seconds: two wires into a glass of liquid, with a battery and a small bulb in the loop. If charges can travel through the liquid, the loop closes and the bulb glows. If they cannot, darkness.

Now the three-glass line-up. Plain water: dark. Salt water: bright. Sugar water: dark. The bulb is a detective, and the only thing it ever detects is IONS.

A current through a liquid needs free-moving charged particles. Salt water teems with them — every dissolved sodium and chloride ion is a swimming charge, and together they ferry the current across the glass. Substances like salt, whose solutions conduct, are electrolytes. Sugar water is crowded with dissolved particles too, but every one is a whole neutral molecule — nothing for the battery to grab. Sugar is a non-electrolyte. And the bulb keeps honest score: more salt, more swimmers, brighter glow. Brightness literally measures how many ions are in the water.

Here comes the favourite trick question: does a dark bulb prove that nothing dissolved? Absolutely not. Sugar dissolves magnificently and leaves the bulb stone dead. Dissolving is the skill of vanishing into water; conducting is the skill of carrying charge through it. Two separate talents. Salt has both. Sugar has only the first. A pebble at the bottom of the glass has neither.

One real-world echo before the questions: sports drinks shout about electrolytes because your nerves and muscles run on ion traffic, and sweat drains ions out of you. Same word the chemists use, same swimming charges — plus a marketing department.

Your questions for this section are up now: the detective circuit, why salt glows while sugar stays dark, what brightness keeps score of, and the dissolving-versus-conducting trap.

## Subtopic: Snow in a Glass — and Naming the Mystery Ion

The closing act is the best magic in Grade 10 chemistry — and it comes in two colours. Trick one: pour clear barium chloride solution into clear sodium sulfate solution, and white snow erupts in the glass. Trick two, the crowd-pleaser: pour clear lead nitrate solution into clear potassium iodide solution, and the snow falls GOLDEN — glittering yellow flakes sinking through the liquid.

No magic in either glass: a partner swap. Each solution was a crowd of swimming ions. Mix the crowds and new couples form — and some couples cannot swim. Barium with sulfate welds into solid barium sulfate; lead with iodide welds into solid yellow lead iodide. Water's grappling hooks are too weak to hold those pairs apart, so the couples lock solid and snow out. Chemists call the snow a precipitate. The leftover couples — sodium with chloride in one glass, potassium with nitrate in the other — swim on unbothered, which is why the liquid above the snow stays clear.

Master the snow and you can run the trick in reverse: use it to NAME an invisible ion, which is exactly what the storeroom bottle needed. The drill, in order. Splash in dilute nitric acid first: fizzing means carbonate just confessed, bubbling away as carbon dioxide. No fizz? Add silver nitrate: white snow says chloride, cream says bromide, yellow says iodide — the halide colour code. Still nothing? Add barium nitrate: white snow that ignores the acid says sulfate. The opening acid splash is the lie detector — carbonates make white snow too, so you fizz them out of the room first, and whatever snow falls afterwards is telling the truth. Our bottle: no fizz, then white snow with silver nitrate — chloride, case closed.

Last, the four family names, each remembered by what DRIVES it: precipitation — a solid escaping the water; gas-forming — a gas escaping, like the carbonate fizz; acid-base — protons handed across; and redox, the outsider — electrons handed across, which you spot because atoms change their charge. Partner-swap families keep every charge; redox rewrites them. One fingerprint sorts all four.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: the two-colour snow explained through couples that cannot swim, the full drill run on a mystery bottle — acid, silver, barium — and the four families matched to their driving forces. Replay the storeroom case once in your head, and this topic belongs to you.
