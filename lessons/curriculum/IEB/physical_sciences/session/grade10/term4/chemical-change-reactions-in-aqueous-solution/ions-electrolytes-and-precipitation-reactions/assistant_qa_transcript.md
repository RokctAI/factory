**Thandi:** Let's tackle the questions this topic always raises. First one: dissolving and dissociating get used as if they were the same word. Salt does both, sugar apparently only one — how should we keep the two verbs straight?

**Tutor:** Judge the particles AFTER the vanishing act, and each verb marks one side of the line. Dissolving is the general skill: a substance disperses into the water and disappears from view — salt does it, sugar does it, both glasses end up clear. Dissociating is the special extra step only ionic substances take: the crystal's charged particles separate into free ions. Salt is an ionic lattice, so its dissolving IS dissociating — NaCl solid becomes Na plus aqueous plus Cl minus aqueous, two kinds of charged swimmer, each wearing a hydration escort. Sugar is molecular: water separates its molecules from each other, but every molecule leaves whole and neutral, so sugar dissolves without dissociating. All the observable consequences hang on that distinction — salt water conducts because it holds mobile charges, sugar water cannot; salt is the electrolyte, sugar the non-electrolyte. In written answers, give each substance its correct verb and, for the ionic one, prove it with the dissociation equation, charges and aqueous labels included. That one habit shows the marker you see the particle picture rather than just the vanishing.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the statement that conductivity relates to the concentration of ions but is not a measure of solubility — that sentence trips people up every time. Can you take it apart?

**Tutor:** It is really two separate promises. The first promise is positive: among solutions that DO contain ions, the bulb keeps honest score. One spoon of salt against three spoons — the three-spoon solution carries triple the swimmers and glows visibly brighter, so conductivity faithfully ranks ion concentration. The second promise is a warning: never promote the bulb into a solubility meter, because the link snaps for any substance that dissolves without ionising. Sugar is the standing counterexample — it dissolves in enormous quantities and conducts precisely nothing, because its dissolved particles are neutral molecules. So a dark bulb is ambiguous on its own: perhaps little dissolved, or perhaps plenty dissolved and none of it ionic. Only other evidence separates those cases. The clean exam sentence goes: conductivity measures the concentration of IONS in a solution; solubility measures how much SUBSTANCE dissolves; the two agree only for electrolytes — and then you quote sugar as your one-line proof. Learn that trio of sentences and this mark is never in danger.

***

**Thandi:** Third one: in the ion tests, everyone remembers the reagents but not the order. Why must the nitric acid go in before the silver nitrate or barium nitrate — what actually breaks if you skip it?

**Tutor:** What breaks is the verdict — you convict the wrong ion with full confidence. Walk the failure through. Suppose the unknown actually holds carbonate and you leap straight to silver nitrate. Silver carbonate is also a whitish insoluble solid, so snow falls, and you announce chloride — wrong, and you have no way of knowing it. Barium nitrate fails identically: barium carbonate is white and insoluble, a flawless impostor for barium sulfate. Carbonate can fake the positive result of nearly every test in the kit, which makes it the serial impostor of Grade 10 chemistry. The acid is the unmasking step: nitric acid destroys carbonate instantly, fizzing it away as carbon dioxide. Both outcomes inform you. Fizzing means the case is already closed — the ion is carbonate. No fizzing means carbonate is certifiably absent, so every precipitate that appears afterwards is genuine. That is why the drill order is untouchable: acid and watch for fizz, then silver nitrate and read white, cream or yellow, then barium nitrate for the stubborn white of sulfate. The acid is not ritual; it is what makes the later observations admissible evidence.

***

**Thandi:** Last question: four reaction types sound like four things to memorise. Is there a shortcut for classifying a reaction quickly under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is a two-question algorithm. Question one, always first: did any atom CHANGE ITS CHARGE from the left of the equation to the right? Scan for it — a neutral magnesium atom appearing as magnesium two plus, a copper two plus ion appearing as neutral copper metal. If any charge changed, electrons were transferred and the reaction is REDOX; classification finished. If every ion keeps its charge, you are inside the partner-swap family, and question two sorts the members: what escaped or formed? A solid crashing out of solution makes it precipitation, driven by the insoluble product escaping. A gas bubbling away — carbonate fizzing carbon dioxide in acid is the model — makes it gas-forming, driven by the escaping gas. Acid meeting base to give salt and water makes it acid-base, driven by protons handed across. Notice the bonus: the second question's answer IS the driving force, which is usually the very next thing the question asks you to name. Two questions, four types, driving force included — that is the whole classification done in under thirty seconds.
