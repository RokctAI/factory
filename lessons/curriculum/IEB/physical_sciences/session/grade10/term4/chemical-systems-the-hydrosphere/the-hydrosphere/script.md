# Part 1 — Expert

This session closes the year's chemistry by aiming everything you have learned at one planetary system: the hydrosphere — every drop of water the Earth owns, in every state and every hiding place. Four goals. First, identify the hydrosphere properly: its three states, their locations, and the proportions that decide who gets to drink. Second, follow water across the borders it refuses to respect — into the atmosphere, through the lithosphere, and inside the biosphere. Third, weigh what happens when people wall a river into a dam: the taps it fills, the valleys it drowns, and how a scientist studies both honestly. Fourth, bring the laboratory home — testing a water sample with the ion tests you already command, and purifying it stage by stage. This topic is examined through application and interpretation rather than recall, so the skill being built today is THINKING with water.

## Subtopic: Identifying the Hydrosphere

The hydrosphere consists of all the water on Earth, and identification starts with the three states and where each one hides. Liquid water: oceans, rivers, lakes and wetlands — and the one learners forget, GROUNDWATER, the water soaked into the pores of rock and soil beneath the surface, the invisible store that boreholes and springs drink from. Frozen water: the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland, mountain glaciers, icebergs — and permafrost, ground in cold regions that stays frozen through every season with ice locked between its grains. Water vapour: the gas phase, invisible and everywhere in the atmosphere, the raw material from which every cloud condenses.

Now the proportions, because they turn a comfortable picture into an uncomfortable one. Roughly 97 percent of the planet's water is ocean — salt water, no good for drinking or irrigation unless someone pays for desalination. Of the thin fresh slice that remains, the majority is locked up as ice at the poles and in glaciers. The fresh, liquid, REACHABLE water — rivers, lakes and accessible groundwater, the share that every city, farm, animal and plant on land must divide — amounts to well under one percent of the total. So the Earth is drenched and humanity is thirsty, and both statements are true simultaneously. South Africa, drier than most of the world, lives that second statement daily — which is why the country meters water, prices it, and stores it behind walls.

One final identification, a favourite in written questions: the hydrosphere is a STORE, not a process. The water cycle is the set of processes that MOVES water between stores; the hydrosphere is the water itself, wherever it happens to be sitting right now. Keep the noun apart from the verb — the reservoir apart from the circulation.

The questions for this section are with you now: three states with two locations each, the 97 percent and the under-one-percent quoted and interpreted, groundwater and permafrost placed correctly, and the store-versus-cycle distinction stated cleanly.

## Subtopic: One Water, Four Spheres

Earth science names its systems like siblings: the atmosphere is the air, the lithosphere the rock and soil, the biosphere the living things — and the hydrosphere threads through all three by processes you must be able to name and chain together.

Crossing into the atmosphere: solar energy drives EVAPORATION from oceans, dams and wet soil, loading the air with invisible vapour. When rising air cools, CONDENSATION turns vapour into droplets and droplets into clouds, and precipitation hands the water back as rain, snow or hail. Warming lifts water; cooling drops it — every thunderstorm is the hydrosphere returning from a visit to the sky.

Crossing into the lithosphere: rain soaks downward through soil into rock, becoming groundwater that creeps through underground pores over years and decades. And the creeping is chemistry — this term's chemistry. Water is the polar solvent, so it DISSOLVES minerals from the rock it passes: calcium, magnesium, carbonates and chlorides climb aboard as aqueous ions. Where conditions change, the water DEPOSITS those minerals again — dripstone caves grow their hanging stone spikes exactly this way, one thin mineral layer per drip, over centuries. Dissolving and depositing, warming and cooling: four verbs that summarise all of water's work in motion.

Crossing into the biosphere: life is mostly water — your own body is roughly 60 percent water — and every reaction of biochemistry runs in aqueous solution, the same solution chemistry you practised with beakers. Plants pull water in through roots and release vapour through their leaves — TRANSPIRATION, a flow so large that forests visibly feed the clouds above them. Animals drink and excrete; every living cell is a miniature beaker of dissolved ions at work. No water, no life — not a poster slogan, but a location statement about where biochemistry is possible.

Now hold the loop as one story you can tell in order: ocean to sky by evaporation, sky to land by rain, land to underground by soaking — dissolving rock along the way — underground and overland back to the sea, with every plant and animal borrowing water at each stage. One water, four spheres, no exit.

Take this section's questions now: each sphere named with its water pathway, evaporation and condensation paired as warming and cooling, dissolving and depositing tied to groundwater and caves, and transpiration placed in the loop.

## Subtopic: Dams — the Price of Stored Water

South Africa deliberately interrupts its rivers, and this syllabus asks a genuinely human question: how does building a dam change the lives of people and the ecology of a region? It is a weigh-both-sides question, and one-sided answers lose the marks.

Why build the wall at all? Because rain arrives in seasons and thirst arrives daily. A dam banks wet-season water against the dry season: drinking water for cities, irrigation for farms, and at the large installations, hydroelectric power from released water spinning turbines. The Katse Dam in the Lesotho Highlands feeds water through mountains to Gauteng's millions; the Theewaterskloof Dam carries Cape Town through its winters — and when years of drought nearly emptied it, the city counted down to a feared Day Zero, a live demonstration of what stored water is worth. Reliable water, where and when people need it: the benefit is enormous and real.

Now the costs, weighed with equal seriousness. Upstream, the rising lake drowns a valley: grazing land, croplands, homes, sometimes entire communities with their histories, and the people are relocated whether they consent or not. Downstream, the river is diminished: natural flooding that once spread nutrient-rich silt across floodplains stops arriving; the seasonal pulses that cue fish spawning and bird breeding flatten into a managed trickle; the wall itself stands as a dead end to migrating fish. And the dam traps SEDIMENT: silt that built riverbanks and fertilised floodplains for millennia now settles uselessly behind the wall — slowly filling the reservoir from the bottom while starving the lands below. Around the still water, the ecology reshuffles again: water weeds and mosquitoes prefer a lake to a current.

How would a scientist actually STUDY these effects rather than assert them? The recommended methods are honest fieldwork: interview people who lived in the region for years before and after the wall — long memory is real data; consult the published literature about the area; and compare the river's ecology upstream and downstream of the dam. That is science pointed at a community question, and strong answers name benefits AND costs, then weigh them for the specific place instead of sloganeering.

Work this section's questions now: two benefits and four costs, each tied to its mechanism; the sediment trap explained in full; and the investigation methods that turn opinions about a dam into evidence.

## Subtopic: Testing and Purifying Water

The year's final chemistry job: decide whether a water sample is fit to drink, and make it so — using tools you already own.

What might be dissolved in the sample? Ions collected from rock, farmland and pipes: carbonates, chlorides, sulfates, nitrates and nitrites among them. Most are harmless in moderation, but some are warning lights. Nitrates and nitrites in a borehole usually point to fertiliser runoff or sewage leaking in, and they matter twice over: at high concentrations they threaten human health — infants most of all — and they overfeed the choking green algal blooms that smother enriched dams.

How do you test? Exactly as you tested the mystery bottles. Start with pH — acids and alkalis declare themselves on the scale from 0 through neutral 7 up to 14. Then run the ion drill: dilute nitric acid first, then silver nitrate — white snow announces chloride. Fresh sample, nitric acid, then barium nitrate — white snow that persists in the acid announces sulfate; and if the very first acid splash fizzed, carbonate confessed before any reagent was needed. Commercial test strips compress pH, nitrate and nitrite checks into a printed colour chart, and a microscope adds the biological layer: one drop of dam water is a crowded zoo, and a few of its citizens cause disease.

How do you purify? A treatment works runs stages in order, each catching what the previous stage cannot. SEDIMENTATION: stand the water still and let suspended grit sink. FILTRATION: pass it through beds of sand and gravel — filter paper and a funnel at laboratory scale — to trap fine particles. Then the stage no filter can perform: DISINFECTION. Microorganisms slip through any filter, so chlorine is dosed to kill them — the faint swimming-pool smell in tap water is that final stage announcing itself. At home during an outage, a rolling boil for a minute achieves the same kill. But mark what even boiling cannot do: DISSOLVED ions pass through every filter and survive any flame — remember how salt vanished into clear solution and returned only when the water evaporated away. Stripping dissolved salt requires distillation or another desalination process, which is exactly why purifying seawater is expensive and why the fresh fraction of the hydrosphere is precious.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the ion tests re-aimed at a water sample, nitrates read as a warning light, the three purification stages in order with the reason filtration alone is never enough, and the one contaminant that defeats both filter and flame.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same hydrosphere at human scale — one bucket, one very long journey, and one river with an argument running down both banks.

## Subtopic: The Bucket Planet

Imagine every drop of water the Earth owns poured into a single twenty-litre bucket. All of it: oceans, ice caps, rivers, clouds, the water under the ground and the water inside every living body. One bucket, whole planet.

Now inspect your stock. Just over nineteen litres of the bucket is sea water — salty, undrinkable, useless on a farm. Scoop out the fresh water and most of THAT is ice, beautiful and locked away at the poles and in glaciers. What is left — the fresh, liquid, reachable water in rivers, lakes and wells, the share that every tap, farm, elephant and maize plant on land must divide — comes to a few tablespoons. Tablespoons, out of twenty litres.

That is the hydrosphere in one image: a soaked planet and a civilisation surviving on the tablespoons. It is why a dry country like ours meters water and prices it, why cities build walls across rivers to bank the wet season against the dry one, and why "Earth has plenty of water" and "people do not have enough water" are both true sentences.

Learn where the tablespoons hide, too. Rivers and lakes are the visible part. A huge share of the usable fresh water is invisible — groundwater, held in rock under your feet the way water is held in a sponge; that is the store a borehole drinks from. And in the coldest lands, the soil itself stays frozen all year with ice locked in its pores — permafrost, water in a vault nobody can open. Liquid, ice and vapour; surface, underground and sky: that is the complete inventory.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: the bucket's proportions, where groundwater and permafrost belong, and why scarce and abundant are both the right word.

## Subtopic: The Longest Round Trip

Follow a single drop of sea water on the journey it has been repeating since long before human beings existed.

Morning sun warms the ocean off the east coast; our drop evaporates and rises as invisible vapour. High up, the air cools and the vapour condenses onto a dust speck — a cloud droplet now; warming lifted it, cooling caught it. The cloud drifts inland, piles against the mountains, and our drop falls as rain onto a high grassland slope. One border crossed already: hydrosphere into atmosphere and back.

On the ground it faces a choice. It could join a stream and sprint for the sea. Instead it soaks in — down through soil, into hairline gaps in the rock, joining the patient underground crowd called groundwater. And here the story becomes chemistry rather than geography: as the drop creeps through the rock, it DISSOLVES it. Polar little solvent that it is, it collects ions on the way — calcium here, magnesium there, a carbonate, a chloride — and carries them along. That is why borehole water tastes faintly of minerals, why kettles in some towns grow a white crust of scale, and how caves manufacture their stone icicles: water dissolving rock in one place and depositing it in another, drip by patient drip across centuries.

Years on, the drop seeps out of a hillside into a river. The roots of a maize plant drink it; it climbs the stalk and exits a leaf as vapour — transpiration, the plant's slow exhale — condenses into another cloud, and rains again. This time it lands in a dam, passes through a treatment works, and a boy fills his water bottle from a school tap and drinks. The drop is now IN the biosphere, running cell chemistry inside a human being — your body is more than half water, and every thought you think happens in solution.

Eventually — river by river, breath by breath — the drop returns to the sea, delivering its dissolved ions on arrival. And that delivery answers an old riddle: why is the sea salty? Because rivers have been couriering dissolved salts to the ocean for billions of years, while evaporation lifts only pure water back out and leaves every salt behind. The ocean is the planet's final ion collection point. Every sphere visited, nothing ever lost, the same water going around forever — the water in your glass has been ocean, cloud, rock, river, and almost certainly a dinosaur.

Your questions for this section are up now: the drop's crossings named — evaporation, condensation, soaking, dissolving, transpiration — and the riddle of the salty sea solved with the round trip.

## Subtopic: The River With Two Banks of Opinions

Last piece: build a dam in your imagination, then stand on each bank of the river and listen to the argument.

The case for the wall is short and forceful. Rain is seasonal; thirst is daily. A dam banks the flood against the drought: taps that still run in late winter, fields irrigated out of season, and at the big schemes, turbines spinning electricity from falling water. The Katse Dam sends mountain water all the way to Gauteng; Theewaterskloof kept Cape Town alive while a record drought marched it toward Day Zero. When the dry years come — and in this country they always come — the dam is the difference between restrictions and disaster.

Now cross the river and walk the costs, upstream to downstream. Behind the wall, a valley drowns: grazing, croplands, ancestral homes, sometimes whole villages — submerged, their people moved. Ask the elders who farmed there what the lake now covers; interviewing them is not sentiment, it is a recognised scientific method for studying a dam's impact, alongside reading the literature about the region. Downstream, the river is tamed: the floods that spread fertile silt across the plains stop coming; fish that swim upstream to breed meet a concrete full stop; and the silt itself piles up uselessly behind the wall, slowly filling the reservoir while the floodplains below grow hungrier. Still water rewrites the ecosystem again — more water weeds, more mosquitoes, different fish. The river below a dam is a different river.

So — is the dam good or bad? Wrong question, and strong candidates know it. The right questions: who gains, who loses, what does the ecology pay, and can the damage be softened — fish ladders through the wall, managed flood releases that mimic the seasons, fair compensation and genuine consultation for the relocated. A full answer names benefits AND costs and weighs them for the specific river. Slogans in either direction earn little; honest weighing earns everything.

And whichever bank convinces you, the stored water still has to pass the chemist's checkpoint before anyone drinks: settle it, filter it, then chlorinate or boil — because filters catch particles but never microbes, and boiling kills microbes but never removes dissolved salts. The tablespoons are precious; chemistry is how we keep them safe. Here come the last questions of the lesson: both banks argued, the sediment trap explained, the study methods named, and the three-stage clean-up in its fixed order.
