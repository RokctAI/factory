# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the chemistry revision sweep for the final examination — the year's matter and chemical change reorganised into the four toolkits that earn the marks: reading the atom and the periodic table, choosing the correct bond, representing reactions properly, and counting with the mole all the way into solution. Nothing today is new; everything today is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples selected to spring the classic traps in practice, where springing them is free.

## Subtopic: The Atom and the Periodic Table

Every atom answers to two numbers. The atomic number is the proton count — it names the element and never changes. The mass number is protons plus neutrons. In a neutral atom, electrons match protons. Isotopes are atoms of the SAME element with DIFFERENT neutron counts: boron-10 holds 5 protons and 5 neutrons; boron-11 holds 5 protons and 6 neutrons. Same chemistry, different mass — and the relative atomic mass printed on the periodic table, 10,8 for boron, is the weighted average of the natural mixture. Check it: about one fifth of natural boron is boron-10 and four fifths is boron-11, and 0,2 times 10 plus 0,8 times 11 is 2 plus 8,8, which is 10,8. The decimal on the table is not an error; it is a recipe.

Electrons occupy orbitals, written in s p notation. Aluminium, thirteen electrons: 1 s 2, 2 s 2, 2 p 6, 3 s 2, 3 p 1. Phosphorus, fifteen: 1 s 2, 2 s 2, 2 p 6, 3 s 2, 3 p 3. The outermost electrons — the valence electrons — conduct all the chemistry: aluminium offers three, phosphorus works with five, and reading that outer count straight off a configuration is a mark waiting to be collected.

The periodic table is the map. Columns are groups — families sharing a valence count and therefore a chemistry: group 1, the alkali metals; group 17, the halogens; group 18, the noble gases, outer shells full, reactions nearly none. Rows are periods — each new row opens one more energy level. And the two trends every final asks: ACROSS a period, atoms shrink and electronegativity climbs; DOWN a group, atoms grow and electronegativity falls.

This section's questions are with you now — the two defining numbers, the boron average checked, both configurations written, and the trends run in both directions.

## Subtopic: Bonding — Ionic, Covalent and Metallic

Three bonds, and the table's two sides decide between them. Metal with non-metal: ionic. Non-metal with non-metal: covalent. Metal with metal: metallic.

Ionic bonding is TRANSFER. Magnesium hands its two valence electrons to two chlorine atoms; magnesium becomes the ion M g 2 plus, each chlorine becomes C l minus, and the opposite charges lock into a crystal lattice: M g C l 2. Formulae follow from charge balance, and the crossover rule automates it: magnesium at 2 plus with nitride at 3 minus crosses to M g 3 N 2 — three magnesiums supply plus 6, two nitrides supply minus 6, and the compound audits to zero. Run that audit on every ionic formula you write; neutrality is not optional.

Covalent bonding is SHARING. Two non-metals both need electrons, so they hold pairs in common: nitrogen shares one pair with each of three hydrogens in N H 3; in carbon dioxide, carbon shares two pairs with each oxygen — a double bond on each side. And seven elements refuse to travel alone — hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine are DIATOMIC, written H 2, N 2, O 2 and so on whenever they appear as free elements. Recite the seven before every equation.

Metallic bonding is POOLING. Positive metal kernels stack into a lattice and tip their valence electrons into a shared, mobile sea. The sea explains the metallic lifestyle in one breath: free electrons carry charge, so metals conduct even as solids; kernel layers slide across each other without severing the bond, so metals bend and hammer instead of shattering.

The properties table follows the bonds like a shadow. Ionic compounds: high melting points, conducting ONLY when molten or dissolved — ions must be freed to move. Covalent molecular substances: low melting points and no conduction — no charged particles exist. Metals: conductors always. The exam runs this in both directions — given properties, name the bond; given the bond, predict the properties.

Take this section's questions now — the metal and non-metal decision made, two formulae built by charge balance, the diatomic seven recited, and each property traced to its bond.

## Subtopic: Representing Reactions — Change, Formulae and Balancing

First, the year's most quietly examined distinction. A physical change makes NO new substance: water freezing is still water, easily reversed. A chemical change makes NEW substances with new properties: calcium burning becomes calcium oxide, with energy released. In BOTH kinds of change, total mass is conserved — atoms are never created or destroyed, only reshuffled. That is the law of conservation of mass, and it is the entire justification for balancing.

A chemical equation is the sentence that records the reshuffle: reactants left, products right, state symbols in brackets — s solid, l liquid, g gas, a q dissolved in water.

Balancing obeys one absolute law: adjust COEFFICIENTS, never subscripts. Subscripts are identity — rewrite C O 2 as C O and you have changed carbon dioxide into carbon monoxide, a different substance with different chemistry. Balance propane burning, the braai-gas reaction. Unbalanced: C 3 H 8 plus O 2 gives C O 2 plus H 2 O. Carbon: three left, so 3 C O 2. Hydrogen: eight left, so 4 H 2 O. Now count oxygen on the right: six in the carbon dioxides plus four in the waters is ten, so 5 O 2 on the left. Balanced: C 3 H 8 plus 5 O 2 gives 3 C O 2 plus 4 H 2 O. Audit every element aloud: three carbons each side; eight hydrogens each side; ten oxygens each side. The audit line costs ten seconds and refunds entire questions.

One more, the metal-in-oxygen classic: calcium burns. Unbalanced: C a plus O 2 gives C a O. Oxygen: two left, one right — 2 C a O. Calcium: now two right, one left — 2 C a. Balanced: 2 C a plus O 2 gives 2 C a O; two calciums and two oxygens on each side. Balanced equations are the grammar of chemistry, and the next subtopic's arithmetic reads its ratios directly off them.

This section's questions are ready — the two changes told apart, state symbols placed, both equations balanced, and every audit spoken aloud.

## Subtopic: The Mole and Reactions in Solution

The mole is the chemist's counting unit: one mole is 6,02 times 10 to the 23 particles — Avogadro's number. Three formulas exchange between the exam's currencies. Mass: n equals m over M, with the molar mass M read from the periodic table. Solution: c equals n over V — concentration in moles per cubic decimetre. Gas at STP: one mole of any gas fills 22,4 cubic decimetres.

Run each exchange once. Carbon dioxide's molar mass: 12 plus two sixteens, giving 44 grams per mole; so 22 grams of carbon dioxide is 22 over 44, which is 0,5 moles. Sodium carbonate, N a 2 C O 3: two twenty-threes plus 12 plus three sixteens, which is 46 plus 12 plus 48, giving 106 grams per mole; 53 grams is 0,5 moles. Solution: 0,3 moles of salt made up to 0,6 cubic decimetres gives c equals 0,3 over 0,6, which is 0,5 moles per cubic decimetre. Gas: 2 moles at STP occupies 2 times 22,4, which is 44,8 cubic decimetres. Four exchanges, one hub — everything routes through moles.

Now carry the chemistry into water, the term's closing topic. Ionic compounds that dissolve DISSOCIATE into free ions: magnesium chloride splits into M g 2 plus and two C l minus, and the freed ions make the solution an ELECTROLYTE — it conducts. Sugar dissolves whole, no ions, no conduction. And when two solutions meet and some ion pairing is insoluble, a PRECIPITATE crashes out: lead nitrate meets potassium iodide and brilliant yellow lead iodide snows through the glass — P b N O 3 twice aqueous plus 2 K I aqueous gives P b I 2 solid plus 2 K N O 3 aqueous. Lead and iodide paired up and left the water; potassium and nitrate stayed dissolved as spectators.

The final questions of this part are with you now — all four exchanges run, the electrolyte explained by free ions, and the precipitation equation written with its state symbols.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision with the lab coat off — a crate of building bricks, a parts catalogue, and a kitchen scale that counts by weighing.

## Subtopic: Bricks, the Catalogue and Three Ways to Click

Picture every atom as a building brick, and the periodic table as the parts catalogue. The catalogue is not sorted alphabetically — it is sorted by BEHAVIOUR. Each column is a family of bricks that click identically, because they carry the same number of connection points: the valence electrons. Group 1 bricks carry one point; group 17 bricks sit one point short of full; group 18 bricks are complete and click with nothing — which is exactly why the noble gases spend the year on the bench.

A brick's serial number is its atomic number — the proton count, the one unchangeable fact. Isotopes are the same brick from a heavier batch: same clicks, same chemistry, extra neutrons in the core. The catalogue's decimal masses, like boron's 10,8, are just the average across the batches in the crate.

And there are exactly three ways bricks click. The swap: a metal brick with spare points meets a non-metal brick with gaps; electrons change owners, the bricks become charged, and they snap together — ionic. The share: two non-metal bricks, both short of full, grip a pair of electrons between them like two hands on one handle — covalent. The pool: metal bricks stack up and empty all their spare electrons into a communal sea that glues the stack — metallic.

Properties follow the click. Swapped bricks build hard crystals that conduct only when melting or dissolving sets the ions loose. Shared bricks build small separate molecules that melt easily and never conduct. Pooled bricks conduct always, because the sea never freezes over. Name the click and the properties recite themselves — in the exam, both directions of that sentence appear.

Your questions for this section are ready — the catalogue read by column, the three clicks told apart, and one property traced back to each.

## Subtopic: Counting Eggs by Weighing the Crate

Chemists face a counting problem: atoms are too small to count, yet reactions happen atom by atom. The fix is a trick every kitchen already uses — count by weighing. If one egg weighs 50 grams, then 600 grams of eggs is 12 eggs. Nobody counted; the scale did.

The mole is the chemist's dozen, scaled up absurdly: 6,02 times 10 to the 23 particles. The periodic table is the price list that makes the trick work — it quotes what one mole of each element weighs. Carbon: 12 grams per mole. Oxygen: 16. So one mole of carbon dioxide — a carbon and two oxygens — weighs 12 plus 32, which is 44 grams. Put 22 grams of the gas on a scale, and you KNOW, without counting, that you hold half a mole: hundreds of billions of trillions of molecules, tallied by grocery-store equipment.

Every mole formula is the same trick aimed through a different measuring tool. Solids: n equals m over M — the scale. Solutions: c equals n over V — the measuring jug, in moles per cubic decimetre. Gases at STP: 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole — the balloon. Three tools, one currency — and every exam question is a currency exchange: grams to moles, moles to concentration, moles to gas volume, always THROUGH the mole, never directly.

The exchange discipline in one line: convert INTO moles first, do the chemistry, convert OUT last. A question quoting grams on one side and cubic decimetres on the other is not difficult — it is two exchanges with a mole step in the middle, and recognising that is most of the answer.

Take this section's questions now — one egg-crate count, one exchange in each direction, and the mole standing in the middle of all of them.

## Subtopic: The Trap List and the Final Audit

Revision is mostly about the traps, because in the examination the chemistry is familiar but the traps are freshly painted. Five exhibits, each with its antidote.

Trap one: balancing by editing subscripts. Turning C O 2 into C O to force a balance does not balance carbon dioxide — it manufactures carbon monoxide. Antidote: subscripts are identity, coefficients are quantity; only coefficients move.

Trap two: forgetting the diatomic seven. Oxygen freed in a reaction leaves as O 2, never O; hydrogen gas is H 2. Antidote: recite the seven — hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine — whenever a free element enters an equation.

Trap three: volume units. Concentration demands cubic decimetres, and the question supplies 250 cubic centimetres. Divide by 1000: 0,25 cubic decimetres. Antidote: convert volume BEFORE touching c equals n over V, every time, no exceptions.

Trap four: feeding grams into mole ratios — dividing 53 grams by a coefficient from the equation. Equation ratios speak MOLES, never grams. Antidote: the exchange discipline — into moles, chemistry, out of moles.

Trap five: calling every dissolving a chemical change. Salt dissolving is physical — ions separate, no new substance forms, and evaporation returns the salt unchanged. Antidote: ask the one deciding question — did a NEW substance appear?

And the final audit, the habit that converts near-marks into full marks. Balanced an equation? Count every element on both sides, aloud. Computed with moles? Check the units cancelled into the unit you wanted. Predicted a precipitate? Name the two spectator ions still swimming. Every toolkit in this lesson ships with its own proof, and the proof costs nothing.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — five traps, five antidotes, and every answer audited before it leaves your desk.
