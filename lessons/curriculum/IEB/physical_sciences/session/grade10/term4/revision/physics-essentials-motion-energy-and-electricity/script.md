# Part 1 — Expert

This session is the physics revision sweep for the final examination — the year's mechanics and electricity reorganised into the four toolkits that earn the marks: describing motion with graphs and equations, running the energy ledger, commanding charge and current, and solving both circuit patterns with a compass-point of magnetism to finish. Nothing today is new; everything today is the exam-day version — compressed, ordered, and drilled through worked examples chosen to spring the classic traps here in practice, where springing them costs nothing.

## Subtopic: Motion — Graphs, Definitions and the Three Equations

Motion questions open with vocabulary, and the vocabulary comes in pairs. Distance is the full length of the path travelled — a scalar, size only. Displacement is the straight line from start to finish with a direction attached — a vector. Speed is distance over time; velocity is displacement over time, direction included. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a equals delta v over delta t.

Run this year's standard worked example. A minibus pulls away from rest and accelerates uniformly at 3 metres per second squared for 4 seconds. Final velocity: v equals u plus a t, which is zero plus 3 times 4, giving 12 metres per second. Displacement: delta x equals u t plus a half a t squared, which is zero plus a half times 3 times 16, giving 24 metres. Cross-check with the third equation: v squared equals u squared plus 2 a delta x — zero plus 2 times 3 times 24 is 144, and the square root of 144 is 12 metres per second. Two equations, one answer: that agreement is your proof, and writing it is a habit worth marks.

Now the graphs, because half the motion marks live in them. On a position-time graph the GRADIENT is the velocity: a straight slope means constant velocity; a curve means acceleration. On a velocity-time graph the gradient is the acceleration, and the AREA between the graph and the time axis is the displacement. Verify it on the minibus: its velocity-time graph is a straight line from zero to 12 over 4 seconds — a triangle of area a half times 4 times 12, which is 24 metres. Graph and equation tell one story, and questions love hearing it told both ways.

The recognition drill: three equations, and the given information selects the one you need. No displacement mentioned — v equals u plus a t. No final velocity — delta x equals u t plus a half a t squared. No time — v squared equals u squared plus 2 a delta x. List the knowns first, and the equation picks itself.

This section's questions are with you now — definitions paired, the minibus numbers reproduced, and the gradient and area rules applied.

## Subtopic: Energy — the Ledger That Never Lies

Two energy accounts, one total. Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object holds because of its position: E p equals m g h, with g at 9,8 metres per second squared. Kinetic energy is the energy of motion: E k equals a half m v squared. Mechanical energy is their sum, and the principle of conservation of mechanical energy states: in the absence of friction and air resistance, the mechanical energy of an object stays constant. In symbols: E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two.

Work the ledger on a 5-kilogram ball dropped from 8 metres, ground as reference. At the top: E p equals 5 times 9,8 times 8, which is 392 joules; E k is zero; total 392 joules — sealed for the whole fall. Halfway down, at 4 metres: E p equals 5 times 9,8 times 4, which is 196 joules, so E k must be the difference, 196 joules. Extract the speed: a half times 5 times v squared equals 196, so v squared equals 78,4, and v equals 8,85 metres per second, to two decimal places. At the ground: E p is zero, E k is the full 392 joules, so v squared equals 156,8 and v equals 12,52 metres per second.

Now the observation this topic loves to test. Set a half m v squared equal to m g h and the mass cancels: v squared equals 2 g h. Landing speed does not depend on mass — a 5-kilogram ball and a 50-kilogram drum from 8 metres both arrive at 12,52 metres per second. Heavier means more energy carried AND more energy needed for the same speed, in exactly cancelling proportion.

The fine print is a mark nearly every time: mechanical energy is conserved ONLY while no friction or air resistance acts. The phrases "ignore air resistance" and "frictionless" are the licence for the equation — underline them on sight. With friction present, total energy survives but mechanical energy leaks away as heat.

Take this section's questions now — both formulas, the three-snapshot ledger, the cancelled mass, and the exact condition on the word conserved.

## Subtopic: Electricity — Charge, Current and Potential Difference

Electricity begins with charge. Two kinds, positive and negative; unit, the coulomb. Charge obeys two unbreakable rules. Conservation: charge is never created or destroyed, only transferred. Quantisation: charge comes in whole-number multiples of the electron's charge, 1,6 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs. The formula Q equals n q converts between total charge and particle count: a sphere carrying minus 4,8 times 10 to the minus 19 coulombs holds 4,8 over 1,6, which is 3 excess electrons — always a whole number, never a fraction.

Conservation drives the classic identical-spheres question. Two identical conducting spheres, one at plus 8 nanocoulombs and one at minus 2 nanocoulombs, touch and separate. Total charge: plus 8 plus minus 2, which is plus 6 nanocoulombs; identical spheres share equally, so each carries plus 3 nanocoulombs. Audit it: total before, plus 6; total after, plus 6 — conservation confirmed in writing, which is a mark.

Now set the charge in motion. Current, symbol I, is the rate of flow of charge: I equals Q over delta t, and one ampere is one coulomb per second. If 20 coulombs pass a point in 5 seconds, the current is 4 amperes. Conventional current runs from the positive terminal through the external circuit to the negative, even though electrons drift the opposite way.

Potential difference, symbol V, is the work done per unit charge: V equals W over Q, in volts, and one volt is one joule per coulomb. If 30 joules are transferred as 2 coulombs pass through a resistor, the potential difference across it is 15 volts. The emf of a battery is the same quantity quoted at the source. And the instrument rules: an ammeter connects in SERIES, because the flow must pass through it; a voltmeter connects in PARALLEL across a component, because it compares energy on the two sides.

This section's questions are ready — Q equals n q run both ways, the spheres shared and audited, and current and potential difference defined word for word.

## Subtopic: Series, Parallel and the Magnetic Field

Ohm's law binds the three circuit quantities: V equals I R — potential difference equals current times resistance. Then every Grade 10 circuit is one of two patterns, with rules in mirrored pairs.

Series: one loop, no junctions. Current is the SAME everywhere; potential differences ADD to the emf; resistances add: R series equals R one plus R two. Work it: an 18-volt battery drives a 3-ohm and a 6-ohm resistor in series. Total resistance 9 ohms; current I equals 18 over 9, which is 2 amperes through both. Potential differences: 2 times 3 is 6 volts, and 2 times 6 is 12 volts — and 6 plus 12 hands back the battery's 18. That closing audit line is a mark; write it every time.

Parallel: branches side by side across the battery. Potential difference is the SAME across each branch — the full 18 volts; currents ADD at the junction. Branch currents: 18 over 3 is 6 amperes, and 18 over 6 is 3 amperes; total from the battery, 9 amperes. Effective resistance: R equals V over I, which is 18 over 9, giving 2 ohms — SMALLER than the smallest branch, always, because a second path eases the flow. Same two resistors, different pattern, completely different numbers: that comparison is the entire topic in one exercise.

Magnetism closes the year in three facts. A magnet carries two poles, north and south; like poles repel and unlike poles attract. The magnetic field is the region where the magnet exerts a force, drawn as field lines running from north to south outside the magnet, never crossing. And the Earth itself is a magnet, which is why a compass needle settles pointing north — and why a needle parked next to a bar magnet ignores the planet and follows the local field lines instead.

The final questions of this part are with you now — the mirrored rule pairs kept apart, both circuits fully solved, and the field-line facts stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same revision with the physics jacket off — three pictures that carry every method: a road-trip logbook, a two-pocket wallet, and water in pipes.

## Subtopic: The Logbook, the Wallet and the Water Pipe

Picture one: motion is a road-trip logbook. The odometer records distance — every winding kilometre, no direction, a scalar. The straight arrow from home to the destination is displacement — a vector. Speed is the odometer's reading per hour; velocity is the arrow per hour. And acceleration is the speedometer needle MOVING: 3 metres per second squared means the reading grows by 3 metres per second, every second. From rest that is 3, then 6, then 9, then 12 after four seconds — exactly the minibus from earlier, before any formula appears.

Picture two: energy is a wallet with two pockets. The height pocket holds m g h; the speed pocket holds a half m v squared. As long as nothing rubs — no friction, no air resistance — the wallet is sealed: coins move between pockets, but the total stands. Drop the 5-kilogram ball and 392 joules stream from the height pocket to the speed pocket all the way down: 392 plus zero, then 196 plus 196, then zero plus 392. If the pockets ever fail to add to the sealed total, an arithmetic slip is hiding — and the count exposes it before any marker can.

Picture three: a circuit is water in pipes. The battery is the pump, and its emf is the pressure it supplies. Current is the flow; resistance is a narrow section of pipe; potential difference across a component is the pressure drop from its entry to its exit. The water itself is never consumed — it circulates — but the pump's pressure is spent forcing the flow through the narrow sections. That is precisely why charge is never used up in a circuit while energy is.

Your questions for this section are ready — one picture matched to each topic, and the sealed wallet counted at three heights.

## Subtopic: Series and Parallel Without Tears

Push the water picture into the two circuit patterns, and the mirrored rules become impossible to swap.

Series is narrow sections one after another on a single loop. The same water threads through every section — so the current is identical everywhere; there is simply nowhere else to go. Each narrow section takes its own bite out of the pump's pressure, and the bites total the full pressure — potential differences add up to the emf. And two narrow sections in a row are harder to push through than one — resistances add, and the whole flow shrinks.

Parallel is a fork: two channels side by side, rejoining beyond. Each channel spans the pump directly, so each feels the FULL pressure — potential difference the same on every branch. The flow divides between the channels — more through the wider one — and the streams merge again at the junction: currents add. And the surprise worth repeating: opening a second channel makes the total flow EASIER, so the combined resistance drops below either branch alone. Two open gates pass more people than one, even when the second gate is narrow.

Now the two classic exam moments. Moment one: a second bulb is added in series with the first — BOTH dim, because total resistance rose and the shared current fell. Moment two: a second bulb is added in parallel — the original bulb keeps its full brightness, because it still spans the full emf, but the battery must now supply extra current and will drain sooner. Reason both through pressure and flow, and the answers write themselves.

Take this section's questions now — the fork against the single loop, and both bulb moments explained through the pipes.

## Subtopic: The Trap List and the Final Check

Revision is mostly about the traps, because in the examination the physics is familiar but the traps are freshly set. Five exhibits, each with its antidote.

Trap one: using 10 for g out of habit. Our value is 9,8 metres per second squared — with 10, the ball's 392 joules corrupts to 400, and the mark walks out with it. Antidote: write g equals 9,8 at the top of every energy and motion solution before anything else.

Trap two: forgetting to square the speed in kinetic energy, or dropping the half. A half times 5 times 12,52 squared is 392 joules — not 31,3, and not 784. Antidote: speak the formula while substituting — a half, times the mass, times the speed, squared.

Trap three: swapping the series and parallel rules under pressure. Antidote: one question before every circuit — is there a junction? No junction: series, same current, potential differences add. Junction: parallel, same potential difference, currents add.

Trap four: treating scalars as vectors and vectors as scalars — a displacement quoted without direction, or a direction pinned onto energy. Antidote: label each quantity scalar or vector before writing its answer line.

Trap five: the misplaced meter — an ammeter wired across a component, or a voltmeter wired into the loop. Antidote: the ammeter is a turnstile, the flow must pass through it; the voltmeter is a comparer, it stands across the gap.

And the closing habit, the one that turns near-marks into full marks: check by a second route. Displacement from the equation, then again as the area under the velocity-time graph. Speed from the energy ledger, then the wallet audit at every snapshot. Series potential differences summed back to the emf; parallel branch currents summed back to the battery. Every toolkit in this lesson carries its own proof, and the proof is free.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — five traps, five antidotes, and every answer checked by its second route.
