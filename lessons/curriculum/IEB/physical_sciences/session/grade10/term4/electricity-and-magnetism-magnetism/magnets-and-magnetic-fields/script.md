# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds magnetism from a single strange observation — a force that crosses empty space — all the way to the planet-sized magnet under your feet. Four pieces of equipment by the end. One: the precise definition of a magnetic field, and how it lines up beside the electric and gravitational fields you already carry. Two: the rule with no exception anywhere in nature — magnetic poles only ever come in pairs. Three: the craft of drawing and reading magnetic field lines, because in this topic the drawings ARE the marks. Four: the Earth's own magnetic field — why a compass works, why navigators track two different norths, and why the polar sky sometimes burns green. No formulas today: this topic is examined in words and diagrams, so precision of language does the work that algebra usually does.

## Subtopic: Force Without Touching — the Idea of a Field

Slide a magnet toward a steel drawing pin on a desk. At some invisible boundary the pin stirs, then leaps the last centimetre through the air. Nothing visible crossed the gap — no thread, no puff of wind — yet a force acted. Physics has one big idea for action at a distance, and it is called the field.

The definition, learned word for word: a magnetic field is a region in space where another magnet or a ferromagnetic material will experience a force. Notice what the definition claims — a REGION in space. Not a ray, not a substance, but a volume of influence wrapped invisibly around the magnet.

This is the third field of your Grade 10 year, and the three deserve one table in your mind. A gravitational field is a region in space where a mass experiences a gravitational force — it is why a dropped pen finds the floor. An electric field is a region in space where an electric charge experiences an electric force — it is why a rubbed ruler lifts scraps of paper without touching them. Line the three up and the grammar repeats perfectly: each is a region of influence; each acts on its own chosen victim — mass for gravity, charge for electricity, magnets and ferromagnetic materials for magnetism; and each works across empty space.

Now the differences, which are just as examinable. Gravity acts on every object with mass, and it only ever attracts. Electricity attracts or repels, because positive and negative charge exist and can exist SEPARATELY — a lone electron is pure negative charge. Magnetism also attracts or repels, but its two kinds — the poles — refuse to exist separately, a strangeness the next section takes on in full.

Which materials answer a magnetic field? The ferromagnetic ones: iron, steel, cobalt and nickel. Copper, aluminium, brass, glass, plastic, wood and paper feel nothing. That short list explains everyday puzzles: a magnet grips steel scissors but ignores a copper coin, and it pins a note to a steel cupboard straight through the paper, because the paper is invisible to the field and the steel behind it does the holding.

The questions for this section are with you now: the field definition stated exactly, the three fields matched to what each acts on, and the ferromagnetic short-list separated from the indifferent materials.

## Subtopic: Poles Come in Pairs — and the Pair Cannot Be Divorced

Every magnet carries two poles, named north and south. Suspend a bar magnet from a thread, wait for it to settle, and it aligns north-south; the end seeking geographic north is the magnet's north pole, the opposite end its south pole.

The interaction rule echoes electrostatics exactly: like poles repel, opposite poles attract. Bring north toward north and your fingers feel an invisible spring resisting; flip one magnet over and the same gap slams shut. Two magnets and thirty seconds teach the rule better than any paragraph.

Now the deepest experimental fact in the topic. Saw a bar magnet in half, intending to keep the north half. You fail — instantly. Each half is a complete magnet, wearing a brand-new south pole at the cut face. Halve the halves; same result. Grind the magnet to filings and every fragment still carries both poles. An isolated magnetic pole — a monopole — has never been observed anywhere, by anyone. Set the contrast with electrostatics deliberately: a lone negative charge sits in every atom, but a lone magnetic north exists nowhere in nature.

The explanation lives inside the material. Every moving electron in any substance generates a tiny magnetic field. In most materials those miniature fields point randomly and cancel to nothing. In ferromagnetic materials, the tiny fields align across small neighbourhoods called DOMAINS. An unmagnetised steel nail has its domains pointing every which way; magnetise the nail and the domains swing into agreement, their fields adding into one net field. And now the cutting puzzle dissolves: the magnetism is a PATTERN of aligned domains running through the entire material, not a north substance stored at one end. Cut anywhere and each piece still contains aligned domains — a complete, two-poled magnet. There was never a north half to keep.

The domain picture pays two everyday dividends. Drop a magnet on a hard floor and it weakens — the jolt knocks domains out of alignment. Stroke a steel needle repeatedly with a magnet, always in the same direction, and it becomes magnetic — stroke by stroke you are recruiting its domains into agreement.

Take this section's questions now: the interaction rule applied to pole pairs, the fate of the sawn magnet stated with its reason, and the domain story told for both the ordinary nail and the magnetised one.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Invisible — Magnetic Field Lines

The field is invisible, so physics draws it — and the drawings are governed by four rules that markers apply strictly.

Rule one, direction: outside a magnet, field lines run FROM north TO south, and every line must wear an arrowhead saying so. Rule two, spacing: where lines crowd together the field is strong; where they spread apart it is weak — so lines pack densest at the poles, the strongest places on any magnet, and thin out with distance. Rule three: field lines never cross; a crossing would assert two directions at a single point, which is nonsense. Rule four: the real field fills three-dimensional space all around the magnet — our page drawings are flat slices of a 3-D pattern.

Build the bar-magnet drawing in your head, arrows included. Lines fountain out of the north pole in every direction, sweep through the surrounding space in long arcs, and dive into the south pole. Tight and crowded at both poles; broad and sparse far away. Practise until your hand produces it automatically — symmetrical arcs, arrows from north to south, dense at the poles.

Two desktop experiments make the invisible visible. Scatter iron filings on a sheet of paper laid over a bar magnet: each filing becomes a miniature magnet, twists to lie along the local field, and the whole pattern of arcs assembles itself in seconds — fur at the poles, sweeping curves between. But filings have no arrowheads: they reveal shape, not direction. For direction, ring the magnet with small plotting compasses; each needle's north end points along the field line at its location, from the magnet's north around toward its south. Filings for shape, compasses for direction — say which does which and the mark is yours.

Two magnets together produce the exam's two favourite drawings. Opposite poles facing each other: the lines pour from one magnet's north straight across the gap into the other's south — a dense bridge of field connecting them; that is what attraction looks like on paper. Like poles facing each other: the lines bend sharply away from the gap, refusing to connect, and directly between the poles lies a point of empty calm — a NEUTRAL POINT where the two fields cancel; that is repulsion drawn.

Work this section's questions now: the four rules, the bar-magnet pattern with correct arrows, filings against compasses, and the two-magnet drawings matched to attraction and repulsion.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is a Magnet

Magnetise a sewing needle, rest it on a cork floating in a bowl of still water, and watch. Slowly, deliberately, the needle swings around and settles pointing north-south. Spin the cork; the needle returns. Nothing visible touches it — so what is turning the needle? The planet is.

The Earth behaves as though a gigantic bar magnet were buried along its axis: field lines emerge near one geographic pole, arc through the space around the planet, and re-enter near the other. A compass is nothing more than a lightweight magnet on a nearly frictionless pivot, and like any magnet in a field it rotates to align with the field it sits in. Anywhere on Earth, that means settling along the planet's field lines — and that single sentence is the complete working principle of the instrument that guided ocean navigation for centuries.

Here is the twist worth two marks. The compass end that points north is itself a NORTH pole. Opposite poles attract — so the place it points toward must behave, magnetically, like a SOUTH pole. The Earth's magnetic pole in the northern hemisphere is magnetically a south pole. We still name that place "magnetic north" because it is where north-seeking needles aim, but the physics behind the name is an attraction between opposites.

Now separate two norths that questions love to confuse. The GEOGRAPHIC North Pole is the fixed point where the Earth's rotation axis meets the surface — the anchor of every map. The MAGNETIC north pole is the point compasses seek: it lies hundreds of kilometres from the geographic pole, and it WANDERS year by year as the molten iron churning in the outer core rearranges the field. Serious navigation corrects for the angle between the two norths.

Finally, what the field does beyond steering needles. The Sun exhales a continuous stream of charged particles — the solar wind — which, unopposed, would strip our atmosphere over time. The Earth's magnetic field deflects most of that stream around the planet: a shield the size of a world. Near the poles, some particles are funnelled down along the field lines into the upper atmosphere, where their collisions set the sky glowing in curtains of green and red — the auroras. And when the Sun erupts violently, the arriving blast shakes the whole field — a magnetic storm — which can garble radio signals, damage satellites and trip power grids.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the compass explained as a small magnet in a planetary field, the two norths kept apart, the south-pole-in-the-north argument run from attraction, and the shield, auroras and storms each tied to the field.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same magnetism from your own kitchen and pencil case — a cupboard door, a pin that refuses to be divided, and a needle that always knows where north is.

## Subtopic: The Invisible Bubble

Take a magnet off the steel cupboard door and bring it back slowly. A little before contact, you feel the door start pulling — and then it snatches the magnet out of your fingers with a click. Pause on how strange that is: the grab happened BEFORE the touch. Something was already reaching across the air.

That something is the magnet's field — an invisible bubble of influence filling the space around it. Inside the bubble, magnets and things made of iron or steel feel the force; outside it, nothing does. And the bubble is not uniform: it is fiercest right at the magnet and fades quickly with distance, which your own fingers can map — lift the magnet slowly off the door and feel the exact height where the tug lets go.

You already live inside two other invisible bubbles, so this is family business. The Earth's gravity bubble grabs anything with mass — step off a kerb and it collects you. A rubbed ruler wears an electric bubble that grabs charge — hold it over torn paper and the scraps jump. Magnetism is the third sibling, and it is the fussiest about guests: its bubble only grips other magnets and a short VIP list of metals — iron, steel, cobalt, nickel.

That VIP list decodes your whole kitchen. The magnet grips the steel door and holds the shopping list against it — right through the paper, because paper is invisible to the field and the steel does the gripping. The same magnet slides straight off the aluminium pot, ignores the copper wiring, and could not care less about glass, plastic or wood. Not on the list, not invited.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: what the bubble is, where it is strongest, the two sibling bubbles you already knew, and which materials make the VIP list.

## Subtopic: The Pin You Cannot Divorce

Every magnet has two ends with two names — north and south — and one rule of manners: like poles shove each other away, opposite poles pull together. Test it with two magnets from the fridge: one way around they snap together eagerly; flip one, and they wrestle your fingers, skidding sideways rather than touching — two norths refusing to meet.

Now for the experiment that sounds trivial and is actually impossible. Take a magnetised pin — north at the point, south at the head. Snip it in the middle, planning to pocket just the north half. Check your half: it has grown a brand-new south end exactly at the cut. Snip again — same trick. Grind the pin to dust and every speck is a complete two-poled magnet. No laboratory on Earth, however equipped, has ever isolated a lone north or a lone south.

Why is the divorce impossible? Because a magnet was never north-stuff welded to south-stuff. Inside the steel, every electron is generating a whisper of magnetic field. In ordinary steel the whispers point in all directions and cancel into silence. In a magnetised pin, they have synchronised — neighbourhood by neighbourhood; the neighbourhoods are called domains — like a stadium crowd all doing the wave in time. The pin's magnetism IS that synchrony, spread through the entire metal. Cut the stadium in half and both halves are still doing the wave. There was never a north half to keep — only one pattern running through the whole.

The crowd picture explains two tricks you can try. Drop a magnet hard on concrete and it comes up weaker — the shock knocked part of the crowd out of rhythm. Stroke a steel needle with a magnet, tip to tail, always the same way, twenty times — and the needle becomes a magnet, because every stroke talks more domains into joining the wave. You will use exactly that needle in the final section.

Your questions for this section are up now: the push-pull rule, the fate of the snipped pin, and the crowd-doing-the-wave picture that explains both the cutting and the stroking.

## Subtopic: The Needle That Knows North

Take the needle you magnetised by stroking, push it through a slice of cork, and float it in a bowl of still water. Watch it swing — slowly, purposefully — and settle pointing north-south. Nudge it; it comes back. Every time. That floating needle is a homemade compass, and the question that unlocks the section is: who keeps turning it?

The planet does. The Earth acts like an enormous bar magnet buried along its core, wrapping the entire world in one gigantic magnetic bubble. Your needle is a tiny magnet sitting inside that bubble, and — like every magnet in every field — it rotates until it lies along the field lines around it. A compass is simply a needle that is free to answer the planet.

Keep the exam's favourite twist ready: the needle end that points north is itself a north pole, and since opposites attract, the spot in the far north it aims for must behave like a magnetic SOUTH pole. We still call that spot magnetic north — it is where north-seeking needles point — but you should be able to argue the south-pole logic out loud. And that spot is not the geographic North Pole: the map's pole is the fixed point of the Earth's spin, while the needle's pole sits hundreds of kilometres away and drifts as the molten iron in the core slowly stirs the field. Two norths, one for maps and one for needles — and navigators correct for the gap.

The planet's bubble earns its keep far beyond navigation. The Sun blasts out an endless storm of charged particles, and without protection that storm would sandblast our atmosphere thin. The Earth's field bends the storm around the planet like a boulder parting a river. Near the poles, some particles slip down along the field lines and strike the high atmosphere, setting it glowing in slow green and red curtains — the auroras, which are the shield making itself visible. And when the Sun erupts hard, the whole shield rings like a struck bell — a magnetic storm — and radios, satellites and power grids can stumble.

So the cupboard-door magnet and the burning polar sky are one physics: fields you cannot see, poles that come only in pairs, and lines you can draw. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the floating needle explained, the two norths kept apart, the south-pole-in-the-north argument spoken aloud, and the glowing shield given its proper name.
