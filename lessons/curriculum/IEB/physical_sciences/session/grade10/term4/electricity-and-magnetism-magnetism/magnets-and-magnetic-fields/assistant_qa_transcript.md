**Thandi:** Let's work through the questions that always come up with magnetism. First: the field definitions for gravity, electricity and magnetism sound almost identical, and people lose marks mixing them. What is the safest way to learn all three?

**Tutor:** Learn them as one sentence template with two blanks, because that is genuinely what they are. The template: a BLANK field is a region in space where a BLANK experiences a force. Fill the blanks in matched pairs — gravitational field, mass; electric field, charge; magnetic field, another magnet or a ferromagnetic material. Recite the template once and the three definitions come free, each worth its mark when quoted exactly. Then attach the two differences that questions probe. Difference one: what the field can do. Gravity only attracts; electric and magnetic fields both attract AND repel, because each has two kinds — positive and negative charge, north and south pole. Difference two, the deep one: separability. The two kinds of charge exist separately — a lone electron is pure negative charge, sitting in every atom. The two kinds of pole have never existed separately anywhere. So when a question asks you to compare fields, structure the answer as template, victims, then the two differences — and for the magnetic case, always name the ferromagnetic short-list, iron, steel, cobalt and nickel, because listing the victims precisely is usually its own mark.

***

**Thandi:** Next, the domain model. It feels like a story we're just told to believe. What is it actually FOR — what questions does it let us answer that we couldn't answer without it?

**Tutor:** The domain model earns its keep by explaining four separate observations with one picture, and exam questions are drawn from all four. The picture: every electron generates a tiny magnetic field; in ferromagnetic materials these align across small regions called domains; in an unmagnetised object the domains point randomly and cancel; in a magnet they agree. Observation one — cutting: a sawn magnet yields two complete magnets, because alignment runs through the whole material; there is no north substance to isolate, which is why monopoles are never found. Observation two — magnetising: stroking a steel needle one way with a magnet recruits domains stroke by stroke into agreement, which is how you make a compass needle from a sewing kit. Observation three — demagnetising: dropping a magnet or heating it strongly knocks domains back into disorder, weakening it — jolts scramble the crowd. Observation four — selectivity: only materials whose electrons can form domains respond at all, which is the ferromagnetic short-list. When a question says EXPLAIN, walk the chain: electrons, tiny fields, domains, aligned or random — then land on the specific observation asked about. The model is not decoration; it is the reason-giving machine for this whole topic.

***

**Thandi:** Third: field-line diagrams. When we draw the bar magnet, or two magnets together, what do markers actually check — and where do people throw marks away?

**Tutor:** Markers check the four rules like a pilot's pre-flight list, so audit your own drawing the same way. One: arrows — every line must carry an arrowhead running from north to south outside the magnet; miss the arrows and the direction mark is gone, reverse them and worse. Two: spacing that means something — lines crowd at the poles, where the field is strongest, and spread with distance; evenly spaced lines everywhere tells the marker you have memorised a shape without the meaning. Three: no crossings, ever — one point in space has one field direction. Four: symmetry — a lopsided fountain suggests guessing. For two-magnet diagrams, each configuration has one signature the marker's eye goes straight to. Opposite poles facing: a dense bridge of lines crossing the gap from north into south — connection is the drawing of attraction. Like poles facing: lines bending sharply away on both sides and a clean, empty NEUTRAL POINT midway between the poles — refusal is the drawing of repulsion, and labelling that neutral point is frequently a mark on its own. And keep the filings-versus-compasses distinction ready: filings show the pattern's shape but carry no arrows; small compasses supply the direction. Shape from filings, direction from compasses — one sentence, one mark.

**Thandi:** Last one: the Earth. The statement that the pole in the north is really a magnetic south pole feels like a trick. Can you give us a way to reason it out instead of memorising it?

**Tutor:** Reason it in three short steps from things you already trust, and it stops being a trick. Step one: the end of a compass needle that points north is, by naming convention, the needle's NORTH pole — that is literally how the ends got their names. Step two: the interaction rule — opposite poles attract; like poles repel. Step three: the needle's north end is ATTRACTED toward the Arctic. Combine: whatever sits in the Arctic and attracts a north pole must be a magnetic SOUTH pole. The place keeps the name magnetic north because it is the location north-seeking needles aim for — the name describes the needles' behaviour, not the pole's identity. Rehearse those three steps aloud once and you can reconstruct the answer under pressure instead of trusting memory. Then hold the second distinction beside it: the GEOGRAPHIC pole is the fixed point of the Earth's spin axis, the anchor of maps; the MAGNETIC pole sits hundreds of kilometres away and wanders as the molten iron of the outer core stirs the field — which is why navigators correct between the map's north and the needle's north. Two norths, one attraction argument, and the whole Earth section is yours.
