# Part 1 — Expert

This session assembles the most powerful bookkeeping tool in Grade 10 physics: mechanical energy, and the law that locks its total in place. The two ingredients are already yours — gravitational potential energy, E p equals m g h, and kinetic energy, E k equals one half m v squared. Today they fuse into a single quantity, mechanical energy, governed by a single principle: while friction and air resistance are absent, that total cannot change. The worked problem that carries the whole method is a 4-kilogram ball nudged off a rooftop 15 metres up, and by the end we will read its complete energy account — and its speed — at the top, at the halfway mark, and at the ground, without touching a single equation of motion.

## Subtopic: Two Accounts, One Total — Defining Mechanical Energy

Recall the two energies an object can hold from where it sits and how it moves. Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object has because of its position in the gravitational field relative to a chosen reference point: E p equals m g h — mass, times the gravitational acceleration of 9,8 metres per second squared, times height above the reference point. Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because of its motion: E k equals one half m v squared — half the mass times the speed squared. Both are measured in joules; both are scalars, carrying size but never direction.

Mechanical energy is nothing more exotic than their sum: E M equals E k plus E p. The definition earns a mark in almost every assessment, so say it exactly — the mechanical energy of an object is the sum of its gravitational potential energy and its kinetic energy.

Feed the rooftop ball its numbers. Mass 4 kilograms, height 15 metres, held at rest at the edge. Potential energy: E p equals m g h, which is 4 times 9,8 times 15, giving 588 joules. Kinetic energy: at rest, v is zero, so E k is zero. Mechanical energy at the top: E M equals zero plus 588, which is 588 joules.

One quiet decision hid inside that calculation: the height was measured from the ground, which means the ground was CHOSEN as the reference point where h equals zero. The choice belongs to you — the ground, a window sill, the lowest point of a swing — but once made it binds the entire problem. Physically, only energy DIFFERENCES matter; the reference point merely fixes where the counting starts.

The questions for this section are with you now: both formulas in symbols and words, mechanical energy defined as a sum, the 588 joules reproduced, and the unit and scalar nature of every energy on the page.

## Subtopic: The Law of Conservation and Its Mechanical Special Case

Begin with the law that governs the whole universe, not just falling balls. The law of conservation of energy states: energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred from one object to another or converted from one form to another. Totals are never minted and never shredded — energy changes hands and changes costume, nothing more.

Now the special case that powers today. In the absence of air resistance and friction — in the absence of any dissipative force — the mechanical energy of an object moving in the Earth's gravitational field is constant: it is conserved. This is the principle of conservation of mechanical energy, and it owns an equation: E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two — kinetic plus potential at the first position equals kinetic plus potential at the second. Positions one and two are ANY two points of the journey you choose to compare.

Be precise about what the principle claims. It does not say the potential energy holds still, nor the kinetic — both change every instant of a fall. It says the SUM holds still: each joule leaving the height account arrives in the speed account at the same moment, all the way down. The accounts trade continuously; the total is frozen.

And read the fine print the way a marker does. "Conserved" applies to MECHANICAL energy only while no friction or air resistance acts. Let a dissipative force into the problem and mechanical energy bleeds away as heat and sound — total energy still conserved, because the universal law never fails, but the mechanical total shrinks. Questions grant the safe case with the phrases "ignore air resistance" and "frictionless"; underline those words whenever they appear, because they are your licence to write the equation at all.

Take this section's questions now: the universal law stated word-perfectly, the mechanical special case with its condition attached, the two-position equation, and the difference between total energy always conserved and mechanical energy conserved only without dissipation.

## Subtopic: The Falling Ball — Working the Energy Ledger

Run the 4-kilogram ball's entire fall through the ledger, and watch one principle deliver what the equations of motion would need three separate setups to match.

Position one, the rooftop: E p is 588 joules, E k is zero, E M is 588 joules. Air resistance is ignored, so that total is now sealed for the whole journey.

Position two, halfway down at 7,5 metres. Potential energy: E p equals 4 times 9,8 times 7,5, which is 294 joules — half the height, half the potential energy. The principle insists the mechanical energy is still 588 joules, so the kinetic energy must be the gap: E k equals 588 minus 294, which is 294 joules. Exactly half the account has migrated from the height column to the speed column. Extract the speed: one half times 4 times v squared equals 294, so 2 v squared equals 294, giving v squared equals 147, and v equals 12,12 metres per second, to two decimal places.

Position three, the instant before impact. Height zero, so E p is zero and the full 588 joules sits in the kinetic account. One half times 4 times v squared equals 588, so v squared equals 294, and v equals 17,15 metres per second — roughly 62 kilometres per hour, which is why rooftops deserve respect.

Now audit the journey in one spoken line: top, 588 plus zero; halfway, 294 plus 294; bottom, zero plus 588. Three snapshots, one unmoving total. That audit line is exactly the presentation that earns full method marks, and it costs five seconds to write.

One more observation — the kind that upgrades a learner into a physicist. Solve symbolically: one half m v squared equals m g h, and the mass cancels from both sides, leaving v squared equals 2 g h. The landing speed of a freely falling object does not depend on its mass. A 4-kilogram ball and a 40-kilogram toolbox dropped from 15 metres both arrive at 17,15 metres per second. The heavier object carries more energy — but demands proportionally more energy for the same speed, and the two effects cancel exactly.

Work this section's questions now: the ledger at all three positions, the 12,12 and the 17,15 recovered from energy differences, and the cancellation that frees the final speed from the mass.

## Subtopic: Reference Points, Leaks and the Habits That Bank the Marks

Compress the method into four steps that work on any conservation problem. Step one: check the licence — friction and air resistance absent, stated or clearly implied. Step two: choose the reference point where h equals zero — usually the lowest point in the problem — and declare it in writing. Step three: write E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two and fill in all four terms, entering zero EXPLICITLY wherever an energy vanishes. Step four: solve for the lone unknown and attach its unit — joules for energy, metres per second for speed.

Now the traps, each named for the mark it eats. Trap one: using 10 for g out of habit — this course works with 9,8 metres per second squared, and 588 joules would corrupt into a wrong 600. Trap two: forgetting to square the speed in kinetic energy, or dropping the half — one half times 4 times 12,12 squared is 294 joules, not 24,24. Trap three: switching reference points mid-problem — measuring the first height from the ground and the second from a balcony feeds the ledger two incompatible currencies; one floor, one problem. Trap four: applying conservation of mechanical energy where friction openly acts — when a surface is described as rough, the mechanical total is NOT constant, and the correct statement is that mechanical energy decreased because some transferred to heat. Trap five: giving energy a direction — energy is a scalar; attaching an arrow to it advertises confusion.

Where does the tool travel next? Everywhere that matters. In Grade 11 it partners with work and forces; in Grade 12 the work-energy theorem and dissipative-force calculations sit at the heart of matric mechanics, and conservation reasoning threads through momentum, electricity, even the physics of light. The habit you are building — total in, total out, every leak named — is permanent equipment.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four steps in order, g at 9,8, the squared speed with its half, one reference point per problem, and the precise condition under which the mechanical total may change.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same law with the physics jacket off — because you have been running this exact accounting since the first time you kept coins in two pockets.

## Subtopic: The Two-Pocket Wallet

Picture a wallet with exactly two pockets, carried by every object that moves near the Earth. Pocket one is the HEIGHT pocket: money lands in it whenever the object climbs, and the balance is the mass times 9,8 times how high the object sits above whatever floor you measure from. Pocket two is the SPEED pocket: money lands in it whenever the object speeds up, and the balance is half the mass times the speed times the speed again.

Physics calls the money energy and counts it in joules. It names the height pocket gravitational potential energy and the speed pocket kinetic energy — and the whole wallet, both pockets counted together, is mechanical energy. That is the entire definition: tip out both pockets, add the coins, and the sum is E M.

Here is the rule the lesson stands on: while nothing rubs — no friction, no air resistance — the wallet is SEALED. Coins may stream between the two pockets as fast as they like, but not one cent can enter or leave the wallet itself. Drop something, and money pours from the height pocket into the speed pocket the whole way down. Toss something upward, and the stream reverses. The total never moves.

That is why physicists say the total is CONSERVED — kept safe. And behind the sealed wallet stands the grander law: in the entire universe, energy is never created and never destroyed; it only changes owners and changes outfits. The wallet is that cosmic law wearing its friction-free special case.

One small decision precedes all counting: you choose where "the floor" is — the level where height equals zero. Ground, table top, bottom of the slide: any choice works. The only crime is switching floors halfway through a sum.

Quick check before anything falls — the questions on this piece are with you now: which pocket is which, what seals the wallet, and who decides where zero height lives.

## Subtopic: Counting the Ball's Money

Now count the rooftop ball properly, coin by coin. A 4-kilogram ball at rest, 15 metres above the ground. Floor chosen and declared: the ground.

At the top, open the wallet. Height pocket: 4 times 9,8 times 15, which is 588 joules. Speed pocket: not moving, so zero. Wallet total: 588 joules. Seal it — this problem ignores air resistance, so 588 is the fixed total for the entire trip, and every question that follows is really asking where those 588 joules are sitting.

Halfway down, at 7,5 metres. The height pocket has thinned to 4 times 9,8 times 7,5, which is 294 joules — half the height money spent. A sealed wallet means the missing 294 joules moved, not vanished: speed pocket, 294 joules. Convert that to a speedometer reading: half of 4 times the speed squared equals 294, so the speed squared is 147, and the speed is 12,12 metres per second.

The instant before the ground: height pocket empty, all 588 joules in the speed pocket. Half of 4 times the speed squared equals 588, so the speed squared is 294, and the speed is 17,15 metres per second — around 62 kilometres per hour, which is why nobody juggles under rooftops.

Admire the economy of it. No stopwatch, no acceleration, no equations of motion — one total counted once at the top, then tracked pocket to pocket. Read the three snapshots out loud like a bank statement: 588 plus nothing; 294 plus 294; nothing plus 588. If the two pockets ever stop adding to the sealed total, an arithmetic slip is hiding in your work — and the statement catches it before any marker does.

Your questions for this section are up now: count the wallet at the top, track the coins at halfway, and squeeze the landing speed out of the bottom pocket.

## Subtopic: Where the Money Leaks

Two loose ends remain, and they are precisely the two things assessments love to probe.

First: why did the ball's mass never touch its speed? A 40-kilogram toolbox shoved off the same roof lands at exactly the same 17,15 metres per second. Think it through in wallet terms. The toolbox starts far richer — ten times the joules in its height pocket. But speed is expensive for heavy objects: filling the speed pocket to any given reading costs ten times the joules too. Richer wallet, pricier speed, in perfect proportion — the two effects cancel to the last cent. That is why, without air resistance, heavy and light objects fall together: a truth that startled people for centuries and still startles candidates who have not run the cancellation themselves.

Second: the wallet's seal is a promise with conditions. Let the moving object rub against anything — rough ground, thick air — and the wallet springs a leak. The leaked joules are not destroyed; the universe never destroys energy. They dribble away as heat and a whisper of sound — press a hand near a bicycle's rim brake after a long downhill stop and you are feeling exactly where the speed money went. But leaked joules sit in neither pocket, so the MECHANICAL total falls, and the sealed-wallet equation turns false. That is why every question that intends you to use it says "ignore air resistance" or "frictionless": that phrase IS the seal. Underline it like it pays you directly — because it does.

Carry the whole lesson in three sentences. Two pockets: height money, m g h, and speed money, half m v squared. Sealed wallet: no rubbing means the total only shifts between pockets, never changes. Leaks are heat: friction drains the mechanical wallet, though never the universe.

Here come the last questions of the lesson: why mass cancels, where leaked energy truly goes, which words in a question guarantee the seal — and one full wallet count from roof to ground, done the two-pocket way.
