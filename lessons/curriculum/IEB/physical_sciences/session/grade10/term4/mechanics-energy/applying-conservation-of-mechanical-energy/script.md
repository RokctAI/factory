# Part 1 — Expert

This session puts the principle of conservation of mechanical energy to work on the four classic situations this topic examines: objects dropped or thrown vertically, the swinging pendulum, roller coasters, and inclined planes. The centrepiece problem is a 500-kilogram roller-coaster car that crosses the top of a 20-metre hill at 4 metres per second, and the question every version of this topic asks: how fast is it moving at the bottom? One principle answers that, answers the pendulum, answers the ball thrown skyward, and answers the water slide — and it does all of it while ignoring the shape of the track completely, which is the superpower we are here to earn.

## Subtopic: The Working Tool — Two Points and One Equality

The tool is one equality: E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two. Kinetic plus potential at the first point equals kinetic plus potential at the second — valid whenever friction and air resistance are absent. Everything in this lesson is that sentence, applied with discipline.

The discipline is four moves. Move one: choose the two points to compare — crest and base of the hill, release point and lowest swing of a pendulum, launch and peak of a throw. The choice is yours; take the points the question actually asks about. Move two: set the reference level where height is zero, and put it at the LOWER of your two points whenever possible, so no height turns negative. Move three: write out all four energy terms, using E p equals m g h and E k equals one half m v squared, and write zero EXPLICITLY wherever a term dies — at rest means E k is zero; on the reference level means E p is zero. Move four: solve for the single unknown left standing.

Respect the zeros, because they carry the physics. A DROPPED object starts with E k zero. An object at the PEAK of vertical flight has, for one instant, zero speed and zero E k. A pendulum at the BOTTOM of its arc has E p zero — provided your reference sits there. Every celebrated special case is just the master equality with the correct terms switched off.

And notice what the equality never requests: the time, the acceleration, the path. Energy accounting is blind to how the journey unfolded — which makes it faster than the equations of motion wherever both could work, and the ONLY working tool once the path curves.

The questions for this section are with you now: the equality itself, the four moves in order, and which energy term is zero at a drop, at a peak, and at the bottom of a swing.

## Subtopic: Straight Up and Straight Down — Throws and the Pendulum

Application one: the vertical throw. A ball leaves a hand at 14 metres per second, straight up. Ignoring air resistance, how high does it climb? Point one is the hand — make it the reference level, so E p one is zero and E k one is one half m times 14 squared. Point two is the peak, where the ball hangs for an instant: E k two is zero, E p two is m g h. The equality reads: one half m times 196 equals m times 9,8 times h. The mass cancels — as it always does when it survives in every term — leaving 98 equals 9,8 h, so h equals 10 metres exactly. A clean answer is not suspicious; it is a gift.

Run the film in reverse and the drop comes free: an object falling 10 metres from rest arrives at 14 metres per second. Without air resistance the upward and downward journeys are perfect mirror images — the speed at any height on the way up equals the speed at that same height on the way down. That symmetry is examined in its own right; state it as a sentence and it is a mark.

Application two: the pendulum. A bob on a string is drawn aside until it hangs 0,45 metres higher than its lowest point, then released. Speed at the bottom of the swing? The bob rides an arc — a curved path with changing direction — and the equations of motion surrender immediately. The energy tool does not even notice the curve. Point one, the release: E k zero, E p equals m g times 0,45. Point two, the bottom: E p zero, E k equals one half m v squared. Cancel the mass: v squared equals 2 times 9,8 times 0,45, which is 8,82, so v equals 2,97 metres per second.

The bob then climbs the far side, repaying every joule, and rises to exactly 0,45 metres — never a millimetre more, because there is no energy to buy more height. A real pendulum sags lower with every pass precisely because air resistance skims a little mechanical energy each swing; it is the leak that dies away, never the principle.

Take this section's questions now: the throw worked to its 10 metres, the mirror symmetry stated, the pendulum's 2,97 metres per second recovered, and the reason the far side can never out-climb the release height.

## Subtopic: The Roller Coaster — Working the Big One

Now the centrepiece, presented exactly as full method marks demand.

A 500-kilogram roller-coaster car crosses the crest of a 20-metre hill at 4 metres per second. Ignoring friction, calculate its speed at the bottom of the hill.

Step one, points and reference: point one the crest, point two the base — and the base is the reference level, h equals zero.

Step two, the account at point one. Kinetic: E k one equals one half times 500 times 4 squared, which is one half times 500 times 16, giving 4 000 joules. Potential: E p one equals 500 times 9,8 times 20, which is 98 000 joules. Mechanical energy at the crest: 102 000 joules.

Step three, the account at point two. Potential: zero, by our choice of reference. So the entire 102 000 joules must sit in the kinetic term: one half times 500 times v squared equals 102 000.

Step four, solve. One half times 500 is 250, so 250 v squared equals 102 000, giving v squared equals 408, and v equals 20,20 metres per second — roughly 73 kilometres per hour.

Two traps live inside this exact problem, and both appear wherever it is set. Trap one: discarding the 4 000. The car was already moving at the crest, and dropping its starting kinetic energy gives v squared equals 392 and a wrong 19,80 metres per second — close enough to look right, which is precisely what makes it lethal. When an object starts in motion, E k one is NOT zero. Trap two: cancelling the mass on reflex. Mass cancels only when it multiplies every term — here it does, and dividing through by 500 leaves one half v squared equals 8 plus 196 — but the moment a question asks for an ENERGY, in joules, the mass must stay in the working.

Halfway checks run beautifully here too. At 8 metres on the descent: E p equals 500 times 9,8 times 8, which is 39 200 joules, so E k equals 102 000 minus 39 200, which is 62 800 joules, and v squared equals 251,2, giving v equals 15,85 metres per second. Any point of the track surrenders its speed this way — the ledger at full power.

Work this section's questions now: the four steps reproduced on the coaster, the 20,20 confirmed, the halfway speed at 8 metres, and both traps named and sidestepped.

## Subtopic: Inclined Planes and the Independence of Path

Application four: the inclined plane — and inside it, the deepest fact of the topic.

A child starts from rest at the top of a water slide 3,2 metres high and swooshes to the pool at the bottom. Ignoring friction, the arrival speed falls straight out of the ledger: m g h at the top becomes one half m v squared at the bottom, the mass cancels, v squared equals 2 times 9,8 times 3,2, which is 62,72, and v equals 7,92 metres per second.

Now the fact. Nothing in that working mentioned the slide's length, its steepness, or its curves. A straight plunge of 3,2 metres, a long lazy chute dropping 3,2 metres, a corkscrew flume 3,2 metres deep — identical arrival speeds, 7,92 metres per second, every time, while friction stays out of the story. In conservation problems the path travelled can be ignored: the only quantities the ledger prices are the object's speed and its height above the reference point. Steeper only means SOONER — the steep slide delivers its speed in less time — never faster at the bottom. Energy accounting does not bill for time; it bills for height.

This is also the engineering grammar of every roller coaster skyline: each successive hill must stand lower than the first crest, because the car's entire budget was banked there, and a later hill taller than the budget simply cannot be climbed. Real rides shave every later hill by more than geometry demands, because real tracks tax the account with friction on every metre.

The boundary of the tool, stated once and precisely: the moment a question admits friction or air resistance, mechanical energy stops being conserved, and E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two becomes false. At Grade 10 such questions ask you to REASON — to state that mechanical energy decreased and that the difference transferred to heat — not to calculate the loss; the calculating machinery arrives with the work-energy theorem in later grades. Know the boundary, and the principle will never be applied where its licence has lapsed.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the slide's 7,92 metres per second, path independence stated as the syllabus states it, the roller-coaster skyline logic, and the exact moment the conservation equation loses validity.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same principle at the playground and the water park, where you have been collecting experimental data your whole life without writing any of it down.

## Subtopic: The Swing Knows Your Height

Go back to the swing you rode as a child. Someone draws you back and UP — and on release you sweep down the arc, fastest at the very bottom, then up the far side, slowing, until you hang weightless for one heartbeat at the top of the swing. Then the whole show runs backwards.

That swing is a savings account with two kinds of money, and you can feel every transaction. Drawn back and up: height money loaded, speed money zero. Bottom of the arc: height money spent to nothing, speed money maxed — which is exactly why the bottom is where your stomach lurches. Far side, top of the rise: speed money traded back into height money, and for an instant you own no speed at all.

And you already know the crucial detail from riding it: the far side lifts you to the SAME height you were released from. Never higher. No trade can leave you richer than you started — the swing owns only the money it was handed at release. Physics states it formally: along a frictionless swing, mechanical energy is conserved, so release height equals far-side height.

Why does an unpushed swing slowly die? Air brushes past you on every pass, and each brush is a tiny withdrawal, paid out as warmth into the air. Small leak, many passes: the arcs shrink until the account is empty. Nothing was destroyed — it was spent, coin by coin, on stirring the air.

And the numbers, because they are wanted in the exam: released from 0,45 metres above the lowest point, bottom speed equals the square root of 2 times 9,8 times 0,45 — 2,97 metres per second — and rider size is irrelevant, because heavier riders carry more money and face proportionally higher prices, which cancels exactly.

Quick check — the questions on this piece are with you now: where the speed peaks, why the far side never wins, and where a dying swing's energy actually goes.

## Subtopic: Only the Drop Counts

Now to the water park, for the most surprising fact in the lesson.

Two slides empty into the same pool. One is a near-vertical drop that makes queues go quiet. The other is a long, winding, gentle flume. Both start 3,2 metres above the water. Two riders push off from rest at the same moment, one on each. Who hits the pool faster?

Neither. Both arrive at exactly 7,92 metres per second. The steep-slide rider arrives SOONER — steep means the trade happens quickly — but not faster. Arrival speed is bought entirely with height, and both riders spent the same 3,2 metres. The slide's shape is just the shop's decor; the price and the purchase are identical.

That is what your syllabus means by saying the path can be ignored: in energy accounting, only two facts about a position ever matter — how high, and how fast. Track length, slope angle, twists and tunnels: invisible to the ledger. It is also why the energy method outruns the equations of motion on any curve: equations of motion demand a straight line and constant acceleration; the ledger demands only a starting balance.

Run the roller coaster in this language and it nearly solves itself. The car crosses the first crest of 20 metres already rolling at 4 metres per second: its account holds 4 000 joules of speed money plus 98 000 of height money — 102 000 in total. At the base, every joule is speed money: half of 500 times the speed squared equals 102 000, and the speed is 20,20 metres per second. The one classic blunder: dropping the 4 000 because the car was "basically at rest". It was not — it was crossing at 4 metres per second, and the ledger counts every coin it carries over that crest.

Your questions for this section are up now: the two-slide race called correctly, the reason the ledger ignores the path, and the coaster's starting kinetic energy kept safely in the sum.

## Subtopic: Why the Second Hill Is Always Lower

Last piece — study the skyline of any roller coaster and read it like a bank statement.

The first hill always stands tallest. Everything the ride will ever do is purchased at that first crest: the chain hauls the car up, loading the account, and from that moment gravity runs the entire show. Every later hill must cost less than the balance — a hill taller than the first would demand more height money than the car ever owned, and the car would stall on the climb, roll backwards, and strand a train of very vocal passengers.

In practice every later hill is lower by MORE than the sealed-account arithmetic requires, because real tracks rub. Wheels, bearings, air: every metre of track collects a small toll, paid in heat and noise, so the balance falls all ride long. Designers measure the leak and trim each hill to fit beneath the sinking balance. At the end, the brakes confiscate whatever money remains — and the warm smell near the platform is the final line of the statement.

This one picture answers the topic's favourite reasoning questions in a breath apiece. Why can a pendulum never out-climb its release? Same balance, no income. Why does a ball thrown up at 14 metres per second return to the hand at 14 metres per second, ignoring air resistance? Perfect trade out, perfect trade back. Why does the water-slide rider eventually drift to a stop in the pool? The rubbing toll drains the account. When may the beautiful equality be used at all? Only while the account is sealed — the words "ignore friction and air resistance" ARE the seal, and without them the equality is a false statement.

So leave with three sentences. One: energy problems are two snapshots and one unchanging total — pick your two points, count height money and speed money at each. Two: only the drop counts — never the path, never the time, and usually not even the mass. Three: rubbing is the only thief, and it steals into heat, never into nothing. Here come the last questions of the lesson: the coaster skyline explained, the seal recognised, and one full two-snapshot count from crest to base.
