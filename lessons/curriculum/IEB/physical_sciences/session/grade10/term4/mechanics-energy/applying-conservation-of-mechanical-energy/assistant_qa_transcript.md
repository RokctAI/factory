**Thandi:** Time for the questions this topic always raises. First: with four different applications — throws, pendulums, coasters, inclines — how do we stop each one feeling like a brand-new method to learn?

**Tutor:** By noticing that there is no new method anywhere — only one equality wearing four costumes. Every single problem is: choose two points, write E k one plus E p one equals E k two plus E p two, kill the dead terms, solve. What changes between applications is only WHICH terms die. The throw: launch point has all kinetic, peak has all potential — E k two is zero because the ball pauses. The pendulum: release has all potential, bottom has all kinetic — and the arc does not matter, because the tool never looks at paths. The coaster: the one where usually NOTHING dies at point one — the car crosses the crest already moving, so both terms live, and that is exactly where the famous trap waits. The incline: release from rest at height, arrive at the reference — potential becomes kinetic, mass cancels, v squared equals 2 g h. So instead of four methods, memorise one method plus a zero-map: for each scenario, ask which of the four terms is zero and why. Practise writing all four terms EVERY time, including the zeros with their reasons — "at rest, so E k one is zero" — because those explicit zeros are both marks on the page and the proof to yourself that you consulted the physics rather than a memory.

**Thandi:** Next, that coaster trap — forgetting the kinetic energy at the crest. Why is it so common, and what habit actually prevents it?

**Tutor:** It is common because the wrong answer looks so respectable. Drop the 4 000 joules of crest kinetic energy from the 500-kilogram car and you get v squared equals 392 instead of 408 — a speed of 19,80 instead of 20,20 metres per second. Nothing about 19,80 smells wrong; no alarm rings. Compare that with dropping a factor of ten somewhere, which produces nonsense you would catch instantly. Traps that produce plausible answers are the dangerous ones. The preventing habit is mechanical, not motivational: always write the full account at point one BEFORE touching point two, as two labelled lines — E k one equals one half times 500 times 4 squared, equals 4 000 joules; E p one equals 500 times 9,8 times 20, equals 98 000 joules — then draw a box around their sum. Once 102 000 sits boxed on your page, it cannot be half-remembered as 98 000 three lines later. The deeper reading habit: when a question gives you a speed at the starting point, that number was given FOR a reason — an examiner rarely spends words on decoration. A given initial speed is a flag that E k one is alive. Circle it as you read, and the trap defuses itself.

**Thandi:** Third: path independence. Two slides, same height, same arrival speed — it works in the calculation, but WHY does the track's shape truly not matter?

**Tutor:** Think about what the ledger actually tracks, and the mystery dissolves. Mechanical energy bookkeeping records only two properties of a position: how high, and how fast. Nothing else is ever written into the accounts. The track's shape influences the JOURNEY — how long it takes, which direction you face, how hard the seat presses on you — but none of those are entries in the energy ledger. Height money converts to speed money at a fixed exchange rate set by gravity, and the exchange rate does not care whether the conversion happened in one terrifying second on a vertical drop or over ten lazy seconds on a winding flume. Same 3,2 metres of height spent; same 62,72 in v squared purchased; 7,92 metres per second either way. Steeper only concentrates the exchange into less time — sooner, never faster. And keep the boundary in the same breath: the argument holds because a frictionless track cannot charge for distance. A REAL track does charge for distance — friction takes a toll on every metre — so on real slides the longer path arrives slightly slower. That is not the principle failing; that is the principle's licence expiring, exactly as advertised whenever the words "ignore friction" disappear from the question.

**Thandi:** Last one: reasoning questions — "explain why the pendulum never rises above its release height", "explain why the second hill is lower". What structure earns full marks on those?

**Tutor:** Use a three-sentence skeleton: budget, claim, boundary. Sentence one establishes the budget — where the object's mechanical energy came from and how much: the pendulum was given m g h at release; the coaster banked its total at the first crest; the ball received its kinetic energy from the hand. Sentence two makes the claim through the sealed total: since friction and air resistance are absent, mechanical energy is conserved, so the object can never occupy a state costing more than its budget — a greater height than the release, a hill taller than the first crest. Sentence three closes the boundary if the question involves real-world decay: in reality air resistance transfers a little mechanical energy to heat on every pass, so the swing sinks, the coaster's later hills are trimmed extra, the bouncing ball loses height — mechanical energy decreased, total energy did not. Budget, claim, boundary — three sentences, each usually its own mark. And resist the urge to decorate with formulas the question did not request: a reasoning question is marked on the logic chain, and the cleanest chain is the one that names the principle, applies its condition, and stops.
