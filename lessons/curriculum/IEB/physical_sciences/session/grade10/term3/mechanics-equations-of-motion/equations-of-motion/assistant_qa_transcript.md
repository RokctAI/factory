**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! First one: do we really have to memorise all four equations, or is there a shortcut? Some people are panicking about mixing them up.

**Tutor:** Memorise them, yes — but memorise them WITH their absences, because the absence is what makes each one usable. Learn them as a matched set of four sentences, each tagged by the character it leaves out: the no-distance equation, the no-final-speed equation, the no-time equation, the no-acceleration equation. Recite them that way and the panic dissolves, because you never again face the question "which equation?" cold — the problem itself tells you. List what is given, note what is wanted, and the fifth character, the one nobody mentioned, points its finger at exactly one equation. There is also a quiet consolation: the four are not four separate laws but one description viewed four ways — each can be built from the others with algebra. So if your memory wobbles in an exam, two remembered equations can regenerate a third. But the working habit to drill is tag-by-absence: say "no time in it" as you write down v f squared equals v i squared plus two a delta x, and the equation arrives already justified — which is precisely what method marks reward.

**Thandi:** Next: someone solved the whole bakkie problem beautifully but got it wrong because they used 72 for a speed given as 72 kilometres per hour. Why is the conversion such a big deal?

**Tutor:** Because the equations speak one language — metres and seconds — and 72 kilometres per hour is a sentence in a different language. Feed it in raw and every later line inherits the corruption: distances balloon, times shrink, and the final answer is wrong by the same factor throughout, even though the algebra was flawless. The conversion itself is worth understanding rather than memorising. Seventy-two kilometres per hour means 72 000 metres covered in 3 600 seconds; divide, and you get 20 metres per second. That ratio — 1 000 over 3 600 — is where the 3,6 comes from: kilometres per hour divided by 3,6 gives metres per second, always. And a slower answer to a faster question: multiply by 3,6 to go back the other way. Anchor the conversion with a sanity landmark: 36 kilometres per hour is 10 metres per second, so highway speeds live around 30-ish metres per second. If you ever see "120 metres per second" describing a car, an unconverted number is almost certainly hiding in the working. Convert at the data-listing stage, before the physics begins, and this whole family of errors disappears.

***

**Thandi:** Then a sharp one: the two roads to the time both gave exactly 15 seconds. Does the check ever actually catch anything, or do the roads always agree automatically?

**Tutor:** They agree automatically only when every step is right — that is exactly what makes the check meaningful. The two roads share the original data but not the intermediate work: road one runs through the acceleration you calculated; road two, through the average velocity, never touches it. Suppose you had slipped upstream and found the acceleration to be 1,0 instead of 0,8 — from a half-squaring error, say. Road one would then report 20 equals 8 plus 1,0 times delta t, giving 12 seconds. Road two, immune to your slip, would still say 210 over 14 is 15 seconds. Twelve against fifteen — alarm bell, while you can still fix it. That is the anatomy of a good check: independence. A check that reuses your possibly-wrong value is a mirror, not a judge — it smiles at whatever you show it. This is also why I nudged you to prefer original-data equations when a choice exists: numbers the question itself gave you cannot carry your mistakes. Two independent roads, one answer — that agreement is the closest thing to certainty an exam room offers.

**Thandi:** Last one: if the bakkie had been slowing down instead of speeding up, what actually changes in the working? People are scared of the minus signs.

**Tutor:** Almost nothing changes — and that is the beauty of it. Same five characters, same four equations, same absent-character rule. The single difference is that the acceleration now points against the motion, so in your direction-of-travel-positive frame it enters the equations as a negative number. Run a braking version: the bakkie at 20 metres per second slows uniformly to 8 metres per second over 210 metres. The no-time equation gives 64 equals 400 plus 420 a, so 420 a equals minus 336, and a equals minus 0,8 metres per second squared. The minus is not an error and not a red flag — it is the physics telling you the acceleration opposes the velocity, which is exactly what braking means. The mistakes to fear are two: dropping the minus midway, which silently converts your braking bakkie into an accelerating one; and panicking at a negative answer and "correcting" it by hand. Do neither. Declare the frame in line one, let every vector carry its honest sign to the final line, and then translate: minus 0,8 in a forward-positive frame reads, in words, as 0,8 metres per second squared opposite to the motion. Signs are directions wearing arithmetic clothes — treat them that way and the fear goes.
