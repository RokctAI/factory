# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the equations of motion — the four-equation toolkit describing anything that moves with constant acceleration in a straight line — and, more importantly, the selection rule that picks the right equation first time. One problem powers the hour: a bakkie accelerates uniformly from 8 metres per second to 20 metres per second while covering 210 metres. We must choose the correct equation, calculate the acceleration, and then find the time taken. The choosing is the genuine skill — and by the end of the hour you will be choosing in seconds.

## Subtopic: The Cast of Symbols and the Four Equations

Every straight-line motion problem speaks in five quantities. Meet them as characters. The initial velocity, v i — how fast the object moves when the clock starts. The final velocity, v f — how fast at the finish. The acceleration, a — the rate at which velocity changes, in metres per second squared. The time, delta t — how long the motion lasts. And the displacement, delta x — the straight-line ground covered, direction included.

One condition rules today: the acceleration must be UNIFORM — constant. The problem's word "uniformly" is your licence to use these equations at all. No uniform acceleration, no equations of motion — which is why the word is planted in the question, and why you should underline it on sight.

Now the four equations. Equation one: v f equals v i plus a delta t — final velocity equals initial velocity plus acceleration times time. It contains no displacement. Equation two: delta x equals v i delta t plus a half a delta t squared — displacement equals initial velocity times time, plus one half times acceleration times time squared. No final velocity. Equation three: v f squared equals v i squared plus two a delta x — final velocity squared equals initial velocity squared plus twice the acceleration times the displacement. No time. Equation four: delta x equals the sum of v i and v f, divided by two, times delta t — displacement equals the average of the two velocities times the time. No acceleration.

Notice what happened as each one was read: the MISSING character was named. Every equation lacks exactly one of the five quantities — and that missing character is the whole secret of choosing. A problem hands you three quantities and asks for a fourth; the fifth, neither given nor wanted, is the one your equation must NOT contain. Identify the absent character; take the equation that excludes it.

The questions for this section are with you now. They cover the five quantities by name and unit, the licence word — uniform — and the absent-character pattern itself: which single quantity each of the four equations leaves out.

## Subtopic: Choosing the Equation and Finding the Acceleration

Back to the bakkie. First, extract the data formally — in an exam this listing earns method marks and prevents chaos.

Given: v i is 8 metres per second. V f is 20 metres per second. Delta x is 210 metres. Wanted: the acceleration, a. Absent — neither given nor wanted: the time, delta t.

The choosing rule now works instantly: we need the equation containing no time. That is equation three: v f squared equals v i squared plus two a delta x. Selected not by luck, not by trial — by the absence of time.

Substitute carefully, units in place: 20 squared equals 8 squared plus two times a times 210.

Evaluate each piece: 20 squared is 400. Eight squared is 64. Two times 210 is 420. So: 400 equals 64 plus 420 a.

Solve: subtract 64 from both sides — 336 equals 420 a. Divide by 420: a equals 336 over 420, which is 0,8.

The acceleration is 0,8 metres per second squared, in the direction of motion — direction spoken, because acceleration is a vector.

Sense-check before moving on, every time. Sign: the bakkie sped up, so a positive acceleration along the direction of travel — correct. Size: gaining just under one metre per second of speed each second is a perfectly ordinary pull-away — believable. An answer of 80 or 0,008 would demand investigation; 0,8 passes cleanly.

Take this section's questions now. They run in the method's order: name the absent quantity, select the equation that excludes it, substitute with units, and judge 0,8 metres per second squared as a physically sensible acceleration rather than a mere calculator output.

## Subtopic: Finding the Time — Two Roads to One Answer

Second task: the time taken. Refresh the data audit — four quantities now stand known: v i is 8 metres per second, v f is 20 metres per second, delta x is 210 metres, and a is 0,8 metres per second squared. Wanted: delta t. With four knowns, more than one equation can carry us — a luxury we will spend on verification.

Road one: equation one, v f equals v i plus a delta t — the simplest equation holding time. Substitute: 20 equals 8 plus 0,8 times delta t. Subtract 8: 12 equals 0,8 delta t. Divide: delta t equals 12 over 0,8 — 15 seconds exactly.

Road two — the check: equation four, which never touches our calculated acceleration: delta x equals the average of v i and v f, times delta t. The average of 8 and 20 metres per second is 14 metres per second. So 210 equals 14 times delta t, giving delta t equals 210 over 14 — 15 seconds again.

Dwell on what just happened. Road two used ONLY original data — had our acceleration been wrong, road one would have delivered a different time from road two, and the disagreement would have exposed the error while it could still be fixed. Two independent roads agreeing is the strongest verification an exam allows you. When the clock permits, travel both.

A strategic footnote: where a choice exists, prefer equations built from originally given data over ones that lean on your own calculated values. Errors cannot travel through numbers you never computed. Equation four was available from the very start — using it first for the time and equation one as the check would have been equally valid. Either order; the agreement is the prize.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine both roads to the 15 seconds, the average velocity of 14 metres per second, and why a check built from original given data is stronger than one resting on your own calculated values.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The complete method, five steps, for every equations-of-motion problem. Step one: confirm uniform acceleration — the licence. Step two: list given, wanted, and absent quantities, converting units as you list — kilometres per hour must become metres per second before anything else, by dividing by 3,6. Step three: choose the equation missing the absent quantity. Step four: substitute with units, solve algebraically, and state the answer with units AND direction wherever the quantity is a vector. Step five: sense-check size and sign; verify along a second road when time allows.

Now the traps that fill marking reports. Trap one: units — a bakkie doing 72 kilometres per hour entered as 72 metres per second corrupts every later line; 72 divided by 3,6 is 20 metres per second. Convert at the listing stage, never later. Trap two: half-squaring equation three — v f squared means 400, not 20; write the squares out immediately. Trap three: sign errors under braking — a slowing vehicle carries a negative acceleration in the direction-of-motion-positive frame, and dropping that minus reverses the physics. Declare a positive direction and keep every vector's sign loyal to it. Trap four: grabbing whichever equation surfaces first in memory instead of the one the ABSENT character selects — the lucky-grab habit works some days; the absent-character rule works every day.

Where does the toolkit lead? Straight through the rest of school physics. Grade 11 embeds these equations inside Newton's-law problems — forces cause the accelerations these equations then track. Grade 12 lifts them into vertical projectile motion, where gravity supplies a constant 9,8 metres per second squared downward. Mechanics carries the heaviest weight in your final physics assessments, and these four equations thread through all of it. The habits drilled today — list the data, find the absent character, convert the units, verify by a second road — are precisely the habits that senior physics rewards.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five steps in order, the kilometres-per-hour conversion by dividing by 3,6, the full squaring in equation three, and the sign a braking vehicle must carry. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same four equations again — from the passenger seat of a bakkie on a straight farm road. Five facts about any trip, and the one you get to ignore.

## Subtopic: Five Facts About Any Trip

Set the symbols aside. Picture a bakkie pulling onto a long, straight gravel road and steadily building speed. If you had to describe that stretch of driving completely to someone on the phone, how many facts would you actually need?

Five — and you know them all already. How fast it was going when you started watching: the initial velocity, v i. How fast when you stopped watching: the final velocity, v f. How quickly the speed was building: the acceleration, a, in metres per second squared — which just means how many metres per second of fresh speed arrive in each second. How long the stretch lasted: the time, delta t. And how much road it covered: the displacement, delta x.

That is the entire cast — two speeds, a rate of gaining speed, a time and a distance. Every straight-line motion question in Grade 10 concerns those five and nothing else.

One condition rides along, and it does not negotiate: the speed-gaining must be steady — the same extra speed every second. When a question says the vehicle accelerates "uniformly", that word is your permission slip. Lurching through stop-start traffic does not qualify, and neither do these equations.

Now the genuinely useful pattern. There are four equations, and each one is missing exactly one of the five facts. Hear them as sentences. The final speed equals the starting speed plus the acceleration times the time — no distance anywhere. The distance equals the starting speed times the time, plus one half times the acceleration times the time squared — no final speed. The final speed squared equals the starting speed squared plus two times the acceleration times the distance — no time. And the distance equals the average of the two speeds times the time — no acceleration.

Your questions for this section are with you now, and they are friendlier than they look. Match each equation to the fact it leaves out, and hold on to the permission word, uniform. Get the five characters straight and the rest of this lesson is ordinary arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The One Fact You Don't Need

Here is the trick that makes choosing painless, and it runs opposite to instinct. Most people hunt for an equation containing what they want. Hunt instead for the fact nobody mentioned.

Our problem: the bakkie speeds up smoothly from 8 metres per second to 20 metres per second while covering 210 metres, and we want the acceleration.

Empty your pockets at the door and write down what you have. Starting speed: 8 metres per second. Final speed: 20 metres per second. Distance: 210 metres. Wanted: the acceleration. Now roll through the five characters — starting speed, final speed, distance, acceleration, time. Time is the one nobody mentioned and nobody requested. So take the equation with no time in it: the final speed squared equals the starting speed squared plus two times the acceleration times the distance.

Feed in the numbers with their units. Twenty squared equals eight squared plus two times the acceleration times 210 metres. Twenty squared is 400; eight squared is 64; two times 210 is 420. So 400 equals 64 plus 420 times the acceleration. Take 64 from both sides: 336 equals 420 times the acceleration. Divide by 420, and the acceleration is 336 over 420 — 0,8 metres per second squared, pointing the way the bakkie travels.

Now say the number out loud as a picture: every second, the bakkie collects 0,8 metres per second of fresh speed. A completely ordinary pull-away on gravel. That sentence is also your free sense check — 80 would mean rocket territory and 0,008 would mean a glacier, but 0,8 sits comfortably in the real world.

That was a full exam calculation, chosen and solved — take the win. The questions for this section are up now: find the fact nobody mentioned, take the equation that leaves it out, substitute with units and solve. And speak the direction along with the acceleration, because acceleration is a vector.

## Subtopic: Checking the Fare Two Ways

Last piece: how long the 210 metres took — and how to know your answer is right before anyone marks it.

The first road uses the acceleration we just found. The final speed equals the starting speed plus the acceleration times the time: 20 equals 8 plus 0,8 times the time. Subtract 8 from both sides: 12 equals 0,8 times the time. Divide by 0,8, and the time is 15 seconds.

The second road never touches that calculated acceleration. The bakkie moved smoothly from 8 to 20 metres per second, so its average speed sat exactly halfway between: 8 plus 20 is 28, and half of 28 is 14 metres per second. Distance equals average speed times time, so 210 metres equals 14 metres per second times the time, and the time is 210 divided by 14 — 15 seconds. The same answer, built purely from numbers that were in the question from the start.

That is the whole point of the second road. Had the acceleration come out wrong, road one would have dragged the mistake forward while road two stayed clean, and the two times would have disagreed — a warning delivered while you can still fix things. It is the same instinct as checking your change at the till twice: once by adding up what you bought, once by subtracting from the note you handed over. Two counts that agree are worth far more than one count made confidently.

Two habits guard everything else. First, units at the door: a speed arriving in kilometres per hour must become metres per second before touching any equation — divide by 3,6. Seventy-two kilometres per hour is 72 000 metres in 3 600 seconds, which is 20 metres per second; feed in 72 as it stands and every later answer inherits the damage. Second, signs: take the direction of travel as positive, and a braking vehicle then carries a negative acceleration — lose that minus and your stopping bakkie becomes a launching one.

These are the last questions of the lesson, and you are ready. Work each one the way you count your change: list what you were given, choose by the missing fact, substitute with units, then check by a second road wherever one exists. That is the habit that turns a good mark into one you can rely on.
