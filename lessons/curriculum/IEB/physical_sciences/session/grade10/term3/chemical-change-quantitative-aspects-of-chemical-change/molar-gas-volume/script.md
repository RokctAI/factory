# Part 1 — Expert

This session puts the molar gas volume to work inside stoichiometry — measuring chemistry not with a balance but with the space a gas fills. One problem drives everything: 13 grams of zinc reacts completely with excess hydrochloric acid, and we must calculate the volume of hydrogen gas produced at STP. Beside it runs a second skill that needs almost no arithmetic at all: reading gas volumes straight off a balanced equation.

## Subtopic: Avogadro's Law and the 22,4 That Fits Every Gas

Begin with the astonishing fact underneath the whole session. Take one mole of carbon dioxide — 44 grams. Take one mole of hydrogen — a feathery 2 grams. Hold both at the same temperature and pressure. They fill exactly the same volume.

That is Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules. The identity of the gas is irrelevant. The explanation is emptiness: a gas is almost entirely space, its molecules separated by distances enormously larger than the molecules themselves. The volume is set by how far apart the molecules sit — and temperature and pressure decide that spacing, not the size or mass of each molecule. Heavy or light, bulky or tiny, the spacing is identical.

Pin the conditions down and the shared volume becomes a working constant. At STP — standard temperature and pressure, zero degrees Celsius, which is 273 kelvin, and 101,3 kilopascals — one mole of ANY gas occupies 22,4 cubic decimetres. That is the molar gas volume, symbol V m, and a cubic decimetre is the same thing as a litre. The working formula as a sentence: V equals n times V m — the volume of a gas equals the moles times 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole, at STP. And inward: n equals V over 22,4 — litres of gas to moles in one division.

Guard the licence conditions from the first minute. The 22,4 belongs to gases only — never to liquids or solids, whose particles touch. And it belongs to STP only — warm the gas or squeeze it and the spacing shifts, which becomes Grade 11's business.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine Avogadro's law and its emptiness argument, the STP conditions by name and number, and the formula V equals n times V m read in both directions.

## Subtopic: From 13 Grams of Solid to Litres of Gas

Now the problem. The reaction: Zn plus 2HCl gives ZnCl2 plus H2 — zinc plus hydrochloric acid gives zinc chloride and hydrogen gas. Check the balance: one zinc each side; two hydrogens each side; two chlorines each side. Balanced.

The word excess earns its place before any arithmetic: the acid is supplied in excess — more than enough — so ALL 13 grams of zinc reacts, and the zinc alone controls the amounts. When one reactant is in excess, base every calculation on the other one.

Now the familiar three-leg journey, with a new exit. Leg one, mass to moles: the molar mass of zinc, straight off the periodic table, is 65 grams per mole; n equals 13 over 65 — 0,2 moles of zinc. Leg two, cross the equation: the ratio of Zn to H2 is one to one, so 0,2 moles of hydrogen gas. Leg three — the new move — moles to VOLUME rather than mass: V equals n times V m — 0,2 moles times 22,4 cubic decimetres per mole — 4,48 cubic decimetres of hydrogen at STP.

Sense-check the size: 4,48 litres is more than two large cooldrink bottles of gas, bubbling out of a spoonful of grey metal. Gases are spectacularly spacious; roomy answers are normal. And had the question wanted mass instead: 0,2 moles times 2 grams per mole is 0,4 grams — the same moles leaving through a different exit door, and a reminder of how nearly weightless hydrogen is.

Take this section's questions now. They walk the legs in order: the role of the word excess, the 0,2 moles at the border, the one-to-one crossing, and the choice of exit — 4,48 cubic decimetres by volume or 0,4 grams by mass.

## Subtopic: Volume Ratios — Stoichiometry Without a Balance

Here is the beautiful shortcut Avogadro's law grants: for gases at the same temperature and pressure, the mole ratio IS the volume ratio. Equal volumes carry equal molecule counts — so measuring volumes is counting molecules.

Take the burning of carbon monoxide: 2CO plus O2 gives 2CO2 — two carbon monoxides plus one oxygen give two carbon dioxides. Read the coefficients directly as volumes: two volumes of carbon monoxide consume one volume of oxygen and deliver two volumes of carbon dioxide. So 20 cubic decimetres of carbon monoxide requires 10 cubic decimetres of oxygen, and produces 20 cubic decimetres of carbon dioxide — no molar masses, no moles, no 22,4; the coefficients simply put on litres.

Notice what the totals whisper: 20 plus 10 is 30 cubic decimetres in, but only 20 come out. Gas volumes need not be conserved — molecules are conserved, and three molecules of reactant become two molecules of product here, so the volume shrinks by a third. Mass is always conserved; volume is not. Keep those two conservation rules in separate pockets and a favourite trick question loses its sting.

Two conditions guard the shortcut. All the gases compared must share the SAME temperature and pressure — though not necessarily STP; the ratio holds at any shared conditions, which is exactly why it never needs the 22,4. And it covers only the gases in the equation — a solid or a liquid holds no volume claim; only species tagged with the little g in brackets may join the ratio.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the ratio read straight from coefficients, the 10 and 20 cubic decimetres of the carbon monoxide example, why total gas volume may shrink while mass cannot, and which species are allowed into a volume ratio.

## Subtopic: The Gas Toolkit, the Traps, and Where This Leads

Assemble the toolkit. Tool one: V equals n times V m — moles to litres at STP, and back by division. Tool two: the three-leg journey with a volume door — mass to moles, ratio across, moles to volume. Tool three: coefficients read directly as volume ratios for gases at shared conditions. Choose by what the question supplies: grams given, travel through moles; litres of one gas given and litres of another wanted, ride the volume ratio straight across.

Now the traps. Trap one: deploying 22,4 when the question never said STP — no standard conditions, no licence; state the assumption or use the data provided. Trap two: applying 22,4 to a liquid or solid — one mole of liquid water occupies about 18 cubic centimetres, nowhere near 22,4 cubic decimetres; the constant is a gas-only privilege. Trap three: unit slippage between cubic centimetres and cubic decimetres — one thousand cubic centimetres make one cubic decimetre, so 560 cubic centimetres is 0,56 cubic decimetres before it meets any formula. Trap four: ignoring the word excess and building the calculation on the wrong reactant. Trap five: expecting gas volumes to balance like masses — volumes answer to molecule counts, not to conservation.

Where this leads: straight into Grade 11, where the ideal gas equation retires the STP shortcut and prices a gas at ANY temperature and pressure — the 22,4 becomes the special case you finally understand from the inside. Volume relationships run the industrial chemistry you will meet in Grade 12: ammonia plants and sulfuric acid plants are engineered around gas ratios. And in the laboratory, collecting gas in a syringe above a fizzing flask, this is the arithmetic that says whether the experiment worked.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the three tools matched to question types, the five traps — STP licence, gas-only, units, excess, and false volume conservation — and the 18 cubic centimetres that expose the liquid-water error. Answer them as a toolkit inspection, tool by tool.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same chemistry again — with courier crates and a fizzing spoonful of grey metal.

## Subtopic: Same Crate, Whatever the Cargo

Picture a courier depot with one iron rule: every crate is the same size, whatever goes inside. A crate of pillows, a crate of engine parts — wildly different masses, identical crates, because the depot packs by COUNT: a thousand items per crate, always.

Gases run on exactly that rule. At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of any gases hold the same number of molecules — Avogadro's law. The molecules sit so far apart that their own size never matters; a gas is mostly nothing, and the nothing is what you measure. Featherweight hydrogen, heavyweight carbon dioxide: same count, same space.

Fix the depot's conditions — zero degrees Celsius and 101,3 kilopascals, the pair called STP — and the crate acquires a number: one mole of any gas fills 22,4 cubic decimetres, and a cubic decimetre is just a litre. That is the molar gas volume. One mole of hydrogen: 22,4 litres. One mole of carbon dioxide, twenty-two times heavier: still 22,4 litres. The crate never asks about the cargo.

And the system works in both directions, like all good packing. Moles to litres: multiply by 22,4. Litres back to moles: divide by 22,4. A classmate reports 5,6 litres of gas collected at STP and you know at a glance — a quarter of a crate — that they hold a quarter of a mole, whatever the gas may be.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Hold the crate picture: one size for every gas, the size is 22,4 at STP only, and multiplying or dividing by 22,4 is nothing but packing or unpacking crates.

## Subtopic: A Spoonful of Zinc, a Balloon of Gas

Now the problem in kitchen terms. Drop 13 grams of zinc granules — a heaped spoonful of grey metal — into plenty of hydrochloric acid, and it fizzes steadily, streaming bubbles you could catch in a balloon. The question: how much gas is that fizz, measured in litres at STP?

The word plenty is working hard. The acid is in excess — more than enough — so every granule of zinc reacts, and the zinc alone dictates the amounts. Always build the calculation on the ingredient that runs out, never the one swimming in surplus.

Walk the journey. Zinc to moles: one mole of zinc weighs 65 grams, straight from the periodic table, so 13 grams is 13 over 65 — a fifth of a mole. Cross the recipe: the balanced equation promises one hydrogen molecule for every zinc atom — one to one — so a fifth of a mole of gas. Pack the crates: a fifth of 22,4 cubic decimetres is 4,48 cubic decimetres. From one spoonful of metal, more than two large cooldrink bottles of hydrogen.

That surprise is worth pocketing: solids are cramped, gases are roomy. The atoms that sat shoulder-to-shoulder in 13 grams of dense metal end up spread through four and a half litres the moment their partners become gas. When a gas answer arrives looking spacious, it is usually right; when a gas answer arrives tiny — a few cubic centimetres from grams of solid — go hunting for the arithmetic slip.

The questions for this section are up now. Follow the same order every time: check who is in excess, get the solid into moles, cross the equation, and only then bring in the 22,4 — it enters at the final step, never the first.

## Subtopic: Recipes Measured in Litres

Last tool, and it is the laziest one in the best possible way. When every substance in the question is a gas, all at the same temperature and pressure, you may skip moles entirely: the equation's coefficients ARE the volume recipe.

Burning carbon monoxide: two carbon monoxides plus one oxygen give two carbon dioxides. Read it like a cooldrink mix: two cups of this, one cup of that, makes two cups of the blend. So 20 litres of carbon monoxide wants 10 litres of oxygen, and the reward is 20 litres of carbon dioxide. No molar masses, no 22,4, barely a calculator — the coefficients were the answer all along.

But look at the strange arithmetic on the totals: 30 litres of ingredients became 20 litres of product. Nothing leaked. Molecules are conserved — three reactant molecules genuinely become two product molecules, and fewer molecules at the same spacing means less volume. Mass always balances; volume follows the molecule count. Hold both sentences and the favourite trick question — where did the volume go? — answers itself: into fewer molecules.

Two lines of small print close the deal. The gases need the SAME conditions as each other, though not necessarily STP — the ratio compares crates, and any shared crate size cancels out. And only gases ride: a solid or liquid in the equation contributes no volume to the recipe — read the state symbols and count only the letter g.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Ask one thing of each question first: is everything here a gas at shared conditions? If yes, read the recipe straight off the coefficients. If no, take the long road through moles — and let the 22,4 wait at the exit where it belongs.
