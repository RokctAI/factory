**Thandi:** Class questions before we close! First: why can't we just divide the grams by the litres and call that the concentration? It feels like the same idea.

**Tutor:** It is the same instinct, but the wrong counting unit — and chemistry lives or dies by its counting unit. Grams weigh matter; moles count particles. Two solutions can hold identical masses of different solutes and contain wildly different numbers of dissolved particles, because particles come in different sizes: 10,6 grams of sodium carbonate is 0,1 mole of formula units, while 10,6 grams of a lighter compound would be far more particles. Since every chemical event — reacting, conducting, changing colour — happens particle by particle, a useful concentration must count, not weigh. That is why the formula insists on n over V, with n in moles. Grams-per-litre does exist as a unit in industry, but the chemistry you are building — titrations next year, equilibrium after that — runs entirely on moles per cubic decimetre. So make the conversion reflexive: any mass in a concentration problem stops at the molar mass tollgate first. Mass, divided by molar mass, becomes moles; only then does the volume get involved. Two divisions, strict order, no shortcuts.

***

**Thandi:** Next: in the empirical formula method, someone asked why we're allowed to just invent a 100 gram sample. Isn't that cheating with data we don't have?

**Tutor:** It looks like cheating and is actually the cleanest move in the method — because the question we are answering is a RATIO question, and ratios are size-blind. The empirical formula asks how many atoms of each kind there are for every atom of another kind. Whether you analyse a gram of the compound or a truckload, that ratio is identical — the way a recipe stays the same whether you bake one loaf or fifty. So we are free to pick the imaginary sample size that makes the arithmetic friendliest, and 100 grams is that size, because percentages convert to masses with no work at all: 52,2 percent becomes 52,2 grams by pure convenience. Pick 200 grams instead and every mass doubles — and then every mole value doubles, and the division by the smallest cancels the doubling perfectly, landing on the same 2 to 6 to 1. Try that once at home as an experiment; watching the assumption wash out of the answer is the moment most people finally trust the method. The 100 grams is scaffolding: it holds the calculation up, and the finished ratio contains no trace of it.

***

**Thandi:** Then the one that half the class got wrong in practice: if a compound is 52,2 percent carbon by mass, why is it wrong to say about half its atoms are carbon?

**Tutor:** Because mass percentages and atom counts speak different languages, and heavy atoms distort the exchange rate. A carbon atom outweighs a hydrogen atom twelve times, so carbon can dominate the scale while being outnumbered on the atom count. Run our own compound as the proof. By mass: 52,2 percent carbon, 13,0 percent hydrogen. Now count atoms from the formula we derived, C2H6O: of every nine atoms, two are carbon — about 22 percent — while six are hydrogen — almost 67 percent. So the element contributing barely 13 percent of the mass supplies two-thirds of the atoms, and the mass-dominant carbon is a minority by count. That inversion is exactly why the method forces every mass through a division by atomic mass before any ratio is formed: dividing by 12 for carbon and by 1 for hydrogen converts scale-language into count-language. Keep the slogan from the lesson: only moles count atoms. Any ratio built straight from masses or percentages is comparing kilograms of bricks with kilograms of feathers and calling it a headcount.

**Thandi:** Last one: the washing soda thing sounds impossible — how can crystals be two-thirds water and still pour like dry salt? And is that water really gone if the box stands open?

**Tutor:** It feels impossible because we picture water only as a liquid, and the water in washing soda is not liquid — it is architecture. Each water molecule occupies a fixed position in the crystal lattice, ten of them arranged around every sodium carbonate unit, held by the same kind of electrostatic attractions that hold the rest of the crystal together. Locked in place, they cannot flow, cannot wet your fingers, cannot drip — so the crystal is genuinely dry to the touch while being 62,9 percent water by mass. It is water serving as building material. And yes — that water can leave. Washing soda gives its water up unusually easily: crystals left in dry air surrender it slowly, losing their glassy transparency and crumbling to a dull powder, and the balance records the loss on the way to that 62,9 percent figure. The reverse happens too — some dried powders grab moisture back from the air to rebuild their lattices. For your exams, the skills are the invoice and the check: price the anhydrous part and the waters separately, add, take the water's fraction, and know that a gentle-heating experiment with two weighings is how a laboratory confirms the number your calculator produced.
