# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds stoichiometry — the craft of predicting exactly how much product a reaction can deliver — and percentage yield, the honest comparison between that prediction and what the laboratory actually collects. One problem carries everything: 42 grams of magnesium carbonate is heated strongly and decomposes into magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide. Calculate the maximum mass of magnesium oxide that can form; then, given that a learner actually collects 17 grams, calculate the percentage yield. Prediction first, honesty second.

## Subtopic: The Balanced Equation Is a Mole Recipe

Write the reaction: MgCO3, heated, gives MgO plus CO2 — magnesium carbonate decomposes into magnesium oxide and carbon dioxide. Check the balance: one magnesium each side, one carbon each side, three oxygens each side. Balanced as written, every coefficient a one.

Now the idea the whole session stands on: the coefficients of a balanced equation are a MOLE ratio. This equation says one mole of magnesium carbonate produces one mole of magnesium oxide and one mole of carbon dioxide. It does NOT say one gram gives one gram — mass ratios are never read from coefficients, because a mole of each substance weighs a different amount. The equation is a recipe written in moles, and every stoichiometric calculation is a journey through that recipe.

The journey always has the same three legs. Leg one: convert the given mass to moles — n equals m over M. Leg two: cross the equation using the mole ratio — multiply by the ratio of coefficients. Leg three: convert the answer back from moles into whatever the question wants — mass, particles, or gas volume. Mass to moles, ratio across, moles to mass. Grams never jump the equation directly; they change into moles at the border and change back on the far side.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine what the coefficients do and do not promise, the three legs of the journey in order, and why a mass may never cross the equation without becoming moles first.

## Subtopic: The Prediction — 42 Grams In, How Much Out

Leg one: the given mass to moles. Molar mass of MgCO3, atom by atom: magnesium 24, carbon 12, three oxygens at 16 make 48 — total 84 grams per mole. So n equals m over M — 42 grams divided by 84 grams per mole is 0,5 moles of magnesium carbonate.

Leg two: cross the equation. The ratio of MgCO3 to MgO is one to one — so 0,5 moles of carbonate yields 0,5 moles of magnesium oxide. This leg looks trivial today precisely because the coefficients are ones; tomorrow's equations will demand two thirds or three halves here, and the habit of writing the leg out will earn its keep.

Leg three: moles back to mass. Molar mass of MgO: magnesium 24 plus oxygen 16 — 40 grams per mole. Mass equals n times M: 0,5 moles times 40 grams per mole — 20 grams. The maximum possible mass of magnesium oxide from 42 grams of magnesium carbonate is 20 grams. This is the theoretical yield.

Now the elegant check, and it costs nothing: conservation of mass. The carbon dioxide escapes as gas — 0,5 moles at 44 grams per mole is 22 grams. Products together: 20 plus 22 is 42 grams — exactly the mass we started with. Atoms are never created or destroyed; if your product masses refuse to total the reactant mass in a decomposition, an error is hiding upstream.

Take this section's questions now. They follow the three legs: the 0,5 moles at the border, the one-to-one crossing, the 20 grams of theoretical yield, and the conservation check that ties 20 plus 22 back to 42.

## Subtopic: Percentage Yield — the Honest Number

The learner heats the 42 grams, collects the white solid, weighs it: 17 grams. Not 20. The laboratory has fallen short of the prediction — and it nearly always does.

Percentage yield measures the shortfall: the actual yield divided by the theoretical yield, times one hundred. Substitute: 17 divided by 20, times 100. Compute: 17 over 20 is 0,85. The percentage yield is 85 percent.

Why not one hundred? For honest, physical reasons. The reaction may be incomplete — carbonate at the centre of the lumps never got hot enough to decompose. Product is lost in transfers — dust left in the crucible, powder clinging to the spatula. Side reactions may consume reactant. And sometimes a reverse reaction claws product back. Every laboratory chemist plans around yields below one hundred percent, and industrial chemists fight for every point, because at factory scale one percent is tonnes.

Now the two directions of suspicion. An 85 percent yield is credible. A 40 percent yield says the technique leaked product at every step. And a yield ABOVE one hundred percent is never good news — it announces a fault: the product is wet, or contaminated with undecomposed carbonate, or the weighing is wrong. More out than the atoms allow is impossible; the balance is weighing something extra, not the reaction overachieving.

This section's questions are in front of you. They examine the formula — actual over theoretical, times one hundred — the 85 percent answer, the physical reasons yields fall short, and the correct diagnosis of a yield over one hundred.

## Subtopic: The Full Method, the Classic Traps, and Where This Leads

The complete method for every stoichiometry problem, five steps. Step one: write and balance the equation — an unbalanced equation poisons every number after it. Step two: convert the given quantity to moles. Step three: cross the equation with the mole ratio — coefficients of wanted over given. Step four: convert to the units the question demands. Step five: if an actual yield is given, close with percentage yield — actual over theoretical, times one hundred — and sense-check that it sits below one hundred.

The traps, in the order markers meet them. Trap one: skipping the balance check — with a wrongly balanced equation the mole ratio is fiction. Trap two: reading coefficients as masses — one to one in moles is 84 to 40 in grams for this reaction; the two ratios agree only by accident. Trap three: molar masses with ignored subscripts — the three oxygens in MgCO3 contribute 48 grams, never 16. Trap four: dividing theoretical by actual — the inverted fraction announces itself as a yield of 118 percent, and the over-one-hundred alarm should ring instantly. Trap five: forgetting that the theoretical yield is a ceiling, not a promise — phrases like maximum mass and at most are stoichiometry's fingerprints in a question.

Where this leads: everywhere. Grade 11 adds the limiting reagent — two given quantities racing to run out first — and the gas laws join the party. Grade 12 runs titrations, electrolysis and industrial chemistry on this identical three-leg journey, and quantitative chemistry carries heavy weight in your final assessments. Master the journey now and every later topic is this lesson wearing new substances.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five steps in order, the traps — especially coefficients misread as masses — and the meaning of theoretical yield as a ceiling. Answer them as method, not memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same calculation again — from a bakery. A recipe, a batch, and the rolls that actually reach the shelf.

## Subtopic: A Recipe Promises, in Its Own Units

A bread recipe says: one batch of dough gives one tray of rolls. That sentence is a ratio — batches to trays — and notice what it never says: it does not promise that a kilogram of dough gives a kilogram of rolls. Dough loses steam in the oven; the promise's units are batches and trays, never kilograms.

A balanced chemical equation is precisely that kind of promise. MgCO3 heated gives MgO plus CO2 reads: one mole of magnesium carbonate delivers one mole of magnesium oxide and releases one mole of carbon dioxide gas. The promise is written in moles — chemistry's batches — and never directly in grams. One mole of the carbonate weighs 84 grams while one mole of the oxide weighs just 40, the way one batch of dough and one tray of rolls weigh different amounts. Same count, different masses.

So every question of the how-much-comes-out type follows the same route through the recipe. Turn your grams into moles at the door — divide by the molar mass. Walk across the equation on the promised ratio. Turn the moles back into grams at the far door — multiply by the new substance's molar mass. In through one door, across the recipe, out through the other — and the two doors have different exchange rates, because the two substances weigh differently per mole.

Your questions for this section are ready. Keep one sentence in your pocket: the equation promises in moles, the balance speaks in grams, and the molar mass translates at each door.

## Subtopic: Forty-Two Grams Through the Bakery

Run the actual problem in bakery language. You carry 42 grams of magnesium carbonate to the door. One mole of it costs 84 grams: magnesium 24, carbon 12, and three oxygens totalling 48. So 42 grams is 42 over 84 — half a mole. Half a batch of raw material.

Cross the recipe: the equation promises one mole of magnesium oxide for every mole of carbonate — one tray per batch. Half a batch in, half a tray out: 0,5 moles of magnesium oxide.

Out through the far door: one mole of magnesium oxide weighs 40 grams — magnesium 24 plus oxygen 16. Half a mole is 20 grams. The oven can deliver at most 20 grams of magnesium oxide. That word — most — matters: 20 grams is the ceiling the atoms allow, the theoretical yield.

And where did the other 22 grams go? Not lost — carried away. The recipe also promised half a mole of carbon dioxide, and half of 44 grams is 22 grams of gas that left for the air. Add the two products: 20 plus 22 is 42, every gram of the original carbonate accounted for. Atoms never vanish in a reaction; they change address. When your products refuse to add up in a decomposition like this, the mistake lives in your arithmetic, not in the atoms.

The questions for this section are up now. Walk each one through the doors in order — grams to moles, ratio across, moles to grams — and let the mass balance be your free final check.

## Subtopic: What Actually Reaches the Shelf

The recipe promised a full tray, and every baker knows the next chapter: a roll sticks to the pan, one scorches at the edge, the tray's corner never rose. The shelf receives less than the promise. Chemistry is the same: our learner weighed the collected magnesium oxide and found 17 grams against a promised 20.

Percentage yield is the shelf count: what you actually got, divided by what the recipe promised, times one hundred. Here: 17 over 20 is 0,85 — an 85 percent yield. Seventeen twentieths of the promise reached the shelf, and in a school laboratory that is respectable baking.

The shortfall has ordinary causes, worth naming because questions ask for them. Some carbonate at the middle of the lumps never got hot enough — an unfinished bake. Some powder stayed behind on the crucible and spatula — rolls stuck to the pan. And in other reactions, side reactions nibble the ingredients — dough pinched before it ever reaches the oven.

Now the diagnosis that catches the most people: a yield above one hundred percent. It sounds like triumph — more product than promised! It is always a fault. The atoms in 42 grams of carbonate can build 20 grams of magnesium oxide and not a gram more; if the balance claims more, it is weighing something extra — moisture the powder pulled from the air, or undecomposed carbonate mixed through the product. Extra mass on the shelf means a contaminated tray, never a generous oven.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Answer with the bakery in your head: the recipe promises in batches, the ceiling is set by the atoms, and the shelf count over the promise — times one hundred — is the honest measure of the day's work.
