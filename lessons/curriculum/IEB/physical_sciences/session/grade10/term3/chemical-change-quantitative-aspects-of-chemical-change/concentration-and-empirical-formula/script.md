# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds two of the sharpest tools in quantitative chemistry: concentration — how much substance is dissolved in each cubic decimetre of solution — and the empirical formula, a compound's recipe recovered from nothing but percentages. Two problems drive everything. First: 10,6 grams of sodium carbonate is dissolved to make 200 cubic centimetres of solution; calculate the concentration. Second: a compound contains 52,2 percent carbon, 13,0 percent hydrogen and 34,8 percent oxygen by mass; determine its empirical formula. Both problems reward one identical habit — moles first, everything else after.

## Subtopic: Concentration — Moles per Cubic Decimetre

Concentration answers a single question: how crowded is this solution? The formal definition: concentration, symbol c, equals the number of moles of solute, n, divided by the volume of solution, V, in cubic decimetres. As a sentence: c equals n over V — the concentration equals the moles dissolved, divided by the volume holding them. The unit is moles per cubic decimetre, written mol per dm cubed — and a cubic decimetre is exactly a litre.

Two conversions stand guard at the formula's door. Volume usually arrives in cubic centimetres, while the formula speaks cubic decimetres: divide cubic centimetres by one thousand — 200 cubic centimetres is 0,2 cubic decimetres. And the solute usually arrives in grams, while the formula speaks moles: divide the mass by the molar mass — n equals m over M, the tool you already carry.

Now the first problem. Sodium carbonate is Na2CO3. Molar mass, atom by atom from the periodic table: two sodiums at 23 give 46, one carbon 12, three oxygens at 16 give 48 — 46 plus 12 plus 48 is 106 grams per mole. Moles: n equals m over M — 10,6 grams divided by 106 grams per mole is 0,1 moles. Volume: 200 cubic centimetres divided by one thousand is 0,2 cubic decimetres. Concentration: c equals n over V — 0,1 moles divided by 0,2 cubic decimetres — 0,5 moles per cubic decimetre.

Sense-check: 0,1 moles crowded into a fifth of a cubic decimetre means a full cubic decimetre at the same crowding would hold five times as much — 0,5 moles. The division simply rescaled the crowd to a standard container.

The questions for this section are with you now. They examine the formula read as a sentence, the two conversions — grams to moles, cubic centimetres to cubic decimetres — and the 0,5 moles per cubic decimetre answer with its reasoning.

## Subtopic: Percentage Composition — the Compound's Mass Recipe

Before recovering formulas, master the reverse skill: given a formula, what percentage of the compound's mass does each element supply?

The rule: percentage of an element equals the mass of that element in one mole of the compound, divided by the molar mass of the compound, times one hundred.

Take ammonia, NH3. Molar mass: one nitrogen at 14 plus three hydrogens at 1 — 17 grams per mole. Nitrogen's share: 14 out of 17 — 14 divided by 17 times 100 is 82,4 percent. Hydrogen's share: 3 out of 17 — 17,6 percent. Check: 82,4 plus 17,6 is one hundred. That check is not decoration; percentages refusing to total one hundred are announcing an arithmetic error before any marker needs to.

One more: methane, CH4, molar mass 16. Carbon's share: 12 divided by 16 times 100 — 75 percent. The four hydrogens together: 4 out of 16 — 25 percent. Note the subscript discipline: the four hydrogens contribute as a team of four grams, never one.

Why this matters beyond marks: percentage composition is measurable in a laboratory. A chemist holding an unknown powder can burn it, weigh the products, and recover these percentages without ever seeing a formula — which sets up the next move: running the whole calculation backwards.

Work through this section's questions now. They examine the rule as a fraction of the molar mass, the ammonia percentages of 82,4 and 17,6, and the totalling check that catches errors before anyone else does.

## Subtopic: Empirical Formula — the Recipe Recovered from Percentages

Now the second problem: 52,2 percent carbon, 13,0 percent hydrogen, 34,8 percent oxygen. Find the empirical formula — the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in the compound.

The method has four steps, and step one is a gift: assume a sample of exactly 100 grams. Every percentage instantly becomes a mass — 52,2 grams of carbon, 13,0 grams of hydrogen, 34,8 grams of oxygen. The assumption is legitimate because a ratio does not care how much sample you imagine; one hundred grams merely makes the arithmetic immediate.

Step two: convert each mass to moles, because atoms combine in mole ratios, never in mass ratios. Carbon: 52,2 divided by 12 is 4,35 moles. Hydrogen: 13,0 divided by 1 is 13,0 moles. Oxygen: 34,8 divided by 16 is 2,175 moles.

Step three: divide every answer by the smallest of them, 2,175. Carbon: 4,35 over 2,175 is 2. Hydrogen: 13,0 over 2,175 is 5,98 — call it 6. Oxygen: 2,175 over 2,175 is 1.

Step four: read off the whole-number ratio — two carbons to six hydrogens to one oxygen. The empirical formula is C2H6O.

One refinement completes the method. If step three leaves a stubborn fraction like 1,5 or 1,33, never round it away — multiply the entire ratio by 2 or by 3 until every number is whole. A ratio of 1 to 1,5 is honestly 2 to 3; rounding the 1,5 down to 1 invents a different compound altogether.

Take this section's questions now. They run the method in order: the 100 gram assumption and why it is allowed, the division by atomic mass, the division by the smallest, and the multiply-up rule for ratios that arrive as fractions.

## Subtopic: Water of Crystallisation, the Full Method and the Traps

Some crystals lock water molecules into their structure in a fixed ratio — water of crystallisation. Washing soda is Na2CO3 dot 10H2O: every formula unit travels with exactly ten water molecules. Warm the crystals and the water departs; the glassy crystals crumble to white powder and the sample loses mass — percentage composition performing live on a laboratory balance.

Compute the water's share. Molar mass of Na2CO3: 46 plus 12 plus 48 — 106 grams per mole. The ten waters: 10 times 18 is 180 grams. Whole crystal: 286 grams per mole. Water's percentage: 180 divided by 286 times 100 — 62,9 percent. Nearly two-thirds of the mass of washing soda crystals is water, sitting inside an apparently dry jar.

Now the traps that fill marking reports on this whole lesson. Trap one: volume left in cubic centimetres — a concentration computed with 200 instead of 0,2 shrinks the answer a thousandfold; convert at the data-listing stage. Trap two: mass ratios treated as atom ratios — 52,2 percent carbon does not mean 52,2 percent of the atoms are carbon; only moles count atoms. Trap three: rounding a 1,5 in the ratio instead of doubling everything. Trap four: subscripts ignored in molar masses — the three in Na2CO3's oxygen trio and the ten in 10H2O are multiplication instructions, not decorations.

Where this leads: concentration is the working language of titrations and acid-base chemistry in Grade 11 and Grade 12, and the empirical formula method returns in organic chemistry, where combustion percentages identify unknown compounds. The moles-first habit drilled today is the entire foundation.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the 62,9 percent of water locked in washing soda, the four traps, and the method steps in order. Answer them as method rather than memory.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

The same two tools again — from the kitchen and the corner shop. Cooldrink strength, and a recipe recovered from its label.

## Subtopic: How Strong Is the Cooldrink

Think about mixing concentrate with water on a hot day. You already understand concentration perfectly; no one ever confuses strong cooldrink with weak. Strong means plenty of concentrate in the jug; weak means the same concentrate drowned in a bigger jug. Chemistry's concentration is precisely that idea with the fuzziness removed: count the dissolved stuff in moles, measure the jug in cubic decimetres — a cubic decimetre is just a litre — and divide. Concentration equals moles over volume: c equals n over V.

Our problem in jug language: 10,6 grams of sodium carbonate in a fifth of a litre of solution. First count the packs, because chemistry counts in moles. One mole of Na2CO3 weighs 106 grams — two sodiums at 23, a carbon at 12, three oxygens at 16, all read off the periodic table. So 10,6 grams is 10,6 divided by 106 — 0,1 moles. The jug: 200 cubic centimetres is 0,2 cubic decimetres; cubic centimetres shrink by a thousand, always. Now divide: 0,1 moles in 0,2 cubic decimetres is 0,5 moles per cubic decimetre.

Say it as cooldrink: this solution carries 0,5 moles in every full litre. Two jugs of different sizes can hold the same total concentrate and taste completely different — concentration is about crowding, not totals. That is exactly why the volume must sit under the division line.

Your questions for this section are ready now. Hold two rules: moles first — grams are never divided by litres directly; and cubic centimetres shrink by a thousand before they enter the formula. Land those two conversions and the formula is a single division.

## Subtopic: Reading the Recipe Backwards

A recipe lists ingredients and you produce the dish. The empirical formula problem is the reverse: you hold the finished dish, and the label admits only what percentage of the mass is flour, what percentage sugar, what percentage butter — and you must reconstruct the recipe.

Here is the trick that makes it easy: pretend you have exactly 100 grams of the dish. Nothing is lost in pretending, because a recipe is a ratio — double the dish, same recipe. With 100 grams in hand, the label's percentages become masses on a scale: 52,2 grams of carbon, 13,0 grams of hydrogen, 34,8 grams of oxygen.

But an atomic recipe is counted in atoms, not weighed in grams — a bag of flour and a teaspoon of yeast can matter equally. So convert each mass to moles, chemistry's counting unit: carbon, 52,2 divided by 12, gives 4,35. Hydrogen, 13,0 divided by 1, gives 13,0. Oxygen, 34,8 divided by 16, gives 2,175.

Now simplify the ratio the way you simplify any recipe — divide everything by the smallest number, 2,175. Carbon becomes 2, hydrogen becomes 6, oxygen becomes 1. Two carbons, six hydrogens, one oxygen: C2H6O. The recipe is recovered from nothing but the label.

The questions for this section are up now. Work them in the same three moves: pretend 100 grams, weigh each ingredient out in moles, then divide by the smallest. And if the ratio hands you one and a half of something, double the whole recipe — half atoms are not on the menu.

## Subtopic: The Water Hiding in Dry Crystals

Last piece — a fact that startles nearly everyone. Washing soda crystals — glassy, dry to the touch, poured from a box — are nearly two-thirds water by mass. The water is built into the crystal itself, ten water molecules escorting every Na2CO3 unit, and the formula admits it openly: Na2CO3 dot 10H2O. Leave the crystals in warm air and you can watch the truth: they lose their glassy shine, crumble to white powder, and weigh less on the balance — lighter by exactly the water's share.

How big is that share? Price the formula like an invoice. Na2CO3: two sodiums 46, one carbon 12, three oxygens 48 — 106 grams per mole. The ten waters: ten times 18 is 180 grams. Whole crystal: 286 grams per mole. Water's slice: 180 out of 286, times 100 — 62,9 percent. The balance in the laboratory agrees with the arithmetic on paper, and that agreement is the whole point of percentage composition: formulas make predictions a scale can check.

Keep the two habits that guard everything in this lesson. Moles before anything — grams never enter a ratio and never sit over a volume without becoming moles first. And subscripts are multipliers — the three oxygens in Na2CO3, the ten in 10H2O, each command a multiplication, and skipping one poisons every later line.

The last questions of the lesson are with you now, and you are ready. Answer with the invoice picture in mind: read the formula atom by atom, price each atom from the periodic table, add carefully, and let the percentages confess what the compound is made of.
