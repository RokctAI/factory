# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the working instrument of all mechanics: the force diagram, and its stripped-down cousin the free-body diagram. One problem anchors the hour — an 8 kilogram wooden box is dragged at constant velocity across a rough workshop floor by a strap held at 25 degrees above the horizontal; draw the free-body diagram, naming the weight, the normal force, the applied force and the friction. Every Newton's-laws question you will meet, through Grade 11 and deep into your final exams, opens with this drawing done properly.

## Subtopic: Forces Are Interactions — the Cast Around the Box

A force is a push or a pull — always an interaction between two objects. That word "interaction" is your shield against invented forces: every arrow on your page must have a second object answerable for it. No responsible object, no force. Full stop.

So interrogate the box. Who, right now, is acting on it?

First, the Earth — acting at a distance, no touching required. Gravity pulls the box straight down, and we call that pull the weight, symbol w or F g. Its size is mass times gravitational acceleration: 8 kilograms times 9,8 metres per second squared gives 78,4 newtons, downward. Keep mass and weight in separate drawers. Mass — 8 kilograms — is the amount of matter, a scalar, identical on the Moon. Weight — 78,4 newtons — is a force, a vector, born of gravity, and smaller on the Moon. Assessors return to this distinction endlessly.

Second, the floor — touching. It presses back on the box, perpendicular to its own surface. That perpendicular press is the normal force, N; "normal" is simply the mathematician's word for perpendicular. Without it, the box would fall through the boards.

Third, the strap — touching. The person pulls through the strap: the applied force, F applied, aimed along the strap at 25 degrees above the horizontal.

Fourth, the roughness of the floor — friction, f. The box slides forward, so friction drags horizontally backward, set against the sliding.

Four forces, four responsible objects: Earth, floor, strap, rough surface. Notice what is absent: no "force of motion", no "force of momentum". Motion is not an object and pushes nothing — smuggling it onto the diagram is the oldest error in mechanics.

The questions for this section are with you now. Put every force they mention through the interaction rule — name the responsible object or throw the force out — and keep 8 kilograms and 78,4 newtons strictly apart: different quantities, different units, and only one of them is a vector.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Free-Body Diagram

Now the drawing itself, and the distinction between the two diagram types you are expected to know. A force diagram lays the force arrows over a sketch of the real scene — box, floor, strap and all. A FREE-body diagram deletes the scene: the box collapses to a single dot, and only the force arrows remain, every one beginning on that dot. Free of the scenery — that is the "free".

Build it arrow by arrow.

Arrow one: the weight, w — from the dot straight DOWN. Label it w or F g, and give 78,4 newtons if values are requested.

Arrow two: the normal force, N — from the dot straight UP, perpendicular to the floor. Hold off on its length: the final subtopic will show it is NOT simply equal to the weight here, and noticing that is what separates the strongest answers.

Arrow three: the applied force, F applied — from the dot, slanting up at 25 degrees above the horizontal, pointing the way the strap pulls. Write the 25-degree angle in the gap between the arrow and the horizontal; an unmarked angle forfeits credit.

Arrow four: friction, f — from the dot, horizontally BACKWARD, against the sliding.

Now the quality rules a marker applies. Every arrow starts ON the dot and points away. Every arrow ends in an arrowhead, because an arrow without a head has not stated a direction. Every arrow carries its label in accepted symbols or words. Longer arrows for larger forces where the sizes are known. And nothing extra: no floor line, no strap, no sketch of the box — and absolutely no velocity arrow, because speed is not a force and drawing it as one is an instant penalty. One dot, four labelled arrows: that is the full-credit picture.

Work this section's questions now. They test the conventions directly: the single dot, the four labelled arrows leaving it, the 25-degree angle marked against the horizontal, and nothing imported from the scenery — no floor, no strap, and never a velocity arrow among the forces.

## Subtopic: Reading the Physics Off the Diagram — Constant Velocity

Why does the question bother to tell you the velocity is CONSTANT? Because that phrase is physics wearing a disguise. Newton's first law says an object at constant velocity experiences ZERO net force — everything balances. So the diagram you drew is more than a picture; it is a set of equations waiting to be read aloud.

Read horizontally first. The forward pull must balance the backward friction. Careful, though — the strap is slanted, so only PART of the applied force works horizontally: the horizontal component, F applied times cosine of 25 degrees. The balance reads: F applied cos 25 equals f — the horizontal share of the strap's pull equals the friction. In Grade 10 you need the component idea qualitatively; the full calculation arrives in Grade 11 — but grasping that one slanted force splits into a horizontal job and a vertical job is the insight everything later stands on.

Read vertically next, where three forces play. The normal force presses up. The weight pulls down — 78,4 newtons. And the strap lends a hand upward: the vertical component, F applied times sine of 25 degrees. Balance: N plus F applied sin 25 equals w — the floor's push and the strap's lift together carry the weight.

Here is the promised subtlety. Because the strap shoulders part of the load, the floor supplies less than 78,4 newtons. The normal force is LESS than the weight: N equals w minus the strap's upward share. A learner who writes "N always equals w" has memorised a coincidence instead of reading the diagram — and this exact problem is where the memorisation collapses. The normal force is whatever perpendicular push the surface must deliver; read it off the vertical balance, never assume it.

One bonus insight: in later grades friction is proportional to the normal force, so pulling upward at a slant genuinely lightens the drag — smaller normal force, smaller friction. Your diagram has just explained something your arms already knew.

This section's questions are in front of you. Expect the constant-velocity condition to be probed, both balance statements — horizontal and vertical — and the normal force taken from the vertical balance rather than assumed equal to 78,4 newtons.

## Subtopic: The Method, the Error Museum, and the Road Ahead

The complete method for any free-body diagram, five steps. One: isolate the object — one object per diagram, and only forces ON it, never forces it exerts on the world. Two: hunt systematically — gravity first, since it never misses a day; then every touching object: surfaces contribute normal forces and possibly friction, ropes and straps contribute tension along their line, hands contribute applied forces. Three: draw the dot and the labelled arrows, angles marked. Four: read the motion state — rest or constant velocity means all balanced; acceleration means the net force points with the acceleration. Five: write the balance statements, one direction at a time.

The error museum, four exhibits. Exhibit one: the phantom forward "force of motion" — motion needs no force to continue; only CHANGES in motion need force. Exhibit two: normal force set automatically equal to weight — our own problem disproves it; read the vertical balance. Exhibit three: friction drawn opposite the applied force instead of opposite the sliding — they happen to coincide here, but they part ways on slopes; friction opposes relative sliding, always. Exhibit four: floating, unlabelled or headless arrows — easy credit handed straight back.

And where does the drawing lead? Everywhere the subject goes. Grade 11 brings Newton's second law — today's reading with the balance broken, net force equal to mass times acceleration — and inclined planes, where the weight itself is the force being split into components. Grade 12 brings work, energy and power, all computed along directions your diagram exposes. Mechanics carries the heaviest weighting in the physics you will write at the end of school, and every one of those solutions opens with a dot and four or five honest arrows. Draw ten free-body diagrams this week — boxes dragged, trolleys pushed, buckets hanging from ropes — until the hunt becomes reflex.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the five-step method, the direction friction actually takes, and the exhibits in the error museum. Answer in drawing order — isolate first, hunt second, motion state last.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same box, but now from the passage at school — a steel trommel, a strap over the shoulder, and four honest arrows.

## Subtopic: Who Is Actually Pulling on the Box?

Picture hauling a packed steel trommel down a hostel corridor with a strap over your shoulder. Before any formulas, ask the playground question: who is actually doing something to that trommel right now? Not who is nearby — who is pushing or pulling it.

The Earth is, without touching it, pulling straight down. That pull is the weight, and its size is the mass times how hard gravity tugs on each kilogram. Our box weighs in at 8 kilograms of stuff, and gravity claims 9,8 newtons for every kilogram, so 8 times 9,8 gives 78,4 newtons, straight down. As a sentence: weight equals mass times gravitational acceleration.

Two words tangle here, so untangle them once and for all. Mass is how much stuff there is — 8 kilograms, and it stays 8 kilograms on the Moon. Weight is how hard gravity pulls on that stuff — 78,4 newtons on Earth, much less on the Moon, because the Moon tugs gently. Kilograms for mass, newtons for weight, and only the weight is a force.

The floor is doing something too: holding the trommel up, pressing straight up at right angles to the surface — otherwise the trommel would sink through the boards. Any surface's square-on push is the normal force; "normal" is just maths-speak for perpendicular.

The strap is doing something: through it you pull, along the strap's own line — forward and a little upward. That is the applied force.

And the rough floor is doing one more thing: rubbing backward against the slide. That is friction, pointing backwards while the trommel scrapes forwards.

Four actions, four culprits — Earth, floor, strap, roughness. And the bouncer at the door of your diagram: if you cannot name who is doing it, it is not a force. There is no "force of moving forward", because moving is not a someone. Moving is what the trommel is doing, not what is being done to it.

The questions for this section are ready, and you have the tools. For each force, name the culprit in your head — who is pushing or pulling — and check whether mass and weight have been swapped anywhere. Two small habits, and these are comfortable marks.

## Subtopic: A Dot and Four Arrows

Now the drawing, which is easier than it sounds, because most of the work is deleting.

Two pictures live in this topic. In the first you sketch the actual scene — trommel, floor, strap rising to a shoulder — and drape the force arrows over it. That is the force diagram: the story with forces added. In the second you erase the scene entirely. The trommel shrinks to one dot. Floor gone, strap gone, person gone. Only the arrows survive, each one springing from that dot. That is the free-body diagram — free of the scenery.

Listen to ours being assembled, arrow by arrow. From the dot, straight down the page, an arrow labelled w — the weight, 78,4 newtons of Earth's pull. From the same dot, straight up, an arrow labelled N — the floor's push, the normal force. From the same dot, slanting up and forward at 25 degrees above the horizontal, an arrow labelled F applied — the strap's pull, with the 25 written in the wedge between the arrow and the horizontal. And from the same dot, flat and backwards, an arrow labelled f — friction, set dead against the slide. Four arrows leaving one dot, like spokes on a wheel.

Markers check the small print, so learn it. Every arrow starts on the dot and points outward. Every arrow finishes with an arrowhead — no head, no direction shown. Every arrow wears its label, because an anonymous arrow says nothing. Where sizes are known, longer arrows mean bigger forces. And nothing else is welcome: no floor, no strap, no box outline — and above all, no arrow for the speed. A velocity arrow among the forces costs you instantly, because speed is not a force.

Take your questions for this section now, holding the dot in your head: down for weight, up for the normal force, slanted up-and-forward for the strap, backwards for friction. If you can recite those four arrows in order out loud, you can reproduce them under pressure — and that is the skill being bought here.

## Subtopic: Why the Slant Helps — and What "Constant Speed" Is Telling You

Two nuggets remain in this problem, and both fall out free once the arrows stand.

First: why does the question insist the trommel moves at constant velocity? Because the phrase is a fact in disguise. Anything travelling at a steady speed in a straight line is, on balance, not being pushed around — the forces on it cancel exactly. "Constant velocity" is the question quietly telling you: forward equals backward, and up equals down. Nothing is winning, so nothing changes.

Take the directions one at a time. Sideways: the strap hauls the trommel forward and friction rakes it back, and those two must match. But the strap is slanted, so only a share of its pull works sideways. Think of pulling a loaded garden cart by its raised handle — part of your effort rolls it forward, part of it lifts the handle end. One pull, two jobs. Physics names the jobs the horizontal component and the vertical component, and the sideways balance says: the horizontal part of the strap's pull equals the friction. In symbols, F applied times cosine of 25 degrees equals f.

Up and down: three workers. The floor pushes up, the 78,4-newton weight pulls down, and the strap's lifting share helps upward. Balance means the two up-effects together match the one down-effect: the normal force plus the strap's upward part equals the weight. In symbols, N plus F applied times sine of 25 degrees equals w.

Now the sentence that lifts an answer above the crowd. Because the strap carries a slice of the load, the floor no longer carries the whole 78,4 newtons — the normal force here is LESS than the weight. Anyone who learned "normal force equals weight" as a law memorised a coincidence: it holds only when nothing else acts vertically. Read the up-and-down balance fresh, every single time.

And a bonus your body already knew: friction rises and falls with the normal force, so easing the floor's burden eases the drag — which is exactly why hauling the trommel with the strap slanted up over your shoulder beats pushing it flat along the floor. The physics just caught up with your muscles.

These are the lesson's last questions, with you now. Ask two things of each one: what is balancing what, and has the strap's upward help been counted? If you can explain in your own words why the floor pushes with less than 78,4 newtons, you own the sharpest idea in the topic — and Newton's laws next year will feel like an old friend.
